# Highly Dynamic Quadruped Locomotion via Whole-Body Impulse Control and Model Predictive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MIT developed a hybrid control architecture for the Mini-Cheetah robot, combining Model Predictive Control with a novel Whole-Body Impulse Control, enabling untethered running at 3.7 m/s (Froude number 4.65). This approach allows the robot to perform versatile and robust locomotion across diverse gaits and challenging terrains, effectively leveraging the robot's full hardware capabilities.

## Problem

- Existing Whole-Body Control (WBC) struggles with highly dynamic gaits involving frequent aerial phases, as it primarily focuses on strictly tracking commanded body trajectories.
- Model Predictive Control (MPC) often relies on simplified models and lower update frequencies, lacking the precision required for full-body dynamic control and handling high-frequency disturbances.
- A need for a control framework that combines the long-horizon planning of MPC with the high-frequency, full-body control of WBC, specifically tailored for highly dynamic motions with significant aerial phases.

## Method

- A hybrid, hierarchical control architecture combines ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== running at 40 Hz for optimal reaction force and general body/foot position planning over a longer horizon.
- **Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC)** operates at a higher frequency (500 Hz), computing precise joint commands using ==full-body dynamics== while prioritizing tracking the MPC's ==reaction force== commands over strict body trajectory adherence.
- An efficient custom ==dynamics engine== and optimized solvers enable the complex control architecture to run on the robot's onboard, low-power computer, facilitating real-time high-frequency feedback.

## Results

- The Mini-Cheetah robot achieved a stable running speed of **3.7 m/s** on a treadmill (Froude number of **4.65**), setting a new benchmark for untethered quadruped robots of its size.
- The controller successfully demonstrated versatility by executing **six different gaits** (trot, gallop, bounding, pronking, pacing, walk) at speeds over **1 m/s** in outdoor environments without changing control parameters.
- Robust locomotion was maintained on challenging outdoor terrains, including wet grass and gravel, and the control scheme fully utilized the robot's hardware capabilities, pushing joints to their velocity and torque limits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prioritizing the tracking of ground reaction forces, as generated by the MPC, within a Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC) framework allows for natural, dynamic motions, especially during aerial phases, overcoming limitations of strict body trajectory adherence.
> - A hierarchical control architecture, combining lower-frequency Model Predictive Control (MPC) for long-horizon planning with higher-frequency Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC) for precise, full-body execution, effectively bridges the gap between global optimality and real-time robustness.
> - Achieving computational efficiency through simplified models in MPC and optimized full-body dynamics in WBIC is crucial for enabling complex, high-frequency control on the limited onboard processing power of dynamic quadruped robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dynamic legged locomotion is one of the most challenging problems in robotics, requiring sophisticated control schemes to manage complex phenomena such as aerial phases, short ground contact times, and high-speed limb movements. This paper directly addresses the limitations of existing approaches to advance the state-of-the-art in this domain.

Historically, successful demonstrations of dynamic locomotion on bipeds, and quadrupeds have often faced issues of scalability to high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) systems, reliance on specific system dynamics, or lack of detailed documentation. Two prominent control paradigms have emerged to tackle these challenges:

- **Whole-Body Control (WBC):** WBC provides a dynamically consistent and general framework for coordinating multiple robot tasks while accounting for the robot's full body dynamics and contact constraints. However, traditional WBC formulations primarily focus on strictly tracking commanded body trajectories. This proves problematic for highly dynamic gaits (e.g., bounding, galloping, jumping) where the robot's center of mass (CoM) is not always controllable or follows a predictable, smooth trajectory during aerial phases. Attempting to force the robot to follow an exact CoM trajectory in such scenarios can lead to inefficient or unstable motion, as it might conflict with the natural dynamics of the robot.
- **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** MPC is adept at planning optimal trajectories over a longer time horizon, using simplified models to make the optimization computationally tractable. It has proven effective for generating various dynamic gaits at high speeds. However, the use of simplified models and relatively low update frequencies (e.g., 40 Hz) in MPC means it cannot precisely control the full-body dynamics or effectively handle high-frequency disturbances and intricate position control. While full-body MPC approaches exist, they are computationally very demanding, often leading to lower control frequencies and less dynamic results compared to controllers using simpler models.

The broader research landscape, therefore, reveals a gap: a need for a control framework that combines the long-horizon planning capabilities of MPC with the high-frequency, full-body dynamic control of WBC, while specifically addressing the challenges posed by highly dynamic motions involving significant aerial phases. This paper's core contribution lies in bridging this gap. It draws inspiration from "impulse planning" demonstrated in earlier work (Cheetah 2), which focused on planning ground reaction forces (impulses) rather than CoM trajectories, acknowledging that CoM tracking is impractical during highly dynamic, under-actuated phases. By integrating this impulse-centric planning into a modified WBC framework, the authors propose a novel solution that allows the robot to achieve highly agile and robust locomotion, representing a significant step forward in the field of dynamic legged robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **develop a versatile and robust control scheme for highly dynamic locomotion of quadruped robots with minimal user intervention.** This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and aimed at achieving distinct objectives:

- **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Control Schemes:** The authors identify the shortcomings of current WBC and MPC approaches when applied to highly dynamic legged locomotion. Existing WBCs struggle with frequent non-contact (aerial) phases due to their focus on strict trajectory tracking, while simplified-model MPCs lack the high-frequency, full-body precision needed for robust control. The motivation is to devise a controller that synergistically combines the strengths of both paradigms to address these limitations.
- **Enabling High-Speed and Aerial Phases:** A key motivation is to achieve "highly dynamic" locomotion, which inherently involves significant aerial phases, short stance times, and rapid leg swings. This necessitates a control framework that can effectively plan for and execute motions where the robot's center of mass might be temporarily uncontrollable. The paper explicitly aims for high-speed running, seeking to push the performance envelope of quadruped robots.
- **Prioritizing Reaction Force Control:** Building upon the success of "impulse planning" from previous work (e.g., Cheetah 2), a central objective is to formulate a Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC) that prioritizes tracking desired ground reaction forces generated by the MPC, rather than strictly tracking body trajectories. This is motivated by the insight that for extremely dynamic motions, controlling the impulse (force over time) is more effective than attempting to perfectly track an often-unfeasible CoM trajectory.
- **Ensuring Versatility:** The controller should be versatile, meaning it can easily switch between different gaits (e.g., trot, bound, gallop, pronk, pace) and adapt to varying speeds and directions by simply re-configuring high-level commands, without requiring extensive manual tuning or re-planning of low-level details.
- **Achieving Robustness in Real-World Scenarios:** A critical objective is to demonstrate the controller's reliability and stability not just in simulated environments, but on real robot hardware (the Mini-Cheetah) in challenging, unstructured outdoor terrains and against disturbances. This includes maintaining balance and executing commands even on slippery surfaces or uneven ground.
- **Maximizing Hardware Capability:** The authors intend for their control scheme to fully exploit the inherent capabilities of the Mini-Cheetah hardware, pushing its limits in terms of joint velocity, torque, and power output to achieve record-breaking speeds for untethered quadruped robots of its size.
- **Computational Efficiency:** A practical objective is to ensure that the proposed control architecture can run efficiently on the robot's onboard, low-power computer, enabling high-frequency feedback loops essential for dynamic stability.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the desire to unlock the full dynamic potential of legged robots, moving beyond stable but slower gaits to achieve truly agile, high-speed, and robust locomotion across diverse terrains and gaits by intelligently integrating model predictive control with a novel, impulse-focused whole-body control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is a **hybrid, hierarchical control architecture** that combines the strengths of Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC). This two-layer structure allows for both long-horizon planning and high-frequency, detailed execution.

**A. Hybrid Control Architecture Overview (Fig. 1 & Fig. 2):**
The system is divided into two main components operating at different frequencies:
1. **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** This high-level controller runs at a lower frequency (40 Hz). It uses a simplified model of the robot to generate an optimal reaction force profile and general body/foot position commands over a longer time horizon (typically one full gait cycle).
2. **Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC):** This low-level controller runs at a much higher frequency (500 Hz). It takes the MPC's reaction force commands and, using the robot's full-body dynamics, computes precise joint torque, position, and velocity commands. Unlike traditional WBC, WBIC prioritizes tracking the *reaction force commands* while allowing for flexibility in the floating base trajectory.

Beyond these two main controllers, the system also incorporates a Kalman Filter-based state estimator (500 Hz) for accurate body state estimation and a custom dynamics engine (500 Hz) that efficiently accounts for rotor dynamics and applies linear algebra optimizations. Joint-level PD controllers (40 kHz) provide the final, rapid control at the actuator level.

**B. Model Predictive Control (MPC):**
- **Model Simplification:** The MPC uses a simple lumped mass model (Equations 1 & 2) that considers only the robot's body position and orientation, and the forces acting on it. This simplification is crucial for maintaining computational tractability and allowing for a longer prediction horizon.
- **Convex Formulation:** To ensure the MPC problem is fast to solve and guarantees a unique global minimum, three key simplifications are applied:
 1. Small roll and pitch angle assumption (Equation 4 & 5) to linearize coordinate transformations.
 2. States are assumed to be close to the commanded trajectory, allowing for time-varying linearization of dynamics using commanded yaw and predetermined moment arms.
 3. Small pitch and roll velocities, and small off-diagonal inertia terms, simplify angular dynamics (Equation 6).
 These simplifications lead to a discrete linear system (Equation 7-9).
- **Optimization Problem:** The MPC formulates a Quadratic Program (QP) that minimizes the deviation from a reference state trajectory and the magnitude of reaction forces (Equation 10). It is subject to linear dynamics constraints and friction cone constraints (Equation 11). Variables corresponding to feet not in contact are eliminated to reduce problem size and increase speed.
- **Gait Scheduler:** A periodic phase-based gait scheduler defines the contact sequence for different gaits by specifying phase offset and stance period for each foot, allowing easy switching between gait types.
- **Foot Step Planner:** The upcoming footstep locations are determined using a modified Raibert heuristic (Equations 12-15). This incorporates the current body position, shoulder location, a symmetry term (to ensure landing and leaving angles are identical at commanded velocity), and a centrifugal term (for turning).

**C. Whole-Body Impulse Control (WBIC):**
- **Objective:** To compute joint position, velocity, and torque commands at a high frequency (500 Hz) using the full-body dynamics (Equation 3) and the reaction forces provided by the MPC.
- **Prioritized Task Execution:** WBIC uses a null-space projection technique (Equations 16-20) to establish a strict task hierarchy. Tasks are executed in priority order, with higher-priority tasks projected into the null space of lower-priority ones. The tasks include:
 1. Body Orientation
 2. Body Position
 3. Foot Position (swing legs)
 This computes desired joint positions ($\Delta q_{j}$), velocities ($\dot{q}^{cmd}_{j}$), and accelerations ($\ddot{q}^{cmd}$). Crucially, WBIC uses joint position feedback (Equation 24) for stabilization, leveraging the high-frequency (40 kHz) joint-level PD controllers.
- **Quadratic Programming for Force and Torque Calculation:** The final reaction forces and joint torques are computed via a QP (Equation 25). The novelty here is the minimization of relaxation variables ($\delta_{f}$, $\delta_{f_r}$) for floating base acceleration and reaction forces. This means WBIC aims to track the *MPC-computed reaction forces* ($f^{MPC}_r$) while *relaxing* the strict tracking of the floating base acceleration. This relaxation is vital for dynamic gaits where the CoM may not be controllable (e.g., during flight phases). The QP balances tracking acceleration commands (from prioritized tasks) and reaction force commands, subject to contact force constraints.
- **Torque Computation:** Once the optimal reaction forces ($f_r$) and configuration space accelerations ($\ddot{q}$) are determined, the joint torque command ($\tau_j$) is directly calculated using the inverse dynamics equation (Equation 26).

**D. Hardware Implementation and Optimization:**
The Mini-Cheetah robot is the experimental platform. To achieve the demanding control frequencies (30 Hz for MPC, 500 Hz for WBIC) on its low-power onboard computer, the authors developed:
- An efficient dynamics engine that incorporates rotor dynamics.
- Linear algebra optimizations through template formatting.

This comprehensive methodology allows the controller to exploit the benefits of both long-term, global optimality from MPC and short-term, detailed, and robust execution from WBIC, specifically tailored for highly dynamic and agile legged locomotion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation on the Mini-Cheetah robot strongly supports the effectiveness, versatility, and robustness of the proposed control scheme.

- **Record-Breaking High-Speed Running:** The Mini-Cheetah achieved a stable forward running speed of **3.7 m/s** on a treadmill, with a peak observed velocity of 4 m/s before stability was compromised. This corresponds to a Froude number of 4.65, making it one of the fastest untethered quadruped robots for its size, significantly surpassing the group's previous record of 2.45 m/s. This speed is comparable to or exceeds most documented legged robots, with the notable exception of the larger MIT Cheetah 2 (6.4 m/s bounding, Froude number 7.1).
- **Full Hardware Utilization:** The controller successfully pushed the Mini-Cheetah's hardware to its limits. During high-speed running, hip joints reached maximum velocities of 34 rad/s and commanded torques of 25.5 N·m (close to the actuator's peak of 40 rad/s and 17 N·m, with commanded torque potentially exceeding physical limits due to truncation). The power output peaked at 280 W, exceeding the specified maximum actuator power of 250 W, confirming that the control scheme efficiently leverages the robot's physical capabilities.
- **Dynamic Trajectory and Force Tracking:** The experiments demonstrated how the "reaction force tracking" approach in WBIC enables highly dynamic motion. While the body posture command remained constant, the robot's actual height and pitch fluctuated significantly, showing the robot "jumps and lands" as planned by MPC's vertical reaction forces. This confirms that WBIC effectively prioritizes reaction force execution over strict body trajectory adherence, allowing for natural, dynamic movements including aerial phases. The higher frequency of WBIC (500 Hz) allowed it to refine MPC's (40 Hz) reaction force commands for continuous body stabilization and swing leg control.
- **Gait Versatility:** The controller demonstrated remarkable versatility by successfully executing **six different gaits** (trot, gallop, bounding, pronking, pacing, walk - implied from "various gaits" and Figure 5) with speed over 1 m/s in outdoor environments. Crucially, this was achieved *without changing any control parameters or gains*, requiring only adjustments to the gait type, speed, and direction commands via a remote controller. This highlights the generality of the control framework.
- **Robustness in Challenging Terrains:** The Mini-Cheetah maintained stable locomotion on diverse and challenging outdoor terrains, including wet and slippery grass (due to recent rain) and gravel. This demonstrates the controller's robustness against real-world disturbances and variations in ground friction and roughness, which typically pose significant challenges for legged robots.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The custom-developed dynamics engine and optimized solvers proved effective, allowing the MPC to run at 30 Hz and the WBIC at 500 Hz on the Mini-Cheetah's onboard, low-power computer. This computational efficiency is key to enabling real-time, high-frequency control necessary for dynamic behaviors.

In summary, the main findings confirm that the proposed hybrid control architecture, with its novel emphasis on whole-body impulse control, successfully enables exceptionally dynamic, versatile, and robust locomotion for quadruped robots, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable on physical hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of legged robotics, with far-reaching implications for the design and deployment of agile robotic systems.

- **Pioneering Dynamic Legged Locomotion:** The achieved running speed of 3.7 m/s and the demonstration of diverse, highly dynamic gaits (Froude number 4.65) on an untethered quadruped robot represent a new benchmark for agility and performance. This pushes the state of the art in developing robots that can truly mimic the dynamic capabilities of biological quadrupeds.
- **Robust and Versatile Control Paradigm:** The hybrid control architecture, effectively combining MPC for long-horizon planning and WBIC for high-frequency, full-body execution, offers a robust and versatile paradigm for dynamic legged locomotion. By intelligently relaxing the floating base trajectory tracking in WBIC in favor of impulse control, the authors have found a crucial balance that allows the robot to "flow" with dynamic motion, rather than rigidly adhering to potentially unachievable CoM paths. This is a fundamental contribution to control theory for highly dynamic systems.
- **Addressing Fundamental Challenges:** The work successfully addresses long-standing challenges in legged locomotion, specifically how to handle aerial phases, short stance times, and high-speed leg swings in a cohesive and computationally efficient manner. It overcomes the limitations of traditional WBC (poor for under-actuated phases) and simplified-model MPC (lacking full-body precision), offering a solution that leverages the strengths of both.
- **Hardware-Software Synergy:** The paper highlights the critical synergy between advanced control algorithms and capable hardware. The Mini-Cheetah robot, with its high-performance proprioceptive actuators and robust design, serves as an ideal platform for validating such dynamic control schemes, demonstrating that pushing hardware limits requires equally sophisticated software.
- **Broad Applicability and Scalability:** The authors emphasize the generality of their control formulation, noting its independence from specific system parameters. This suggests that the framework is highly scalable and transferable to other legged robots, including larger quadrupeds (like Cheetah 3, as noted in future work) and even bipeds. The stated goal of extending it to loco-manipulation tasks further underscores its potential for controlling complex multi-task robots.
- **Towards Real-World Autonomy:** The robust performance in challenging outdoor environments (wet grass, gravel) signifies a crucial step towards deploying legged robots in unstructured, real-world scenarios. Robots capable of such agile and robust locomotion could be transformative for applications like disaster response, search and rescue, infrastructure inspection, logistics in difficult terrains, and even entertainment.
- **Open-Source Contribution (Implied):** While not explicitly stated as open-source in the paper, the authors use an open-source QP solver and the MIT Biomimetics Lab often releases code and hardware designs (e.g., Mini-Cheetah hardware details are openly available). This contributes to the broader research community by providing a strong foundation for future work.

In conclusion, this paper does more than just demonstrate impressive robotic feats; it offers a well-reasoned and experimentally validated control framework that fundamentally advances our understanding and capability in dynamic legged locomotion. Its impact will likely be seen in the development of more agile, robust, and autonomous legged robots in the coming years.
