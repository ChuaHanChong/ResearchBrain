# Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from OpenAI and DeepMind developed a method to train deep reinforcement learning agents using human preferences over trajectory segments, rather than explicit reward functions. This approach enables agents to learn complex behaviors and achieve performance comparable to or exceeding those trained with ground-truth rewards, while drastically reducing the required human feedback.

## Problem

- Applying deep reinforcement learning to real-world tasks is limited by the difficulty of designing explicit, quantifiable reward functions that fully capture complex or ambiguous human goals.
- Existing methods like Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) and Imitation Learning (IL) often require expert demonstrations which are impractical or impossible for many complex tasks.
- Direct, real-time human supervision for deep RL is prohibitively expensive due to the immense sample complexity and long training times required by agents.

## Method

- The method learns a deep neural network ==reward function== from ==pairwise human preferences== over short video segments of agent behavior, fit with a ==Bradley-Terry model== and ==cross-entropy loss==.
- A standard deep reinforcement learning algorithm (==A2C== or ==TRPO==) then trains the agent's policy by maximizing the sum of predicted rewards from this learned function, operating ==asynchronously== with human feedback collection.
- Human queries are prioritized using ==uncertainty sampling==, selecting trajectory pairs where an ==ensemble of reward predictors== shows the most disagreement to efficiently gather informative feedback.

## Results

- Achieved performance on par with or occasionally exceeding agents trained with ground truth rewards on **eight** simulated robotics tasks (MuJoCo) and **seven** Atari games, using as few as **700 to 5,500 human queries**.
- Demonstrated the ability to learn novel, complex behaviors such as robot backflips, one-legged locomotion for a half-cheetah, and specific gameplay styles (e.g., 'even mode' in Enduro), requiring **800 to 1,300 human queries**.
- Reduced the cost of human supervision by approximately **three orders of magnitude** compared to direct human reward provision, making human oversight economically feasible for state-of-the-art deep RL systems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning a reward function from human preferences is a viable and efficient method for deep reinforcement learning, drastically reducing the amount of human feedback required.
> - Online, asynchronous interaction between policy optimization, human preference elicitation, and reward function fitting is crucial; offline reward prediction is insufficient due to behavioral non-stationarity.
> - Presenting human overseers with short video clips (trajectory segments) for pairwise comparison is significantly more effective and informative than single frames, improving feedback efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) has seen tremendous success in domains where a clear, well-defined reward function can be provided to the agent. Iconic examples include games like Go (Silver et al., 2016) and Atari (Mnih et al., 2015), where victory conditions and scoring mechanisms provide immediate and unambiguous feedback. However, a significant limitation arises when attempting to apply RL to real-world tasks where goals are complex, ambiguous, or difficult to quantify into a simple programmatic reward function. Tasks such as "cleaning a table" or "scrambling an egg" for a robot exemplify this challenge, as designing a precise reward function that fully captures human preferences without leading to unintended or undesirable behavior is remarkably difficult. This issue is at the heart of the "AI alignment problem," a critical concern in AI safety, where an agent might optimize a poorly specified objective function in ways that deviate from human values (Bostrom, 2014; Russell, 2016; Amodei et al., 2016).

Prior attempts to address the problem of missing reward functions include:
- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** This approach infers a reward function from expert demonstrations (Ng and Russell, 2000). While powerful, it requires demonstrations of the desired behavior, which may be impossible or impractical for complex tasks, especially those requiring non-human morphology or expertise.
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** Directly clones demonstrated behavior. Similar to IRL, it suffers from the need for demonstrations and can struggle with tasks beyond the demonstrator's capabilities.
- **Direct Human Feedback:** Humans could directly provide rewards during training. However, the sheer volume of interactions required by deep RL agents (thousands of hours of experience) makes direct, real-time human supervision prohibitively expensive and impractical.

This paper positions itself as a scalable solution to this fundamental problem. It builds upon a line of prior work in reinforcement learning from human ratings or preferences (e.g., Akrour et al., 2011; Wilson et al., 2012), which have explored learning from implicit or explicit human feedback rather than explicit reward signals. However, these earlier works were typically applied to simpler domains with hand-coded features or limited degrees of freedom.

The key contribution of this work is to **scale up the concept of learning a reward function from human preferences to state-of-the-art deep reinforcement learning systems**, which operate in high-dimensional state and action spaces without hand-engineered features. By reducing the required human feedback by several orders of magnitude, it makes human oversight practically applicable to contemporary RL agents. This is a significant step towards enabling deep RL to tackle real-world tasks where the reward function is elusive, directly addressing a critical aspect of the AI alignment problem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to bridge the gap between the remarkable capabilities of deep reinforcement learning in well-defined environments and its limited applicability to complex real-world tasks that lack explicit, easily quantifiable reward functions. The authors articulate four key objectives for their proposed solution:

1. **Enable solving tasks where desired behavior is recognizable but not demonstratable:** Many complex tasks, especially those involving intricate robot movements or strategic decisions, are difficult for humans to physically demonstrate. However, humans can often readily judge whether a given behavior is desirable or not. The solution aims to leverage this discriminative ability.
2. **Allow teaching by non-expert users:** To maximize the practical utility and accessibility of RL, the system should not require expert programmers or domain specialists to define the task. Instead, it should enable general users to provide meaningful feedback.
3. **Scale to large problems:** The approach must be compatible with modern deep RL architectures and capable of handling high-dimensional observation spaces (e.g., raw pixel inputs) and complex action spaces (e.g., continuous robot control) without requiring manual feature engineering.
4. **Be economical with user feedback:** This is perhaps the most crucial objective. Given the immense sample complexity of deep RL, direct human supervision (e.g., providing a reward at every timestep) is impractical. The system must drastically reduce the amount of human interaction needed, making the cost of human oversight feasible.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to expand the impact of deep RL, allowing it to be applied to a much broader range of real-world problems that are currently intractable due to the "reward specification problem."

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core idea of the proposed method is to **learn a reward function from human preferences and then use this learned reward function to train a policy using standard reinforcement learning algorithms.** This process runs asynchronously, efficiently integrating human feedback without requiring constant, real-time human presence during the majority of the agent's experience generation.

The system maintains two key components:
- A **policy (π)**: A deep neural network that dictates the agent's actions based on observations.
- A **reward function estimate (ˆr)**: Another deep neural network that predicts the reward for a given observation-action pair.

These components are updated through three asynchronously running processes (illustrated in Figure 1):

1. **Policy Optimization:** The policy π interacts with the environment, generating trajectories. Importantly, it maximizes the sum of **predicted rewards** from the current estimate ˆr, rather than an explicit environmental reward. For policy optimization, the authors use established deep RL algorithms:
 - **Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C)** for Atari games (Mnih et al., 2016).
 - **Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)** for simulated robotics tasks (Schulman et al., 2015).
 The choice of policy gradient methods is intentional due to their robustness to non-stationary reward functions, as ˆr is continuously updated. Rewards from ˆr are normalized to have zero mean and constant standard deviation, a common preprocessing step that also addresses the arbitrary scale of preferences.

2. **Preference Elicitation:** This is the interface for human interaction. Instead of asking for absolute numerical scores (which are often inconsistent and difficult for humans to provide), the human overseer is presented with **pairs of short video clips (trajectory segments)**, typically 1 to 2 seconds long. The human's task is simply to indicate which segment they prefer, if they are equally good, or if they are incomparable. This pairwise comparison approach is intuitive and consistent for human raters. These judgments are stored in a database of triples (σ₁, σ₂, μ), where μ represents the user's preference distribution.

3. **Reward Function Fitting:** The reward predictor ˆr is updated via supervised learning to fit the collected human preferences. The authors adopt a variation of the **Bradley-Terry model** (Bradley and Terry, 1952) for estimating score functions from pairwise comparisons. It postulates that the probability of a human preferring one segment over another is exponentially related to the difference in their summed predicted rewards (Equation 1 in the paper). The reward function ˆr's parameters are optimized by minimizing the cross-entropy loss between its predictions and the human labels.

To enhance robustness and performance, several modifications are incorporated into the reward function fitting:
- **Ensemble of Predictors:** An ensemble of multiple reward predictors is trained, each on a bootstrapped sample of the human preference data. The final reward estimate is an average of the normalized predictions from these ensemble members. This helps to reduce variance and improve generalization.
- **Regularization:** L2 regularization and dropout are applied during training to prevent overfitting of the reward predictor to the potentially noisy human data.
- **Handling Human Error:** A small probability (10%) is assumed for uniform random human responses, accounting for potential human errors or inconsistencies, especially when reward differences are extreme.

**Query Selection:** To make efficient use of human feedback, the system prioritizes queries. Candidate pairs of trajectory segments are sampled, and those for which the ensemble of reward predictors exhibits the highest variance in their predictions (i.e., where the models "disagree" most) are selected for human review. This is an approximate form of uncertainty sampling, aiming to gather information where it is most impactful for improving the reward model.

The asynchronous nature of these processes is crucial. The RL agent can explore and gather experience rapidly, while human feedback is collected and the reward model refined in parallel, allowing for continuous adaptation and improvement of the agent's objective.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling results demonstrating the effectiveness and scalability of learning deep reinforcement learning policies from human preferences.

#### 4.1. Reinforcement Learning Tasks with Unobserved Rewards (Quantitative Evaluation)

- **Simulated Robotics (MuJoCo):** The method was tested on eight standard MuJoCo tasks. With just **700 queries to a human rater (corresponding to 30 minutes to 5 hours of human time)**, the agent's performance, measured by the true underlying reward, nearly matched that of agents trained directly with the ground truth reward function. Surprisingly, with **1400 labels, the algorithm sometimes slightly *outperformed* RL using the true reward**, suggesting that human feedback can effectively provide a better-shaped or more useful reward signal than the original, hand-engineered one (e.g., on the Ant task, human preference for "standing upright" was more effective than the built-in penalty). Real human feedback was consistently only slightly less effective than synthetic oracle feedback, indicating its practical viability.
- **Atari Games:** Seven Atari games were used, with the in-game score blanked out to force learning purely from visual dynamics and human preferences. With **5,500 human queries**, the method showed substantial learning, matching or even exceeding RL with true rewards on some games like BeamRider and Pong. On others (Seaquest, Qbert), it performed near RL levels but learned slower. On SpaceInvaders and Breakout, while not fully matching RL, agents still improved substantially, passing initial levels. Real human feedback generally performed similarly to or slightly worse than synthetic feedback, attributed to human labeling errors, inconsistencies, or uneven labeling rates. Notably, on Enduro, human feedback *outperformed* A3C with true rewards, implying that the human feedback implicitly provided superior reward shaping for that specific game.

#### 4.2. Novel Behaviors (Qualitative Evaluation)

To demonstrate flexibility beyond replicating existing tasks, the authors showed that their algorithm could train novel, complex behaviors from scratch using human preferences:
- **Hopper Robot performing backflips:** Learned a sequence of backflips, landing upright and repeating, with **900 queries in under an hour** of human time.
- **Half-Cheetah Robot moving on one leg:** Achieved this unique locomotion with **800 queries in under an hour**.
- **Enduro (Atari) "even mode":** The agent learned to stay almost exactly even with other moving cars, rather than passing them, using **1,300 queries**.

These qualitative results are particularly significant as they showcase the ability to teach behaviors for which designing a programmatic reward function would be extremely challenging or require significant trial-and-error.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

The ablation studies provided critical insights into the algorithmic components:
- **Offline Reward Prediction is Flawed:** Training the reward predictor only at the beginning (offline) led to significantly worse performance. Due to the non-stationarity of the agent's behavior and the changing distribution of visited states, an offline predictor can become misaligned, leading the agent to exploit weaknesses in the learned reward and develop undesirable behaviors (e.g., in Pong, avoiding loss without scoring). This highlights the **critical importance of online, intertwined human feedback and RL training.**
- **Ensemble and Query Selection:** Using an ensemble of predictors and uncertainty-based query selection generally helped performance, though not always universally better than random queries across all tasks.
- **Importance of Clips over Single Frames:** Comparing short trajectory segments (clips) was found to be **significantly more helpful per clip than comparing individual frames**. Clips provide richer context, making human judgments easier and more informative. This directly reduces the number of comparisons needed.

In summary, the main findings affirm that learning from human preferences is a viable and highly efficient method for training deep RL agents, capable of solving both benchmark tasks and novel behaviors with minimal human intervention. The critical role of online feedback and the benefits of trajectory segment comparisons were empirically demonstrated.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a pivotal advancement in the field of reinforcement learning and has profound significance for the future development and deployment of AI systems.

**1. Enabling RL for Ill-Defined Real-World Tasks:** The most immediate impact is on expanding the applicability of deep reinforcement learning. By demonstrating that complex RL tasks can be effectively solved without a pre-defined, programmatic reward function, this work opens the door for RL to tackle real-world problems where objectives are inherently qualitative, multi-faceted, or hard to quantify. Industries ranging from robotics (e.g., household robots, assistive technologies) to complex simulations and even creative tasks could benefit from this paradigm shift.

**2. Drastically Reducing the Cost of Human Supervision:** The paper's key quantitative contribution is showing that learning from human preferences can reduce the cost of human oversight by **roughly three orders of magnitude** compared to direct human reward provision. This efficiency breakthrough makes human supervision economically feasible for state-of-the-art deep RL systems, where compute costs are already becoming comparable to, or even lower than, the cost of non-expert human labor for feedback. This addresses a major practical bottleneck in scaling up RL.

**3. A Step Towards Solving the AI Alignment Problem:** This work directly contributes to the ongoing efforts in AI safety and alignment research. The ability to communicate complex goals to AI systems through human preferences, rather than relying on brittle hand-coded reward functions, is a crucial step towards ensuring that powerful AI systems act in accordance with human values and intentions. It mitigates the risk of "reward hacking" or agents optimizing for proxies that do not truly reflect the desired outcome. The success in training novel behaviors that are hard to specify programmatically underscores this potential for value alignment.

**4. Democratization of AI Training:** By enabling non-expert users to teach agents, the approach has the potential to democratize AI development. Users without programming expertise could guide AI behavior, fostering broader participation and diverse applications.

**5. Foundational for Future Research:** This paper lays a strong foundation for future research in several areas:
 - **Improved Efficiency:** Further work can explore more sophisticated active learning strategies for query selection (e.g., based on expected value of information) to further minimize human feedback.
 - **Complex Human Feedback:** Investigating richer forms of human feedback beyond simple pairwise preferences, such as natural language explanations or demonstrations within the preference framework, could enhance learning.
 - **Robustness to Human Error:** Developing methods more robust to noisy, inconsistent, or even biased human feedback will be crucial for real-world deployment.
 - **Generalization of Learned Rewards:** How well learned reward functions generalize to novel environments or tasks remains an open question for long-term impact.

In conclusion, "Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences" is a landmark paper that transforms the landscape of RL applications. By demonstrating a practical and scalable method for incorporating human values into complex learning systems, it represents a significant stride towards developing AI that is not only powerful but also aligned with human interests and goals.
