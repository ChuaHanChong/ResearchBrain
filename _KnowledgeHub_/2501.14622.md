# ACT-JEPA: Novel Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture for Efficient Policy Representation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> ACT-JEPA, an end-to-end architecture, unifies imitation learning with a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) for policy representation by jointly optimizing for action sequence prediction and future latent observation prediction. This framework achieved absolute task success rate improvements of up to +53.7% over autoregressive transformers and +10% over the ACT baseline across robotic manipulation tasks, alongside 29-40% lower prediction errors for future states.

## Problem

- Imitation learning policies rely on costly expert data and often lack an explicit understanding of environment dynamics, leading to compounding errors and poor generalization.
- Many self-supervised learning methods for robotics operate on raw input data, leading to computational inefficiencies and requiring large datasets for learning relevant features.
- Current methods struggle to develop robust policy representations that are simultaneously efficient for action generation and capable of predicting future environmental states.

## Method

- **ACT-JEPA**, a transformer-based architecture, unifies ==imitation learning== (IL) and Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) in an end-to-end framework.
- It jointly optimizes for predicting sequences of actions and future latent observations, aiming to learn a robust world model and executable policies simultaneously.
- The JEPA component learns environment dynamics in a compact latent space, filtering out irrelevant details, while the IL component learns to mimic expert actions for policy generation.

## Results

- ACT-JEPA achieved substantially higher task success rates, with absolute improvements of up to +**53.7%** compared to autoregressive transformer policies and up to +**10%** over the ACT baseline across simulated robotic tasks.
- Its integrated world model demonstrated superior understanding of environment dynamics, yielding 29-**37%** lower Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and 29-**40%** lower Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) in future state prediction than the ACT baseline.
- Joint, end-to-end optimization of both imitation learning and self-supervised objectives proved more effective than a two-stage training approach, leading to markedly higher task success rates (e.g., **36%** vs **0%** in ManiSkill).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Jointly optimizing imitation learning with a self-supervised JEPA objective yields more robust and generalizable policy representations compared to training these objectives separately.
> - Learning to predict future observations in a latent space, rather than raw pixel space, enables the model to efficiently capture relevant environment dynamics and ignore unpredictable details.
> - The representations developed through the JEPA objective are directly beneficial for action prediction, indicating a strong connection between environmental understanding and effective control policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of robotic learning, specifically focusing on policy representation learning for decision-making tasks, primarily within the paradigm of imitation learning (IL). The broader context involves addressing the limitations of existing IL methods and exploring the integration of self-supervised learning (SSL) techniques to enhance learning efficiency and generalization.

Current imitation learning approaches often rely on expert demonstrations, which are costly and time-consuming to acquire. A notable limitation of these methods is their frequent inability to develop an explicit world model, which would allow them to understand environmental dynamics and predict future states. This deficiency can restrict their adaptability to novel situations and their capacity to recover from errors not present in the training data. Some IL methods, particularly autoregressive (AR) models, also face challenges such as compounding errors and difficulties with non-Markovian expert behaviors, leading to limited generalizability. Non-autoregressive models, which predict action sequences (or "action chunks"), have mitigated some of these issues, but they still depend heavily on labeled expert actions.

Self-supervised learning offers an alternative by enabling models to learn from diverse, unlabeled data, including failure cases. However, many SSL methods historically operate in raw input space (e.g., pixel space), which can be computationally inefficient and necessitate extensive datasets due to the need to model irrelevant or unpredictable details.

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) represent a recent advancement in SSL that addresses this inefficiency by learning representations in latent space, thereby allowing models to disregard irrelevant information. This paradigm has shown promise across various domains, including vision, video, audio, and 3D data.

In the context of policy learning, some recent SSL efforts have focused on improving visual representations or learning environment dynamics in latent space. Examples include DynaMo, which uses a two-stage process for pretraining an encoder on environment dynamics before policy learning; DINO-WM, which develops a world model from pre-trained visual features in latent space for model predictive control (MPC); and V-JEPA 2-AC, which also employs a two-stage approach with JEPA for pretraining and MPC for action selection. While these methods demonstrate progress in learning robust world models, they often involve two-stage training processes, rely on pre-trained visual features or ground-truth actions for dynamics learning, or use MPC for action selection, which can be slower during inference.

ACT-JEPA positions itself within this landscape by integrating the strengths of IL and JEPA-based SSL in an end-to-end architecture. It aims to simultaneously learn executable action sequences and a robust world model by predicting latent observation sequences. This work seeks to overcome the data dependency of traditional IL, the inefficiency of pixel-space SSL, and the architectural limitations of prior SSL-for-policy-learning approaches (such as two-stage training and reliance on MPC). By doing so, ACT-JEPA endeavors to produce efficient, generalizable, and robust policy representations, particularly relevant for complex robotic systems with multi-modal, high-dimensional sensor inputs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an architecture that enhances policy representation learning by unifying imitation learning (IL) and self-supervised learning (SSL). Specifically, the proposed ACT-JEPA model aims to achieve efficient learning of policy representations, improve world model understanding, and increase task success rates in decision-making scenarios.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in existing approaches to robot control and representation learning:

1. **Limitations of Current Imitation Learning:** Traditional IL methods, such as behavior cloning, are fundamentally reliant on high-quality expert demonstrations. Collecting such data is often expensive, time-consuming, and resource-intensive. Furthermore, policies learned solely from expert demonstrations tend to lack an explicit understanding of the environment's dynamics, or a "world model." This absence limits their capacity to predict future states, generalize to unseen situations (including failure cases), or recover from errors that were not part of the expert trajectory. Autoregressive IL models also suffer from compounding errors, where small prediction inaccuracies accumulate over time, and can struggle with non-Markovian expert behaviors, like pauses or deliberate slow movements.

2. **Inefficiencies of Conventional Self-Supervised Learning:** While SSL offers a method to learn from unlabeled data, many SSL techniques, particularly those operating in raw input space (e.g., pixels), are computationally demanding. They force the model to predict all details, including those that are irrelevant or unpredictable, which requires large datasets and extensive computational resources, hindering their practical application in complex robotic domains.

3. **Need for Robust and Generalizable Policy Representations:** Modern robotic systems are increasingly complex, integrating high-dimensional data from various sensors (e.g., cameras, tactile sensors, proprioception). Processing this vast amount of information efficiently while distinguishing between relevant and irrelevant details is crucial for robust policy learning. There is a need for representations that can generalize across different tasks and conditions, reduce dependence on specific expert data, and enable recovery from unforeseen situations.

4. **Desire for End-to-End Joint Optimization:** Existing approaches that combine SSL and supervised learning (SL) for policy tasks often employ a two-stage process: pretraining with SSL followed by fine-tuning with SL. This can lead to representation misalignment, where the features learned during pretraining are not optimally suited for the specific downstream task, potentially underperforming compared to jointly optimized approaches.

To address these motivations, ACT-JEPA aims to:
- Develop an architecture that can simultaneously learn executable actions and a world model in latent space.
- Utilize the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) principle to make world model learning efficient by predicting in a low-dimensional latent space, thereby filtering out irrelevant and unpredictable details.
- Predict sequences of both actions and latent observations to mitigate compounding errors and build richer, more robust representations of environmental dynamics.
- Implement an end-to-end training strategy that jointly optimizes both IL and SSL objectives, aiming for more coherent and generalizable shared representations.
- Reduce reliance on solely expert data by leveraging self-supervised learning from diverse unlabeled observations, including non-expert trajectories.

Ultimately, the goal is to develop policies that are more efficient to train, demonstrate improved understanding of environment dynamics, and achieve higher success rates in diverse robotic manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ACT-JEPA is presented as a novel end-to-end architecture that unifies Imitation Learning (IL) and Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) objectives to enhance policy representation learning. The model is designed to jointly predict action sequences and latent observation sequences, optimized within a single training pipeline.

**Architecture Overview:**
The architecture is transformer-based and comprises four main components: a context encoder, a target encoder, a predictor, and an action decoder. During training, all components are active. For inference, only the context encoder and the action decoder are utilized to generate action sequences. The input modalities can be diverse, but the implementation specifically uses current images, proprioceptive states, and task labels.

**Components:**

1. **Context Encoder (E_θ):**
 - **Input:** Receives observations at the current timestep `t`, including an image, the proprioceptive state, and a task label.
 - **Function:** Encodes these inputs into a shared latent representation `s_x`. This representation is designed to capture information relevant for both predicting actions and understanding world dynamics.
 - **Implementation:** Each modality is encoded using dedicated functions. Proprioceptive states are encoded via linear layers. Task labels are one-hot encoded. Images are processed by a pre-trained ResNet-18 model to extract feature maps, which are then flattened into a sequence of tokens with positional encoding. These modality-specific tokens are concatenated and fed into a transformer model to produce `s_x`.

2. **Target Encoder (E_θ'):**
 - **Input:** Receives a sequence of future observations `o_t:t+n`. In the described implementation, this specifically consists of a sequence of proprioceptive states.
 - **Function:** Encodes this sequence into a sequence of latent observations `s_y_t:t+n`. These latent representations serve as the ground-truth targets for the SSL objective, capturing how the environment evolves over time while filtering out irrelevant details.
 - **Implementation:** The input sequence (proprioceptive states) is processed by a linear projection layer, followed by positional encoding to retain temporal information. The resulting tokens are then fed into a transformer model, producing `s_y_t:t+n`. The parameters of the target encoder (`θ'`) are updated using an exponential moving average (EMA) of the context encoder's parameters to prevent representation collapse.

3. **Predictor (P_ϕ):**
 - **Input:** Takes the latent representation `s_x` from the context encoder and a sequence of `n` learnable mask tokens `m_t:t+n`. Each mask token conceptually corresponds to an abstract future observation to be predicted.
 - **Function:** Learns to predict the latent observation sequence `s_y_t:t+n`. It is designed to model the temporal evolution of the environment based on the current context.
 - **Implementation:** Unlike some JEPA architectures that use self-attention over all inputs, the predictor employs a cross-attention block to condition the mask tokens on `s_x`. This enriches the mask tokens with contextual information efficiently before they are further processed by a transformer model. The output is a sequence of predicted latent observations, `s_hat_y_t:t+n`.

4. **Action Decoder (D_τ):**
 - **Input:** Receives the latent representation `s_x` from the context encoder and a sequence of `n` learnable mask tokens `m_t:t+n`. Each mask token here corresponds to an action to be predicted.
 - **Function:** Generates a sequence of `n` future actions `a_hat_t:t+n` to be executed in the environment.
 - **Implementation:** Similar to the predictor, it uses a cross-attention block to condition the mask tokens on `s_x` before they are processed by a transformer model. The output is a sequence of predicted continuous actions.

**Objectives:**
ACT-JEPA is trained with two primary loss functions, which are summed to form the final objective:

1. **Action Loss (L_actions):** This is a supervised objective for imitation learning. It quantifies the difference between the predicted action sequence `a_hat_t:t+n` and the expert-provided target action sequence `a_t:t+n`.
 - **Loss Function:** L1 loss is used: `L_actions = (1/n) * sum(from i=0 to n-1) ||a_hat_t+i - a_t+i||_1`.

2. **Observation Loss (L_observations):** This is a self-supervised objective for world model learning. It measures the difference between the predicted latent observation sequence `s_hat_y_t:t+n` from the predictor and the ground-truth latent observation sequence `s_y_t:t+n` from the target encoder.
 - **Loss Function:** L1 loss is also used here: `L_observations = (1/n) * sum(from i=0 to n-1) ||s_hat_y_t+i - s_y_t+i||_1`.

**Final Loss:** The overall loss function is `L = L_actions + L_observations`. This joint optimization contrasts with multi-stage approaches, where SSL pretraining and supervised fine-tuning are performed sequentially. The parameters of the context encoder, predictor, and action decoder are updated via gradient descent, while the target encoder's parameters are updated via EMA of the context encoder's parameters.

**Experimental Setup:**
The architecture was evaluated in three simulated robotic environments with continuous observation and action spaces:
- **Push-T:** A 2D environment requiring precise object manipulation. Dataset: 206 human-collected demonstrations, RGB images (96x96).
- **Meta-World:** A suite of 15 diverse 3D manipulation tasks with a shared agent and workspace, focusing on generalization across similar environment dynamics. Dataset: 40 scripted demonstrations per task, RGB images (128x128).
- **ManiSkill:** A suite of 5 3D robot manipulation tasks (e.g., pick, push, stack cubes, insert peg) using a two-finger gripper arm. Dataset: 50 demonstrations per task, RGB images (128x128).

**Baselines for Comparison:**
- **Autoregressive (AR) transformer:** A GPT-style transformer decoder policy predicting the next action token based on previous actions and observations. It uses L2 loss and operates without rewards or action discretization, conceptually similar to Decision Transformer without rewards or Behavior Transformer without discretization.
- **ACT (Action Chunking with Transformer):** A non-autoregressive transformer-based policy that predicts action sequences (chunks) from observations. The AE variant is used, minimizing L1 loss on continuous action sequences. This baseline is considered highly relevant as it shares the action chunking and transformer-based design with ACT-JEPA but lacks the JEPA-based world modeling objective.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ACT-JEPA focused on its performance in world model understanding, decision-making, transferability of learned features, and the efficacy of joint versus separate optimization strategies.

#### 4.1. World Model Understanding through Latent Observation Sequences

To assess ACT-JEPA's ability to learn a world model, a probing task was designed. The context encoder of a trained policy was frozen, and a randomly initialized decoder was trained to reconstruct future proprioceptive states from these frozen representations. Performance was measured using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE).

- **Results:** ACT-JEPA consistently demonstrated superior world model understanding compared to the ACT baseline across all evaluated benchmarks. It achieved a 29–37% reduction in RMSE and a 29–40% reduction in ATE. For instance, in Push-T, ACT-JEPA's RMSE was 0.0895 compared to ACT's 0.1424, and its ATE was 0.0915 compared to ACT's 0.1518. Similar improvements were observed in ManiSkill and Meta-World.
- **Training Progression:** Analysis of representation quality during training (exemplified in ManiSkill) indicated that ACT-JEPA converged faster and achieved lower prediction errors (RMSE and ATE) earlier than ACT, sustaining these lower errors throughout training. This suggests more efficient and effective learning of environmental dynamics by ACT-JEPA.

#### 4.2. Decision-Making Performance

ACT-JEPA's performance in decision-making tasks was evaluated against baseline policies using the task success rate (percentage of successfully solved tasks).

- **Results:** ACT-JEPA outperformed all baselines across the Push-T, ManiSkill, and Meta-World environments.
 - **Compared to AR transformer:** ACT-JEPA showed substantial absolute improvements: +41% in Push-T (41% vs 0%), +28% in ManiSkill (36% vs 8%), and +53.7% in Meta-World (92% vs 38.3%). This performance gap was attributed to the AR policy's vulnerability to compounding errors and challenges with non-Markovian data.
 - **Compared to ACT:** ACT-JEPA achieved absolute improvements of +7% in Push-T (41% vs 34%), +10% in ManiSkill (36% vs 26%), and +2% in Meta-World (92% vs 90%). The smaller gain in Meta-World was attributed to ACT already reaching near-ceiling performance, suggesting that the benefits of the JEPA-based world model are more pronounced in less saturated performance regimes. These results indicate that integrating a JEPA-based world model enhances policy robustness and generalization.

#### 4.3. Transferability of JEPA-Learned Features to Policy Learning

An experiment was conducted to determine if a world model learned with JEPA captures features useful for action prediction. A two-stage training process was used: first, JEPA components (context encoder, predictor, target encoder) were pretrained with SSL to learn latent observation sequences. Then, with the encoder frozen, a new action decoder was trained with IL to predict action sequences. The action reconstruction loss was monitored during pretraining.

- **Results:** The action reconstruction loss consistently decreased throughout the JEPA pretraining phase. This outcome suggested that the representations learned by the world model became increasingly informative for downstream action prediction tasks, indicating that the JEPA world model effectively captures policy-relevant features.

#### 4.4. Joint vs. Separate Optimization of IL and SSL Objectives

The effectiveness of jointly optimizing IL and SSL objectives was compared against a conventional two-stage approach (SSL pretraining followed by IL fine-tuning).

- **Results:** Joint optimization, as implemented in ACT-JEPA, consistently outperformed the two-stage approach in terms of task success rate across all benchmarks. For example, in Push-T, ACT-JEPA achieved 41% success rate compared to 27% for the two-stage approach. In ManiSkill, ACT-JEPA achieved 36% compared to 0%, and in Meta-World, 92% compared to 23.3%.
- **Analysis:** This performance difference was attributed to potential representation misalignment in the two-stage approach, where SSL pretraining might not fully optimize for the fine-grained action generation needed for IL. Joint optimization, conversely, allows gradients from both objectives to shape a more coherent and robust shared representation space, leading to improved performance and a mutual regularization effect.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented on ACT-JEPA demonstrates a methodological advancement in learning policy representations for robotic control by integrating imitation learning (IL) with a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)-based self-supervised learning (SSL) framework. This work contributes to the field in several ways.

#### 5.1. Significance of Findings

- **Effective Unification of IL and SSL:** The core contribution is the successful end-to-end unification of IL for action sequence generation and JEPA for latent observation sequence prediction. This demonstrates a viable approach to learning both low-level control and high-level environmental dynamics concurrently.
- **Improved World Model Understanding:** ACT-JEPA consistently yielded superior performance in world model understanding tasks compared to IL-only baselines, evidenced by significantly lower prediction errors for future states. This indicates that learning in latent space through JEPA effectively captures richer and more accurate representations of environmental dynamics.
- **Enhanced Policy Performance:** The architecture demonstrated improved task success rates across various decision-making benchmarks, outperforming established IL baselines. This suggests that the internal representations learned by ACT-JEPA, benefiting from explicit world modeling, contribute to more robust and generalizable policies.
- **Efficiency of Latent Space Prediction:** By leveraging JEPA to predict in latent space, the model is able to disregard irrelevant and unpredictable details in raw observations, leading to more efficient learning and representation quality compared to pixel-space prediction methods.
- **Advantage of Joint Optimization:** The findings indicate that jointly optimizing IL and SSL objectives in an end-to-end manner is more effective than a conventional two-stage training approach. This is attributed to the creation of more coherent, shared representations and a mutual regularization effect that benefits both action generation and world modeling.
- **Transferability of Learned World Models:** The research showed that features learned by the JEPA world model are transferable and useful for downstream tasks such as action prediction, suggesting that the learned dynamics are relevant for control.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Robust and Generalizable Robotic Policies:** ACT-JEPA offers a direction for developing more robust and generalizable policies that can adapt to varying conditions, handle complex, high-dimensional sensor data, and potentially recover from unscripted situations or failures, which is critical for real-world robotic deployments (e.g., robodogs, humanoids, dexterous hands).
- **Reduced Data Dependency:** By integrating SSL, ACT-JEPA can reduce the heavy reliance on costly and time-consuming expert demonstrations, allowing models to learn from a broader range of unlabeled or less-curated interaction data.
- **Foundation Policy Models:** The architecture's ability to efficiently learn rich representations from diverse data and support various input modalities suggests its potential as a foundation policy model that could be scaled with larger and more diverse datasets, similar to trends in computer vision and natural language processing.
- **Broader Control Applications:** The learned world model in ACT-JEPA, beyond its immediate use in IL, opens avenues for other control paradigms, such as planning or model predictive control, where an accurate understanding of environmental dynamics is paramount.
- **Simplified Training Pipelines:** For practical robotic deployments, the efficacy of end-to-end joint optimization over multi-stage training could simplify engineering pipelines and resource management, especially in settings with constrained data and computational budgets.

#### 5.3. Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors

The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Simulated Environments:** The empirical evaluation was conducted exclusively in simulated environments, which may not fully capture the complexities and challenges of real-world robotic systems.
- **Target Modality:** The JEPA component was trained specifically with proprioceptive states as the target modality, leaving other potentially informative modalities such as images, depth, or tactile feedback unexplored.
- **Dataset Diversity and Size:** The datasets used were from simulated environments and thus inherently limited in diversity (e.g., uniform lighting, camera views in Meta-World) and size (hundreds of trajectories), which might not fully harness the benefits of a self-supervised approach like JEPA, which thrives on large, varied data. Scaling ACT-JEPA to larger, real-world datasets remains an area for future investigation.

Despite these limitations, ACT-JEPA represents a promising step towards creating more efficient and capable policies for decision-making by synergistically combining the strengths of imitation learning and joint-embedding predictive architectures.
