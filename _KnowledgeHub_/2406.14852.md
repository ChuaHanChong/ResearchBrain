# Is A Picture Worth A Thousand Words? Delving Into Spatial Reasoning for Vision Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Wisconsin–Madison, Salesforce AI Research, and Microsoft Research introduced SpatialEval, a benchmark designed to assess spatial reasoning in Vision-Language Models (VLMs). The study found that current VLMs often struggle with spatial reasoning from visual inputs alone, frequently relying more on textual information even when images are present, and sometimes perform better without visual input or with noisy images.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) demonstrate limited and inadequately explored proficiency in spatial understanding and reasoning.
- Existing benchmarks for LLMs and multi-modal models predominantly focus on textual or relational reasoning, lacking systematic evaluation of diverse spatial reasoning aspects.
- There is a need to systematically investigate the assumption that providing visual input inherently improves VLM performance across all types of tasks, especially those requiring detailed visual spatial understanding.

## Method

- Researchers developed ==SpatialEval==, a novel benchmark comprising four tasks (Spatial-Map, Maze-Nav, Spatial-Grid, Spatial-Real) to assess spatial reasoning abilities.
- SpatialEval is designed to support three distinct input modalities: ==Text-only (TQA)==, ==Vision-only (VQA)==, and redundant Vision-text (VTQA), enabling systematic study of modality impact.
- A wide range of state-of-the-art open-source and proprietary LLMs and VLMs were systematically evaluated on SpatialEval to analyze their performance and identify limitations.

## Results

- Most VLMs, when provided with Vision-only (VQA) input for synthetic spatial tasks, performed at levels similar to or below random guessing.
- VLMs with Vision-only (VQA) input often underperformed their LLM counterparts (TQA format); for example, LLaVA-v1.6-Mistral-7B (VQA) was **15%** worse than Mistral-7B (TQA) on Spatial-Grid.
- VLM performance on spatial reasoning tasks often improved when the original image was entirely removed (TQA for VLM) or replaced with Gaussian noise/random mismatched images, particularly when textual information was sufficient.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current VLMs often struggle with complex spatial reasoning when relying solely on visual information, sometimes performing at or below random chance.
> - VLMs tend to prioritize textual information for spatial reasoning, with performance sometimes improving when images are removed or replaced with noise, particularly if sufficient text is provided.
> - The current paradigm of "translating" visual input into a language space is insufficient for robust spatial reasoning, suggesting a need for fundamentally new architectures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have revolutionized many domains, demonstrating impressive, often human-level performance across a wide range of tasks. However, the authors argue that despite this progress, a fundamental aspect of human cognition—spatial understanding and reasoning—remains significantly "under-explored" and constitutes a critical limitation for current models. This paper directly addresses this gap by introducing a novel benchmark designed specifically to probe the spatial reasoning capabilities of these advanced AI systems.

Existing benchmarks for LLMs and multi-modal large language models (MLLMs), such as those evaluating common sense reasoning, mathematical problem-solving, or scientific question answering (e.g.,), have predominantly focused on textual or relational reasoning. The paper acknowledges prior work that touches upon related concepts, such as Zhang et al. suggesting MLLMs rely more on textual cues than visual diagrams for math, and Yamada et al. exploring simple navigation tasks. However, this paper distinguishes itself by offering a *systematic and comprehensive* evaluation across *diverse aspects* of spatial reasoning (relationship understanding, navigation, position understanding, and counting), and by explicitly investigating the interplay and redundancy between visual and textual modalities.

By proposing *SpatialEval*, the authors challenge the common assumption that simply providing visual input to an LLM (transforming it into a VLM) inherently improves its performance across all types of tasks, especially those requiring detailed visual spatial understanding. The work provides empirical evidence that, for spatial reasoning, current VLMs often struggle, sometimes performing worse than random guessing or even their text-only counterparts. This counter-intuitive finding contributes significantly to the ongoing discourse about the true capabilities and limitations of foundation models, pushing the research community to move beyond simply scaling models and data, towards developing architectures that genuinely integrate and reason with multimodal information. It highlights a critical area where current models, despite their apparent sophistication, fall short of human cognitive abilities, thereby informing future directions for multimodal AI research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the observation that while LLMs and VLMs have achieved remarkable success in various domains, their proficiency in spatial understanding and reasoning—a fundamental component of human cognition—remains inadequately explored and significantly limited. Humans effortlessly navigate environments, interpret maps, and manipulate objects based on spatial relationships, yet the ability of AI models to emulate these crucial skills is largely unknown.

The key objectives of this paper are explicitly outlined:
1. **Propose a Novel Benchmark:** Introduce *SpatialEval*, a comprehensive benchmark specifically designed to evaluate diverse aspects of spatial reasoning. This benchmark covers a spectrum of tasks including relationship understanding (e.g., relative positions), navigation (e.g., pathfinding, turn counting), position understanding (e.g., identifying objects at specific coordinates), and object counting within spatial contexts.
2. **Systematically Evaluate Competitive Models:** Conduct a thorough evaluation of a wide range of state-of-the-art open-source and proprietary LLMs and VLMs on SpatialEval.
3. **Investigate Modality Impact:** Uniquely, SpatialEval is designed such that each problem can be presented in three distinct input formats:
 - **TQA (Text-only):** Relying solely on textual descriptions.
 - **VQA (Vision-only):** Relying solely on visual input (images).
 - **VTQA (Vision-text):** Providing both redundant textual and visual information.
 The primary motivation for this design is to systematically study the impact of different modalities on spatial reasoning performance, particularly how VLMs process and integrate visual information when text is also available.
4. **Unveil Counter-intuitive Insights:** Through this systematic evaluation, uncover novel and surprising findings that challenge existing assumptions about how these models process spatial information and the efficacy of multimodal inputs.
5. **Inform Future Model Development:** Provide insights that can guide the design and development of next-generation multimodal models, aiming to enhance their spatial intelligence and ultimately close the significant gap with human-level understanding in this crucial cognitive area.

In essence, the authors are motivated to shine a light on a neglected yet critical aspect of AI intelligence, providing tools and analyses to understand current shortcomings and pave the way for more robust and human-like spatial reasoning capabilities in AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is the development and application of **SpatialEval**, a novel benchmark meticulously designed to assess spatial reasoning abilities across different input modalities.

#### 3.1. Dataset Setup (SpatialEval Tasks)

SpatialEval comprises four diverse tasks, each constructed to probe specific facets of spatial understanding and reasoning:

1. **Spatial-Map:** Simulates map reading, where models must understand spatial relationships (e.g., "Southeast of") and count objects based on these relationships. Objects have unique names and configurable quantities (K). Examples include questions about relative direction between locations or the number of objects meeting certain spatial criteria.
2. **Maze-Nav:** Evaluates navigation abilities within a maze-like environment. The task involves tracing a path from a start (S) to an exit (E) and answering questions like counting turns (right/left) or determining the spatial relationship between S and E. Inputs are presented as colored blocks (visual) or ASCII characters (textual).
3. **Spatial-Grid:** Focuses on spatial understanding within a rigid grid structure. Models are asked to count specific objects (e.g., "rabbits") or identify objects at precise coordinates (e.g., "first row, second column"). Visual input is a grid of images, and textual input is a corresponding structured text representation.
4. **Spatial-Real:** Extends evaluation to real-world images. This task is built upon the Densely Captioned Images (DCI) dataset, which features real images accompanied by extensive captions (averaging over 1,000 words). The authors manually curate multiple-choice questions related to object counting, relations, and position understanding within these complex real-world visual contexts.

#### 3.2. Input Modalities

A crucial aspect of SpatialEval is its design to support three distinct input formats for each task, enabling systematic study of modality impact:
- **TQA (Text-only):** The input contains only textual information, sufficient for human understanding. This is used for LLMs directly and also for VLMs to assess their language backbone's performance without visual input.
- **VQA (Vision-only):** The input consists solely of an image, which is also sufficient for human understanding. This is the standard Visual Question Answering format for VLMs.
- **VTQA (Vision-text):** The input includes both the image and its detailed textual representation, making the information redundant. This tests how VLMs integrate or prioritize information from both modalities.

The dataset construction adheres to key guidelines:
- **Avoidance of data leakage:** Ensuring test data is novel to models pre-trained on web-scale data.
- **Configurability:** Allowing for controlled experiments and easy extension.
- **Scalability:** Enabling generation of sufficient test samples for statistical significance.

#### 3.3. Models Evaluated

A comprehensive set of models was tested:
- **Open-source LLMs:** Phi2-2.7B, LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-2-13B, LLaMA-3-8B, Mistral-7B, Vicuna-7B-1.5, Vicuna-13B-1.5, Nous-Hermes-2-Yi-34B.
- **Open-source VLMs:** Bunny family (Phi-2-SigLIP, Phi-1.5-SigLIP, Phi-2-EVA, Phi-1.5-EVA), CogVLM, CogAgent, InstructBLIP family (Vicuna-7B, Vicuna-13B), LLaVA family (1.6-Mistral-7B, 1.6-Vicuna-7B, 1.6-Vicuna-13B, 1.6-34B), Qwen-VL.
- **Proprietary Models:** OpenAI's GPT-4V, GPT-4o, GPT-4, Google Gemini Pro 1.0, Anthropic Claude 3 Opus.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Protocol

- **Metric:** Accuracy, given the multiple-choice question format (four options per question).
- **Prompting:** A consistent prompt was used: "First, provide a concise answer in one sentence. Then, elaborate on the reasoning behind your answer in a detailed, step-by-step explanation." An ablation study confirmed this "step-by-step explanation" prompt significantly outperformed a simpler "Answer:" prompt.
- **Decoding:** Models used their default configurations (argmax for deterministic, top-p for non-deterministic). Open-source model results were averaged over three independent runs, while proprietary models were run once due to cost and availability.
- **Ablation Studies:** To deeply understand VLM behavior, controlled experiments were conducted in the VTQA setting:
 - **"No Image" (TQA for VLM):** Removing the image entirely, leaving only textual input.
 - **"Noise Image":** Replacing the original image with Gaussian noise.
 - **"Random Image":** Replacing the original image with an image from the dataset that does not match the textual description.
 - These ablations aimed to determine the extent to which VLMs rely on, or are distracted by, visual information when sufficient textual cues are present.
- **Comparative Analysis:** The study systematically compared:
 - TQA (LLM) vs. VQA (VLM)
 - VTQA (VLM) vs. TQA (VLM) / "No Image"
 - VQA (VLM) vs. VTQA (VLM)
 - TQA (VLM) vs. TQA (LLM)

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The comprehensive evaluation across SpatialEval tasks and diverse models yielded several significant and often counter-intuitive findings:

1. **Spatial Reasoning Remains Surprisingly Challenging for VLMs (Vision-only):** For the synthetic tasks (Spatial-Map, Maze-Nav, Spatial-Grid), most competitive multimodal models, when provided with *Vision-only (VQA)* input, perform at levels similar to or even below random guessing (Figure 5). This directly contradicts the intuition that visual input inherently enables spatial understanding in VLMs, demonstrating a critical limitation.

2. **Visual Input Can Hinder Performance (VQA vs. TQA (LLM)):** Despite having an additional visual encoder, VLMs (VQA format) often *under-perform* compared to their LLM counterparts (TQA format) with the same language backbone (Figure 6). For instance, LLaVA-v1.6-Mistral-7B (VQA) performed 15% worse than Mistral-7B (TQA) on Spatial-Grid. This suggests that the process of encoding visual information into a format usable by the language model backbone for spatial reasoning is inefficient or even detrimental.

3. **Reduced Reliance on Visual Information When Text is Sufficient:**
 - **VLMs Perform Better Without Images:** When the original image is entirely removed and VLMs are given *only textual input* (TQA for VLM), their performance on spatial reasoning tasks *improves* across a range of architectures compared to when the (redundant) original image is present (Figure 8). For example, LLaVA-1.6-34B's accuracy on Spatial-Grid improved by 20.1% when no image was presented. This strongly indicates that if textual information alone can answer the question, additional visual input does not necessarily help, and can even hinder, performance.
 - **Noise/Random Images Don't Necessarily Hurt:** Replacing the original image with a *Gaussian noise image* (Figure 9) or a *random, mismatched image* (Figure 10) often *improves* or *does not significantly degrade* performance compared to using the original image in the VTQA setting. This further reinforces the finding that VLMs tend to prioritize textual information, especially when visual cues are either irrelevant or contradictory, implying a limited ability to extract and rely on precise spatial details from images.

4. **Leveraging Redundancy: Text Enhances VLM Performance:** When provided with both visual and textual input (VTQA), VLMs consistently show *enhanced performance* compared to Vision-only (VQA) input (Figure 11). This highlights the significant benefit of having *redundant textual descriptions* alongside images for VLMs, suggesting that current architectures primarily rely on text to resolve spatial reasoning questions, even when an image is present.

5. **Multimodal Training Benefits Language Backbones:** VLMs, when evaluated with *text-only inputs (TQA for VLM)*, often *outperform their corresponding LLM backbones (TQA for LLM)* (Figure 12). This suggests that the multimodal training process, even if not fully enabling visual spatial reasoning, somehow enhances the language model's inherent ability to process and reason with spatial concepts when presented textually.

6. **Proprietary Models Show Similar Trends:** While top proprietary models (GPT-4V, GPT-4o, Claude 3 Opus, Gemini Pro 1.0) generally outperform open-source models, the *fundamental trends* observed still hold true (Figure 13). Specifically, VQA inputs consistently lead to lower performance compared to TQA and VTQA, indicating that even the most advanced commercial models do not fully leverage visual inputs for spatial reasoning, showing similar limitations as their open-source counterparts.

These findings collectively challenge the notion that current VLMs inherently excel at spatial reasoning simply by having access to visual data, suggesting a deeper architectural or training limitation in how they integrate and interpret visual spatial information.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant contribution to the field of artificial intelligence, particularly concerning the understanding and development of multimodal foundation models.

**Novelty and Diagnostic Power of SpatialEval:** The introduction of *SpatialEval* is a major contribution. It is the first comprehensive benchmark designed to systematically probe diverse aspects of spatial reasoning in LLMs and VLMs across various modalities (text-only, vision-only, vision-text). Unlike previous benchmarks that primarily focused on image-text retrieval or general visual question answering without deep spatial understanding, SpatialEval explicitly tests relationship understanding, navigation, position, and counting in spatial contexts, exposing weaknesses that were previously overlooked. Its configurable and scalable nature makes it a valuable tool for future research.

**Unveiling Counter-intuitive Limitations:** The paper's main findings challenge prevailing assumptions about the capabilities of modern VLMs. The discovery that VLMs often struggle with spatial reasoning tasks, sometimes performing worse than random guessing or their text-only counterparts, is a striking and counter-intuitive insight. Even more surprisingly, the observation that providing *no image*, a *noise image*, or a *mismatched random image* can lead to *better* or *unchanged* performance compared to the correct original image in the presence of sufficient text, profoundly impacts our understanding of how these models process visual information. It strongly suggests that current VLMs predominantly rely on textual cues, effectively "seeing without understanding" when it comes to precise spatial visual details.

**Informative for Future Model Architecture Design:** The authors directly attribute these limitations to "overly simplistic handling of visual information in current architectures and training pipelines." This work provides critical evidence that merely "translating" visual input into the language space (the current paradigm) is insufficient for robust spatial reasoning. The paper makes a compelling call for a fundamental shift: future models need new architectures that treat "vision input as a first-class source of information and reason in a joint vision-language space." This insight will drive research toward more sophisticated and truly integrated multimodal architectures, moving beyond simple concatenation or attention mechanisms that might prioritize language due to its inherent structure.

**Bridging the Gap Towards Human Intelligence:** Spatial reasoning is foundational to human cognition, enabling everyday activities from navigation to complex problem-solving. By highlighting the current shortcomings of state-of-the-art AI models in this area, this research provides a clear roadmap for improving AI's cognitive abilities. Successfully developing models with enhanced spatial intelligence would have profound implications across numerous applications:
- **Robotics:** More robust navigation, object manipulation, and interaction with physical environments.
- **Augmented/Virtual Reality:** More intuitive and spatially aware user interfaces and experiences.
- **Autonomous Systems:** Improved decision-making in complex dynamic spaces.
- **Medical Imaging:** Better interpretation of spatial relationships in diagnostic images.
- **Geospatial Analysis:** More accurate understanding of maps and geographical data.

In summary, this paper not only provides a crucial diagnostic tool but also delivers profound, actionable insights into the fundamental limitations of current multimodal AI. Its findings are poised to accelerate the development of more intelligent, spatially aware AI systems, significantly narrowing the gap between artificial and human intelligence.
