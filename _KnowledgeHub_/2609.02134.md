# Unified Motion Retargeting for Humanoids with Learned Point Cloud Correspondence

> [!summary] Summary
> UMR (Unified Motion Retargeting) transfers human motion to humanoid robots via learned dense point-cloud correspondence instead of hand-crafted joint mappings. A PointNet encoder plus MLP decoder predicts deformation vectors pairing canonical human and robot surface points; those pairs anchor a constrained Gauss-Newton QP matching positions, surface normals, and contact maps. It beats GMR and OmniRetarget on tracking success and contact-rich interaction, and deploys on real hardware.

## Problem

- Prior humanoid retargeting is skeleton-centric, pairing manually selected human joints to robot links, so each new embodiment needs redefined mappings and retuned fitting objectives.
- Sparse joint or keypoint correspondence constrains only a small subset of body geometry, giving too little morphological information for fine-grained pose alignment or reliable contact transfer.
- Point-cloud body-registration methods assume a shared human template and compatible anatomy, and interaction methods rely on heuristic stance detection that mismatches across motion distributions.

## Method

- **Point Cloud Correspondence Learning**: ==PointNet== encoder plus MLP decoder predicts per-point deformation vectors mapping ordered canonical human T-pose points onto unordered robot T-pose points, trained once and reused.
- Correspondence loss combines symmetric ==Chamfer== geometric supervision, a repulsion term preventing point collapse, and geodesic-graph edge smoothness enforcing coherent neighboring deformation vectors.
- **Correspondence-Guided Retargeting**: damped constrained ==Gauss-Newton QP== minimizes position, surface-normal, and contact-vector residuals under joint limits, floor-clearance inequalities, and a trust region.

## Results

- On LAFAN1 with BeyondMimic, UMR raises Sim2Sim tracking success over GMR on all categories (Fight **95.3** vs **79.5**) and cuts mean global body-part error to **89.6 mm** vs **198.9 mm**.
- On contact-rich tasks UMR beats OmniRetarget: Carry **99.28** vs **82.89** percent success, Stair **43.53** vs **11.01**, with joint error reduced roughly **40** to **56** percent on object tasks.
- Under SONIC on BONES-SEED without the SMPL encoder, UMR references improve reward and tracking errors about **10** percent relatively; setup costs **25.79 s**, retargeting runs **65.29 FPS**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense surface geometry can replace hand-designed semantic correspondence: shared point ordering lets segment weights and contact maps transfer to new embodiments with no remapping.
> - Learning correspondence in a canonical T-pose isolates stable morphological differences from pose-dependent deformation, so one cheap setup amortizes across an entire motion corpus.
> - Hard hand-crafted constraints like foot-sticking can hurt out of distribution; softer geometric residuals proved more robust on noisy video-reconstructed scene-interaction motion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics extensively utilizes human motion data as a reference for teaching robots complex behaviors such as locomotion, manipulation, and interaction with environments. However, directly applying human motion to robots is challenging due to fundamental differences in morphology, degrees of freedom, joint kinematics, and physical constraints. This necessitates motion retargeting, a process of transforming human motion into robot-executable trajectories.

Historically, motion retargeting has been prevalent in computer animation, adapting motion capture data to various character skeletons. These early methods often involved spacetime optimization or online tracking, primarily defining correspondence in a "skeleton-centric" manner using selected joints or end-effectors. For humanoid robotics, these techniques evolved to incorporate robot-specific kinematic, dynamic, and physical constraints, focusing on balance, whole-body feasibility, and adapting morphology. Learning-based approaches have also emerged to infer human-robot joint mappings from paired or unpaired data.

A consistent limitation across these prior methods is their reliance on sparse, hand-crafted, skeleton-level correspondences between humans and robots. This approach makes retargeting highly specific to particular embodiments, requiring redefinition and retuning for each new robot morphology or motion source. Such specificity hinders scalability and the ability to leverage the vast and diverse human motion datasets available. Furthermore, sparse joint-level mappings provide limited geometric information, making fine-grained pose alignment and accurate contact transfer challenging.

In parallel, advancements in human motion representation have increasingly moved towards deformable mesh models (e.g., SMPL-X, SOMA), which offer dense surface information rather than just skeletal structures. This shift presents an opportunity for surface-based correspondence, but a comprehensive whole-body surface mapping that can bridge disparate human and robot embodiments for motion retargeting has been a gap in the literature. Previous work has explored geometry in motion transfer or surface-based contact representation separately, but not a unified, dense surface-to-surface mapping for general retargeting.

This work, Unified Motion Retargeting (UMR), positions itself to address this gap by proposing a framework that moves beyond skeleton-centric paradigms. It introduces a surface-centric approach that learns dense point cloud correspondence, aiming to unify retargeting across heterogeneous motion sources and robot embodiments. By doing so, it contributes to building a more scalable and robust interface for converting large-scale human motion corpora into high-quality, robot-ready training data for learning complex behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and scalable framework for retargeting diverse human motion data to humanoid robots, producing high-quality robot reference trajectories. The authors are motivated by several challenges and limitations inherent in existing motion retargeting methodologies:

1. **Bridging Morphological Discrepancies:** Humanoid robots exhibit significant differences from humans in terms of body proportions, joint topology, degrees of freedom, and kinematic constraints. Directly applying human motion data to robots is often infeasible, necessitating a sophisticated retargeting mechanism.
2. **Addressing Scalability and Generality Limitations of Existing Methods:** Traditional retargeting methods largely depend on manually defined, sparse skeletal or body-part correspondences. This manual semantic design renders these methods embodiment-specific, requiring considerable effort to redefine mappings and readjust parameters when switching to new motion sources or target robots. As the scale and diversity of human motion data grow, such a labor-intensive approach becomes a significant bottleneck. The authors aim to overcome this by introducing a method that requires no manual skeletal or body-part mappings.
3. **Enhancing Motion Fidelity and Plausibility:** Sparse, joint-level representations inherently provide limited morphological information. This restricts the quality of fine-grained pose alignment and hinders reliable transfer of interaction contacts. The motivation is to achieve surface-level pose alignment and direct transfer of interaction contacts, which requires a denser and more geometrically-aware correspondence.
4. **Creating a Unified Interface:** The diverse nature of human motion representations (e.g., SMPL-X, SOMA, character animations, scanned meshes) and robot embodiments (varying sizes, kinematics) complicates the development of a general-purpose retargeting solution. The authors aim to establish a "unified interface" that decouples retargeting from source-specific skeletal semantics and robot-specific topology, thereby enabling broader applicability.
5. **Supporting Downstream Robot Learning Tasks:** The ultimate motivation is to provide a foundation for transforming large-scale human motion references into high-quality, robot-ready training data. This data is critical for training robot control policies for a wide range of whole-body behaviors, from basic locomotion to complex object and scene interactions. The quality and robustness of retargeted motion directly impact the success and realism of learned robot behaviors.

In summary, the authors seek to develop a unified, surface-centric retargeting framework that learns dense point cloud correspondence to enable scalable, high-fidelity, and generalizable motion transfer, ultimately facilitating the large-scale training of humanoid robot behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Unified Motion Retargeting (UMR) framework operates on the principle of representing motion through the moving exterior body surface, rather than relying on source skeletons. This surface-centric approach allows UMR to treat any motion source providing a canonical T-pose mesh and its posed surfaces over time (e.g., SMPL-family models, SOMA, rigged characters, scanned human meshes) through the same pipeline. It samples these surfaces as point clouds, which removes the requirement for compatible skeletons or mesh topology between the human source and the robot target, while retaining essential geometric information for pose and contact.

The UMR pipeline is organized into two primary stages:

#### 3.1. A. Point Cloud Correspondence Learning

This initial stage focuses on establishing a reusable, ordered mapping between source and robot surface point clouds. This mapping is learned once from aligned canonical T-poses, ensuring stability by isolating geometric differences from pose-dependent deformations. The paired points are subsequently bound to their respective meshes to maintain the correspondence throughout the motion sequence.

1. **Input:**
 - An ordered exterior point cloud (X_h) sampled from the aligned human T-pose. The ordering of X_h provides the indices for the learned correspondence.
 - An unordered exterior point cloud (X_r) sampled from the aligned robot T-pose. This serves as the target surface geometry.
2. **Network Architecture:**
 - A PointNet-style encoder (E_θ) processes the unordered robot point cloud (X_r) into a latent representation.
 - A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) decoder (D_θ) takes this latent representation and predicts a deformation vector for each indexed human point.
3. **Correspondence Generation:** The deformation vector is added to the corresponding human point (x_h_i), producing its estimated location on the robot (x̂_r_i). This results in a reconstructed robot-side correspondence point cloud (x̂_r) where each point inherits the index from its human counterpart, establishing an ordered human-robot point pair.
4. **Training Objective (L_corr):** The learning process minimizes a combined loss function to ensure accurate and coherent correspondence:
 - **Chamfer Loss (L_c):** This is the primary geometric supervision, minimizing the symmetric squared nearest-neighbor distance between the reconstructed robot point cloud (x̂_r) and the actual target robot point cloud (X_r). It ensures that the reconstructed points accurately cover the target robot surface.
 - **Repulsion Term (L_r):** This term prevents multiple reconstructed points from collapsing into the same local region on the robot point cloud, promoting a more uniform and diverse distribution of correspondence points.
 - **Edge Smoothness Term (L_e):** Applied to the human template's geodesic graph, this term encourages similar deformation vectors between topologically adjacent human points. This is crucial for learning coherent and smooth point cloud correspondences, ensuring that the learned mapping respects the underlying body structure.

This learned, indexed correspondence ensures that robot points effectively inherit human body segment labels, providing a unified interface for the subsequent optimization stage where segment-specific weights and parameters can be reused across different robots.

#### 3.2. B. Correspondence-Guided Retargeting

Once the canonical point pairs are established, they become motion targets for the robot. During motion, human points move with the source mesh via barycentric transport, while robot points follow their link-local bindings through forward kinematics. This converts retargeting into a surface matching problem, solved frame-by-frame.

1. **Optimization Objective:** For each frame, UMR estimates the robot's generalized coordinates (q_t) by minimizing a stack of point-level residuals, balancing whole-body pose matching with emphasis on active contacts:
 - **Pose Residual (r_p):** For each correspondence point *i*, this residual aims to match both its position and local surface orientation. It compares the robot point's position (x_r_i(q_t)) and normalized normal vector (n̄_r_i(q_t)) with their human counterparts (x_h_t,i and n̄_h_t,i). Segment-dependent weights (w_p_i, w_n_i) are applied to balance contributions from different body regions.
 - **Contact Residual (r_c):** This residual facilitates direct transfer of contact maps using the same point indexing from the correspondence learning stage. Contact is represented by directional vectors from a correspondence point to its nearest environment point (y_t,π_t(i)). The residual (r_c_i) minimizes the difference between the human contact vector (c_h_t,i) and the robot's contact vector (c_r_i(q_t)). An "active contact map set" (C_t) includes points within a defined contact threshold (τ_c). This mechanism applies uniformly to contacts with the ground, manipulated objects, or other body segments (self-contact).
2. **Constrained Update:** The optimization problem involves non-linear dependencies on q_t through forward kinematics. UMR addresses this through a constrained Gauss-Newton Quadratic Programming (QP) solver:
 - **Linearization:** The combined residuals (r(q_t)) are linearized around the current pose estimate (q_t) to derive an update step (∆q).
 - **Quadratic Program:** The update ∆q is found by solving a convex QP that minimizes the squared norm of the linearized residuals, regularized by a damping coefficient (μ).
 - **Constraints:** The QP incorporates several constraints to ensure physical plausibility and robot capabilities:
 - **Joint Limits:** Lower (q−) and upper (q+) bounds on joint angles.
 - **Trust-Region Radius:** A limit (η) on the magnitude of the update step (∆q) to ensure local validity of the linearization.
 - **Floor Clearance:** Linear inequality constraints (A_t∆q ≤ b_t) are derived from the Jacobian of active robot surface points near the floor height (z_f), ensuring the robot does not penetrate the ground.

UMR is implemented using the MuJoCo physics engine, and the constrained Gauss-Newton subproblems are solved with Clarabel. This comprehensive methodology, from learning dense surface correspondence to solving constrained optimization, aims to provide a unified and effective solution for humanoid motion retargeting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents an extensive evaluation of UMR across various dimensions, including its ability to unify retargeting, whole-body tracking performance, and efficacy in contact-rich interaction tasks.

#### 4.1. A. Unified Retargeting Across Sources and Embodiments

UMR demonstrated its capability to unify retargeting across diverse motion sources and robot embodiments.
- **Source Diversity:** The framework successfully retargeted motions from four distinct source representations: humanoid character animations (MimicKit), SOMA motions (BONES-SEED), SMPL-X motions (LAFAN1), and custom scanned human mesh animations.
- **Embodiment Diversity:** Retargeting was performed on five different humanoid robot embodiments, which varied substantially in morphology and height (from 0.75m to 1.83m).
- **Consistent Pipeline:** A key finding is that the core correspondence learning and retargeting processes remained unchanged across all source-robot pairs. Source-specific adaptations were limited to the definition and availability of body segments used for point selection and weighting, demonstrating the method's generality.

#### 4.2. B. Whole-Body Tracking Performance Comparison

UMR's performance was quantitatively compared against state-of-the-art baselines, GMR and Unitree retargeted references, using two scales of evaluation: per-motion tracking and large-scale policy learning.

1. **Per-Motion Tracking Quality (LAFAN1 dataset with BeyondMimic):**
 - **Success Rate (Table II):** UMR consistently achieved higher motion tracking success rates than GMR, particularly on challenging motions like "Fall and GetUp" and "Fight" in nominal simulation (Sim w/o DR). The advantage of UMR became more pronounced under domain randomization (Sim w/ DR) and Sim2Sim deployment settings, where it outperformed GMR across all motion categories and remained competitive with the Unitree retargeted references. This suggests UMR produces more plausible reference motions that lead to robust policy tracking.
 - **Tracking Errors (Table III):** UMR exhibited improved tracking accuracy over GMR across all metrics. It was comparable to Unitree retargeted references in global and root-relative body-part position errors (E_g-mpbpe, E_mpbpe) and achieved the lowest mean joint-angle errors (E_mpjpe) in both simulation settings. These results indicate that UMR yields references that support more accurate and robust downstream tracking.
 - **Real-World Validation:** A high-dynamic spin kick motion, retargeted by UMR from MimicKit, was successfully tracked on a physical robot, providing practical validation.

2. **Large-Scale Policy Learning (BONES-SEED dataset with SONIC framework):**
 - UMR directly retargets actor-specific SOMA-Proportional motions, a key advantage over GMR which uses SOMA-Uniform motions. UMR demonstrated better preservation of actor-specific body geometry, posture, and reduced ground contact artifacts compared to GMR (Fig. 6).
 - **SONIC Training Curves (Fig. 7):**
 - When the SMPL encoder (leveraging source human motion and latent-space alignment) was enabled, UMR-retargeted references yielded performance comparable to the released Unitree references.
 - Without the SMPL encoder, UMR consistently outperformed the released references across total reward, anchor-position tracking error, and mean joint-angle tracking error, showing approximately a 10% relative improvement in total reward towards the end of training. This highlights the intrinsic quality of UMR's robot references without additional guidance from the human source.

#### 4.3. C. Contact-Rich Interaction Retargeting

UMR was evaluated against OmniRetarget on robot-object (OmniContact) and robot-scene (GRAIL) interaction tasks.

1. **Robot-Object Interaction:**
 - **Contact Preservation (Fig. 8):** UMR demonstrated superior preservation of intended bimanual contact geometry (e.g., hand-object contact), whereas OmniRetarget sometimes shifted contact to incorrect surface regions.
 - **Performance Metrics (Table IV):** UMR outperformed OmniRetarget across all reported metrics (success rate, joint error, object error) on "Carry," "Kick," and "Push" tasks. Policies trained on UMR references reduced joint error by approximately 40% to 56% across these tasks, indicating higher-quality references for accurate interaction tracking.
 - **Real-World Validation:** Successful deployment of the resulting object-interaction policy on a physical robot was demonstrated.

2. **Robot-Scene Interaction:**
 - **Performance Metrics (Table IV):** Despite using noisier GRAIL motions (reconstructed from VFM-generated videos) as a source, UMR substantially outperformed OmniRetarget on "Stair" traversal and performed better on both metrics for "Slope." OmniRetarget had a slight advantage on "Chair." The authors suggest that OmniRetarget's reliance on heuristic stance detection and hard foot-sticking constraints may be a limitation when applied across diverse motion distributions. UMR, by avoiding such hand-crafted heuristics, maintained effectiveness across these scene-interaction motions.
 - **Real-World Validation:** The stair-traversal policy, retargeted by UMR, was successfully deployed on a physical robot.

In conclusion, the findings indicate that UMR provides a robust, generalizable, and high-fidelity motion retargeting solution that improves upon existing methods, particularly in scenarios requiring fine-grained pose control and accurate contact transfer, and that it supports effective downstream robot learning and real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Unified Motion Retargeting (UMR) framework presents several significant contributions and holds substantial potential impact within the fields of humanoid robotics and motion synthesis:

1. **Enhanced Scalability and Efficiency:** By moving beyond manually defined skeletal mappings, UMR offers a scalable solution for transforming large-scale, heterogeneous human motion datasets into robot-ready training data. This reduces the significant manual effort and domain expertise traditionally required for retargeting, enabling faster iteration and broader exploration of complex robot behaviors. This is particularly impactful given the increasing availability of diverse human motion capture data and simulated human movements.

2. **Increased Generality and Unification:** UMR provides a unified, surface-centric interface that decouples motion retargeting from the specific skeletal semantics of the human source and the topological constraints of the robot embodiment. This generalizability means the same framework can process a wide array of human motion representations (e.g., SMPL-X, SOMA, custom meshes) and target various robot morphologies without substantial reconfiguration. This unification streamlines the workflow for robot researchers and developers, making motion transfer more accessible and efficient across different platforms.

3. **Higher Motion Fidelity and Plausibility:** The learned dense point cloud correspondence provides fine-grained geometric anchors, enabling surface-level pose alignment and direct transfer of interaction contacts. This leads to retargeted motions that exhibit higher fidelity to the original human intent, are more plausible for robot execution, and reduce artifacts often seen with sparse, joint-based methods. The quantitative results on tracking errors and success rates validate this improvement, showing UMR generates more accurate and robust reference trajectories.

4. **Improved Handling of Contact-Rich Interactions:** A notable impact of UMR is its ability to accurately preserve and transfer complex contact relationships between the robot and its environment (objects, ground, scene). By directly operating on surface geometry and contact maps, UMR mitigates issues like mismatched contact points or distorted interaction poses, which are common limitations in methods relying on sparse joint correspondences. This capability is crucial for training robots to perform dexterous manipulation, human-robot collaboration, and navigation in complex environments.

5. **Robust Foundation for Robot Learning:** The high quality and plausibility of UMR-generated references directly translate into more effective and robust downstream policy learning for humanoid robots. As demonstrated in the experiments, policies trained on UMR references achieve higher success rates and lower tracking errors, even under challenging conditions like domain randomization. This provides a solid foundation for developing generalizable and versatile robot control policies, accelerating the deployment of advanced behaviors on physical hardware.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The work also opens several avenues for future research. The current limitation of requiring mesh-based source geometry and a canonical template suggests potential extensions to less structured motion observations (e.g., directly from video or raw sensor data). Furthermore, applying this surface-centric approach to more articulated embodiments like dexterous hands or to complex multi-agent interactions could further expand its impact.

Overall, UMR represents a significant step forward in motion retargeting, providing a robust, scalable, and unified framework that addresses long-standing challenges in transferring human motion to humanoid robots. Its impact is primarily in enabling the efficient generation of high-quality training data, thereby accelerating the development of advanced, human-like behaviors in robotics.
