# Sampling-based Algorithms for Optimal Motion Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> PRM* and RRT* are sampling-based path planners that connect each new sample to all neighbors inside a ball whose radius shrinks as (log n / n)^(1/d), then rewire the tree so every vertex keeps its lowest-cost parent. The paper proves both are asymptotically optimal, unlike PRM and RRT, at only a constant-factor overhead in running time.

## Problem

- Sampling-based planners such as PRM and RRT were analyzed only for probabilistic completeness; nobody characterized how the returned solution cost behaves as sample count grows.
- Practical quality improvements relied on heuristics: biased tree growth, repeated RRT restarts, or post-hoc smoothing, none of which guarantee convergence to an optimal path.
- Grid-based optimal planners such as A* guarantee optimality only up to grid resolution, and their cell count grows exponentially with configuration-space dimension.

## Method

- ==PRM*==: a batch roadmap whose connection radius scales as (log n / n)^(1/d) instead of staying fixed, giving on average log n connections per sample and O(n log n) time.
- **RRG**: an incremental algorithm extending RRT by adding every collision-free edge within that same shrinking ball, building a connected roadmap that improves in anytime fashion.
- **RRT***: an RRG variant keeping a tree, selecting each new vertex's minimum-cost parent from the near set, then rewiring near vertices whose cost improves through the new vertex.

## Results

- Obstacle-free square, 500 Monte-Carlo runs of 20,000 iterations: RRT cost settled near sqrt(2) times optimum with variance approaching **2.5**, while RRT* converged to optimum with variance approaching **zero**.
- With obstacles, over 500 runs of 20,000 iterations RRT averaged about **1.5x** optimum, reaching roughly **2x** optimum when trapped in the wrong homotopy class; all RRT* runs converged.
- Running-time ratio of RRT* to RRT converged to a constant over **one million** iterations averaged across 50 runs; k-nearest sPRM (k = 5 to 15) plateaued above optimum while PRM* converged.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Neighbor-ball radius scaling as (log n / n)^(1/d) is the knife edge: shrink faster and optimality is lost, shrink slower and complexity suffers.
> - Asymptotic optimality is nearly free, costing only a constant factor over probabilistic completeness, so fixed-radius neighbor rules are rarely worth keeping in a planner.
> - Fixed k nearest-neighbor connection is worse than it looks: for small fixed k it can forfeit even probabilistic completeness, not just optimality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic motion planning has evolved significantly over decades, driven by the increasing complexity of robotic systems and environments. Early approaches, such as complete algorithms (e.g., those based on cell decomposition or algebraic methods), while theoretically sound in guaranteeing a solution if one exists, suffered from prohibitive computational complexity, particularly in high-dimensional configuration spaces (known as the "curse of dimensionality"). This limited their practical applicability to relatively low-dimensional problems.

The quest for more practical solutions led to the development of resolution-complete methods (e.g., potential fields), which offered faster computation but introduced challenges like local minima and still struggled with higher dimensions. The last decade saw a paradigm shift with the advent of **sampling-based algorithms (SBAs)**, most notably Probabilistic RoadMaps (PRM) (Kavraki et al., 1996) and Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) (LaValle & Kuffner, 2001). These algorithms circumvent the curse of dimensionality by avoiding explicit representation of the obstacle space. Instead, they randomly sample points from the obstacle-free space and attempt to connect them, building a graph (roadmap) or a tree of feasible trajectories. A key theoretical advantage of these SBAs was their **probabilistic completeness**, meaning that as the number of samples approaches infinity, the probability of finding a solution (if one exists) converges to one. This probabilistic guarantee, combined with their empirical success in high-dimensional problems, propelled SBAs to the forefront of motion planning research.

However, a critical gap remained: while SBAs could *find* a path, there was little formal analysis regarding the *quality* or *optimality* of the found path. Existing efforts to improve path quality were largely heuristic, such as biasing tree growth or repeatedly restarting the planner. Graph search algorithms (like A* or D*) could provide optimal solutions on a discretized space, but their optimality was limited by the grid resolution, and they, too, faced the curse of dimensionality.

This paper by Karaman and Frazzoli precisely addresses this fundamental gap. It delves into the formal analysis of solution quality for SBAs, asking whether the paths found by PRM and RRT converge to the true optimal path. By rigorously demonstrating that they do not, and then proposing new algorithms that *do* achieve asymptotic optimality while maintaining computational efficiency, the paper redefines the theoretical landscape for sampling-based motion planning. It moves the field beyond merely finding a feasible path to finding a *provably optimal* path, offering stronger guarantees essential for advanced robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from a critical oversight in the formal analysis of widely used sampling-based motion planning algorithms like PRM and RRT. While these algorithms had demonstrated practical success and possessed strong theoretical guarantees of "probabilistic completeness" (i.e., the ability to find *a* feasible path if one exists, with high probability), little effort had been devoted to rigorously analyzing the *quality* or *optimality* of the solutions they returned. In many real-world robotic applications, such as autonomous driving, industrial manipulation, or space exploration, merely finding a path is insufficient; the path must also be optimal with respect to certain criteria (e.g., shortest length, minimum time, lowest energy consumption, safest trajectory).

Given this motivation, the paper sets out to achieve several key objectives:

1. **Rigorously Analyze Asymptotic Optimality:** The primary objective is to formally investigate the asymptotic behavior of the cost of the solution returned by stochastic sampling-based algorithms as the number of samples increases. This involves determining whether the cost converges to the true optimal cost.
2. **Characterize Existing Algorithms:** A crucial goal is to rigorously characterize the optimality properties of current, broadly used sampling-based algorithms, specifically PRM and RRT. This involves providing formal "negative results," demonstrating that these algorithms, under mild technical conditions, converge almost surely to a *non-optimal* value.
3. **Introduce Asymptotically Optimal Algorithms:** The main contribution is the development of novel sampling-based algorithms – namely PRM*, RRG (Rapidly-exploring Random Graph), and RRT* (Optimal Rapidly-exploring Random Tree) – that are provably asymptotically optimal. This means the cost of the returned solution converges almost surely to the true optimum as the number of samples increases.
4. **Evaluate Computational Efficiency:** Alongside optimality, the paper aims to show that the new, asymptotically optimal algorithms are computationally efficient, specifically demonstrating that their complexity is within a constant factor of their probabilistically complete (but not asymptotically optimal) counterparts.
5. **Establish Novel Theoretical Connections:** A significant underlying objective and methodological contribution is to establish novel connections between stochastic sampling-based path planning algorithms and the theory of random geometric graphs, using insights from the latter to derive rigorous proofs for the former.

In essence, the paper seeks to fill a critical theoretical gap, moving sampling-based motion planning from merely "finding a path" to "finding the optimal path" with rigorous, provable guarantees, all while maintaining computational efficiency relevant for practical applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of Karaman and Frazzoli's work is a rigorous theoretical analysis grounded in the mathematical framework of **random geometric graphs (RGGs)**, complemented by the design and empirical validation of new algorithms.

**Theoretical Foundation in Random Geometric Graphs:**
The paper meticulously connects the structures built by sampling-based planners (roadmaps, trees) to well-studied models in RGG theory.
- **RGG Models:** It introduces and leverages concepts like Gilbert's r-disc graph (where nodes are connected if within radius 'r'), k-nearest neighbor graphs (nodes connected to their k closest neighbors), and online nearest neighbor graphs (nodes connected to the closest previously sampled node).
- **RGG Properties:** The analysis draws heavily on RGG properties like percolation (formation of an infinite connected component) and connectivity (the entire graph becomes connected). Key theorems from RGG literature (e.g., Penrose, Balister et al.) are directly applied, particularly those concerning critical thresholds where phase transitions in connectivity occur. This connection is fundamental to determining the necessary scaling laws for asymptotic optimality.

**Formal Problem Formulation and Definitions:**
The authors begin by precisely defining the path planning problem:
- **Configuration Space:** A d-dimensional Euclidean space `X`.
- **Obstacle-Free Space:** `X_free`.
- **Path Properties:** Path, collision-free path, feasible path, total variation (length/cost).
- **Robust Feasibility:** A problem is "robustly feasible" if there exists a path with "strong δ-clearance" (meaning the path lies entirely within a δ-interior of `X_free`, i.e., at least distance δ from obstacles). This ensures there's enough "space" for random sampling to work.
- **Asymptotic Optimality:** Defined as the almost-sure convergence of the solution cost to the true optimal cost (`c*`) as the number of samples (`n`) approaches infinity.
- **Robust Optimality:** An optimal path is "robustly optimal" if it has "weak δ-clearance" (it is homotopic to a path with strong δ-clearance) and the cost function is continuous with respect to path convergence in a defined bounded variation norm.
- **Zero-measure Optimal Paths:** An assumption (Assumption 27) that the set of all points traversed by an optimal trajectory has measure zero, ensuring that optimal solutions are not found in finite time merely by chance sampling.

**Analysis of Existing Algorithms:**
- **PRM and RRT:** The paper rigorously proves that standard PRM and RRT algorithms are *not* asymptotically optimal. For PRM, this is shown by demonstrating an equivalence to RRT under certain conditions, and then proving RRT's non-optimality. For RRT, the proof relies on showing that the length of paths in its branches does not converge to zero sufficiently fast to reach the optimal path. The core issue is that existing algorithms, while probabilistically complete, do not refine the path quality over time.
- **sPRM (simplified PRM):** This batch algorithm is shown to be asymptotically optimal, but at a high computational cost.
- **k-nearest PRM (fixed k):** This heuristic is shown to be neither probabilistically complete (for k=1) nor asymptotically optimal for any fixed k, reinforcing the idea that heuristics without theoretical basis can be problematic.

**Design of New Algorithms (PRM*, RRG, RRT*):**
The crucial insight for achieving optimality lies in how connections are made between sampled nodes. The authors propose that the **connection radius `r` (or number of neighbors `k`) must scale appropriately with the number of samples `n`**.
- **Scaling Law:** The proposed algorithms use a variable connection radius `r(n) = γ (log(n)/n)^(1/d)` (where `d` is the dimension and `γ` is a constant derived from RGG connectivity thresholds) or a number of neighbors `k(n) = k_constant * log(n)`. This ensures that as `n` increases, each new sample can "see" enough neighbors to eventually form connections that approach the optimal path, while simultaneously keeping the computational burden manageable.
- **PRM*:** A batch-processing algorithm similar to sPRM but with the variable connection radius `r(n)`.
- **RRG (Rapidly-exploring Random Graph):** An incremental algorithm that builds a graph (potentially with cycles). It extends RRT by checking all neighbors within `r(n)` for potential new edges, not just the single nearest neighbor.
- **RRT* (Optimal RRT):** An incremental algorithm that maintains a tree structure (like RRT) but incorporates "rewiring" steps. When a new node `x_new` is added, it connects to the *cheapest* parent among its neighbors within `r(n)`. Subsequently, other existing nodes in its neighborhood are checked: if connecting them through `x_new` would result in a lower-cost path from the root, their existing parent connection is replaced with an edge to `x_new`. This rewiring ensures continuous path optimization.

**Proof Techniques:**
The proofs for asymptotic optimality of the new algorithms primarily use:
- **Borel-Cantelli Lemma:** To establish almost-sure convergence. This typically involves showing that the sum of probabilities of "bad" events (e.g., a critical region not being sampled, or a path not being connected) is finite, implying that these bad events occur only a finite number of times.
- **Chernoff Bounds:** For bounding large deviations in the number of samples falling into specific regions.
- **Geometric Arguments:** To show that the selected scaling laws for `r(n)` or `k(n)` ensure sufficient coverage and connectivity to approximate the optimal path arbitrarily closely.
- **Poissonization/De-Poissonization:** Approximating the binomial point process (fixed number of samples) with a Poisson process (random number of samples) for easier mathematical tractability, then demonstrating the results hold for the original process.

**Computational Complexity Analysis:**
The paper meticulously analyzes the time and space complexity of algorithms in terms of calls to `CollisionFree` procedures, nearest/near neighbor searches, and overall graph operations (Dijkstra's for query phase). It relies on existing efficient data structures (e.g., k-d trees, BBD trees) for neighbor searches.

**Numerical Experiments:**
Simulations in 2D to 10D environments, with various obstacles and cost functions, empirically validate the theoretical findings. They show that RRT* indeed converges to the optimal solution while RRT does not, and that the computational overhead of RRT* over RRT converges to a constant factor, confirming its efficiency claims.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper delivers a set of profound theoretical and practical results that reshape the understanding of sampling-based motion planning algorithms:

#### 4.1. Negative Results for Existing Sampling-Based Algorithms

- **Non-Optimality of PRM and RRT:** A central and significant finding is the formal proof that standard Probabilistic RoadMaps (PRM) and Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) are *not asymptotically optimal*. This means that even with an infinite number of samples, the solution paths returned by these widely used algorithms converge almost surely to a cost that is *strictly greater* than the true optimal cost. This debunks the implicit assumption that more samples automatically lead to better solutions in these algorithms.
- **Computational Cost of sPRM:** The "simplified PRM" (sPRM) is shown to be asymptotically optimal, but at a significant computational cost, with a time complexity of O(n^2), rendering it impractical for large-scale problems.
- **Incompleteness and Non-Optimality of k-nearest sPRM (fixed k):** Heuristic variants like the k-nearest sPRM (where k is a fixed constant) are demonstrated to be neither probabilistically complete (e.g., for k=1, the probability of finding a solution converges to zero) nor asymptotically optimal for any fixed k. This highlights the dangers of relying on intuitively appealing but unproven heuristics.
- **Non-Optimality of Variable-Radius sPRM (gamma n^(-1/d)):** The paper also shows that a variable-radius sPRM where the connection radius `r(n)` shrinks too quickly (specifically as `γn^(-1/d)`) is not asymptotically optimal. This reinforces the necessity of the precise scaling laws derived.

#### 4.2. Positive Results for Proposed Asymptotically Optimal Algorithms

The main contribution is the introduction and rigorous proof of properties for three new algorithms: PRM*, RRG, and RRT*.
- **PRM* (Optimal Probabilistic RoadMaps):**
 - **Probabilistically Complete:** Yes.
 - **Asymptotically Optimal:** Yes, provably.
 - **Monotone Convergence:** No (solution quality can fluctuate).
 - **Computational Efficiency:** O(n log n) processing time, O(n log n) query time, O(n log n) space complexity. This achieves asymptotic optimality with a modest, near-linear increase in computational cost compared to basic PRM.
- **RRG (Rapidly-exploring Random Graph):**
 - **Probabilistically Complete:** Yes.
 - **Asymptotically Optimal:** Yes, provably.
 - **Monotone Convergence:** Yes (solution quality monotonically improves over time).
 - **Computational Efficiency:** O(n log n) processing time, O(n log n) query time, O(n log n) space complexity.
- **RRT* (Optimal Rapidly-exploring Random Tree):**
 - **Probabilistically Complete:** Yes.
 - **Asymptotically Optimal:** Yes, provably.
 - **Monotone Convergence:** Yes (solution quality monotonically improves over time).
 - **Computational Efficiency:** O(n log n) processing time, O(n) query time, O(n) space complexity. RRT* emerges as the most efficient and practical asymptotically optimal algorithm among those proposed, maintaining a tree structure while converging to the optimum.
- **Key Scaling Law:** The provable optimality and efficiency of these new algorithms hinge on a crucial connection radius `r(n)` (or number of neighbors `k(n)`) that scales as `γ(log(n)/n)^(1/d)` (or `k_constant * log(n)` for k-nearest variants). This ensures that connections are sparse enough for efficiency but dense enough for optimality.

#### 4.3. Novel Theoretical Connections

- The paper establishes a deep and explicit link between sampling-based motion planning algorithms and the theory of random geometric graphs. This connection is used to derive the critical scaling factors for optimality and offers new insights into phenomena like percolation and connectivity in the context of motion planning. The authors conjecture that an SBA is probabilistically complete if its underlying RGG percolates, and asymptotically optimal if its underlying RGG is connected.

#### 4.4. Experimental Validation

- Numerical experiments validate the theoretical findings across various dimensions (2D to 10D) and environments (obstacle-free, cluttered). Results clearly show RRT* converging to the optimal path while RRT's path quality stagnates at a suboptimal level. Crucially, the experiments demonstrate that the computational overhead of RRT* over RRT converges to a constant factor, confirming its practical viability. The figures illustrate RRT*’s ability to find better paths over time, even discovering different homotopy classes if they offer lower costs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work by Karaman and Frazzoli marks a watershed moment in the field of motion planning, fundamentally changing the theoretical understanding and practical design of sampling-based algorithms. Its significance and potential impact are manifold:

1. **Redefining Performance Guarantees:** Prior to this work, the primary theoretical guarantee for SBAs was probabilistic completeness – the ability to *find* a path. This paper elevates the standard by introducing and proving **asymptotic optimality**, ensuring that the found path not only exists but also converges to the *best possible* path. This is a crucial distinction for real-world applications where path quality (e.g., shortest distance, minimum energy, safest route) is paramount.
2. **Theoretical Foundation for Optimal Planning:** The paper provides a rigorous mathematical framework, bridging sampling-based motion planning with the well-established theory of random geometric graphs. This connection is not merely academic; it provides the *exact scaling laws* (e.g., `(log(n)/n)^(1/d)` for connection radius, or `log(n)` for number of neighbors) required to guarantee optimality while maintaining efficiency. This prescriptive guidance is invaluable for future algorithm design.
3. **Enabling "Anytime" Optimal Planning:** The proposed RRG and RRT* algorithms are inherently "anytime" algorithms. They quickly provide a first feasible solution and then continuously improve its quality as more computation time becomes available, eventually converging to the global optimum. This property is highly desirable for online or time-constrained robotic applications where an initial solution is needed fast, but refinement is beneficial.
4. **Practical Impact on Robotics:** For high-dimensional robotic systems (e.g., robot manipulators, autonomous vehicles, humanoid robots), finding optimal trajectories has always been a grand challenge. The RRT* algorithm, in particular, offers a practical and provably optimal solution for these complex systems, making it a cornerstone for advanced robotic capabilities. Its demonstrated performance in varying dimensions and environments, coupled with its relatively low computational overhead, ensures its widespread adoption.
5. **Catalyst for Further Research:**
 - **Beyond Path Planning:** The paper explicitly notes that its methodology can be extended to more complex problems like kinodynamic planning (considering robot dynamics), planning with temporal or logical constraints (e.g., "rules of the road"), and even solving partial differential equations like the Eikonal or Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equations. Preliminary work on some of these extensions (e.g., Karaman and Frazzoli, 2010a, 2010c) is cited.
 - **Deterministic Sampling:** The authors suggest exploring deterministic sampling strategies (e.g., using low-dispersion sequences) as an alternative to random sampling, potentially leading to faster convergence or tighter bounds.
 - **Deeper RGG Connections:** The conjecture regarding the equivalence of probabilistic completeness/asymptotic optimality with RGG percolation/connectivity opens up a rich vein of future theoretical research.
6. **Open-Source Contribution:** The release of an open-source software library for the new algorithms is a significant contribution to the research community. It lowers the barrier to entry for other researchers, facilitates replication of results, and accelerates the development of new applications and extensions, ensuring the broad impact of this fundamental work.

In summary, Karaman and Frazzoli's paper fundamentally advances the theoretical understanding of sampling-based motion planning, providing the first provably asymptotically optimal and computationally efficient algorithms in this class. Its profound implications extend from theoretical robotics to practical applications across various industries, solidifying its status as a landmark paper in the field.
