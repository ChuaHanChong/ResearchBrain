# PixelVLA: Advancing Pixel-level Understanding in Vision-Language-Action Model

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Trento and South China University of Technology developed PixelVLA, a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that enhances robotic manipulation through explicit pixel-level understanding and multimodal visual prompting. The model demonstrated a 28.7% increase in Visual Matching score over OpenVLA on zero-shot tasks and achieved an 86.7% average success rate on the LIBERO benchmark.

## Problem

- Existing VLA models primarily process visual information at an image-level resolution, limiting their spatial reasoning and out-of-distribution generalization in complex manipulation tasks.
- Prevailing VLA approaches rely heavily on textual instructions, which can miss subtle visual cues and restrict the flexibility of human-robot interaction, especially for precise spatial grounding.
- Current robotic datasets lack multimodal prompts and pixel-level annotations necessary for training models with fine-grained perception.

## Method

- **PixelVLA** introduces a novel architecture with a ==visual prompt-aware encoder== and a ==multiscale pixel-aware encoder==, integrated into a VLM backbone, along with a continuous action decoder.
- A two-stage automated annotation pipeline was developed to generate **Pixel-160K**, a dataset with pixel-level annotations and diverse visual prompts from existing robot data.
- A ==two-stage visuomotor instruction tuning== procedure first trains the model for continuous action capabilities and then fine-tunes for pixel-level understanding and multimodal prompt processing using the **Pixel-160K** dataset.

## Results

- PixelVLA achieved a **61.4%** Visual Matching (VM) score and **50.1%** Variant Aggregation (VA) score on SimplerEnv-Google Robot, surpassing OpenVLA by **28.7%** (VM) and **10.1%** (VA) in zero-shot tasks.
- The model attained a state-of-the-art average success rate of **86.7%** across all LIBERO task suites, outperforming strong baselines like OpenVLA (**76.5%**) and Dita (**82.4%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the pixel-level understanding enhancement stage contributed an **8.0%** gain in the average Variant Aggregation score over basic fine-tuning, validating the architectural components.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Injecting explicit pixel-level understanding into VLA models significantly improves spatial reasoning and out-of-distribution generalization for robotic manipulation.
> - Multimodal prompting, combining textual and diverse visual cues (points, lines, regions, masks), enhances human-robot interaction flexibility and precision.
> - A two-stage training approach, focusing first on continuous actions and then on pixel-level understanding and multimodal prompts, effectively leverages existing VLA knowledge for fine-grained capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a progression in robotic policy learning, moving beyond traditional methods that rely on task-specific datasets to leverage large-scale robot datasets and pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs). This paradigm shift, exemplified by models like RT-2 (Brohan et al. (2023)) and OpenVLA (Kim et al. (2024)), has led to improvements in generalization and instruction-following capabilities in visuomotor control. These VLA models integrate internet-scale VLMs with robotic control, enabling semantic reasoning and manipulation of novel objects and achieving significant out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization.

However, the paper identifies two primary limitations within the current VLA landscape. Firstly, most existing VLA models, inheriting their architectures from VLMs, primarily process visual information at an image-level resolution. This coarse-grained understanding limits their spatial reasoning abilities and, consequently, their OOD generalization performance in complex manipulation tasks. In contrast, pixel-level comprehension has been demonstrated to enhance object perception and spatial awareness in the VLM domain (Ren et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2024b)), suggesting a similar potential for VLAs.

Secondly, prevailing VLA approaches predominantly rely on textual instructions for prompting. This dependency can overlook subtle visual cues and restrict the flexibility of human-robot interaction, especially in scenarios where precise spatial grounding is required. While some efforts, such as TraceVLA (Zheng et al. (2024)) and LLaRA (Li et al. (2025b)), have explored visual prompting to improve spatial-temporal awareness or encode object locations, these methods still face challenges in achieving fine-grained pixel-level understanding and seamlessly integrating diverse multimodal prompts (e.g., points, lines, regions, masks).

This work addresses these limitations by introducing PixelVLA, aiming to bridge the gap between image-level VLA understanding and the fine-grained perception and multimodal interaction capabilities demonstrated in advanced VLMs. It seeks to equip VLA models with explicit pixel-level reasoning and support for various visual prompts, thereby enhancing spatial precision and expanding the scope of human-robot interaction in manipulation tasks. The paper positions PixelVLA as a novel visuomotor instruction tuning framework, inspired by successful visual instruction tuning in VLMs, to advance VLA capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a VLA model, named PixelVLA, that addresses the two key limitations identified in existing VLA models:
1. **Lack of pixel-level scene understanding:** Current VLA models struggle with fine-grained perception, which is crucial for precise robotic manipulation in cluttered and diverse environments. The motivation is to enable VLAs to process visual observations at a detailed pixel level, moving beyond image-level understanding to improve spatial reasoning and OOD generalization.
2. **Over-reliance on textual prompts:** Existing VLAs primarily depend on text-based instructions, which can be insufficient for conveying subtle spatial cues and limits the expressiveness of human-robot interaction. The motivation is to enhance VLA flexibility by supporting multimodal prompting, incorporating both textual and diverse visual inputs (e.g., points, lines, regions, masks) to facilitate more intuitive and precise control.

To achieve these objectives, the research also defines secondary motivations:
- **Developing a novel architecture:** The need to integrate specific components capable of processing diverse visual prompts and injecting pixel-level information into the VLA's internal representations.
- **Creating a suitable dataset:** Recognizing that current robotic datasets lack multimodal prompts and pixel-level annotations, there is a motivation to develop an automated pipeline for generating such a dataset, tailored to train models for pixel-level understanding and multimodal prompting from existing, often cluttered, robot data.
- **Proposing an effective training framework:** The motivation to design a visuomotor instruction tuning framework that can effectively leverage the new architecture and dataset to train VLA models, enabling them to acquire robust continuous action representations and enhance pixel-level understanding.
- **Demonstrating practical applicability:** To show that the proposed PixelVLA can be integrated into existing VLA frameworks, providing significant performance improvements in manipulation success rates and adaptability to new robot setups, without incurring a prohibitive pretraining cost.

In essence, the research aims to make robot control more accurate, efficient, and versatile by fundamentally improving how VLA models perceive environments and interpret instructions, moving towards a more human-like, intuitive interaction paradigm.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PixelVLA approach comprises three main components: a novel model architecture, a two-stage automated annotation pipeline for dataset generation, and a two-stage visuomotor instruction tuning procedure.

#### 3.1. PixelVLA Architecture

The PixelVLA architecture is built upon pre-trained VLMs and integrates three novel components designed for pixel-level understanding and multimodal prompting.
1. **Vision Encoder and MLP Projector:** Similar to OpenVLA (Kim et al. (2024)), PixelVLA uses pre-trained DinoV2 (Oquab et al. (2023)) and SigLIP (Zhai etal. (2023)) models as its vision encoder. A lightweight 2-layer MLP projector maps the extracted visual features into the input space of the Large Language Model (LLM).
2. **Visual Prompt-aware Encoder:** This component processes diverse visual prompts (points, lines, regions, masks). It employs a lightweight prompt encoder, conceptually similar to SAM (Kirillov et al. (2023)). User-provided prompts are converted into continuous positional embeddings based on normalized image coordinates. These are then combined with learned prompt-type embeddings to generate prompt features, which are further transformed by an MLP to produce prompt-aware embeddings ($E^0_s$). This process preserves the spatial positional information of the visual prompts.
3. **Multiscale Pixel-aware Encoder:** Designed to inject pixel-level understanding, this encoder utilizes multi-level visual features ($F^0_v$) from the SigLIP vision encoder and a pixel-aware mask input ($p^0$). It computes pixel-aware embeddings ($E^0_p$) by applying a pixel mask to the multi-level features, followed by linear projections and an MLP. The model learns to associate this pixel-level information with action generation, enhancing the VLA backbone.
4. **LLM Backbone:** A Llama 2-7B (Touvron et al. (2023)) model, serving as the LLM backbone from the Prismatic-7B VLM (Karamcheti et al. (2024)), processes the combined textual, visual, pixel-aware, and prompt-aware embeddings.
5. **Continuous Action Decoder:** Unlike VLAs that predict discrete action tokens, PixelVLA employs a continuous action decoder, inspired by $\pi^0$ (Black et al. (2024)). It directly predicts 7D continuous action representations. Hidden states from the LLM's last layer are sequentially processed by a linear projector, multiple ResNet blocks, and an MLP projector to output actions in chunks. This design aims to capture fine-grained action details and avoid discretization errors.

#### 3.2. Visuomotor Tuning Data Generation (Pixel-160K)

To address the absence of multimodal prompts and pixel-level annotations in existing robotic datasets, the authors propose a two-stage automated annotation pipeline to create Pixel-160K, a dataset comprising approximately 160K manipulation episodes with pixel-level annotations and visual prompts derived from the Fractal (Brohan et al. (2022)) and Bridge v2 (Walke et al. (2023)) datasets.

1. **Gripper-aware Region Proposal Stage:**
 - **Gripper-close State Extraction:** Identifies frames where the robot gripper is interacting with an object (gripper-close state). The first such frame in each episode is selected as a key observation.
 - **Discrete Video Composition:** These selected gripper-close observations from all episodes are collected to form a discrete video set.
 - **Gripper Detection:** SAM 2 (Ravi et al. (2024)) is applied to these frames to segment the gripper region, generating bounding boxes.
 - **Region Proposals Generation:** The detected gripper regions are cropped and enlarged slightly to serve as region proposals for target objects, effectively filtering background clutter.

2. **Multimodal Object Segmentation Stage:**
 - **Target Object Reasoning:** A Llama 2-7B LLM is used to parse the manipulation instruction and extract the textual description of the primary target object to be manipulated.
 - **Annotations Generation:** The extracted target object text, along with the generated region proposals, is fed into an open-vocabulary object detection and segmentation pipeline consisting of Grounding DINO (Liu et al. (2024a)) and SAM (Kirillov et al. (2023)). This pipeline detects, segments, and associates object instances with language expressions. The highest confidence mask within the region proposal is retained. From this object mask, diverse visual prompts (random points, lines, bounding boxes via contour detection) are derived.

This pipeline filters out approximately 19.2% of generated samples due to failed or incorrect mask generation, resulting in Pixel-160K containing 160K episodes and 6.5M image-text-action triplets with visual prompts and mask annotations.

#### 3.3. Visuomotor Instruction Tuning Procedure

PixelVLA is trained using a novel two-stage visuomotor instruction tuning framework:

1. **Continuous Action Training Stage:**
 - **Initialization:** The vision encoder, MLP projector, and LLM backbone of PixelVLA are initialized with pre-trained weights from existing VLAs (OpenVLA or $\pi^0$) that were trained on the OXE dataset (O’Neill et al. (2024)).
 - **Module Configuration:** The visual prompt-aware encoder and multiscale pixel-aware encoder are deactivated in this stage. Most modules, except the continuous action decoder, are frozen to preserve general manipulation knowledge.
 - **Training Objective:** The continuous action decoder is trained to directly predict continuous action representations using an L1 regression loss, aligning predicted actions with ground-truth actions. This stage trains PixelVLA on a mixture of the Fractal and Bridge v2 datasets.

2. **Pixel-level Understanding Enhancement Stage:**
 - **Fine-tuning:** This stage explicitly enhances pixel-level understanding by fine-tuning the LLM backbone of PixelVLA using LoRA adaptation (Hu et al. (2022)) on the newly created Pixel-160K dataset.
 - **Joint Training:** The visual prompt-aware encoder and the multiscale pixel-aware encoder are jointly trained in this stage. The continuous action decoder continues to be optimized with L1 regression.
 - **Input Formulation:** At each training step, input includes image observation, pixel-aware mask, ground-truth action, language instruction, and diverse visual prompts. The loss function combines the predictions from the LLM and the continuous action decoder.

This two-stage procedure aims to first equip the VLA with robust continuous action capabilities from large-scale data and then specifically enhance its pixel-level understanding and multimodal prompt processing using the rich annotations of Pixel-160K.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of PixelVLA was evaluated through extensive experiments across three standard VLA benchmarks, demonstrating its capabilities in zero-shot object manipulation and adaptation to new robot setups.

#### 4.1. Zero-Shot Object Manipulation Comparisons

Experiments on the SimplerEnv-Google Robot setup (Table 1) show that PixelVLA significantly improves performance in zero-shot manipulation tasks. PixelVLA achieved an average Visual Matching (VM) score of 61.4% and Variant Aggregation (VA) score of 50.1%, surpassing OpenVLA (baseline) by 28.7% (VM) and 10.1% (VA), and OpenVLA-SFT by 29.8% (VM) and 11.5% (VA). This indicates robust capabilities in both pixel-level understanding and adaptation to multimodal prompts for out-of-domain tasks. Furthermore, PixelVLA demonstrated superior robustness across various environmental variations (camera orientations, lighting, background, distractors, and table texture) compared to TraceVLA and OpenVLA (Figure 4), validating the visuomotor instruction tuning procedure for OOD generalization.

On the SimplerEnv-WidowX robot setup (Table 2), PixelVLA-π0, an adaptation of PixelVLA to the $\pi^0$ baseline, achieved an average grasp success rate of 55.1% and overall task success rate of 33.8%. This performance surpassed the baseline $\pi^0$ by 2.0% (grasp) and 6.7% (success), and exceeded RoboVLMs by 35.3% (grasp) and 18.2% (success). These results suggest that PixelVLA’s architectural innovations, particularly the multiscale pixel-aware encoder and visual prompt integration, enhance zero-shot perceptual and operational capabilities, enabling more effective adaptation to unseen objects.

#### 4.2. New Robot Setups Adaptation Comparisons

To evaluate adaptability, PixelVLA was fine-tuned on the LIBERO-Pixel dataset (derived from the LIBERO benchmark using the automated annotation pipeline). As summarized in Table 3, PixelVLA achieved a state-of-the-art average success rate of 86.7% across all LIBERO task suites, outperforming strong baselines like OpenVLA (76.5%), TraceVLA (78.1%), Dita (82.4%), and SpatialVLA (78.1%). Notably, PixelVLA demonstrated significant gains on LIBERO-Long tasks, suggesting the effectiveness of its continuous action decoder in capturing longer-range temporal dependencies and mitigating discretization errors over long-horizon manipulations. These results underscore the enhanced adaptability of PixelVLA to new robotic setups due to its pixel-level visual understanding and continuous action representation.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies conducted on the SimplerEnv-Google Robot setup (Table 4) quantified the contribution of PixelVLA’s individual components. Using OpenVLA as the baseline, the continuous action training stage (Baseline+FT+CAT) improved the average Variant Aggregation (VA) score by 3.8% compared to a basic fine-tuning (Baseline+FT), highlighting the benefits of the continuous action decoder. Further incorporating the pixel-level understanding enhancement stage (Baseline+FT+PUE) yielded a more substantial gain of 8.0%. PixelVLA, which combines both stages, ultimately outperformed Baseline+FT+CAT by 6.3%. While a minor drop was observed in the "Open/Close Drawer" task for PixelVLA compared to Baseline+FT+PUE, attributed to catastrophic forgetting during joint two-stage optimization, the overall progressive improvement validates the effectiveness of both pixel-level understanding and multimodal prompts in advancing generalization capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents PixelVLA, a novel Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model designed to enhance the capabilities of robotic systems by addressing key limitations in existing VLA architectures. The introduction of pixel-level understanding and multimodal prompting significantly advances the field in several ways:

Firstly, **enhanced spatial precision and robustness**: By integrating a multiscale pixel-aware encoder and processing visual information at a finer granularity, PixelVLA enables robots to achieve a more precise understanding of their environment. This is critical for tasks requiring fine motor control and interaction with small or complex objects. The improved performance in zero-shot object manipulation and robustness to environmental variations, as demonstrated on the SimplerEnv benchmarks, indicates a step towards more reliable and adaptable robot control in real-world, visually complex scenarios.

Secondly, **flexible human-robot interaction**: The lightweight visual prompt-aware encoder allows PixelVLA to interpret a wider range of instructions, including textual commands combined with visual cues like points, lines, regions, or masks. This multimodal prompting capability expands the avenues for human-robot communication, moving beyond text-only instructions to enable more intuitive and expressive ways for humans to guide robotic actions. This could lead to more versatile and user-friendly robotic systems.

Thirdly, **efficient and scalable training**: The proposed two-stage automated annotation pipeline, which creates the Pixel-160K dataset from existing robot data, addresses a significant challenge in developing high-quality, pixel-annotated datasets. This automated approach makes it feasible to scale data generation for visuomotor instruction tuning. Furthermore, PixelVLA achieves substantial performance improvements while requiring only 1.5% of the pretraining cost of models like OpenVLA, suggesting a more efficient pathway to enhancing VLA capabilities.

Finally, **broader applicability and integration**: The design of PixelVLA allows it to be integrated into existing VLA frameworks (e.g., OpenVLA, $\pi^0$), leveraging their foundational knowledge while adding advanced pixel-level and multimodal capabilities. This demonstrates its potential to serve as an enhancement module for current and future VLA models, making them more accurate, efficient, and versatile for a variety of robot control applications. The superior performance on the LIBERO benchmark, especially in long-horizon tasks, indicates its potential for complex, multi-step manipulation sequences.

While the current work primarily validates PixelVLA in simulated environments, its advancements lay a foundation for more sophisticated and adaptable robotic systems, paving the way for future developments in areas such as 3D perception, richer visual prompting techniques, and ultimately, more robust real-world robot deployments.
