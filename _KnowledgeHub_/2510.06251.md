# Physics Knowledge in Frontier Models: A Diagnostic Study of Failure Modes

> [!summary] Summary
> A diagnostic study analyzes the failure modes of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on physics-based reasoning benchmarks, disentangling whether performance comes from accurate perception or genuine physics reasoning. The research found a weak correlation between a model's mastery of diagnostic subtests and its overall benchmark accuracy, indicating reliance on superficial patterns rather than integrated physical understanding.

## Problem

- Existing benchmarks provide aggregate accuracy scores that offer limited insight into *why* Vision-Language Models succeed or fail on complex physical reasoning tasks.
- It is unclear whether VLM performance on physics benchmarks reflects a genuine understanding of physical dynamics (e.g., mass, friction) or if it relies on superficial pattern matching.
- Current evaluation methods may reward models for achieving correct answers through 'shortcut reasoning' rather than through robust, integrated perception and causal inference.

## Method

- A two-stage diagnostic evaluation framework was developed to assess ==VLM== performance on physics-based reasoning tasks, including Physion/Physion++ and CLEVRER.
- The framework first measures overall task accuracy on original benchmark questions to establish a baseline.
- In the second stage, models are tested on additional subtests specifically designed to isolate and probe perception (e.g., object detection, descriptive questions) and physics understanding (e.g., motion prediction, predictive questions).

## Results

- Frontier VLMs achieved modest overall performance on physics reasoning benchmarks, with Physion/Physion++ accuracy ranging from **36.1%** to **61.4%** and CLEVRER counterfactual questions between **19.0%** and **42.4%**.
- Models generally performed better on perception-based diagnostics (e.g., object detection **41.0%**-**79.6%**) than on physics understanding diagnostics (e.g., motion prediction **37.9%**-**67.9%**), highlighting specific areas of weakness.
- For correctly answered benchmark questions, a significant portion occurred even when models failed one or more diagnostic subtests (e.g., GPT-4o showing **36.4%** correct when 4/5 subtests passed), implying successful outcomes often lack comprehensive underlying knowledge.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A weak correlation exists between a model's mastery of diagnostic subtests (perception and physics reasoning) and its overall accuracy on complex physical reasoning benchmarks.
> - Models often provide correct answers to the main task without fully mastering the foundational perceptual or physical sub-skills, suggesting reliance on shortcut reasoning or superficial cues.
> - Improved performance on perception or predictive diagnostic tasks does not reliably lead to better counterfactual reasoning, indicating a gap in how models connect observed events to hypothetical outcomes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Artificial Intelligence has witnessed substantial advancements in Vision–Language Models (VLMs), enabling them to process and interpret multimodal data, leading to progress in tasks such as scene description and event prediction from visual input. Despite these achievements, a comprehensive understanding of *why* these models succeed or fail, particularly on complex reasoning tasks involving physical interactions, remains elusive.

Existing benchmarks typically provide aggregate accuracy scores, which indicate *what* models can answer correctly but offer limited insight into the underlying mechanisms or specific points of failure. This lack of granularity often obscures whether a model's performance reflects genuine understanding of physical dynamics (e.g., mass, friction, gravity, collisions, occlusion) or if it relies on superficial pattern matching.

Human cognition integrates perception and reasoning, where accurate perception is foundational for effective prediction and causal inference. This integration is crucial for intelligent systems operating in real-world environments, underpinning safe decision-making and planning. The current research landscape often struggles to replicate this integrated understanding in AI models, particularly in the domain of intuitive physics.

Previous critiques, cited by the authors (Lake et al., Marcus, Holzinger et al.), have suggested that many advanced AI models, including large language models and multimodal systems, may function as sophisticated pattern matchers rather than true causal reasoners, potentially forming shallow representations of data. This paper contributes to this ongoing discourse by empirically investigating whether VLM success on physics benchmarks is genuinely grounded in both accurate perception and physical reasoning, or if it is achieved through less robust, shortcut-based strategies. By focusing on diagnostic evaluations that decompose complex tasks, this work aims to refine the methodology for assessing AI capabilities, moving beyond simple accuracy metrics to expose the latent failure modes that current benchmarks may overlook. It seeks to bridge the gap between observed performance and the qualitative nature of a model's underlying knowledge.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a detailed failure-mode analysis of frontier Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on physics-based reasoning benchmarks. The authors aim to disentangle the sources of model success or failure, specifically determining whether performance stems from accurate perception, genuine physics reasoning, or other mechanisms.

The motivation for this study arises from several limitations identified in the current evaluation paradigms for advanced AI models:

- **Limited Insight from Aggregate Scores:** Traditional benchmarks typically yield a single accuracy score for complex reasoning tasks. While these scores indicate the level of correctness, they do not explain the underlying reasons for success or failure. For instance, an error might be due to a misidentification of an object (perception failure) or a misunderstanding of how objects interact physically (reasoning failure). Current aggregate metrics conflate these distinct error types.
- **Need for Deeper Understanding of Physical Dynamics:** For AI systems to operate reliably and safely in the physical world, they require an understanding of intuitive physics, including properties like mass, friction, gravity, collisions, and occlusion. Simply achieving correct answers without grounding in these physical principles could indicate a brittle system. The authors emphasize that human cognition tightly couples perception with reasoning, enabling robust prediction and causal inference—a characteristic often lacking in current AI models.
- **Identifying "Right Reasons" for Success:** The research is motivated by the hypothesis that models might achieve high benchmark scores for "the wrong reasons," meaning they could arrive at correct answers by exploiting superficial cues or patterns rather than forming coherent causal representations based on integrated perception and physical understanding. The example of a cone rolling towards a wall with a hole versus a solid wall illustrates this; a model must not only perceive the cone and wall but also reason about the wall's penetrability to predict the outcome.
- **Developing Diagnostic Evaluation Frameworks:** To address these limitations, the authors are motivated to introduce a diagnostic evaluation framework. This framework is designed to decompose benchmark questions into distinct, testable capabilities—specifically isolating perception (object, color, occlusion recognition) and physics understanding (motion prediction, spatial reasoning). This decomposition is intended to reveal whether models attend to the correct entities and dynamics necessary for their answers, thereby exposing hidden failure modes that aggregate metrics obscure.

In essence, the core motivation is to move beyond superficial evaluations to a more granular assessment that can diagnose the specific cognitive components (perception, physical reasoning) contributing to a VLM's performance, thereby fostering the development of more robust and genuinely intelligent AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a two-stage diagnostic evaluation framework to analyze the performance of frontier Vision-Language Models (VLMs) on physics-based reasoning tasks. The methodology systematically disentangles model success by assessing both overall task accuracy and proficiency in specific perception and physics sub-skills.

**Models Evaluated:**
The study evaluated six contemporary VLMs:
- GPT-4o
- GPT-O1
- VideoLLaVA-7B
- VideoLLaVA-NeXT-7B
- Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct
- InternVL-4B

**Benchmarks Utilized:**
Three established physics-based reasoning benchmarks were used:
- **Physion and Physion++:** These datasets primarily test intuitive physics through video-based prediction of object motion and interaction outcomes. Videos depict a target object interacting with other elements (e.g., walls, ramps) as it moves towards a goal (e.g., a mat).
- **CLEVRER (CoLlision Events for Video REpresentation and Reasoning):** This dataset features synthetic object-collision videos and includes four distinct categories of questions: descriptive, explanatory, predictive, and counterfactual. Each category probes different levels of understanding, from basic perception to causal reasoning.

**Diagnostic Framework:**
The diagnostic evaluation pipeline consists of two primary stages, as summarized in Figure 2 of the paper:

1. **Standard Benchmark Evaluation (Evaluation Stage):**
 - Models are presented with the original, high-level task questions from the chosen benchmarks.
 - The objective is to measure the model's overall task accuracy and establish a performance baseline for subsequent diagnostic analysis.
 - For Physion/Physion++, the benchmark task asks whether the target object reaches the goal at the end of a video. Two evaluation modes are considered: "with property" (WP), where videos are truncated requiring outcome prediction, and "fully observed" (WOP), where full trajectories are shown for verification.
 - For CLEVRER, counterfactual questions serve as the primary evaluation metric, as they necessitate reasoning about full scene dynamics and hypothetical outcomes.

2. **Diagnostic Analysis (Perception and Physics Reasoning Stages):**
 - Following the initial evaluation, models are tested on additional subtests specifically designed to probe their perception and physics understanding. These subtests are constructed to isolate key visual elements and physical interactions required for correct reasoning in the evaluation stage.

**Specific Diagnostic Structures for Each Benchmark:**

- **Physion and Physion++ Setup:**
 - **Perception Diagnostics:** Three distinct subtests were introduced to assess whether the model correctly detects:
 - The **target object** (the main moving object).
 - The **goal** (the destination object or area).
 - The **latent object** (e.g., occluders, holes, or other elements that significantly affect the target object's motion or interaction).
 - These subtests aim to verify if the model grounds its understanding in the fundamental visual elements of the scene.
 - **Physics Reasoning Diagnostics:** Two further subtests were developed to probe the model's understanding of physical principles:
 - **Motion prediction:** Tests the model's ability to anticipate the future trajectories of objects based on observed initial states and interactions.
 - **Spatial relationships:** Assesses the model's comprehension of causal and spatial interactions between objects.

- **CLEVRER Setup:**
 - Leveraging CLEVRER's inherent question categorization, the diagnostic framework mapped existing question types to perception and physics reasoning:
 - **Perception Diagnostics:**
 - **Descriptive questions:** Probe basic object recognition and event description.
 - **Explanatory questions:** Assess causal attribution of observed events.
 - **Physics Reasoning Diagnostics:**
 - **Predictive questions:** Test the model’s ability to anticipate future physical events based on the observed video.

**Implementation Details:**
All experiments were conducted using a custom pipeline built upon the EUREKA framework, which supports modular dataset integration. Models were queried independently for each diagnostic question, utilizing ground-truth binary or multiple-choice answers for evaluation. The study analyzed 2,110 Physion/Physion++ videos with full diagnostic coverage. For CLEVRER, validation videos with one question per category were used to ensure consistency across model evaluations. This meticulous approach allowed for a systematic comparison of model performance across both high-level tasks and granular underlying capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's findings reveal insights into the capabilities and limitations of frontier Vision-Language Models (VLMs) concerning physical reasoning, particularly highlighting a disconnect between benchmark accuracy and underlying competence.

**Part I: Evaluation and Diagnostic Subtests**

- **Overall Evaluation Accuracy:**
 - Frontier VLMs generally exhibited modest performance on the core physical reasoning benchmarks. For Physion/Physion++, aggregate accuracy under both "with property" (WP) and "fully observed" (WOP) conditions remained in the range of 36.1% to 61.4%, with some models averaging above the 50% chance baseline.
 - On CLEVRER counterfactual questions, evaluation accuracy was lower, ranging from 19.0% to 42.4%, indicating significant challenges in reasoning about hypothetical outcomes.

- **Diagnostic Subtest Performance:**
 - **Perception Diagnostics:** Across both Physion/Physion++ and CLEVRER, models generally demonstrated stronger performance on perception-based subtests compared to physics-based subtests. For Physion/Physion++, accuracy for detecting target, goal, and latent objects ranged from 41.0% to 79.6%. For CLEVRER, descriptive and explanatory question accuracy ranged from 25.0% to 79.4%. While perception was generally stronger, certain models showed variability in specific contexts.
 - **Physics Reasoning Diagnostics:** Performance on physics understanding subtests (motion prediction, spatial relationships for Physion/Physion++; predictive for CLEVRER) revealed clear gaps in dynamic understanding. Physion/Physion++ physics accuracy ranged from 37.9% to 67.9%. CLEVRER predictive accuracy ranged from 20.0% to 69.0%, often lower than perception scores.

**Part II: Failure-Mode Analysis – Weak Correlation Between Subtest Mastery and Benchmark Accuracy**

A central and counterintuitive finding of the study is the weak correlation observed between a model's mastery of diagnostic subtests and its overall benchmark accuracy.

- **Physion/Physion++ Analysis (Tables 1-2):**
 - **Block Pass vs. Evaluation Accuracy:** The analysis of Physion/Physion++ (WP mode) showed that evaluation accuracy when models passed *all* diagnostic subtests in a block (P(correct|passed)) was only marginally higher, or even negatively correlated, with accuracy when models failed at least one subtest (P(correct|failed)). For instance, for GPT-4o, passing all perception subtests only improved accuracy by 1.5% points. For Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and InternVL-4B, passing all perception subtests led to a *decrease* in accuracy compared to failing some.
 - **Diagnostic Subtest Breakdown for Correct Evaluations:** Crucially, Table 2 illustrates that for videos where models provided a correct evaluation, a significant portion of these correct answers did *not* occur when all 5 diagnostic subtests were passed. Instead, the highest proportion of correct answers often corresponded to scenarios where only 3 or 4 subtests were passed (e.g., GPT-4o: 36.4% for k=4, 24.2% for k=3; GPT-O1: 38.0% for k=4, 29.0% for k=3). This suggests that models frequently succeed on the main task without full perceptual or physical grounding, indicating a reliance on shortcut reasoning rather than a comprehensive understanding of the scene's dynamics.

- **CLEVRER Analysis (Tables 3-5):**
 - **Conditioning on Counterfactual Performance:** The study examined the accuracy of descriptive, explanatory, and predictive questions conditioned on different levels of counterfactual success (CF = 100%, 50%, 0%). The trends were largely non-monotonic across models.
 - **Lack of Reliable Implication:** Stronger performance on perception (descriptive/explanatory) or predictive tasks did not reliably translate to improved counterfactual success. In many instances, models maintained stable or even showed improved accuracy on diagnostic questions when their counterfactual overlap decreased (i.e., when they performed worse on counterfactuals). This indicates that the ability to perceive or causally explain observed events does not necessarily equip models to reason about unseen alternative outcomes, despite the cognitive dependency of counterfactual reasoning on accurately understanding the initial scene.

In summary, the findings indicate that while VLMs can achieve certain levels of accuracy on physical reasoning benchmarks, this performance often does not align with a robust, integrated understanding of both perception and physics. Models frequently provide correct answers without fully mastering the foundational sub-skills, pointing towards a reliance on superficial patterns or incomplete reasoning pathways.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research have several significant implications for the understanding, evaluation, and future development of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and AI in general.

Firstly, the study **reinforces long-standing critiques** that current multimodal models, despite their advanced capabilities, often function as "sophisticated pattern matchers" rather than "causal reasoners." By demonstrating that models can achieve correct benchmark answers without fully grounding their responses in both accurate perception and physics understanding, the paper provides empirical evidence that challenges the notion of genuine physical comprehension in these systems. This suggests a persistent gap where models recognize surface features or statistical correlations rather than forming coherent, integrated causal representations of physical phenomena.

Secondly, the research **exposes a fundamental limitation in current benchmark design and interpretation**. The weak correlation between diagnostic subtest mastery and aggregate benchmark accuracy indicates that conventional, single-score evaluations are insufficient for truly assessing a model's competence. Such benchmarks can inadvertently reward models that exploit superficial cues or statistical regularities in the data, rather than those that build robust internal representations of physical laws and object interactions. This "scoring for the wrong reasons" can lead to an overestimation of a model's capabilities and mask critical underlying deficiencies.

Thirdly, this work highlights the **critical need for more granular and structured benchmarks**. The diagnostic framework introduced—which systematically decomposes complex tasks into interconnected sub-tasks covering perception, causal inference, and counterfactual reasoning—serves as a model for future evaluation methodologies. By separating these distinct capabilities, evaluators can gain a clearer understanding of where models truly succeed, where they consistently fail, and which specific forms of reasoning remain unsolved. This approach does not replace existing benchmarks but complements them, offering a more detailed "lens" through which to analyze model performance.

The potential impact extends to the **future development of robust and reliable AI systems**. For AI to operate safely and effectively in real-world physical environments, whether in robotics, autonomous driving, or assistive technologies, a genuine understanding of intuitive physics is paramount. Without this, systems may exhibit brittle behavior, failing unpredictably in novel situations or when superficial cues are absent. By identifying the specific failure modes (e.g., misperceiving a latent object or failing motion prediction despite object recognition), researchers can more precisely target interventions and design architectures that explicitly integrate perceptual and physical reasoning.

In conclusion, this research significantly contributes to the field by advocating for a paradigm shift in AI evaluation. Its findings underscore that achieving high scores on complex tasks does not automatically equate to deep understanding. The diagnostic framework and the observed failure modes provide a roadmap for developing AI models that not only perform well but also reason correctly, possessing a more human-like, integrated understanding of the physical world. This is crucial for advancing AI towards genuinely intelligent systems capable of safe decision-making and planning in diverse, dynamic environments.
