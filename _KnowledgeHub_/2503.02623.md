# Rewarding Doubt: A Reinforcement Learning Approach to Calibrated Confidence Expression of Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The “Rewarding Doubt” framework leverages a Reinforcement Learning approach to enable large language models (LLMs) to express factually calibrated confidence alongside their generated answers. By utilizing a theoretically grounded logarithmic scoring rule as its reward function, the method significantly improves confidence calibration and generalization across various factual question-answering tasks without compromising the LLM's answer accuracy.

## Problem

- Large Language Models frequently generate inaccurate or fabricated information (hallucinations) and often express uncalibrated, overconfident predictions.
- Existing methods for confidence estimation often decouple uncertainty estimation from the core text generation, or rely on external mechanisms, failing to instill inherent uncertainty awareness within the LLM.
- The lack of reliable and calibrated confidence expression hinders the safe and trustworthy deployment of LLMs in high-stakes real-world applications.

## Method

- A Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework, named “**Rewarding Doubt**,” directly fine-tunes LLMs to express ==calibrated confidence==, formalizing confidence estimation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
- The core of the approach is a reward function based on the logarithmic scoring rule, a strictly proper scoring rule, which incentivizes the LLM to align its predicted confidence with the true epistemic probability of its answer being correct.
- Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for training, with a two-step response generation process (answer first, then confidence) to ensure calibration training targets confidence estimation without altering the model's inherent answering ability.

## Results

- The method substantially reduced Expected Calibration Error (ECE) (e.g., to **0.0226** from **0.3459** in zero-shot) and increased AUROC (e.g., to **0.8592** from **0.5858** in zero-shot) on factual Q&A tasks like TriviaQA and QAMPARI, outperforming various baselines.
- Fine-tuned models exhibited a more nuanced confidence distribution, shifting away from the overconfident high scores typically seen in zero-shot LLMs towards a wider, more accurate range of expressed uncertainty.
- Learned confidence calibration generalized effectively to out-of-domain datasets (MedQA, CommonsenseQA) and maintained stable answer correctness across all experiments, demonstrating the method's robustness without degrading core task performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing an LLM's policy with a strictly proper scoring rule (logarithmic scoring rule) provably encourages the model to generate perfectly calibrated confidence expressions.
> - Integrating confidence learning directly into the LLM's generative process via RL allows the model to develop an intrinsic “uncertainty-awareness” that is seamlessly tied to its output, unlike post-hoc or external methods.
> - The fine-tuning approach is model-agnostic and efficient, requiring only minimal additional tokens for confidence expression during inference, and can generalize learned confidence awareness to unseen tasks and out-of-domain datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The reliable and safe deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) in real-world applications necessitates not only the generation of accurate information but also the accurate communication of their confidence in that information. LLMs frequently exhibit a tendency to generate inaccurate or fabricated information, referred to as hallucinations, which raises concerns about their trustworthiness. This issue is particularly critical in high-stakes environments such as medical diagnosis, legal consultation, and customer service, where overconfident erroneous predictions can lead to serious consequences. The ability of an LLM to express calibrated confidence—meaning its expressed numerical confidence aligns with the actual probability of its answer being correct—would enable users to discern when to trust, verify, or defer to human expertise.

Prior research in confidence estimation for LLMs broadly falls into several categories, each with limitations that this work aims to address. Many methods infer confidence from the LLM's internal states in a zero-shot manner (e.g., logit-based techniques, consistency-based approaches). While these can be valuable for their simplicity, they do not intrinsically train the model to be aware of and express confidence, and often exhibit suboptimal calibration performance. Other approaches involve training, but frequently decouple the uncertainty estimation from the core text generation process, for instance, by training external probes or classifiers. While these methods may achieve good calibration, they do not integrate confidence expression seamlessly into the LLM's generative mechanism, thus not fostering an inherent "uncertainty-awareness" within the model itself. Furthermore, fine-tuning methods that construct datasets by appending estimated uncertainty as a supervised signal are often limited by the quality of these constructed ground-truth estimates and can lose theoretical guarantees for calibration during supervised training.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has been explored for aligning LLMs with human preferences (RLHF) and for agentic interactions. More recently, some RL-based approaches have been applied to confidence estimation, but they typically either require human-annotated preference data, exhibit biases towards high verbalized confidence, focus on refusal without quantifying confidence, or align confidence with a listener's interpretation rather than factual calibration.

This research distinguishes itself by proposing a novel RL approach that directly fine-tunes LLMs to express factually calibrated confidence alongside their answers. Crucially, it integrates this confidence calibration into the LLM's generative process and leverages a theoretically grounded, strictly proper scoring rule (the logarithmic scoring rule) as its reward signal. This design aims to overcome the limitations of prior methods by providing a mechanism for LLMs to inherently learn and express their true epistemic probability of correctness, thereby enhancing both calibration and trustworthiness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enhance the safety and trustworthiness of Large Language Models by enabling them to express accurate and calibrated confidence estimates alongside their generated answers to factual questions. This involves addressing the prevalent issues of LLM hallucinations and overconfidence, which hinder their reliable deployment in critical applications.

The key specific objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

1. **Directly Fine-tuning LLMs for Calibrated Confidence**: A primary objective is to develop and implement a Reinforcement Learning framework that allows for the direct fine-tuning of LLMs. Unlike many existing methods that infer confidence post-hoc or rely on external mechanisms, this approach aims to instill an intrinsic ability within the LLM to quantify and articulate its uncertainty. The motivation stems from the observation that current LLMs often lack an inherent "awareness" of their own uncertainty, leading to uncalibrated confidence expressions.

2. **Seamless Integration of Confidence Calibration**: The research seeks to integrate confidence calibration directly into the generative process of the LLM. This contrasts with approaches that decouple confidence estimation from response generation, where a separate module or process is responsible for predicting uncertainty. The motivation here is to enable the LLM to dynamically factor its uncertainty into its output generation, making confidence expression an organic part of its response rather than an external annotation. This is crucial for developing models that are inherently "uncertainty-aware."

3. **Optimization with a Theoretically Grounded Reward Function**: A central objective is to design and optimize a reward function that explicitly incentivizes calibrated confidence expressions. The authors propose using the logarithmic scoring rule, which is a strictly proper scoring rule. The motivation for this choice is its theoretical property: optimizing this function provably encourages the model to align its predicted confidence with the true epistemic probability of correctness. This means the model is penalized for both overconfidence (high confidence in incorrect answers) and underconfidence (low confidence in correct answers), thereby promoting accurate and granular uncertainty estimation.

4. **Demonstrating Substantially Improved Calibration**: The research aims to empirically demonstrate that models fine-tuned with the proposed RL approach exhibit significantly improved calibration performance compared to baseline methods. This includes quantitative evaluation using metrics like Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), as well as qualitative visualization through calibration curves.

5. **Assessing Generalization Capabilities**: An important objective is to evaluate whether the learned confidence awareness generalizes to unseen tasks and out-of-domain datasets without further fine-tuning. This would suggest the emergence of a more general capability within the LLM, rather than just task-specific adaptation. The motivation is to prove the robustness and practical utility of the method in diverse real-world scenarios.

6. **Ensuring Stability of Task Performance**: The methodology aims to ensure that the process of training for confidence calibration does not negatively impact the LLM's primary task performance (i.e., the accuracy of its answers). The motivation is to provide a method that enhances trustworthiness without compromising the model's core utility.

In summary, the motivation for "Rewarding Doubt" arises from the critical need for trustworthy and safe AI systems, particularly LLMs, that can transparently communicate their uncertainty. By directly training LLMs with a theoretically sound reward for calibrated confidence, the authors seek to mitigate overconfidence, improve human-AI collaboration, and enable more informed decision-making in sensitive applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, termed "Rewarding Doubt," employs a novel Reinforcement Learning (RL) approach to enable Large Language Models to express calibrated confidence alongside their answers. The core idea is to train the LLM as an agent in a simulated environment that presents factual questions, rewarding it for aligning its expressed confidence with the correctness of its answers.

#### 3.1. Markov Decision Process (MDP) Formulation

The confidence estimation task is formalized as an MDP, defined by the tuple (S, A, T, R):
- **State space (S):** A state `s_t` comprises the natural language question `q`, the model's generated answer `a`, and any partial sequence of confidence tokens generated so far (`c_1:t-1`).
- **Action space (A):** The action `c_t` involves selecting the next token for the confidence score from the LLM's vocabulary. This includes numerical tokens (e.g., to form percentages or probability values) and an end-of-sequence token that signals the completion of the confidence prediction.
- **Transition function (T(s_t+1 | s_t, a_t)):** The environment transitions deterministically. Given a state `s_t = (q, a, c_1:t-1)` and an action `c_t`, the next state is `s_t+1 = (q, a, c_1:t)`. The episode terminates once the end-of-sequence token for confidence is generated.
- **Reward function (R):** The reward `R(a, c, j)` is calculated based on the final confidence score `c` (denoted as `p_hat`, a numerical value between 0 and 1) and the correctness of the answer `j(a)`.

#### 3.2. Reward Function Design

The central element of the methodology is its reward function, which is based on the **logarithmic scoring rule**, a strictly proper scoring rule. This rule is designed to ensure that the expected reward is maximized precisely when the model's predicted confidence (`p_hat`) aligns with the true epistemic probability (`p_star`) of the answer being correct.

The reward function is defined as:
- `R(a, p_hat, j) = log(p_hat)` if `j(a) = 1` (answer is correct)
- `R(a, p_hat, j) = log(1 - p_hat)` if `j(a) = 0` (answer is incorrect)

This function incentivizes the LLM to express high confidence when its answer is correct and low confidence when it is incorrect. Conversely, it heavily penalizes high confidence in incorrect answers (overconfidence) and low confidence in correct answers (underconfidence). The authors provide a proposition demonstrating that under this reward design, the optimal policy leads to perfectly calibrated confidence expressions.

For numerical stability, especially to avoid `log(0)`, a small positive constant `epsilon` (set to 0.001) is used to clip `p_hat` and `1 - p_hat`. Specifically, the function becomes `log(max(p_hat, epsilon))` for correct answers and `log(min(1 - p_hat, 1 - epsilon))` for incorrect answers. The reward is then normalized to the range of `[-1, 1]` for practical training purposes.

#### 3.3. Training Process

- **Optimization Algorithm:** The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is utilized to optimize the LLM's policy based on the designed reward function.
- **Base Models:** The primary base model used is Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct. An ablation study also explores Qwen-2.5 (3B and 7B) and Gemma-2 (9B) models to assess architectural robustness.
- **Fine-tuning:** Low-rank Adaptation (LoRA) is employed for efficient fine-tuning.
- **Two-Step Response Generation:** To ensure that the calibration training solely targets confidence estimation without altering the model's inherent answering ability, the generation process is divided into two steps during training:
 1. **Answer Generation:** The LLM first generates an answer to the question.
 2. **Confidence Generation:** Subsequently, this generated answer is treated as a fixed input alongside the question, and the LLM then generates its confidence score. The reward and policy updates are applied based only on this confidence generation step.
- **Confidence Expression Format:** The model is prompted to provide confidence as an integer between 0 and 10, which is then normalized to `` for reward calculation. A confidence of 0 signifies certainty of incorrectness, while 10 signifies certainty of correctness.
- **Correctness Assessment:**
 - For multiple-choice datasets (MedQA, CommonsenseQA), correctness is determined via exact string matching.
 - For open-ended datasets (TriviaQA, QAMPARI), the F1 score of word overlap between the model's response and ground truth candidates is used, with a threshold of 0.5 defining correctness.
- **Penalties:** If the model fails to generate an answer in the specified format, it receives an "out-of-format" penalty of -3.

#### 3.4. Datasets

- **Single-Answer Setting:**
 - Training: TriviaQA (factual question-answering).
 - Generalization Evaluation: CommonsenseQA (commonsense reasoning) and MedQA (medical domain, multiple-choice).
- **Multiple-Answer Setting:**
 - Training and Evaluation: QAMPARI (questions with multiple possible answers).

#### 3.5. Evaluation Metrics

The effectiveness of the method is evaluated using:
- **Expected Calibration Error (ECE):** Measures the absolute difference between predicted confidence and true accuracy across confidence bins (lower is better).
- **Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC):** Measures the model's ability to discriminate between correct and incorrect answers across all confidence thresholds (higher is better).
- **Accuracy:** Used to monitor the stability of the model's task performance.
- **Calibration curves and confidence distribution histograms:** Provide visual insights into calibration and confidence expression patterns.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across single-answer and multiple-answer tasks, as well as generalization settings, demonstrate the effectiveness of the Rewarding Doubt approach in improving LLM confidence calibration.

#### 4.1. Improved Calibration Performance

- **Single-Answer Setting (TriviaQA):** Rewarding Doubt achieved an Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.0226 and an AUROC of 0.8592. This represents a substantial improvement over the zero-shot Verbalize baseline (ECE 0.3459, AUROC 0.5858) and outperformed other zero-shot methods (Chain-of-Thought, Top-K, Surrogate Token, Sequence Probability, Self-Consistency) and the DPO-based LACIE. While the Trained Probe baseline showed a slightly lower ECE (0.0189), Rewarding Doubt demonstrated a notably higher AUROC, indicating superior discriminative ability between correct and incorrect answers.
- **Multiple-Answer Setting (QAMPARI):** In this more complex setting, Rewarding Doubt also outperformed baselines, achieving an ECE of 0.0816 and an AUROC of 0.6947, significantly better than Verbalize (ECE 0.5319, AUROC 0.6047), Sequence Probability, and Trained Probe.

#### 4.2. Shift in Confidence Distribution

Analysis of confidence distributions revealed that zero-shot LLMs (Verbalize) tend to assign predominantly high confidence scores (8 or above), indicating a tendency towards overconfidence. After fine-tuning with Rewarding Doubt, the models' confidence scores spanned a wider range, including lower values. This shift suggests that the fine-tuning successfully mitigates overconfidence and encourages a more nuanced and accurate expression of uncertainty, aligning confidence with actual performance. Calibration curves further illustrated this, showing fine-tuned models' confidence aligning much more closely with the ideal 45° line than zero-shot baselines.

#### 4.3. Generalization Capabilities

- **Out-of-Domain Generalization:** The model trained on TriviaQA demonstrated promising generalization to unseen domains:
 - **MedQA (Medical Domain):** Rewarding Doubt significantly outperformed Verbalize in both ECE and AUROC.
 - **CommonsenseQA (Commonsense Reasoning):** It achieved a comparable ECE to Verbalize but a much higher AUROC. The authors highlight AUROC as a crucial metric for discriminative ability, suggesting Rewarding Doubt produces more actionable confidence estimates. Critically, Rewarding Doubt consistently outperformed the Trained Probe baseline in these generalization tasks.
- **Cross-Setting Generalization:** A model trained in a Single-Answer setting, when applied to a Multiple-Answer task (QAMPARI), still considerably outperformed the base model, despite not being specifically trained for the multi-answer format. This suggests the transferability of the learned confidence estimation patterns.

#### 4.4. Stability of Answer Correctness

A key finding was that the fine-tuning process, designed with a two-step generation mechanism (answer first, then confidence), maintained stable task performance. The accuracy of the LLM's answers remained consistent between the base model (Verbalize) and the model fine-tuned with Rewarding Doubt across all experiments, indicating that confidence calibration training did not adversely affect the model's ability to generate correct responses.

#### 4.5. Model Agnosticism

An ablation study across various LLM architectures and sizes (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-3B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Gemma-2-9B) consistently showed that Rewarding Doubt reduced calibration error and improved AUROC without degrading downstream accuracy. This suggests the method's robustness and applicability across different LLM backbones.

#### 4.6. Efficiency

Compared to computationally intensive zero-shot methods like Chain-of-Thought or Self-Consistency, which require lengthy reasoning chains or multiple generations during inference, Rewarding Doubt introduces minimal overhead. Inference only requires generating a small, constant number of tokens for confidence expression, and it does not rely on an additional model, making it efficient for real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Rewarding Doubt" approach introduces a significant advancement in enhancing the reliability and trustworthiness of Large Language Models, with several potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Trustworthiness and Reliability**: By enabling LLMs to express demonstrably calibrated confidence in their factual answers, the method directly addresses a core limitation of current models – their propensity for uncalibrated overconfidence and hallucination. This improved transparency can foster greater user trust in AI-generated information, which is critical for their wider adoption.

2. **Improved Human-AI Collaboration in High-Stakes Environments**: The ability of LLMs to accurately communicate uncertainty is particularly impactful in domains like medicine, law, and customer service. In these contexts, LLMs can serve as support tools, and their capacity to flag uncertain outputs allows human experts to critically review or defer decisions, thereby preventing misinformed choices or adverse outcomes based on overconfident AI responses. This capability facilitates safer and more effective human-AI collaborative workflows.

3. **Theoretically Grounded Calibration**: The use of a strictly proper scoring rule (the logarithmic scoring rule) as the reward signal provides a strong theoretical foundation for the method. This grounding offers a robust and principled mechanism for incentivizing true calibration, differentiating it from empirical or heuristic approaches that might lack such guarantees.

4. **Emergence of General Uncertainty Awareness**: The observed generalization of calibrated confidence to unseen tasks and out-of-domain datasets suggests that the fine-tuning process may instill a more fundamental "confidence awareness" within the LLM. This implies that the model is not merely memorizing task-specific confidence patterns but is learning a more generalizable meta-skill of assessing its own knowledge and uncertainty.

5. **Practical Efficiency for Deployment**: The method's design, which integrates confidence expression seamlessly into the LLM's generative process with minimal inference overhead, makes it highly practical for real-world deployment. Unlike some multi-pass or reasoning-intensive methods, "Rewarding Doubt" provides actionable confidence estimates directly and efficiently, without requiring additional external models during inference.

6. **Foundation for Future Research**: The framework is extensible, opening avenues for future research to explore more complex correctness signals beyond binary factual assessment. For instance, integrating LLM-as-a-judge systems, human preference-based reward models, or continuous NLG metrics could broaden its applicability to subjective or open-ended generation tasks, such as fact verification in free-text generation.

7. **Mitigation of Societal Risks**: By directly confronting and mitigating the issue of LLM overconfidence, the research contributes to reducing potential societal risks associated with unchecked AI deployment. However, the authors also acknowledge a limitation: the expression of numerical confidence, even if statistically well-calibrated, might lead to undue user trust in individual cases. This highlights the need for careful deployment strategies, appropriate user interfaces that contextualize model confidence, and safeguards against over-reliance on AI systems.

In conclusion, "Rewarding Doubt" presents a robust and effective methodology for integrating calibrated confidence expression into LLMs, marking a notable step towards building more trustworthy, reliable, and accountable AI systems capable of transparently communicating their own limitations.
