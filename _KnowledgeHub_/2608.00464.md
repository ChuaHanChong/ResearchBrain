# Learning to Predict Contact Force Distributions from Vision Leveraging Object Geometry Priors

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a framework that predicts 3D contact force distributions in multi-object scenes from a single RGB image. The method incorporates geometry-aware statistical smoothing of simulated forces, enabling a model trained exclusively on synthetic data to generalize effectively to real-world environments, leading to reduced disturbance during robotic manipulation.

## Problem

- Robots lack the ability to predict physical interactions and contact force distributions from visual input in cluttered multi-object scenes, especially for objects not directly manipulated.
- Rigid-body simulators generate sparse point forces that do not accurately represent continuous real-world surface or line contacts, creating a sim-to-real gap for force prediction models.
- A scarcity of large, real-world datasets exists for training vision-based force prediction models in complex, cluttered environments.

## Method

- An end-to-end learning framework is developed to predict 3D force distributions from a single RGB image, trained entirely on simulation data enhanced with ==visual domain randomization==.
- A **Geometry-Aware Force Smoothing (GAFS)** technique is introduced to convert sparse simulated point forces into smooth 3D force distributions by leveraging object geometry priors, making them more physically plausible.
- A ==ResNet-based encoder-decoder== model is trained to predict the force distribution, represented as a log-scaled and normalized 3D voxel map, using ==Mean Squared Error== as the loss function.

## Results

- GAFS consistently yielded lower prediction errors compared to isotropic smoothing, achieving an L(d=0.5cm) error of **0.04240**, demonstrating better alignment with actual contact surfaces.
- Force-aware lifting strategies using GAFS reduced the maximum velocity of surrounding objects by **23-24%** and maximum contact force by **9-14%** in simulation compared to a simple upward lifting approach.
- The model, trained exclusively in simulation, successfully transferred to real-world scenes, with GAFS generally achieving the smallest disturbances to surrounding objects during lifting tasks, outperforming baseline methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Smoothing simulated point forces into continuous distributions significantly improves the predictability and consistency of vision-based force estimation.
> - Integrating object geometry priors into the force smoothing process is critical for generating more physically intuitive and accurate force distributions that account for various contact types (e.g., surface, line, point).
> - Even coarse, vision-based predictions of force distributions, when smoothed appropriately, provide valuable information for robotic manipulation, allowing for planning actions that minimize disturbance in cluttered scenes and facilitating sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in robotic manipulation: endowing robots with the ability to make physical predictions about object interactions from visual input, similar to human cognitive abilities. Existing research in vision-based force prediction primarily focuses on human-object interaction, interaction forces involving robot grippers, or general extrinsic contact. However, there has been a gap in studies estimating contact forces in static, multi-object cluttered scenes, particularly for objects not directly manipulated by the robot. This paper aims to fill this gap by proposing a framework for predicting force distributions in such complex environments.

Current approaches to learning force prediction models are largely divided into simulation-based and real-data-based methods. While real-data collection is labor-intensive, simulation offers access to data that is difficult to measure in reality. This study adopts a simulation-based approach, acknowledging the inherent uncertainty and variability in real-world scenarios, and focuses on learning rough, yet useful, force patterns that can generalize to unseen objects and real-world conditions. A specific challenge in simulation-based approaches, particularly with rigid-body simulators, is that they typically output sparse point forces, which often do not accurately represent real-world surface or line contacts. Previous work has explored statistical smoothing to mitigate this disparity, but without fully accounting for varying contact patterns. This paper extends prior work by integrating object geometry into the smoothing process, aiming for more consistent and physically intuitive force predictions. It builds on the concept of transferring models trained on simplified simulation conditions to more complex real-world scenarios, similar to approaches in quadrupedal locomotion. The work also relates to visual physical reasoning and scene understanding, particularly stability prediction, but distinguishes itself by directly predicting interaction forces.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to make coarse yet useful physical predictions about contact force distributions in multi-object piled scenes, solely based on visual information. This capability is motivated by the observation that humans can anticipate physical interactions in unfamiliar situations and adapt their manipulation strategies accordingly (e.g., choosing a horizontal extraction for an object at the bottom of a pile).

The work aims to address several specific challenges:
- **Ill-posed Nature of Vision-based Force Prediction:** Directly estimating exact contact forces from vision is inherently difficult and prone to inaccuracy, especially for objects with identical appearances but different physical properties or hidden contents. The paper posits that approximate, consistent predictions are still valuable for constraining environmental states and improving downstream tasks.
- **Lack of Training Data:** There is a scarcity of large datasets pairing visual information with ground-truth contact forces in cluttered multi-object environments. Physics simulators offer a practical solution by providing access to inter-object forces.
- **Mismatch between Simulated and Real-World Contact Forces:** Rigid-body simulators, while stable, output sparse point forces that are highly sensitive to fine contact states and mesh structures. In reality, contacts often occur along surfaces or lines. This discrepancy makes direct prediction challenging and prone to overfitting, hindering generalization.
- **Limitations of Previous Smoothing Methods:** Prior attempts at statistical smoothing of simulated point forces did not account for different contact patterns (e.g., surface vs. point contact) and could spread forces isotropically, leading to undesired expansion beyond actual contact areas.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes and validates the hypothesis that predicting smooth force distributions, rather than raw point forces, can improve prediction accuracy and downstream task performance. Furthermore, it hypothesizes that incorporating object geometry into the smoothing process will account for variations in contact states, resulting in more consistent and intuitive vision-based predictions that better align with real-world physical contacts. The ultimate goal is to enable effective sim-to-real transfer for robotic manipulation tasks like object picking in cluttered scenes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves an end-to-end learning framework to predict a 3D force distribution from a single RGB image, trained entirely on synthetic data.

**Overall Framework (Figure 1):**
1. **Data Generation in Simulation:** Paired data of RGB images and corresponding contact forces are generated using a rigid-body simulator (Isaac Sim). To bridge the sim-to-real gap, domain randomization is applied during image generation, perturbing camera parameters, illumination, and object colors.
2. **Statistical Smoothing of Force Labels:** The raw point forces from the simulator are statistically converted into a smooth, consistent 3D force distribution, which serves as the ground truth for training. This is a core component, detailed below.
3. **Model Training:** A ResNet-based encoder-decoder model is trained to predict the 3D force distribution. The encoder uses the feature extraction part of ResNet50, initialized with ImageNet pre-trained weights. The decoder comprises residual blocks from a Residual U-Net. The force distribution is represented as a 3D voxel map, and prediction is framed as an image-to-image translation problem.
4. **Real-World Application:** The trained model predicts force distributions for real-world scenes from a single RGB image. This prediction is then used for downstream tasks, such as planning an object's lifting direction to minimize disturbance to surrounding objects.

**Geometry-aware Statistical Smoothing of Force Labels (Figure 2, 3, Equation 2):**
This is a key innovation to address the disparity between simulated point forces and real-world contacts.
- **Previous Isotropic Smoothing:** Acknowledged prior work used isotropic Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) to smooth point forces. However, this method spreads forces uniformly, potentially blurring contact information and expanding distributions perpendicular to contact surfaces, leading to force prediction errors and inconsistent lifting directions.
- **Proposed Geometry-Aware Smoothing (GAFS):** To improve upon isotropic smoothing, GAFS incorporates a prior on object geometry. The force density at a voxel `p_j` is calculated as a weighted sum of forces `f_i` at contact points `c_i`. The weights `K(p_j, c_i)` include three Gaussian terms:
 1. `K(D(p_j, A_i)/σ_g)`: Attenuation based on the distance `D` from `p_j` to the region `A_i` of the first contacting object.
 2. `K(D(p_j, B_i)/σ_g)`: Attenuation based on the distance `D` from `p_j` to the region `B_i` of the second contacting object.
 3. `K(||p_j - c_i||/σ_f)`: Standard Gaussian smoothing term based on the distance from the contact point `c_i`.
 `σ_g` controls geometry-based attenuation, and `σ_f` controls force smoothing. This method ensures that force distribution spreads along contact surfaces (where `D` to both objects is zero) but decays rapidly perpendicular to them. A normalization factor `α_i` preserves total force. Visual meshes are used for detailed geometry, and a Voxel-based Signed Distance Function (SDF) efficiently computes distances.

**Force Label Scaling and Normalization:**
To handle a wide range of force magnitudes across diverse daily objects and to emphasize relative force patterns, the target force distribution is converted to a log scale and normalized to. Mean Squared Error (MSE) is used as the loss function, which penalizes small errors more heavily on a log scale.

**Dataset Generation (Section 4.1):**
- **Object Selection:** Objects from the YCB dataset and scanned convenience store items are used, categorized into "seen" and "unseen" for generalization evaluation.
- **Scene Generation:** Piled scenes are created by randomly placing objects, with the number of items sampled from a Poisson distribution.
- **Simulator Settings:** Isaac Sim is used. Visual domain randomization is applied (Table B1) by varying lighting, camera parameters, and object colors. Constant mass density and fixed friction coefficients are assumed.
- **Lifting Episode Generation (Appendix C):** For evaluation, specific lifting scenarios are curated. Grasp candidates are generated, and scenes where the target object can be lifted straight upward without collisions with surrounding objects are selected.

**Evaluation Setup (Section 4.4.1, 4.4.2):**
- **Real-World System:** A UR5e robot with a Robotiq 2F-140 gripper (modified with gel for better grip) is used.
- **Pose Tracking:** FoundationPose, combined with YOLOv5 for instance segmentation and initial pose estimation, tracks 6DoF poses of surrounding objects from an RGBD camera (RealSense D415). This enables disturbance measurement in real environments without direct force sensing.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Prediction Accuracy (RQ1):** MSE and normalized prediction error `L(d)` near object surfaces.
 - **Downstream Task Performance (RQ2 & RQ3):**
 - **Disturbance Metrics (Simulation & Real):** Total distance traveled by surrounding objects, maximum velocity of surrounding objects, maximum contact force on surrounding objects (simulation only). For real-world, max translation and rotation velocities/distances are used (excluding rotation around symmetry axis for symmetric objects).
 - **Physical Interpretation Metrics (Simulation):** Gravity Support (GS) and Torque Balance (TB) measure how well predicted forces support weight and balance torques.
 - **Task Relevance (Simulation):** Low Resistance Direction Agreement (LRDA) evaluates how well the predicted distribution identifies low-resistance lifting directions compared to ground truth.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments address three research questions:

**RQ1: Does the smoothed distribution make consistent predictions easier compared to the point forces?**
- **Qualitative Observation (Figure 5, 6):** Isotropic Force Smoothing (IFS) with small `σ_f` (0.005m) yields highly localized, point-like predictions. Increasing `σ_f` (0.015m) broadens the distribution spherically but still poorly covers surface or line contacts. Geometry-Aware Force Smoothing (GAFS) with appropriate parameters (e.g., `σ_f` = 0.030m, `σ_g` = 0.010m) produces distributions that align more closely with actual contact surfaces, showing elongated patterns for line contacts and flatter patterns for surface contacts. GAFS more effectively covers contact areas without significant spread in the surface normal direction.
- **Quantitative Results (Table 2):**
 - Applying smoothing (both IFS and GAFS) generally improves prediction accuracy compared to weakly smoothed IFS (`σ_f` = 0.005).
 - GAFS consistently reduces prediction error more significantly than IFS, especially when evaluating closer to the object surface (smaller `d` in `L(d)`). For instance, GAFS(`σ_f`=0.060, `σ_g`=0.010) achieved the lowest L(d=0.5cm) error (0.04240).
 - Stronger smoothing in GAFS (larger `σ_f`) generally led to better prediction accuracy.
These results indicate that smoothed distributions are more predictable, and geometry-aware smoothing further enhances this predictability.

**RQ2: Is the predicted distribution useful for manipulation tasks?**
- **Lifting Performance in Simulation (Table 3, Figure 7):**
 - All force-aware methods (GAFS and IFS) substantially reduced maximum velocity (23-24%) and maximum contact force (9-14%) on surrounding objects compared to a simple upward lifting strategy (No-Force(UP)).
 - Smoothing force labels improved performance, particularly in reducing variance in maximum velocity and force, indicating more consistent disturbance minimization.
 - GAFS, by incorporating geometry, caused smaller disturbances than IFS. However, excessive smoothing in GAFS (`σ_f` = 0.060m) led to a slight degradation in lifting performance compared to moderate smoothing (`σ_f` = 0.030m), suggesting a balance is needed to retain locality of forces.
- **Physical Interpretation (Table 4):**
 - GAFS generally yielded smaller Gravity Support (GS) and Torque Balance (TB) values, suggesting better physical consistency in the predicted force distributions. There were exceptions in challenging scenes (e.g., light object supporting heavy one) where approximations led to higher values.
 - Low Resistance Direction Agreement (LRDA) was significantly improved by smoothing. GAFS performed better in cases with surface or flat contacts (e.g., tuna can, scissors), while IFS was sometimes better for critical point contacts (e.g., curry box, bowl). Weakly smoothed IFS showed poor and variable performance across metrics.

**RQ3: Does the prediction model transfer well to real-world scenes?**
- **Qualitative Assessment (Figure 10):** The model trained solely in simulation successfully predicted 3D force distributions in real-world piled scenes composed of unseen objects. GAFS predicted smoother patterns aligned with object geometry, while IFS predicted more point-like patterns. GAFS often planned lifting directions that appeared more appropriate for multi-contact situations, such as avoiding pushing adjacent objects.
- **Quantitative Results (Figure 11):**
 - In most real-world scenes, GAFS achieved the smallest disturbances to surrounding objects, measured by maximum translation and rotation velocities/distances.
 - GAFS exhibited small standard deviations, comparable to the No-Force(UP) baseline, and smaller than IFS, indicating more consistent performance in minimizing disturbances.
 - For scenes where the target object was largely hidden or small, all methods, including GAFS and IFS, were less effective, and performance differences were minimal.
 - The overall trend was consistent: GAFS generally outperformed IFS and No-Force(UP) in reducing real-world disturbances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by advancing the capability of robots to infer complex physical interactions from visual input. The key significance and potential impact include:

- **Enhanced Robotic Manipulation:** The ability to predict rough contact force distributions from vision, especially in cluttered static scenes, can significantly improve robotic manipulation strategies. This enables robots to anticipate consequences of actions and plan movements that minimize disturbance to the environment, leading to more robust and dexterous object handling.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The successful transfer of a model trained exclusively in simulation to real-world environments, despite inherent differences in contact physics, demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed approach. This reduces the need for costly and time-consuming real-world data collection for force prediction.
- **Improved Force Prediction Consistency:** The introduction of geometry-aware statistical smoothing addresses a fundamental limitation of rigid-body simulators by transforming sparse point forces into more physically plausible and consistent distributions that account for various contact types (point, line, surface). This method provides a more stable and meaningful learning target for neural networks.
- **Robustness to Simulation Artifacts:** By smoothing force labels, the method reduces sensitivity to specific simulator settings, mesh structures, and numerical inconsistencies that can affect raw point force outputs.
- **Generalization to Novelty:** The experiments show the model's ability to generalize to unseen objects and novel piled scenes, an important step towards deployment in unpredictable real-world environments.

Potential future impacts could include:
- **More Autonomous and Adaptive Robots:** Robots capable of better physical reasoning from vision could operate more autonomously in unstructured environments, such as warehouses, homes, or disaster zones.
- **Foundation for Multi-Modal Sensing:** While vision-based, the work sets a foundation for future integration with other modalities (e.g., tactile, force sensing) to further refine predictions or handle situations where visual information is ambiguous (e.g., hidden contents).
- **New Benchmarking and Evaluation Methodologies:** The proposed metrics and real-world evaluation protocols, utilizing visual tracking for disturbance assessment, offer valuable tools for comparing and advancing research in this domain.

The limitations, such as reliance on rigid-body assumptions, uniform mass density, and fixed friction coefficients, as well as the challenges with very strong smoothing, highlight avenues for future research to further refine the physical modeling and generalize to even broader scenarios and material properties. The concept of continuously predicting forces during manipulation is also identified as a promising future direction.
