# RubricEM: Meta-RL with Rubric-guided Policy Decomposition beyond Verifiable Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> RubricEM, a reinforcement learning framework, trains deep research agents to handle open-ended, long-form information-seeking tasks by integrating rubric-guided policy decomposition and a reflection meta-policy. The framework achieved an average score of 55.5 across four long-form research benchmarks, outperforming open-source models and approaching proprietary systems.

## Problem

- Training deep research agents with reinforcement learning (RL) in domains where verifiable ground-truth answers for their outputs are often absent.
- Addressing the challenge of long-horizon credit assignment due to delayed and coarse judge feedback over extended tool-augmented rollouts.
- Inefficient experience reuse, as traditional RL post-training often lacks explicit mechanisms for distilling past attempts into reusable, interpretable guidance.

## Method

- **RubricEM** uses a ==rubric-guided structured reasoning scaffold== that imposes a ==four-stage structure== (Plan, Research, Review, Answer) on agent trajectories, leveraging self-generated rubrics to guide decisions (instilled via ==supervised fine-tuning== distillation).
- **Stage-Structured GRPO (SS-GRPO)** assigns ==stage-specific LLM-judge scores== and uses a ==causal stage-dependence matrix== for fine-grained ==credit assignment==, improving upon single terminal rewards.
- A ==shared-backbone reflection meta-policy==, jointly trained with the task policy, generates and distills past experiences into reusable natural-language guidance stored in an ==agent rubric bank== for both within-episode refinement and cross-episode transfer.

## Results

- **RubricEM-8B** (RL, 1400 steps) achieved an average score of **55.5** across HealthBench, ResearchQA, DeepResearchBench, and ResearchRubrics (up from **49.2** at the SFT checkpoint), outperforming all comparable open-source deep research systems.
- The framework approached the performance of proprietary deep research systems, outperforming **Perplexity Deep Research** and specifically surpassing **OpenAI Deep Research** on the DeepResearchBench benchmark (within **4.4 points** on average).
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the SS-GRPO and Reflection Meta-Policy components individually contributed to performance improvements, and their combination yielded the best overall results.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rubrics can serve as a fundamental 'shared interface' throughout the entire RL process, guiding policy execution, supporting process-level judgment, and enabling experience distillation, rather than just being final-answer evaluators.
> - Explicitly structuring agent trajectories into distinct stages allows for more granular and semantically aligned credit assignment, providing denser learning signals over long horizons.
> - Learning to generate reflective lessons from past interactions, coupled with an asynchronous training pipeline, enables efficient meta-learning and explicit experience reuse without significant wall-clock overhead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of deep research agents, defined as systems capable of planning, searching, evaluating evidence, and synthesizing long-form reports autonomously, represents a challenge for reinforcement learning (RL). These systems operate in domains where verifiable ground-truth answers for their outputs are often absent, and their operational trajectories involve numerous tool-augmented decisions. Existing proprietary deep research systems, such as Gemini and OpenAI’s deep research agents, generally do not disclose their specific training methodologies. In the open-source domain, most efforts focus on tasks with verifiable search proxies or rely heavily on high-quality imitation data. End-to-end RL for long-form research is particularly difficult due to the lack of objective ground-truth for outputs, the delayed and coarse nature of judge feedback over extended tool-augmented rollouts, and the limited mechanisms for converting past attempts into reusable experience within conventional post-training paradigms.

This work positions itself within the broader landscape of agentic reinforcement learning, specifically addressing the challenges of long-horizon credit assignment and experience reuse in open-ended tasks where rewards are not easily verifiable. It builds upon previous research, such as DR Tulu, which explored end-to-end RL for deep research beyond verifiable rewards. RubricEM extends this foundation by proposing mechanisms for finer-grained credit assignment and the joint training of a meta-policy for experience evolution. The paper also relates to contemporary efforts in improving credit assignment for LLM agents, distinguishing itself by focusing on semantic, task-dependent feedback in unverifiable settings, rather than domains with objectively checkable intermediate correctness. Furthermore, it contributes to the field of Meta-RL for language models by integrating a reflection mechanism designed for open-ended real-world tasks, with an emphasis on efficient asynchronous execution to mitigate the training overhead often associated with meta-learning approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to address the challenge of training deep research agents using reinforcement learning in scenarios where rewards are not verifiable, while simultaneously enabling effective long-horizon credit assignment and facilitating learning from experience.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations regarding the limitations of current RL approaches for deep research agents:
1. **Lack of Verifiable Rewards**: Unlike many traditional RL tasks, the quality of long-form research outputs cannot be objectively verified with ground-truth answers. This makes standard reward functions, which typically rely on exact matches or quantifiable metrics, unsuitable.
2. **Long-Horizon Credit Assignment**: Deep research trajectories are often extended and involve multiple sequential steps (planning, searching, reviewing, synthesizing). Judge feedback, when available, is typically delayed and applies to the final output, making it difficult to attribute credit or blame to specific intermediate decisions or actions within a long rollout. This sparsity of feedback impedes effective learning.
3. **Inefficient Experience Reuse**: Traditional RL post-training primarily updates model parameters based on judged attempts. However, it often lacks explicit mechanisms for distilling past experiences into reusable, interpretable guidance that can inform future attempts on the same or related tasks. This limits the agent's ability to meta-learn and adapt efficiently.

The authors propose that **rubrics** can serve as a fundamental "shared interface" throughout the entire reinforcement learning process, moving beyond their traditional role as mere final-answer evaluators. This perspective is motivated by the idea that the same criteria used to define success can also:
- **Guide Policy Execution**: Inform the agent's planning and search strategies proactively.
- **Support Process-Level Judgment**: Enable fine-grained feedback on intermediate decisions, rather than just the final output.
- **Enable Experience Distillation**: Facilitate the extraction of reusable reflections and lessons from judged trajectories.

This view leads to RubricEM, an EM-inspired framework where the latent structure of an open-ended research task—what constitutes quality, where credit belongs, and what knowledge should be retained—is "estimated" through rubrics. These rubric-conditioned estimates then guide the training process to "maximize" both the task policy and a reflection meta-policy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RubricEM integrates three main components: a rubric-guided structured reasoning scaffold, Stage-Structured GRPO (SS-GRPO) for credit assignment, and reflection meta-policy training with an asynchronous execution pipeline.

#### 3.1. Structured Reasoning Scaffold

RubricEM explicitly imposes a stage structure on agent trajectories, guided by self-generated rubrics. This addresses the problem of flat, long-horizon rollouts lacking internal organization. The framework formalizes this benefit, demonstrating that explicit stage information can improve decision-making by conditioning actions on the current decision mode, especially when local contexts are aliased across different stages.

The scaffold defines four sequential stages, each marked by XML tags and an internal schema:
- **Plan**: The agent begins with unstructured <think>ing, then creates a <structured_plan>. This plan includes a <deep_analysis> of the query, self-generated <rubrics> (knowledge checklist, analytical criteria, negative constraints), and a <research_plan> to satisfy these rubrics. The agent then executes its first tool call. Rubrics generated at this stage serve as *prospective* targets.
- **Research**: An iterative loop where the agent issues <call_tool> actions. After each tool output, it performs <state_evaluation> by comparing evidence against the plan and rubrics. The agent either continues searching (directly or with a revised <structured_plan>) or proceeds to the review stage if sufficient evidence is gathered.
- **Review**: A mandatory structured self-evaluation stage. The agent performs a <rubric_review> to map collected evidence back to its self-generated rubrics and then prepares a <writing_plan>, outlining the final answer's structure and core thesis.
- **Answer**: The agent synthesizes the final long-form response within <answer> tags, adhering to the writing plan, rubric criteria, and grounding claims with inline citations.

**Key Design Principles**: Rubrics are central, guiding decisions across all stages. The agent is constrained to one action per turn and must cite non-trivial claims. Adaptive cognitive effort ensures planning depth scales with query complexity.

**SFT Distillation**: The scaffold is instilled into a Qwen3-8B model through supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Gemini-3.1-Pro acts as a teacher, generating stage-structured trajectories. A rigorous rejection sampling process is applied to discard teacher outputs that violate structural or behavioral constraints, ensuring the student model learns the intended scaffold discipline and rubric conditioning.

#### 3.2. Stage-Structured GRPO (SS-GRPO)

Building on the structured scaffold, SS-GRPO addresses the sparse and delayed credit assignment problem in open-ended deep research. It does so without requiring oracle intermediate rewards by leveraging the explicit stage boundaries and rubric-guided judging.

- **Stagewise Scores and Returns**: Instead of broadcasting a single terminal score, SS-GRPO assigns stage-specific LLM-judge scores ($R_{i,k}$) to each of the four stages for a given rollout. A causal stage-dependence matrix ($\Lambda$) then defines a stage-weighted return ($G_{\Lambda,i,k}$), which combines a stage's local quality with the impact of downstream stages it influences.
- **Theoretical Justification**: The paper presents a theoretical analysis (Theorem 3/4 in main text, Theorem 4 in appendix) showing that stage-weighted credit can yield a better gradient approximation than terminal rewards, provided that the recovered intermediate signal outweighs the cumulative noise from the judge's assessment.
- **Stagewise Evolving-Rubric Judge**: An LLM judge (Gemini-3-Flash) contrasts multiple rollouts for the same query and dynamically proposes discriminative rubrics for each stage. It maintains separate rubric buffers for each stage (Plan, Research, Review, Answer), reusing high-discrimination rubrics and removing less useful ones. The judge can reference the agent's self-generated rubrics, but scores trajectories against its own evolving judge-side rubric buffer.
- **Stagewise Normalization and Objective**: SS-GRPO is implemented as a critic-free variant of GRPO. Stage-specific advantages ($A_{i,k}$) are computed by normalizing returns within each stage across the rollout group. The objective function penalizes large policy changes while maximizing the sum of these stage-specific advantages.

#### 3.3. Meta-Policy Training with Reinforcement Learning

RubricEM makes experience reuse an explicit RL objective.
- **Shared-Backbone Reflection Meta-Policy**: The core of the system is a shared backbone, meaning the same Qwen3-8B model parameters are used for both task execution (generating trajectories) and reflection generation.
- **Joint Training**: After task rollouts are judged, a query-trajectory pair is sampled. The shared backbone generates multiple reflection candidates. A privileged LLM judge scores these candidates based on their usefulness for (i) *within-episode refinement* (improving future attempts on the same query) and (ii) *cross-episode transfer* (guiding related future queries). These scores provide auxiliary RL rewards, updating the shared backbone parameters.
- **Agent Rubric Bank**: Only the highest-scored, accepted reflection is written into an agent rubric bank as natural-language memory. These bank items distill outcome-aware lessons from completed trajectories.
- **Coupled Agent–Judge Co-evolution**: The reflection loop is intertwined with the evolving judge. As the agent's policy improves and generates new failure modes or criteria, the judge's rubric buffer updates, which in turn influences how reflections are scored and thus how the agent's memory evolves.
- **Two Modes of Adaptation**: The rubric bank supports:
 - **Within-episode refinement**: Retrieving a query's own prior reflection when attempting it again.
 - **Cross-episode transfer**: Retrieving reflections from semantically related past questions.
 - A "windowed curriculum" ensures sufficient temporal separation for reflections to be generated and stored before within-episode retrieval is attempted.
- **Efficient Asynchronous Execution**: To avoid performance bottlenecks, reflection generation, judging, and meta-policy updates are run asynchronously, lagging the main task-policy RL loop by one step. This ensures continuous GPU and API utilization, effectively adding no extra wall-clock overhead to the core SS-GRPO loop.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RubricEM-8B was conducted across four long-form research benchmarks and four short-form benchmarks, comparing its performance against both proprietary and open-source models.

#### 4.1. Long-Form Research Performance

- **Overall Performance**: RubricEM-8B (RL, 1400 steps) achieved an average score of 55.5 across HealthBench, ResearchQA, DeepResearchBench (DRB), and ResearchRubrics. This represents the highest average score among the non-proprietary deep research systems evaluated.
- **Comparison to Open Models**: RubricEM-8B surpassed strong open baselines, including DR Tulu-8B (RL, 1900 steps), Tongyi DeepResearch-30B-A3B, and WebThinker-32B-DPO.
- **Comparison to Proprietary Systems**: RubricEM-8B approached the performance of proprietary deep research systems. On average, it outperformed Perplexity Deep Research and remained within 4.4 average points of OpenAI Deep Research, while specifically outperforming OpenAI Deep Research on the DRB benchmark.
- **RL Effectiveness and Efficiency**: The RL training process significantly improved performance from the structured SFT checkpoint, increasing the average score from 49.2 to 55.5 across all four benchmarks. The final RL model (RubricEM-8B-RL) surpassed its own teacher model (Gemini-3.1-Pro) on average. Furthermore, RubricEM achieved these gains with fewer RL training steps (1400) compared to the closest prior RL system, DR Tulu (1900 steps).

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies on RL Training Recipes

- Under a fixed 600-step budget, both SS-GRPO and the Meta-Policy components individually demonstrated performance improvements over a Baseline-RL (standard answer-only GRPO).
- The full RubricEM recipe, combining both SS-GRPO and the Meta-Policy, yielded the best performance across benchmarks, indicating complementary gains from these components.

#### 4.3. Structured Scaffolding and Inference-Time Experience Reuse

- **Scaffold Impact on SFT**: The rubric-guided structured scaffold improved the quality of SFT distillation, resulting in a stronger initial checkpoint.
- **Scaffold Impact on RL**: The structured scaffold was shown to enhance the effectiveness and stability of subsequent RL training. Without the scaffold, RL gains were smaller and less consistent.
- **Prompt-Level Effect**: Running Gemini-3.1-Pro with the RubricEM scaffold produced higher DRB performance compared to a standard ReAct (think & act) prompt, suggesting that the structural guidance itself contributes to better deep research behavior independent of student model training.
- **Inference-Time Experience Reuse**: RubricEM demonstrated benefits from injecting reflections from the rubric bank at inference time, both for cross-episode transfer (retrieving reflections from similar past questions) and within-episode refinement (retrieving prior reflections for the same question). In contrast, the Baseline-RL model did not benefit from the same experience reuse mechanisms. This indicates that the Reflection Meta-Policy training learns actionable, reusable guidance.

#### 4.4. Short-Form Benchmark Performance

- Despite being primarily trained on long-form data, RubricEM exhibited strong generalization capabilities to out-of-domain short-form search benchmarks (SimpleQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, WebWalker, DeepSearchQA).
- RubricEM-SFT already showed effective search-and-answer behavior, which was further improved by long-form RL training, even without specific short-form RL data. The most pronounced gains were observed on more complex short-form tasks, suggesting that the RL recipe teaches transferable tool-use and evidence-grounding skills.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RubricEM demonstrates a robust and efficient reinforcement learning framework for developing deep research agents that can tackle open-ended, long-form information-seeking tasks. The research makes several significant contributions:

1. **Framework for Unverifiable Rewards**: It provides a concrete and empirically supported method for training RL agents in domains where precise, verifiable ground-truth rewards are unavailable. This expands the applicability of RL to a broader range of complex, human-like cognitive tasks.
2. **Rubrics as a Shared Interface**: The work redefines the role of rubrics from mere evaluation tools to a central interface that structures policy execution, guides intermediate judgment, and facilitates experience distillation. This paradigm shift offers a principled way to instill human-like assessment criteria into agent behavior.
3. **Enhanced Credit Assignment**: By introducing stagewise policy decomposition and Stage-Structured GRPO, RubricEM enables denser and more semantically aligned credit assignment over long-horizon trajectories. This mechanism provides more informative learning signals than traditional sparse, terminal rewards, addressing a fundamental challenge in complex agent training.
4. **Effective Experience Reuse**: The integration of a shared-backbone reflection meta-policy, coupled with a rubric bank and an asynchronous training pipeline, allows agents to explicitly learn from past attempts and distill experiences into reusable guidance. This promotes meta-learning and improves the efficiency of future task executions through both parametric updates and textual memory.
5. **Practical Performance**: The achieved performance of RubricEM-8B, outperforming comparable open models and approaching proprietary systems, indicates that the proposed techniques are effective and can yield competitive capabilities even at a smaller model scale.
6. **Transferability of Skills**: The observed generalization to out-of-domain short-form benchmarks suggests that the RL recipe fosters transferable skills in tool use, reasoning, and evidence grounding, rather than simply optimizing for long-form report writing.

The methodological contributions of RubricEM, particularly the use of explicit task structure, structured credit assignment, and integrated memory evolution, offer a recipe for advancing long-horizon RL in complex, open-ended scenarios. This approach has potential implications for developing agents in various domains beyond research, such as creative writing, data analysis, scientific review, educational tutoring, and intricate tool-use workflows, where task quality is multidimensional and not amenable to simple verification. Future work could explore the impact of stronger judges, ensemble judging, and human-auditable rubric banks to further enhance robustness and address potential risks related to biased or shallow rubric criteria.
