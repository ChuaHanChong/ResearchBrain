# DynaFLIP: Rethinking Robotics Perception via Tri-Modal-Dynamics Guided Representation

> [!summary] Summary
> A pre-training framework named DynaFLIP learns dynamics-aware visual representations for robotics by aligning image transitions, natural language, and 3D flow within a higher-order multimodal geometry. This approach enables enhanced robot generalization, leading to superior performance across simulation benchmarks and improved robustness in real-world manipulation tasks under diverse out-of-distribution conditions.

## Problem

- Existing robot learning relies on visual encoders pre-trained for static object recognition, limiting their ability to understand motion and dynamics critical for manipulation tasks.
- These conventional encoders often focus on visually salient but control-irrelevant scene regions, hindering effective robot generalization.
- Integrating explicit dynamics awareness into visual perception earlier in the pipeline remains a challenge for current robot learning systems.

## Method

- **DynaFLIP** introduces a tri-modal-dynamics guided pre-training framework that distills supervision from image transitions, language, and 3D flow into a single-image encoder.
- It employs a simplex-guided multimodal alignment objective, minimizing the geometric volume spanned by the three modalities in a shared embedding space.
- Optimization pitfalls like geometric ambiguity and trivial collapse are addressed through a cosine regularizer and an InfoNCE-style contrastive objective, ensuring robust alignment.

## Results

- DynaFLIP's image encoder consistently achieved the highest success rates as a frozen backbone on MetaWorld and RLBench, demonstrating control-relevant representations.
- On the LIBERO benchmark, DynaFLIP showed superior performance with Diffusion Policy, achieving the highest mean success rate in both frozen and LoRA fine-tuned settings.
- In real-world UR3 robot tasks, DynaFLIP improved manipulation success rates and exhibited enhanced robustness against various out-of-distribution visual, spatial, and semantic perturbations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robotic perception benefits significantly from visual representations that are explicitly dynamics-aware, encoding how scenes change under action rather than just their static content.
> - A higher-order multimodal alignment approach, aligning three distinct transition-centric modalities (image transitions, language, 3D flow) jointly, yields more effective control-relevant representations than pairwise alignment.
> - Robust multimodal alignment requires specific mechanisms, like cosine regularization and contrastive learning, to mitigate geometric ambiguity and prevent trivial embedding collapse.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is actively pursuing the development of agents capable of generalizing across diverse real-world environments and tasks. A common approach in recent robot learning systems involves leveraging powerful vision encoders, such as CLIP, SigLIP, and DINOv2, as visual backbones for various policies, including imitation learning and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This practice operates under the premise that perception can be effectively borrowed from encoders pre-trained on mainstream computer vision objectives, leaving the understanding of motion and dynamics primarily to downstream planning or control modules.

This paper challenges that assumption, arguing that it fundamentally limits robot generalization, particularly for manipulation tasks. Robot manipulation inherently involves understanding how actions induce state transitions, a concept not explicitly addressed by visual encoders primarily pre-trained for static object recognition or vision-language alignment. Such conventional encoders may direct attention to visually salient but control-irrelevant regions of a scene, rather than focusing on the manipulated objects or contact areas critical for task success.

DynaFLIP is introduced to address this limitation by pushing "dynamics awareness" upstream into the perception pipeline. Instead of relying solely on post-perception processing for motion understanding, DynaFLIP aims to train visual encoders to represent not only the static content of a scene but also how that scene changes under action. This approach differentiates it from other robotic-specific pre-training methods that might focus on single-modality self-supervised objectives (e.g., MVP, VC-1, VIP, HRP) or multimodal alignment with fewer modalities (e.g., R3M, LIV, MCR, LaDA). Specifically, while some prior works incorporate language or action, DynaFLIP distinguishes itself by jointly aligning three distinct transition-centric modalities—image transitions, language, and 3D flow—within a higher-order multimodal geometry framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a dynamics-aware multimodal pre-training framework, termed DynaFLIP, that can shape an image-only encoder to produce control-relevant visual representations for robot manipulation. This endeavor is motivated by the observed limitations of current robot learning pipelines, which predominantly rely on visual encoders pre-trained for static recognition tasks.

The detailed motivations and challenges addressed are as follows:

- **Limitation of Static Perception for Dynamic Tasks:** Robot manipulation is fundamentally about instigating and understanding state transitions through actions. Existing visual encoders, primarily trained for static scene understanding or vision-language alignment, are not inherently exposed to motion and dynamics during their pre-training phase. Consequently, they often fail to capture the causality of actions on scene changes, leading to representations that may highlight visually prominent but control-irrelevant features. The motivation is to overcome this by enabling the visual encoder to represent "how the world changes under action," rather than just "what is present."

- **Injecting Dynamics Awareness into Single-Image Encoders:** A significant challenge lies in how to imbue a visual encoder with dynamics awareness when, at test time, the encoder is designed to operate on single images. A single image alone may not fully convey which aspects of a scene are causally relevant for an action.
 - **Proposed Solution:** To address this, DynaFLIP proposes using complementary modalities as supervisory signals during training, rather than as additional inputs during inference. These modalities provide richer information about intended and realized state changes.
 - **Selection of Three Modalities:** The paper deliberately selects three specific modalities due to their distinct and complementary contributions:
 - **Image Transitions:** Provide the most direct visual evidence of observable state changes between two points in time.
 - **Language:** Offers a semantic-level description of the *intended* transition, providing high-level context and task goals that visual changes alone might not explain.
 - **3D Flow:** Contributes an explicit, viewpoint-invariant account of physical motion in 3D space, decoupling scene dynamics from 2D appearance variations caused by camera movement. This modality offers granular information about how objects and the environment are physically changing.
 - **Data Scalability:** A further motivation for these modalities is their extractability from large-scale, action-free human and robot video data, allowing for extensive pre-training beyond the confines of limited robot-collected datasets.

- **Challenges in Multimodal Alignment:** The process of transferring supervisory signals from these three modalities into the latent space of an image-only encoder presents its own challenges.
 - **Limitations of Standard Anchor-Based Objectives:** Traditional multimodal alignment methods, such as anchor-based contrastive learning, typically align auxiliary modalities independently to a designated anchor modality. This design ensures pairwise alignment with the anchor but does not guarantee mutual alignment or specific geometric relationships among the non-anchor modalities.
 - **Pitfalls of Naive Simplex-Volume Minimization:** Inspired by multimodal retrieval research, an alternative involves constraining all modality embeddings jointly through the simplex they span. However, directly minimizing simplex volume faces two specific pitfalls:
 - **Geometric Ambiguity:** A small simplex volume does not inherently guarantee strong mutual alignment. For instance, embeddings can lie nearly on a single line (forming a "flat triangle") resulting in near-zero volume despite poor mutual alignment.
 - **Trivial Collapse:** Without mechanisms to push apart mismatched or negative tuples, the simplex volume could trivially collapse to zero if all modality embeddings converge to a single point, rendering the learned representations uninformative.
 - **DynaFLIP's Solutions:** To overcome these pitfalls, DynaFLIP introduces a combination of techniques: a cosine regularizer between selected modality pairs to resolve geometric ambiguity by enforcing pairwise attraction, and an InfoNCE-style contrastive objective that embeds the joint alignment energy within a framework that uses negative tuples to prevent trivial collapse.

By addressing these motivations and challenges, DynaFLIP aims to improve robot generalization by enabling visual representations to more effectively encode dynamics- and control-relevant structures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DynaFLIP is a Dynamics-aware 3D Flow-Language-Image Pre-training framework designed to distill supervision from three modalities into an image-only encoder, yielding control-relevant visual representations.

#### 3.1. Encoder Architecture

DynaFLIP utilizes three distinct encoders for image, language, and 3D flow, all mapping their respective inputs into a shared ℓ₂-normalized embedding space on the unit sphere.

- **Image Encoder:**
 - Initialized with a DINOv2-Base (ViT-B/14) backbone, which is kept fully trainable during pre-training.
 - For a given input image I_t, the encoder produces a [CLS] token and a sequence of patch tokens.
 - The per-frame embedding `d_t` is formed by concatenating the [CLS] token with the average-pooled patch tokens.
 - The image-transition embedding `z_I` is derived as the normalized feature difference between a future observation `I_{t+H}` and the current observation `I_t`, thereby focusing on visual state change rather than static appearance. An MLP fusion block combines embeddings from adjacent sampled frame pairs.

- **Language Encoder:**
 - Consists of a frozen T5-Base encoder augmented with a learnable adapter.
 - Task instructions, tokenized to a maximum length of 77 tokens, are processed by the encoder.
 - The sentence-level representation is extracted via EOS-token pooling, then projected through the adapter to produce the language embedding `z_L`.

- **3D Flow Encoder:**
 - Processes a sequence of `K` timesteps of 20 × 20 × 3 flow data, representing 3D displacement vectors.
 - It comprises two stages:
 - **3D Motion Encoder:** A 4-layer CNN independently encodes each timestep into a per-timestep feature.
 - **Temporal Motion Transformer:** A 4-layer transformer encoder aggregates temporal information. It incorporates visual context by prepending the current-frame image embedding `d_t` (with stop-gradient applied) as a conditioning token. A learnable temporal [CLS] token and positional embeddings are added. The output of the temporal [CLS] token is then projected via a linear layer to yield the 3D flow embedding `z_F`. The stop-gradient on `d_t` aims to preserve semantic grounding while preventing trivial shortcut solutions through the image branch.

#### 3.2. Simplex-Guided Multimodal Alignment Objective (L_align)

The core of DynaFLIP's approach is to encourage mutual alignment among the three modalities (image transitions `z_I`, language `z_L`, and 3D flow `z_F`) by minimizing the volume of the simplex they span in the shared hyperspherical space. In the three-modal setting, this corresponds to minimizing the triangle area `A(z_L, z_I, z_F)`.

- **Cosine Regularization:** To mitigate the "geometric ambiguity" pitfall (where a flat triangle can have near-zero area despite poor mutual alignment), the alignment energy `E` is augmented with a cosine regularizer. The energy is defined as `E(z_L, z_I, z_F) = A(z_L, z_I, z_F) - α⟨z_L, z_F⟩`, where `α ≥ 0` balances triangle-area minimization with pairwise cosine alignment. This term explicitly pulls `z_L` and `z_F` closer, penalizing configurations where they remain far apart.

- **Contrastive Framework:** To prevent "trivial collapse" (where all embeddings converge to a single point), the joint alignment energy `E` is embedded within an InfoNCE-style contrastive objective. For each matched positive tuple, the loss function minimizes its energy relative to a set of mismatched negative tuples, forcing matched tuples to achieve lower energy than mismatched ones. This mechanism ensures representation dispersion and prevents collapse.

#### 3.3. Auxiliary Objectives

Two auxiliary objectives further strengthen the dynamics-aware representations.

- **Temporal Contrastive Loss (L_tcn):** This objective, inspired by prior work, enforces trajectory-level temporal structure. It is an InfoNCE-style loss that pulls embeddings of nearby frames closer together than embeddings of more distant frames within the same video trajectory.

- **Actor Loss (L_act):** This objective directly reinforces dynamics awareness by requiring the image encoder to explicitly predict 3D flow from a single frame. Given the current image feature `f_ϕ(I_t)`, a 3D flow prediction head outputs `F̂_t`, and the mean squared error between `F̂_t` and the ground-truth flow `F_t` is minimized.

The full pre-training objective `L_DynaFLIP` is a weighted sum of these three components: `L_align + λ_tcn * L_tcn + λ_act * L_act`.

#### 3.4. Dataset Construction

The pre-training framework relies solely on RGB videos. The image–language–3D flow triplets are derived from video data through a modified TraceForge pipeline.

- **Dataset Composition:** A large-scale dataset comprising 260K trajectories (190K from robot demonstrations and 70K from human videos) is constructed. Sources include AgiBot, Droid, Open X-Embodiment, BridgeData V2 for robot videos, and Ego4D, Something-Something V2 for human videos, ensuring diverse objects, environments, and interaction patterns.
- **Language Instruction Generation:** A vision-language model generates natural language instructions describing the manipulation intent for each sampled trajectory based on representative frames and specific prompts.
- **3D Flow Generation:** 3D flow is constructed to be viewpoint-invariant. Keypoints are placed on a uniform 20 × 20 grid in a reference frame and tracked throughout the trajectory. Using TAPIP3D, CoTracker3, and a fine-tuned VGGT model (from SpatialTrackerV2), per-frame depth maps, camera poses, and tracked 2D keypoint trajectories are estimated. These tracked 2D points are unprojected with estimated depth and transformed into the reference camera coordinate system, compensating for camera motion and isolating true scene motion. The final 3D flow is stored as a screen-aligned sequence of `(x, y, z)` where `z` is depth in the reference camera frame.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates DynaFLIP through a series of experiments across simulation and real-world setups, addressing four main questions regarding its learned representations, downstream performance, real-world generalization, and critical design choices.

**Q1: Does DynaFLIP learn dynamics-aware and control-relevant representations?**
- **Quantitative Analysis:** DynaFLIP's pre-trained image encoder was evaluated on MetaWorld and RLBench. When used as a frozen backbone for a lightweight MLP policy, DynaFLIP achieved the highest downstream success rates. This correlated with high control-relevant scores (S_m), a metric quantifying how well a visual representation preserves state information necessary for control. This suggests that DynaFLIP effectively encodes control-relevant information.
- **Qualitative Analysis (Grad-CAM):** Grad-CAM heatmaps, generated over action prediction, indicated that DynaFLIP consistently attended to manipulated objects and interaction regions. In contrast, baseline methods often focused on visually salient but task-irrelevant areas (e.g., backgrounds).
- **Qualitative Analysis (PCA):** Principal Component Analysis (PCA) visualizations of patch features revealed that DynaFLIP produced more spatially coherent and object-level feature structures compared to baselines.
 - *Conclusion:* These results collectively support the claim that DynaFLIP learns representations that are both dynamics-aware and specifically relevant for robotic control.

**Q2: Do dynamics-aware representations improve downstream policy learning compared to strong baselines?**
- **LIBERO Benchmark (Diffusion Policy):** DynaFLIP was evaluated on five LIBERO suites (LIBERO-90, Goal, Object, Spatial, Long) using Diffusion Policy as the imitation-learning backbone.
 - **Frozen Setting:** DynaFLIP achieved the highest mean success rate among all baselines when both image and language encoders were kept frozen. This indicates that DynaFLIP's pre-trained features can be effectively reused without task-specific encoder adaptation.
 - **LoRA Fine-tuned Setting:** Even with LoRA adapters allowing for task-specific adaptation of both encoders, DynaFLIP maintained the highest mean success rate, confirming its sustained advantage.
 - *Conclusion:* The consistent outperformance is attributed to DynaFLIP's pre-training paradigm, which explicitly aligns transition-centric modalities, encouraging focus on control-relevant regions, unlike baselines primarily trained on static visual data.

**Q3: Can DynaFLIP improve real-world manipulation under both in-distribution and out-of-distribution scenarios?**
- **Real-World Manipulation (UR3 robot, π_0.5 VLA model):** DynaFLIP's frozen pre-trained image encoder was integrated into the π_0.5 VLA model using a lightweight visual-injection design.
 - **In-Distribution Tasks:** DynaFLIP achieved the highest success rates across three in-distribution tasks: "Pick <object> into Sink," "Pour almonds into <object>," and "Unfold Towel." This demonstrated robust transferability across diverse downstream policies (MLP, Diffusion Policy, VLA) without task-specific visual fine-tuning.
 - **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Scenarios:** DynaFLIP's advantage became more prominent under OOD perturbations.
 - **Visual and Spatial Perturbations (e.g., unseen object positions, distractors):** DynaFLIP maintained robustness, whereas baselines like CLIP and SigLIP often failed at precise grasping, indicating DynaFLIP's ability to handle layout changes and distractors due to its focus on control-relevant regions.
 - **Semantic Perturbations (e.g., unseen objects, instructions):** DynaFLIP remained robust, while DINOv2 frequently interacted with objects irrelevant to the instruction, reflecting its lack of direct language grounding. DynaFLIP's incorporation of language during pre-training, aligning visual changes with task-relevant instructions, contributed to its robustness under semantic shifts.
 - *Conclusion:* DynaFLIP significantly improves real-world manipulation performance, particularly under various forms of distribution shift, owing to its dynamics-aware and language-grounded representations.

**Q4: Which design choices in DynaFLIP are most critical to its performance?**
Ablation studies were conducted, focusing on multimodal input, alignment design, optimization-pitfall mitigation, and auxiliary objectives.
- **Multimodal Input:** Removing either 3D flow or language from the pre-training process resulted in a clear drop in performance. This indicates that all three modalities contribute complementary signals essential for dynamics-aware representation learning.
- **Alignment Design:** Replacing the simplex-guided alignment with an anchor-based pairwise loss led to a substantial performance degradation. This suggests that DynaFLIP's benefits are derived from its higher-order multimodal geometry alignment, rather than merely incorporating multiple modalities.
- **Optimization Pitfall Mitigation:**
 - Removing the contrastive framework (i.e., minimizing joint alignment energy without negative tuples) caused the most severe performance drop, confirming its necessity in preventing trivial collapse.
 - Removing the cosine regularizer also reduced performance, supporting its role in mitigating geometric ambiguity and stabilizing positive alignment gradients.
- **Auxiliary Objectives:** Removing either the actor loss (`L_act`) or the temporal contrastive loss (`L_tcn`) degraded performance. The larger drop observed when removing `L_tcn` highlights its importance in capturing trajectory-level temporal structure beyond what `L_align` covers.
 - *Conclusion:* All proposed design choices contribute to DynaFLIP's overall performance, with the simplex-guided alignment, its pitfall mitigation strategies, and the inclusion of all three modalities being particularly critical.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DynaFLIP introduces a shift in the paradigm of robotic perception, advocating for a focus on dynamics-aware visual representations rather than those primarily trained for static scene understanding. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Rethinking Robotics Perception:** The core contribution is the argument and demonstration that robust robot manipulation necessitates visual representations encoding dynamics- and control-relevant structure. This challenges the common practice of reusing general computer vision encoders that lack explicit dynamics awareness. By pushing motion understanding upstream into the perception pipeline, DynaFLIP encourages encoders to learn how the world *changes* under action, rather than just *what is present*.

- **Enhanced Robot Generalization:** The consistent outperformance of DynaFLIP across diverse simulation and real-world manipulation tasks, especially under out-of-distribution scenarios (visual, spatial, and semantic perturbations), indicates a substantial improvement in robot generalization capabilities. This is critical for deploying robots in varied and unpredictable real-world environments.

- **Development of a Reusable Visual Backbone:** DynaFLIP provides a pre-trained visual backbone that effectively transfers across a variety of downstream policies, including lightweight MLPs, diffusion policies, and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, often without requiring extensive task-specific visual fine-tuning. This reusability reduces the burden of data collection and training for new tasks.

- **Leveraging Heterogeneous Data Sources:** The methodology demonstrates an effective way to synthesize image-language-3D flow triplets from heterogeneous human and robot video data. This allows for scaling pre-training with readily available action-free video content, circumventing the limitations of sparse robot-collected datasets.

- **Robust Multimodal Alignment Technique:** The proposed simplex-guided multimodal alignment, combined with a cosine regularizer and a contrastive framework, offers a robust and theoretically grounded approach for aligning multiple modalities. This technique addresses specific optimization pitfalls (geometric ambiguity and trivial collapse) that can arise in higher-order multimodal alignment, contributing to the broader field of multimodal representation learning.

- **Influence on Future Robotics Research:** The findings suggest that future research in robot learning could benefit from prioritizing dynamics and control relevance in visual representation learning. This could lead to the development of more capable and adaptive robotic agents. Future work could include scaling DynaFLIP to even larger datasets and refining 3D flow extraction to focus more precisely on task-relevant motion, further enhancing its impact.
