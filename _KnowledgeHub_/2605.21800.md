# stable-worldmodel: A Platform for Reproducible World Modeling Research and Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> A new open-source platform, stable-worldmodel, standardizes the research and evaluation pipeline for world models, from data collection to comprehensive testing. Its application revealed that contemporary world models exhibit limited zero-shot generalization and robustness, with planning performance degrading significantly even under minor distribution shifts.

## Problem

- World modeling research suffers from fragmentation due to disparate codebases, varying data pipelines, and inconsistent evaluation protocols, hindering reproducibility and fair comparisons.
- Inefficient data loading processes, especially for high-dimensional multimodal data like video, create bottlenecks and underutilize GPUs, slowing down research.
- A lack of standardized benchmarks for assessing generalization capabilities, particularly for out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, prevents rigorous evaluation of model robustness.

## Method

- Introduces `**stable-worldmodel**` (swm), an open-source, modular PyTorch and Gymnasium-based platform that unifies the entire world modeling pipeline.
- Implements a high-performance data layer using Lance for storage, offering fast random access, high compression, and efficient streaming, alongside robust planning solvers and ==world model== baselines.
- Provides a comprehensive evaluation suite with controllable Factors of Variation (FoV) at both simulator and boundary levels, enabling systematic studies of robustness and generalization.

## Results

- The Lance data format achieved approximately 4,815 samples/sec locally and 3,184 samples/sec from S3 for the Push-T environment, outperforming HDF5 and MP4 in loading throughput.
- Baseline world models like LeWM and DINO-WM reproduced reported in-distribution performance on Push-T, achieving **94%** and **92%** success rates respectively.
- Planning success rates for most models dropped sharply even under mild visual perturbations (e.g., a quadratic decay under occlusion) and changes in target anchor position or agent size.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current world models often exhibit limited zero-shot generalization and brittleness, relying on specific visual cues rather than robust underlying dynamics.
> - Raw prediction error magnitude shows a weak correlation with planning success, suggesting it is not a reliable indicator of task performance, especially under distribution shifts.
> - Efficient data management, particularly using columnar storage like Lance, significantly improves data loading throughput, addressing a critical bottleneck in world model training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The concept of using predictive models to guide decision-making has roots in control theory from the 1960s-1970s, where approaches relied on analytical or hand-crafted models. With advancements in deep learning, a new paradigm emerged: "world models," which leverage neural networks to learn predictive models directly from raw data. These models are designed to capture complex real-world phenomena without explicit derivation of dynamics equations. World models are considered a foundational component for developing intelligent agents capable of reasoning, planning, and generalizing beyond their training data.

However, the current landscape of world modeling research is characterized by fragmentation. This fragmentation manifests through disparate codebases, varying data pipelines, and inconsistent evaluation protocols. Such inconsistencies hinder reproducibility, complicate fair comparisons between different approaches, and can lead to the reporting of results that are difficult to verify or trust. Specific bottlenecks identified include the prevalence of fragile, one-off code implementations, inefficient data loading processes for high-dimensional multimodal data (like video), and the absence of standardized benchmarks for assessing generalization capabilities, particularly for out-of-distribution scenarios.

Existing efforts to standardize parts of machine learning research include frameworks for reinforcement learning (RL) such as D4RL, OGBench, Natural RL Environment, Distracting Control Suite, DMC Generalization Benchmark, and DMC-VB. While these benchmarks address aspects like offline RL, goal-conditioned RL, or visual robustness, they often remain tied to specific environment families or primarily focus on model-free approaches. Similarly, platforms like WorldMark and WorldTest aim to standardize evaluation for interactive video and behavior-based models but do not provide a comprehensive training infrastructure. Complementary frameworks in robotics (e.g., LeRobot, RoboHive) or model-free RL (e.g., stable-baselines3, CleanRL) also exist, but the infrastructure specifically for training and evaluating world models, encompassing data management, baseline implementations, planning solvers, and rigorous generalization testing, remains largely unaddressed.

The `stable-worldmodel` (swm) platform aims to bridge this gap by offering a unified, open-source solution that integrates the entire world modeling pipeline from data collection to comprehensive evaluation, thereby addressing the identified challenges of fragmentation and lack of standardization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to establish an open-source, standardized platform named `stable-worldmodel` (swm) to facilitate reproducible research and evaluation in the field of world modeling. This overarching goal is motivated by several challenges observed in current world model development:

1. **Reproducibility and Implementation Fragmentation:** Current research often involves individual laboratories developing their own end-to-end pipelines for data collection, model training, and evaluation. This leads to frequent re-implementation of established algorithms and baselines, such as the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) planner, across different codebases. Such fragmentation can introduce subtle inconsistencies, make fair comparison difficult, and diminish the credibility of reported results. The platform aims to consolidate these implementations into well-tested, common components.

2. **Data Bottlenecks in World Model Learning:** Training world models, especially those operating on high-dimensional sensory inputs like video, requires loading multimodal data (e.g., video frames, actions, proprioception) as contiguous temporal blocks. Existing data storage and loading practices present a trade-off: storing individual frames allows for fast random access but incurs high I/O overhead and storage costs, while compressed video formats reduce disk footprint but severely degrade random access efficiency. This bottleneck can lead to GPU underutilization, slowing down research. The objective is to develop a high-performance data layer that balances these requirements.

3. **Limitations in Evaluating Robust World Models:** The majority of world model research relies on simulator-based evaluation, often under conditions similar to the training distribution. This practice can mask whether a model has learned fundamental environment dynamics or merely exploitable correlations within the training data. Standard benchmarks are insufficient for probing robustness, zero-shot generalization, and understanding how learned dynamics perform under out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios involving changes in appearance, geometry, object properties, or physical parameters. The motivation is to provide a comprehensive evaluation suite that enables systematic assessment of model limitations and generalization capabilities.

By addressing these bottlenecks, swm aims to dramatically reduce research overhead, accelerate progress towards reliable world models, foster fairer comparisons between different approaches, and enable systematic identification of model limitations that are critical for real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The `stable-worldmodel` (swm) platform is designed as an open-source, modular test-bed built on PyTorch and Gymnasium. Its design principles prioritize minimal restrictions on user model architecture and training code while enforcing strong standardization for data collection, evaluation, and control.

**Core Abstractions:**
swm is structured around three primary abstractions:
- **World:** A unified wrapper for vectorized Gymnasium environments, providing interfaces for simulation, dataset recording, visualization, and evaluation. It manages an internal `world.infos` dictionary for consistent access to environment information (RGB frames, states, rewards). It supports controllable interventions on visual, geometric, and physical properties, termed Factors of Variation (FoV).
- **Policy:** A simple interface (e.g., `get_actions(info)`) that maps observations or latent states to actions. This allows for flexible swapping between different policy types, including random, expert, learned policies from RL, or `MPCPolicy` which wraps a world model and planning solver.
- **Solver:** A collection of robust implementations for single-shooting planning algorithms used in Model Predictive Control (MPC). Solvers expect the world model to implement a `get_cost` method and return an optimized finite-horizon action sequence. These solvers are tested for numerical stability and performance.

**System Components:**

- **Data Layer:**
 - **Collection:** Supports both offline and online data collection using random, expert, or pre-trained policies. This enables studying the impact of data composition and iterative data collection-training loops.
 - **Storage:** `Lance` is adopted as the primary storage format due to its columnar, ML-optimized design, offering fast random access, high compression ratios, zero-copy operations, versioning, and seamless streaming from cloud object stores.
 - **Compatibility:** Provides native support and conversion tools for common formats like MP4, HDF5, and LeRobot datasets, ensuring compatibility with existing datasets and facilitating integration of real-robot trajectories. Benchmarks indicate Lance significantly outperforms HDF5 and MP4 in loading throughput, both locally and from S3, while maintaining a reasonable disk storage footprint.

- **Control Layer (Planning Solvers):**
 - swm provides implementations of commonly used planning solvers, pluggable with any trained world model via the `MPCPolicy` interface.
 - **Sampling-based methods:** These are zeroth-order, not requiring differentiability of the world model cost function. They include Predictive Sampling, Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), Improved CEM (iCEM), Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), and Categorical CEM (for discrete actions). These methods iteratively sample, evaluate, and refine populations of candidate action sequences.
 - **Gradient-based methods:** These require a differentiable world model predictor. They include Gradient Descent (GD), Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) for discrete actions via continuous relaxation, GRASP (jointly optimizes action sequence and intermediate virtual states), and Lagrangian (for inequality-constrained objectives).
 - Implemented solvers reproduce planning success rates reported in original papers when paired with their respective world models, indicating the unified interface maintains performance fidelity.

- **Model Layer (Baselines):**
 - swm implements several world model baselines covering two paradigms:
 - **Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL):** Includes Goal-Conditioned Behavioral Cloning (GCBC), Goal-Conditioned Implicit Q-Learning (GCIQL), and Goal-Conditioned Implicit V-Learning (GCIVL). These methods typically use a DINOv2 encoder and directly learn policies or Q-functions.
 - **Latent World Models with Test-Time Planning:** These models typically consist of an encoder and a predictor. Examples include DINO-WM (frozen DINOv2 encoder, learned Transformer predictor), Planning with Latent Dynamics Models (PLDM) (end-to-end JEPA with multi-term regularization), LeWorldModel (LeWM) (end-to-end JEPA with SIGReg regularization), and TD-MPC2 (reward-driven implicit world model with encoder, latent dynamics, reward predictor, Q-function, and stochastic policy prior). These models are wrapped by `MPCPolicy` at test time.

**Benchmarking World Models:**
- **Evaluation Protocols:** Supports online evaluation where tasks are sampled by the environment, and dataset-driven evaluation where initial states and goals are sampled from existing trajectories with a specified temporal offset. The latter allows controlled distribution shift studies.
- **Task Definition:** Goal-conditioned tasks are specified through target observations, where the agent aims to reach a desired state within a budget.
- **Metrics:** Primary metrics include success rate, time-to-goal, and wall-clock latency per planning step.
- **Environment Suite:** Includes diverse environments: Classic Control, MuJoCo, Atari, OGBench, Push-T, Two-Room, and Craftax.
- **Factors of Variation (FoV):**
 - **Simulator-level (Native Environments):** Exposes hierarchical `variation_space` to control visual (colors, lighting, textures), geometric (shapes, sizes), and physical properties (masses, friction, dynamics) directly within the simulator (e.g., Push-T, MuJoCo). These can be fixed, randomly sampled, or systematically varied.
 - **Boundary-level (External Environments):** For environments with inaccessible internals (e.g., Atari ROMs, Craftax), Gymnasium wrappers apply visual perturbations at the observation level (e.g., ChromaKey, Noise, Blur, ColorJitter, Occlusion).
 - These mechanisms enable systematic studies of robustness, domain shift, and zero-shot generalization.

**Tooling:**
The platform provides Hydra-configured scripts for data collection, expert policy training, and baseline training/evaluation. A command-line interface (CLI) allows users to inspect datasets, environments, and manage checkpoints.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using `stable-worldmodel` on the Push-T manipulation benchmark and other environments yielded several findings regarding data efficiency, in-distribution performance, and robustness under distribution shift.

**Data Layer Performance:**
- **Throughput and Storage:** Benchmarks on Push-T (Figure 3) and Two-Room (Figure 8) environments demonstrate that the Lance format achieves the highest data loading throughput. For Push-T, Lance delivers approximately 4,815 samples/sec locally and 3,184 samples/sec from S3, outperforming HDF5 (1,416 local, 9 S3) and MP4 (1,331 local). For Two-Room, Lance maintains superior throughput (5.2k local, 3.4k S3). While MP4 offers the lowest disk usage (e.g., 496 MB for Push-T, 220 MB for Two-Room), Lance provides a balance between high throughput and compression (e.g., 13.31 GB for Push-T, 3.87 GB for Two-Room). This indicates Lance effectively addresses the I/O bottleneck.

**In-distribution Planning Performance:**
- **Baseline Consistency:** Comparisons of state-of-the-art world models (TD-MPC2, GCBC, LeWM, PLDM, DINO-WM) on Push-T (Table 1) show performance values consistent with those reported in their original publications. For instance, LeWM achieved a 94% success rate, and DINO-WM achieved 92%, while TD-MPC2 showed 12%.
- **Offline vs. Online Performance for TD-MPC2:** While TD-MPC2 performed poorly in an offline setting on Push-T, online benchmarking on DeepMind Control Suite tasks demonstrated competitive asymptotic rewards and sample efficiency comparable to a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) baseline (Table 5, Figure 12). This outcome suggests the TD-MPC2 implementation within swm is correct, and its poor offline performance is an algorithmic limitation related to out-of-distribution (OOD) action generation, where its actor quickly drifts outside the training data manifold (Figure 13).

**Brittleness Under Distribution Shift:**
- **Prediction Error vs. Planning Success:** Analyses for LeWM (Figure 4) and PLDM (Figure 10) on Push-T reveal a weak correlation between prediction error (MSE) and planning success. As the evaluation regime shifts from expert training data to held-out expert validation, random-policy trajectories, and finally random-policy trajectories with full variations, prediction error distributions generally flatten and shift toward higher errors (Figure 9). However, the distributions of prediction MSE for successful and failed plans largely overlap even under strong distribution shifts, suggesting that raw prediction error magnitude is not a reliable indicator of planning success, and OOD inputs are a primary driver of failures.
- **Targeted Visual Perturbations (FoVs):**
 - **General Sensitivity:** Planning success rates for most models drop sharply even under mild visual perturbations introduced via swm's factors-of-variation interface (Table 5b).
 - **Occlusion:** Introducing visual distractor squares leads to a quadratic decay in planning success rate across baselines on Push-T (Figure 5a), indicating limited tolerance to visual occlusions.
 - **Individual FoV Impact:** A detailed breakdown of individual factors of variation (Table 4) shows varying degrees of sensitivity. For example, altering the target anchor's position or the agent's size drastically reduces planning success across all baselines. PLDM showed robustness to agent shape variations but suffered significantly from agent color perturbations.
 - **Background Color Intensity:** A continuous view of LeWM's planning success rate across the chromatic wheel (Figure 11) demonstrated that performance remains high near the original white background and along the green axis (matching the target anchor's color). Success degrades sharply as the background shifts toward red, blue, or purple, suggesting models may latch onto specific background-foreground color contrasts rather than learning underlying task geometry.

In summary, the experiments performed using swm indicate that current world models exhibit limited zero-shot generalization and robustness. Even modest shifts outside the training distribution can lead to substantial degradation in planning performance, highlighting that models often rely on specific visual cues or correlations present in the training data rather than robust representations of underlying dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of `stable-worldmodel` (swm) represents a notable contribution to the machine learning community, particularly in the domain of reinforcement learning and autonomous agents. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Standardization:** By providing a unified, open-source platform with well-tested implementations of world model baselines, planning solvers, and standardized evaluation protocols, swm directly addresses the fragmentation and reproducibility crisis in world modeling research. This standardization promotes fair comparisons between different algorithms and fosters a more trustworthy research environment, reducing the common issue of inconsistent results stemming from disparate codebases.

2. **Acceleration of Research Progress:** The platform significantly reduces research overhead by centralizing data management, model training utilities, and evaluation infrastructure. The high-performance Lance-based data layer specifically alleviates I/O bottlenecks, which can be a major impediment for training large world models on multimodal data. This efficiency allows researchers to iterate faster on new algorithmic ideas, dedicating more time to innovation rather than re-implementing foundational components.

3. **Facilitation of Robustness and Generalization Studies:** A core strength of swm is its comprehensive suite of environments augmented with controllable Factors of Variation (FoV). These FoVs, ranging from visual and geometric to physical perturbations, enable systematic and rigorous testing of world models under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions. This capability is crucial for identifying the limitations of current models, probing their generalization capabilities, and driving the development of more robust and reliable world models essential for real-world deployment. The case studies presented in the paper highlight how swm transforms ad-hoc analyses into reproducible experiments, facilitating the diagnosis of model failures.

4. **Identification of Current Limitations:** The experimental findings, particularly the observed brittleness of world models under even mild distribution shifts and the poor correlation between prediction error and planning success, underscore significant open challenges in the field. These results provide concrete evidence that current world models are far from achieving robust zero-shot generalization. This direct identification of limitations can guide future research towards improving visual and physical robustness, as well as developing models capable of long-horizon consistency.

5. **Enabling Future Research Directions:** The architecture and features of swm position it as a foundational tool for exploring several critical research avenues:
 - **Zero-Shot World Models:** The platform's FoV system makes studying and overcoming the brittleness under distribution shifts a tractable research agenda.
 - **Long-Horizon Planning:** By providing diverse planning solvers and controllable temporal offsets for goal sampling, swm facilitates stress-testing models beyond short-horizon, in-distribution scenarios.
 - **Scaling Laws for World Models:** The high-throughput data layer and standardized evaluation enable systematic investigation of how scaling data, parameters, and compute impacts world model generalization, akin to the scaling laws observed in large language models.
 - **Sim-to-Real Transfer and Online Learning:** Future extensions aim to support these complex areas, broadening the applicability of the platform beyond simulation.

Overall, swm aims to become a standard reference library for the world modeling community, fostering collaborative development and accelerating the progression towards robust, trustworthy, and generalizable AI agents.
