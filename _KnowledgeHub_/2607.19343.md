# Masked Visual Actions for Unified World Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford University and collaborators developed Masked Visual Actions (MVA), a pixel-space control interface that enables a single pre-trained video model to function as a unified world model for both forward and inverse dynamics in robot manipulation. This approach achieved superior visual fidelity and cross-embodiment generalization in controllable video generation, improved planning success rates by up to 26% on Robocasa tasks, and demonstrated zero-shot inverse modeling with 90% success on a complex pick-and-place task.

## Problem

- Existing action representations for large-scale video models are often sparse, embodiment-specific, or visually misaligned, limiting their ability to leverage learned visual priors and generalize across different robots.
- Traditional robotic world models typically require separate architectures or distinct training for forward (predicting scene changes from actions) and inverse (inferring actions for desired outcomes) dynamics.
- Adapting large pre-trained video models for specific robotic tasks is often computationally expensive and requires significant robot interaction data.

## Method

- Introduced **Masked Visual Actions (MVA)**, a ==pixel-space control interface== that represents robot actions as partially revealed spatiotemporal patterns in a video, finetuning a pre-trained video model (==Wan-Fun-Control 2.2 14B==) with ==LoRA==.
- Unified forward and inverse modeling capabilities within a single video model by conceptually partitioning entities into 'active' (robot) and 'passive' (object) and conditioning the model through ==spatial masking==.
- Constructed a masked modeling dataset using both ==segmentation-based== (SegmentAnything on DROID) and ==rendering-based== methods (robot meshes with known calibration) to provide diverse masked conditioning.

## Results

- MVA achieved superior visual fidelity and controllability in video generation, with an LPIPS score of **0.0945** on the DROID dataset and strong generalization to unseen bimanual robots (LPIPS **0.123** on BEHAVIOR-1K).
- Model-based planning using MVA improved task success rates on Robocasa by up to **+26%** (e.g., 'Open drawer' task) by allowing a Vision-Language Model to evaluate simulated rollouts.
- Demonstrated zero-shot inverse modeling capabilities, with MVA synthesizing robot actions from desired object motions, achieving a **90%** success rate on the RoboCasa COFFEESERVEMUG task when combined with a learned inverse dynamics model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Expressing actions as masked visual trajectories in pixel space directly aligns with the visual priors of video models, enabling enhanced generalization and embodiment agnosticism.
> - A single video model, trained on masked video completion without explicit agency, can perform zero-shot inverse dynamics modeling by simply varying which entities are masked as conditioning inputs.
> - Lightweight finetuning (LoRA) of a pre-trained video model with masked visual actions can efficiently inject robust world modeling capabilities for robot interaction with limited data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The development of robotic world models capable of predicting the effects of actions and inferring actions needed to achieve desired outcomes is a central challenge in sensorimotor control. Traditional approaches often describe skilled behavior through a coupling of forward models (anticipating sensory consequences of movement) and inverse models (recovering movement for a desired state). Recent advancements in large-scale video models, trained on extensive observational data, offer a promising foundation for such robotic world modeling by capturing broad priors over motion, contact, and interaction dynamics.

However, existing video models largely function as passive observers. Integrating actions into these models typically involves conditioning on signals such as text, motion tracks, forces, keypoints, or low-level motor commands. A limitation of these existing action representations is their frequent sparsity, embodiment-specificity, or misalignment with the visual space in which pre-trained video models acquire their interaction priors. This mismatch can hinder the models' ability to generalize across different robotic embodiments or to leverage their inherent visual understanding effectively.

This work introduces "Masked Visual Actions" (MVA) to address this gap by proposing an action representation expressed directly within the visual space. It draws inspiration from controllable video generation techniques that add control signals to video models to transform them into simulators, and from visual prompting methods (e.g., inpainting, conditional inference) that leverage masked regions for generic task parameterization. While previous pixel-grounded action conditioning methods often treat the robot as the active entity during training and operate in a forward-only manner, or unify modalities by masking channels (action vector vs. video), MVA distinguishes itself by implementing spatial masking on the pixel canvas. This spatial masking enables the same model to serve as both a forward and inverse dynamics model by varying which entities are revealed, thus bridging the embodiment gap and offering a unified predictive framework without requiring separate architectures or extensive re-training for different reasoning directions (e.g., dedicated inverse dynamics model heads or flow predictors). This approach aims to enhance generalization, visual fidelity, and controllability in robot manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a unified predictive framework for robotic world modeling that can seamlessly support both forward and inverse reasoning within a single model. This ambition stems from the fundamental requirement in purposeful interaction to connect an agent's actions to their worldly effects, and conversely, to determine the actions necessary to achieve a desired state.

The motivation for introducing Masked Visual Actions (MVA) arises from several specific challenges identified in the current state of video models and robotic control:

1. **Bridging the Gap between Visual Priors and Action Representations:** Large-scale video models accumulate rich priors about visual world dynamics. However, existing methods for communicating actions to these models (e.g., low-dimensional action commands, text, sparse keypoints) are often misaligned with the visual space where these priors are learned. This misalignment limits the models' ability to leverage their inherent visual understanding for active intervention. The objective is to create an action representation that is "native" to video and visually aligned.
2. **Developing a Unified Forward and Inverse Model:** Current robotic world models typically require separate architectures or distinct training paradigms for forward dynamics (predicting scene response given actions) and inverse modeling (recovering actions for a desired outcome). The research aims to unify these capabilities within a single video model, conceptualizing active and passive roles as different queries to the same underlying interaction prior, rather than properties of separate architectures.
3. **Enhancing Generalization and Embodiment Agnosticism:** Many existing action representations are embodiment-specific, meaning they do not transfer well across different robot types or configurations. A key objective is to develop a control interface that is pixel-aligned and embodiment-agnostic, allowing a single model to interact with diverse robotic platforms and scene entities without re-training or significant adaptation.
4. **Improving Efficiency in Model Adaptation:** Adapting large pre-trained video models for specific robotic tasks can be computationally expensive. The research aims for an efficient adaptation strategy that can inject action capabilities into a pre-trained model with limited robot interaction data, thereby lowering the barrier to entry for developing robotic systems.
5. **Validation in Downstream Robotic Applications:** The ultimate motivation is to demonstrate the practical utility of this unified world model in real-world robot manipulation tasks. Specific objectives include validating its efficacy in:
 - **Policy Evaluation:** Assessing the correlation between imagined rollouts in the model and real-world execution outcomes.
 - **Model-Based Planning:** Using the model to simulate and rank candidate future action trajectories to improve decision-making.
 - **Inverse Modeling/Action Extraction:** Synthesizing robot motion from desired object motion, thereby enabling the generation of executable robot behaviors.

In summary, the core motivation is to move video models from passive observation to active intervention by introducing a visually grounded, unified, generalizable, and efficient action interface that can support a broad spectrum of robotic control and planning problems.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The methodology introduces Masked Visual Actions (MVA) as a pixel-space control interface for pre-trained video models, enabling them to act as unified world models for robot manipulation.

**Core Concept: Action as Masked Visual Trajectories**
The central idea is to recast action as a visual primitive by finetuning a pre-trained video model to ingest actions as partially revealed spatiotemporal patterns in pixel space. This involves providing a masked trajectory of an entity within the scene to the model.
The video model, which typically learns the distribution `p(V)` over videos `V`, is conceptualized as capturing the joint distribution over all entities `p(e_1, e_2, ..., e_n)`. Conditioning is achieved by providing a binary mask `M` that indicates revealed pixels (`M=1`) versus predicted pixels (`M=0`). For a chosen set of conditioning entities `S`, the mask is the union of their pixel regions (`M(S) = U_i∈S e_i`), and the model predicts `p(V | M ⊙ V, I_0)`, where `I_0` is the initial reference image.

**Forward and Inverse Modeling through Masking:**
The approach partitions entities into "active" (e.g., robot arm) and "passive" (e.g., manipulated object). This distinction facilitates two primary modes of operation:

1. **Forward Model:** By setting `S = A` (conditioning on active entities), the model predicts the response of passive entities to the robot's motion (`p({e_i} i∈P | {e_j} j∈A, I_0)`). This simulates how the scene evolves given specified robot actions.
2. **Inverse Model:** By setting `S = P` (conditioning on passive entities), the model recovers the active entity's behavior consistent with a desired outcome (`p({e_i} i∈A | {e_j} j∈P, I_0)`). The user specifies desired object motion, and the model synthesizes the corresponding robot motion.

Crucially, the model is trained on masked video completion without explicit notions of agency. It generalizes to inverse queries in a zero-shot manner, even when trained primarily on active robotic entity masks.

**Dataset Construction:**
A masked modeling dataset is constructed using both real-world videos from DROID and simulation data from Robocasa, incorporating both successful and failed trajectories. Two methods are used to generate masked conditioning:

1. **Segmentation-based Dataset:** The SegmentAnything model, prompted with "A robotic arm," segments the robot from DROID videos. This method is general and helps the model learn to inpaint missing regions and model joint distributions. However, it faces challenges in providing precise masks at inference and potential information leakage from occluded regions.
2. **Rendering-based Dataset:** To enable user-provided arbitrary action trajectories at inference, robot meshes are aligned with input videos using recorded robot states and camera calibration (DROID, Robocasa). Only the robot is rendered (translucent, with bright red gripper fingers to highlight actions) to generate masked conditioning. This approach requires known camera calibration and is limited to robot rendering. The combination of both approaches is considered complementary.

**Model Implementation and Training:**
- **Base Model:** Wan-Fun-Control 2.2 14B, a pre-trained video model, is used as the backbone.
- **Conditioning Mechanism:** The masked conditioning video is encoded using the same autoencoder as the video model and concatenated with the input. Missing (masked) regions are set to a uniform gray background.
- **Finetuning:** Instead of finetuning the entire model, LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) with a rank of 256 is employed for efficient adaptation.
- **Resources:** Training is performed on 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs with a batch size of 4 for approximately 10,000 steps over 4 days.
- **Prompts:** A high-level text prompt is included, but the model primarily relies on the visual conditioning signal.

**Downstream Robotic Applications:**
The framework is evaluated across three applications in simulation (Robocasa) and the real world:

1. **Controllable Video Generation:** Assesses visual fidelity, controllability, and generalization to diverse and unseen robotic embodiments (e.g., bimanual robots from BEHAVIOR-1K). Baselines include Ctrl-World (raw robot actions), Wan-Move (trajectory conditioning), and Wan I2V (image-to-video). Ablation studies compare MVA against sparse conditioning signals like end-effector pose visualization and robot skeleton visualization.
2. **Model-Based Planning:** A stochastic policy (Diffusion Policy) generates multiple action candidates. These candidates are simulated using the MVA-finetuned video model. A Vision-Language Model (VLM, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview) evaluates the simulated rollouts for task success, interaction fidelity, and physical realism. The best action sequence is then selected for execution (Best-of-N planning).
3. **Policy Evaluation:** The model evaluates the success rate of a policy by simulating its rollouts and comparing the video model's imagined outcomes to ground-truth environment executions. Both simulation and real-world policy rollouts are assessed for task progress and realism.
4. **Action Extraction (Inverse Modeling):** Given a desired object motion as a masked visual action, the video model synthesizes a video of a robot achieving that outcome. A separate, learned inverse dynamics model then extracts low-level executable action sequences from the synthesized robot video. This process leverages the zero-shot inverse generalization capabilities of the MVA model.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents findings validating Masked Visual Actions (MVA) across controllable video generation and various robotic applications, demonstrating strong performance, generalization, and practical utility.

#### 4.1. Controllable Video Generation

- **Visual Fidelity and Controllability:** The MVA model is shown to accurately follow robot actions and maintain scene integrity, outperforming reference video models (Wan-Move and image-to-video models) that often collapse or transform the input scene.
- **Baseline Comparison (Table 1, Figure 6):**
 - On the DROID dataset (within the training domain), MVA achieves superior performance compared to Ctrl-World (a State-of-the-Art method using raw robot actions), Wan-Move (trajectory-conditioned), and generic image-to-video models across metrics like LPIPS (0.0945 vs. 0.362 for Ctrl-World), SSIM (0.887 vs. 0.708), and PSNR (23.74 vs. 18.15).
 - On the BEHAVIOR-1K dataset (an unseen bimanual embodiment), MVA maintains its performance lead, demonstrating robust generalization (LPIPS 0.123, SSIM 0.843, PSNR 22.90) while Ctrl-World produces static or corrupted videos (Figure 5), indicating a lack of generalization for methods conditioned on raw, embodiment-specific action states.
- **Comparison of Visual Action Conditioning (Table 2, Figure 7):**
 - Within the training domain (DROID), sparse visual conditioning signals (end-effector visualization, robot skeleton visualization) perform similarly to MVA.
 - However, when generalizing to unseen contexts, such as a real-world robot with a custom 3D-printed end-effector or the bimanual R1 Pro robot in BEHAVIOR-1K, MVA significantly outperforms sparse conditioning. Models trained with skeleton or end-effector conditioning tend to hallucinate or transform the robot to match the training data, or completely collapse, whereas MVA generalizes gracefully, simulating physical interaction with the novel embodiment. For instance, on real-world data with an unseen gripper, MVA achieves LPIPS 0.148, compared to 0.183 for end-effector visualization and 0.169 for skeleton visualization.

#### 4.2. Robotics Applications

- **Planning (Figure 8):**
 - Using MVA for model-based planning (Best-of-N with a Diffusion Policy and VLM evaluation) consistently improves task success rates across diverse Robocasa tasks (e.g., "Close microwave" sees a +24% increase, "Open drawer" +26%).
 - The success rate positively correlates with the number of action samples (`N`) evaluated, indicating the model's ability to simulate counterfactuals effectively and aid in selecting better action sequences.
- **Policy Evaluation (Figures 9 & 10):**
 - In Robocasa, MVA's simulated rollouts exhibit a strong correlation (r=0.982) with ground-truth success rates across tasks, demonstrating its utility as a reliable proxy for real-world execution. The model shows a slight positive bias towards task progress in its imagination.
 - For real-world policy evaluation (e.g., "bagging orange," "stacking blocks"), the per-trial progress distribution in the video model closely matches the ground-truth distribution, also with a positive bias toward task progress.
- **Action Extraction (Inverse Modeling) (Figure 11):**
 - Without task-specific inverse-modeling finetuning, the MVA model demonstrates zero-shot generalization to inverse problems. When prompted with desired object motion, it synthesizes robot behavior that achieves the goal.
 - Combining MVA with a learned inverse dynamics model for action extraction achieves a 90% success rate on the COFFEESERVEMUG task in RoboCasa. This performance is competitive with, and in this specific instance, surpasses, dedicated imitation learning baselines such as Diffusion Policy (50%), ACT (80%), and SmolVLA (85%). This indicates the model's ability to extract executable robot actions from its rich interaction priors.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that Masked Visual Actions provide an effective, generalizable, and efficient approach for unified robot world modeling, offering substantial improvements in visual fidelity, cross-embodiment generalization, and practical utility for various robot manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The introduction of Masked Visual Actions (MVA) represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic world modeling and video-based generative AI. Its primary contributions and potential impacts are multifaceted:

1. **Unified World Modeling Framework:** MVA establishes a unified predictive framework where a single video model can seamlessly perform both forward dynamics modeling (predicting scene evolution from robot actions) and inverse modeling (inferring robot actions from desired object motions). This unification, achieved through a simple masking interface, is a notable departure from approaches requiring separate models or extensive retraining for different reasoning directions. It treats active and passive roles as flexible queries to a shared interaction prior, enhancing the versatility of video models.
2. **Visually Aligned and Embodiment-Agnostic Action Representation:** By expressing actions as pixel-space masked trajectories, MVA directly aligns the action representation with the visual priors learned by large-scale video models. This dense, pixel-aligned, and embodiment-agnostic interface addresses a critical limitation of previous sparse or embodiment-specific action signals, which often hindered generalization. This enables the model to effectively interact with diverse robotic platforms and novel end-effectors without explicit retraining for each new embodiment.
3. **Efficiency in Adaptation:** The method demonstrates that robust world modeling capabilities can be efficiently injected into a pre-trained video model using lightweight LoRA finetuning on a relatively small amount of data (as few as 15 hours). This efficiency is crucial for rapid prototyping and deployment in robotics, where large-scale, embodiment-specific data collection is often costly and time-consuming.
4. **Enhanced Performance and Generalization:** MVA achieves superior visual fidelity and controllability in video generation compared to prior work. More importantly, its ability to generalize gracefully to unseen robotic embodiments and custom grippers, where other methods often fail or hallucinate, marks a significant step towards more adaptable and robust robotic systems.
5. **Practical Utility in Robotic Applications:** The validation across policy evaluation, model-based planning, and inverse modeling showcases the immediate practical applicability of MVA.
 - **Policy Evaluation:** Providing a highly correlated proxy for real-world task success, it enables efficient and safe evaluation of robotic policies in imagination.
 - **Model-Based Planning:** By simulating counterfactuals and allowing for selection of optimal action sequences, it can significantly improve robot decision-making and task success rates.
 - **Inverse Modeling and Action Extraction:** The zero-shot generalization to inverse problems allows the model to synthesize robot behaviors from desired outcomes, facilitating the generation of executable actions for complex manipulation tasks, even outperforming some dedicated imitation learning methods.
6. **Lowering Barriers to Robot Learning:** By offering a more accessible and efficient way to simulate, plan for, and evaluate robotic interactions, this work has the potential to lower the cost and technical expertise required for developing and deploying robotic systems. This could accelerate research and application in robot learning across various domains.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**
The model, like other generative models, primarily learns correlations rather than causal relationships, which remains an open research area. Its performance is inherently limited by the capabilities (e.g., inference speed, expressiveness) of the underlying base video model.

**Societal and Broader Impact:**
The research contributes to making robot learning more accessible and scalable, potentially leading to safer and more capable autonomous systems in various industries and daily life. However, these advanced capabilities also carry a responsibility for careful deployment. The ability to simulate and generate complex robotic behaviors could, if misused, enable unsafe or unauthorized actions, highlighting the importance of ethical considerations and responsible development practices in future research and application.
