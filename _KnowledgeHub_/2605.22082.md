# CoRMA: Contrastive RMA for Contact-Rich Meta-Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> CoRMA adapts Rapid Motor Adaptation to force-dominant assembly by replacing raw simulator-parameter context with a compact 6D semantic contact latent covering onset, lateral engagement, guided transition, direction, and jamming. A causal Transformer adapter infers this latent online from force, proprioceptive, and action history via semantic regression plus a force-regime contrastive loss. On real hardware it retains higher verified success than FORGE across three assembly tasks.

## Problem

- Contact-rich assembly policies are trained task-specifically, so new parts, fixtures, or objectives demand fresh training or real calibration despite shared contact mechanisms.
- Standard privileged-adaptation frameworks target raw simulator parameters or visual object cues, which say little about whether interaction is free, engaging, sliding, or jammed.
- Strong simulation success does not predict hardware performance; force-guided baselines reach near-perfect sim rates yet collapse under real pose noise and contact mismatch.

## Method

- ==Privileged Z==: a simulator-only 6D semantic contact context (onset, lateral engagement, guided transition, direction along two axes, jam tendency) augmenting teacher observations during ==PPO== training.
- A causal ==Transformer== adapter with a learned readout token encodes force, proprioceptive, and action history into two heads: semantic regression of the 6D context and an auxiliary contrastive embedding.
- ==Force-regime InfoNCE== groups histories by coarse regimes (free, first contact, guided slide, jam) across task identity; at deployment the frozen adapter's prediction replaces oracle context.

## Results

- Real verified success beat the force-guided baseline on all three tasks: PegInsert **50.0%** versus **12.5%**, GearMesh **65.0%** versus **25.0%**, NutThread **59.3%** versus **0%**.
- Sim-to-real degradation shrank markedly, from **-86.25** to **-10.00** points on PegInsert and **-75.00** to **-27.50** on GearMesh, despite lower or comparable simulation success.
- Ablations show the causal Transformer dominates: validation R-squared **0.8792** versus **0.4336** for a convolutional encoder; the contrastive term adds a smaller gain over **0.8688**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High simulation success is a poor proxy for deployment; a policy weaker in simulation can transfer far better when conditioned on inferred interaction semantics.
> - Choosing a semantically meaningful privileged target beats regressing raw simulator parameters, giving related tasks a shared contact vocabulary that supports reuse.
> - Contrastive objectives work best as weak regularizers over a supervised context head; large weights degrade numerical fidelity of the deployed latent.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic assembly, particularly tasks involving sustained physical contact, represents a persistent challenge in robotics. These "contact-rich" tasks, such as inserting a peg into a hole, meshing gears, or threading a nut, demand precise alignment, meticulous regulation of contact forces, and the ability to recover from intermittent failures over extended operational periods. Unlike free-space manipulation, where robots operate without direct physical interaction, contact-rich scenarios are highly sensitive to minor discrepancies in geometry, friction, compliance, sensing, or actuation. Such mismatches can result in critical failures like jamming, unstable transitions, or irreversible damage.

Existing robotic systems for contact-rich assembly often fall into several categories, each with limitations that this work aims to address:

- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Systems:** Frameworks like FORGE have demonstrated that RL can generate robust contact-rich behaviors under uncertainty. However, these policies are typically trained and tuned for specific tasks, parts, or fixtures. Adapting to new scenarios often necessitates extensive retraining or real-world calibration, even when the underlying physical contact mechanisms are similar across tasks.
- **Demonstration-Heavy Pipelines:** Many successful manipulation systems rely on human demonstrations, utilizing techniques such as behavior cloning, imitation learning, or residual sim-to-real fine-tuning. These methods are data-intensive and can be costly to scale or apply to novel scenarios without new demonstrations.
- **Classical Adaptive Control:** Traditional robotics has employed adaptive control and disturbance compensation to manage manipulation uncertainty, primarily for tracking and stabilization. While effective for their intended purposes, these approaches are generally not designed to learn reusable, cross-task contact representations, and extending them to new task variants can be resource-intensive due to requirements for repeated data collection or calibration.

The paper situates CoRMA within the meta-adaptation paradigm, which proposes that instead of learning each assembly policy in isolation, robots should infer an interaction context online and reuse this context across related tasks. This approach seeks to overcome the limitations of task-specific training and data-intensive methods by enabling faster adaptation to variations. The Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) framework serves as a template, where a privileged teacher policy trained in simulation guides the learning of an adapter that predicts contextual information from deployable sensor history. However, standard RMA formulations often rely on visual or object-centric cues. CoRMA distinguishes itself by modifying RMA to specifically leverage force, proprioception, and action history for force-dominant assembly, focusing on interpreting the *meaning* of contact forces rather than just object location. This addresses a gap in robust, force-aware, and adaptable sim-to-real transfer for complex manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a context-based meta-adaptation framework, CoRMA, specifically tailored for force-dominant, contact-rich robotic assembly. This framework aims to enhance sim-to-real transfer reliability and enable online adaptation without relying on human demonstrations, privileged (simulator-only) inputs during deployment, or test-time gradient updates.

The detailed objectives and motivations are:

- **Addressing Sim-to-Real Degradation and Task Specificity:** Contact-rich assembly policies trained purely in simulation often exhibit significant performance degradation when deployed on real hardware. This is due to discrepancies between simulation and reality regarding contact geometry, friction, compliance, and sensor/actuator dynamics. Existing RL methods, while robust in simulation, frequently require extensive, task-specific training or calibration for real-world application. CoRMA aims to mitigate this by learning a representation that is more transferable across the sim-to-real boundary and reusable across tasks.
- **Enabling Online, Within-Episode Adaptation:** The framework seeks to allow robots to interpret and adapt to the current contact interaction state dynamically during an assembly episode. This "meta-adaptation" ability is crucial for handling unforeseen variations or accumulating errors without needing to restart the task or undergo offline retraining. The motivation is to achieve this adaptation using only deployable sensor data (force, proprioception, and actions) rather than privileged simulator information or demonstrations.
- **Developing a Semantic Contact Context:** Instead of adapting based on raw simulator parameters, which may be too general or ambiguous for contact interpretation, a key motivation is to define a compact, semantically meaningful representation of the contact state. This "Privileged Z" is hypothesized to capture essential contact characteristics (e.g., contact onset, lateral engagement, jamming) that are critical for control across various assembly tasks. This semantic context is intended to serve as a shared vocabulary for related assembly operations.
- **Leveraging Force-Dominant Cues:** Many manipulation-oriented adaptation systems utilize visual or object-centric information. For contact-rich assembly, however, once contact is established, the critical information shifts from visual object location to the nature of the physical interaction. Therefore, a core objective is to develop an adaptation mechanism that primarily processes force, proprioceptive, and action histories, as these directly reflect the contact state.
- **Structuring Representations with Contrastive Learning:** Raw force histories can be ambiguous when only pointwise regression is used. The motivation here is to enhance the adapter's ability to interpret these histories by structuring its internal representation. This is achieved through a contrastive objective that groups histories based on coarse semantic contact regimes (e.g., free motion, first contact, guided sliding, jamming), thereby providing a more robust understanding of the interaction's meaning, irrespective of minor trajectory variations.
- **Maintaining a Non-Cheating Deployment Interface:** Following the RMA principle, the system is designed such that privileged simulator-only information is exclusively used during training to supervise the learning process. At deployment, the robot relies solely on its onboard sensors to infer the necessary context, ensuring a "non-cheating" real-world application. This maintains a clear separation between training-time supervision and deployment-time autonomy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CoRMA formulates contact-rich assembly as a partially observable context-adaptation problem, implemented through a three-stage pipeline (Figure 1). The core idea is to train an adapter that infers a compact semantic contact context from deployable history and injects this context into a policy, thereby enabling online adaptation.

#### 3.1. Semantic Contact Context (Privileged Z)

Instead of directly adapting to raw simulator parameters, CoRMA defines a compact 6-dimensional simulator-only semantic latent variable, `Privileged Z` (z_t). This vector comprises:
- `z_onset`: Contact onset.
- `z_lateral`: Lateral engagement.
- `z_guided`: Guided contact transition.
- `z_dir-x`: Contact direction along the x-axis.
- `z_dir-y`: Contact direction along the y-axis.
- `z_jam`: Jamming or stick-slip tendency.
These scores are computed from simulator contact and force dynamics and are used exclusively during the training phase. The intention is for force to serve as deployable evidence, while `z_t` represents the semantic interpretation of that evidence, providing a shared contact vocabulary across different assembly tasks (PegInsert, GearMesh, NutThread).

#### 3.2. Privileged Teacher Rollouts (Stage 1)

For each target assembly task (PegInsert, GearMesh, NutThread), a privileged teacher policy (π_i) is trained within the Isaac Lab FORGE environment using RL-Games PPO. The key aspect of this training is that the observations are augmented with the simulator-only `Privileged Z` (`o_t`, `z_t`). The teacher policy learns to perform the task effectively when provided with this ground-truth semantic contact information. Its primary role is to generate diverse, contact-rich trajectories in simulation, which then provide the supervised semantic-context targets and deployable history data for training the adapter in the subsequent stage. These teacher rollouts are processed into fixed-length history-window samples `D = {(o_{t−H+1:t}, a_{t−H+1:t}, z_t, c_t)}`, where `H` is the history length and `c_t` is a coarse force-regime label. This sampling ensures the adapter is exposed to various phases of interaction, including approach, first contact, guided motion, and failure/jamming.

#### 3.3. CoRMA Adapter and Contrastive Objective (Stage 2)

The CoRMA adapter (Figure 2) is trained to infer `ˆz_t` (the predicted semantic context) from deployable history (`o_{t−H+1:t}`, `a_{t−H+1:t}`). The adapter does not use camera input; its evidence for adaptation comes from force, proprioception, and previous actions.

- **Causal Transformer Encoder:** A causal Transformer architecture with a learned readout token processes the history sequence (`o_{t−H+1:t}`, `a_{t−H+1:t}`) to produce a history embedding `h_t`. The causal nature ensures that the prediction at time `t` only depends on information available up to `t`.
- **Dual-Head Output:** The readout representation `h_t` is passed to two distinct heads:
 - **Semantic Head (`g_sem`):** This head directly predicts the 6D semantic context `ˆz_t = g_sem(h_t)`. It is trained using an L2 regression loss (`L_sem = ||ˆz_t − z_t||^2_2`) against the oracle `Privileged Z` from the teacher rollouts. All six dimensions of `Privileged Z` are active regression targets.
 - **Auxiliary Contrastive Head (`g_nce`):** This head produces an auxiliary embedding `u_t = g_nce(h_t)`. This embedding is not used by the policy but is trained with a force-regime InfoNCE objective to structure the representation space.
- **Force-Regime Contrastive Objective (`L_nce`):** This objective is designed to teach the adapter that histories from different tasks can share similar contact meanings. It uses weak semantic regimes derived from deployable force evidence as labels: `free`, `first_contact`, `guided_slide`, and `jam`. For an anchor history, positive pairs are other histories sharing the same force-regime label, while negative pairs have different labels, irrespective of the specific task. The `InfoNCE` loss (`L_nce`) encourages embeddings of similar regimes to cluster and different regimes to separate.
- **Total Adapter Loss:** The overall objective for Stage 2 is a weighted sum: `L_adapter = L_sem + λ_nce L_nce`. The contrastive term (`L_nce`) acts as a semantic regularizer, complementing the primary supervised regression for `Privileged Z`.

#### 3.4. Latent Injection for Deployment (Stage 3)

During the fine-tuning and real-world deployment phases, the oracle `Privileged Z` is no longer available. Instead, the trained CoRMA adapter (now frozen) predicts `ˆz_t` online from the robot's current and historical deployable observations (`o_t`, `a_t`). This predicted context `ˆz_t` is then injected directly into the policy as part of its observation space (`a_t ~ π(a_t | o_t, ˆz_t)`). This approach adheres to the RMA non-cheating principle: simulator-derived privileged information is used only for supervision during training, while the deployed system relies solely on onboard sensor data. The same latent-injection interface is used across all evaluated tasks.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Simulator:** Isaac Lab / Isaac Sim 5.0, utilizing the FORGE direct RL environment.
- **Robot:** Real Marvin arm (7-DoF). Cartesian policy targets are converted to joint commands using TRAC-IK.
- **Tasks:** PegInsert, GearMesh, and NutThread, all sharing a common observation and action interface, but with task-specific geometries, reset distributions, rewards, and success conditions.
- **Real-world noise:** Approximately 3mm target-position noise is injected to simulate camera-based localization errors, evaluating adaptation under controlled pose perturbations.
- **Baselines:** FORGE policies trained under the same task family and deployed on the Marvin arm, but without `Privileged Z` or the CoRMA adapter.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CoRMA focused on its performance in simulation and on a real Marvin arm across three contact-rich assembly tasks: PegInsert, GearMesh, and NutThread.

#### 4.1. Simulation and Real-Robot Success Rates (Table 1)

The results demonstrate a notable divergence between simulation success and real-world deployment performance, particularly for the baseline.
- **FORGE Baseline Performance:**
 - Achieved high simulation success rates: 98.75% for PegInsert and 100.00% for GearMesh. NutThread's simulation success was 67.50%.
 - Experienced substantial degradation during real-robot deployment: PegInsert dropped to 12.5% (3/24 successes), GearMesh to 25.0% (5/20 successes). For NutThread, FORGE recorded 0% verified real success (0/20 trials).
 - The sim-to-real degradation gap for FORGE was -86.25% for PegInsert, -75.00% for GearMesh, and -67.50% for NutThread.
- **CoRMA Performance:**
 - Showed varying simulation success: 60.00% for PegInsert, 92.50% for GearMesh, and 91.25% for NutThread. CoRMA's simulation performance was sometimes lower than FORGE (e.g., PegInsert) but sometimes higher (NutThread).
 - Maintained higher verified real success compared to FORGE under the same hardware interface: 50.0% (11/22 successes) for PegInsert and 65.0% (13/20 successes) for GearMesh. For NutThread, CoRMA achieved 59.3% (16/27 successes).
 - Exhibited a smaller sim-to-real degradation gap: -10.00% for PegInsert, -27.50% for GearMesh, and -31.95% for NutThread. This indicates that CoRMA's real-world performance was closer to its simulation performance than FORGE's.

These results suggest that while high simulation success does not guarantee real-world performance, CoRMA's approach, incorporating semantic contact inference, leads to a smaller sim-to-real degradation.

#### 4.2. Adapter and Representation Analysis (Table 2, Appendix D)

The analysis of the Stage 2 adapter's performance provides insight into the contributions of its architectural components and objectives.
- **Causal Transformer's Contribution:** The causal Transformer encoder was identified as the dominant architectural factor. Replacing it with an RMA-style Conv1D adapter significantly reduced prediction accuracy of the 6D Privileged Z, with mean validation R² dropping from 0.8792 to 0.4336 and mean MSE increasing from 0.1208 to 0.5664.
- **Force-Regime Contrastive Objective (InfoNCE):** The addition of the force-regime InfoNCE objective provided a smaller but consistent gain. It improved mean R² from 0.8688 (Transformer-MSE only) to 0.8792 (CoRMA) and reduced mean MSE from 0.1312 to 0.1208. This suggests InfoNCE acts as a semantic structuring regularizer, organizing contact histories effectively without compromising numerical prediction fidelity.
- **Representation Organization (Appendix D.1):** PCA visualizations comparing the supervised semantic output (ˆz_t) and the auxiliary contrastive embedding (u_t) showed that u_t exhibited clearer organization by coarse contact regimes (e.g., first contact, guided sliding) across task pairs. This supports the intended role of InfoNCE in structuring the representation space based on semantic interaction types.
- **Semantic Context Prediction Fidelity (Appendix D.2):** Scatter plots of predicted ˆz_t against oracle z_t for the six semantic dimensions showed concentration around the diagonal. This indicates that the adapter accurately learns a deployable approximation of the privileged semantic context, demonstrating its ability to predict control-relevant information.
- **Force-Regime Separability (Appendix D.3):** Probes designed to predict the four force-regime labels from either oracle z_t or predicted ˆz_t demonstrated that ˆz_t retained regime-relevant semantic information, with prediction accuracy close to that of the oracle context and well above chance (25%).

#### 4.3. Real-Force Motivation for Contact Regime Labels (Appendix C)

Diagnostic analysis of real wrench-derived features aligned at contact onset provided evidence for the chosen coarse force-regime labels. Distinct patterns were observed for transitions from free motion to first contact (sharp changes in lateral force ratio and derivative) and for guided sliding along an inner rim (sustained force texture and distinct contact-direction signatures). These findings support the plausibility and utility of using these force-regime labels as weak semantic supervision for the contrastive objective.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CoRMA introduces a specific meta-adaptation framework designed for robotic assembly tasks characterized by frequent and critical physical contact. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through its methodological innovations and empirical results.

#### 5.1. Core Contribution and Innovations

The primary contribution is the development of CoRMA, which adapts the Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) framework to the specific requirements of force-dominant assembly. Key innovations include:
- **Semantic Contact Context:** Replacing general simulator-parameter adaptation with a compact, 6-dimensional `Privileged Z` that semantically describes critical contact states (onset, lateral engagement, guided transition, direction, jamming). This provides a more focused and reusable representation for contact interpretation.
- **Force-Based Adaptation Interface:** Shifting the adaptation evidence from visual or object-centric cues to deployable force, proprioceptive, and action histories. This is directly relevant for interpreting contact mechanics once interaction begins.
- **Causal Transformer and Contrastive Learning:** Utilizing a causal Transformer to encode long-horizon histories and integrating a force-regime contrastive objective (InfoNCE). The contrastive objective is specifically designed to structure the representation space by coarse contact semantics, enhancing the adapter's ability to interpret ambiguous force signals without replacing the supervised regression task.

#### 5.2. Addressing Key Challenges in Robotics

- **Reduced Sim-to-Real Gap:** The empirical results demonstrate that CoRMA achieves higher verified real success rates and significantly reduces the performance degradation observed between simulation and real-world deployment compared to strong task-specific RL baselines (FORGE). This suggests a more robust and transferable approach for contact-rich assembly.
- **Online Adaptation and Efficiency:** CoRMA enables online, within-episode adaptation without requiring human demonstrations, privileged simulator inputs during deployment, or test-time gradient updates. This capability can reduce the need for extensive retraining and calibration for new parts or task variations, potentially lowering operational costs and increasing autonomy.
- **Reusable Task Family Interface:** The framework provides a reusable adaptation interface within a family of related assembly tasks. This implies a step towards more generalizable robotic skills that can adapt to different, but conceptually similar, manipulation challenges.

#### 5.3. Potential Impact

The proposed methodology has the potential to enhance the reliability and flexibility of robotic systems in industrial and service applications that involve intricate physical interaction. By improving the robot's ability to interpret and adapt to contact forces, CoRMA could lead to:
- More robust automation of complex assembly processes, reducing failure rates and manual intervention.
- Faster deployment of robotic solutions for new product variants or dynamic environments, as less task-specific training and calibration might be required.
- A deeper understanding of how robots can learn and generalize semantic interpretations of physical interaction, paving the way for more intelligent and adaptable robotic agents.

#### 5.4. Identified Limitations and Future Work

The paper acknowledges several limitations, which also highlight avenues for future research:
- **Task Generalization:** The evaluation was limited to reuse within a family of three related assembly tasks (PegInsert, GearMesh, NutThread), not including completely unseen tasks. Future work would involve evaluating generalization to broader, held-out tasks.
- **Deployment Stack Dependencies:** The use of a Marvin arm with TRAC-IK introduces additional variables (IK convergence, joint-limit handling, tracking error, arm compliance), meaning real success depends on the entire deployment stack, not solely the learned policy.
- **Remaining Sim-to-Real Gap:** While reduced, a sim-to-real gap persists, indicating potential mismatches in dynamics, contact compliance, friction, sensing, and control loop timing that require further investigation.
- **Force Estimation:** The current force pipeline uses basic smoothing. Integrating principled state estimation for force sensing could further reduce noise and improve contact-onset detection.
- **Scale of Real-Robot Evaluation:** The real-robot study involved tens of trials per task. Larger-scale reliability evaluations are needed for comprehensive validation.

Future directions include training a single multi-task teacher and a shared adapter for broader applicability, performing Real2Sim calibration using real logs to further reduce the reality gap, and improving deployment-time force estimation.
