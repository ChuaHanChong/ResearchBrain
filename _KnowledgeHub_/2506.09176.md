# Robot-Gated Interactive Imitation Learning with Adaptive Intervention Mechanism

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UCLA developed the Adaptive Intervention Mechanism (AIM), a robot-gated interactive imitation learning algorithm that learns when to request human assistance by mimicking human intervention patterns. AIM adaptively reduces expert involvement as the agent improves, achieving an 82% success rate in a driving simulator while requiring significantly fewer expert-involved steps compared to previous robot-gated methods.

## Problem

- Existing Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL) methods face a trade-off: human-gated approaches are effective but require continuous human monitoring, leading to high cognitive burden.
- Robot-gated IIL methods, while reducing human effort, rely on simplistic uncertainty-based heuristics (e.g., ensemble disagreement) that often do not align with human judgment, leading to inefficient or misplaced intervention requests.
- There is a need for a robot-gated system that can accurately predict when human intervention is most valuable and adapt its requests as the agent's competency improves, without extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## Method

- The Adaptive Intervention Mechanism (AIM) is introduced, a robot-gated IIL algorithm that learns when to request human assistance by mimicking human intervention patterns.
- A proxy Q-function, QIθ(s,ar)Q_I^{θ}(s, a_r)QIθ(s,ar), is trained to estimate the likelihood and necessity of human intervention given the current state and robot's action, using supervised labels for 'no intervention' and 'intervention'.
- An adaptive threshold mechanism dynamically sets the intervention threshold as a quantile of observed Q-values, automatically becoming more selective as the agent improves and eliminating the need for manual tuning.

## Results

- AIM achieved a success rate of **0.82** in MetaDrive with approximately 1.9K expert-involved steps, outperforming Thrifty-DAgger (**0.58**) and Ensemble-DAgger (**0.60**) with the same expert data budget.
- The method demonstrates more strategic and informative interventions, showing a faster reduction in deviation rates in safety-critical states and naturally reducing intervention frequency as training progressed.
- Policies trained solely on AIM-collected expert data achieved performance nearly matching those trained on data collected via human-gated approaches (e.g., PVP), indicating high-quality and efficient data collection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning an intervention criterion based on a proxy Q-function can effectively mimic human intervention patterns, moving beyond simplistic uncertainty-based heuristics.
> - Dynamically adjusting the intervention threshold based on the agent's improving performance (quantiles of Q-values) allows for adaptive and efficient human assistance without manual tuning.
> - By focusing interventions on truly critical states, the approach collects higher quality expert data, leading to more robust policies with less human effort.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL) represents a promising approach to training autonomous agents by incorporating human expertise during the learning process. However, existing methods face a fundamental trade-off: human-gated approaches achieve high performance but require continuous human monitoring, while robot-gated approaches reduce human burden but rely on simplistic heuristics that may not align with human judgment.

This paper introduces the Adaptive Intervention Mechanism (AIM), a robot-gated IIL algorithm developed by researchers at UCLA that learns when to request human assistance by mimicking human intervention patterns. AIM addresses the limitations of existing robot-gated methods by developing an adaptive criterion that becomes more selective as the agent improves, eliminating the need for manual threshold tuning while ensuring interventions occur in truly critical situations.

### 2. Background and Problem Formulation

Traditional reinforcement learning and imitation learning face significant challenges in real-world deployment. Reinforcement learning suffers from poor sample efficiency and difficulty in reward specification, while standard imitation learning is vulnerable to distribution shift when agents encounter states not covered in training demonstrations.

Interactive Imitation Learning emerges as a solution by incorporating human feedback during training. Current IIL approaches fall into two categories:

**Human-Gated Methods** require continuous human monitoring, where supervisors proactively intervene when they observe mistakes or dangerous situations. While effective, this approach imposes substantial cognitive burden on human operators who must maintain constant attention throughout the training process.

**Robot-Gated Methods** attempt to reduce human effort by having the agent autonomously request assistance. However, existing approaches like Ensemble-DAgger and Thrifty-DAgger rely on uncertainty-based heuristics that often misalign with human intervention strategies. These methods frequently request help in situations where humans wouldn't intervene or fail to seek assistance in truly critical scenarios.

The core challenge lies in developing a robot-gated system that can accurately predict when human intervention would be most valuable, adapting its requests as the agent's competency improves without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning.

### 3. The Adaptive Intervention Mechanism Approach

AIM introduces a learned intervention criterion based on a proxy Q-function that estimates the likelihood and necessity of human intervention. The approach centers on training a function $Q_I^{\theta}(s, a_r)$ that quantifies whether a human would intervene given the current state $s$ and the robot's proposed action $a_r$.

#### 3.1. Learning the Intervention Criterion

The proxy Q-function is trained using a supervised learning approach with carefully designed labels:

- **No Intervention Label (-1)**: When the robot's action perfectly matches the expert's action ($a_r = a_h$), indicating no intervention is needed
- **Intervention Label (+1)**: When the robot's action significantly deviates from the expert's action ($||a_r - a_h||^2 > \epsilon$), suggesting human correction would be beneficial

The training objective combines this supervised signal with temporal difference learning to enable generalization across states:

$$J(\theta) = J_{AIM}(\theta) + J_{TD}(\theta)$$

where $J_{AIM}(\theta)$ enforces the intervention labeling and $J_{TD}(\theta)$ propagates Q-values across state transitions.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Threshold Mechanism

A key innovation of AIM is its dynamic threshold system. The intervention threshold $\beta$ is set as the $(1-\delta)$-th quantile of observed Q-values, where $\delta$ controls the initial intervention frequency. As the agent improves and its actions align more closely with expert behavior, the Q-values naturally decrease, causing $\beta$ to adapt automatically without manual tuning.

#### 3.3. Algorithm Workflow

AIM operates through the following process:

1. **Warm-up Phase**: Collect initial demonstrations using human-gated intervention
2. **Q-function Training**: Train the initial proxy Q-function on collected data
3. **Dynamic Interaction**:
 - If $Q_I^{\theta}(s, a_r) > \beta$, request human takeover
 - Otherwise, continue with robot control
 - Continuously update the Q-function and recalculate thresholds
4. **Policy Learning**: Train the robot policy via behavioral cloning on human demonstrations

### 4. Experimental Results

The effectiveness of AIM was evaluated in two distinct environments: MetaDrive (continuous control driving simulator) and MiniGrid Four Room (discrete navigation task). The experiments compared AIM against both robot-gated baselines (Ensemble-DAgger, Thrifty-DAgger) and human-gated methods (HG-DAgger, PVP).

#### 4.1. Performance Efficiency

AIM consistently outperformed all baselines in achieving high-quality policies with minimal expert involvement. In MetaDrive, AIM achieved a success rate of 0.82 with approximately 1.9K expert-involved steps, significantly outperforming Thrifty-DAgger (0.58 success rate) and Ensemble-DAgger (0.60 success rate) with the same expert data budget.

#### 4.2. Quality of Interventions

The experimental results demonstrate that AIM's interventions are both more strategic and informative than existing methods:

- **Safety-Critical Focus**: AIM showed faster reduction in deviation rates in safety-critical states compared to uncertainty-based methods
- **Adaptive Intervention Rate**: Unlike fixed-threshold approaches, AIM naturally reduced its intervention frequency as training progressed
- **High-Quality Demonstrations**: Policies trained exclusively on AIM-collected expert data achieved performance nearly matching those trained on human-gated collected data

#### 4.3. Visualization of Learning Progress

The visualization clearly shows AIM's adaptive nature: initially requesting help in critical areas like curves and obstacles, then progressively reducing interventions as competency improves, while Thrifty-DAgger continues inefficient querying throughout training.

### 5. Implications and Significance

AIM represents a significant advancement in making interactive imitation learning practical for real-world deployment. The method addresses several critical challenges:

**Reduced Human Burden**: By learning to mimic human intervention strategies, AIM dramatically reduces the cognitive load on human supervisors while maintaining the effectiveness of human-gated approaches.

**Improved Efficiency**: The adaptive nature of AIM's intervention criterion means fewer, more targeted expert demonstrations are needed, leading to faster learning and reduced training costs.

**Enhanced Safety**: By focusing interventions on safety-critical states where the agent genuinely needs guidance, AIM ensures that learned policies are robust in high-stakes scenarios.

**Broad Applicability**: The method's effectiveness across both continuous and discrete action spaces suggests wide applicability to various robotic and AI tasks.

The approach has particular relevance for autonomous systems where safety is paramount, such as autonomous driving, robotic manipulation in hazardous environments, and other applications where human expertise is valuable but human attention is limited.

### 6. Conclusion

The Adaptive Intervention Mechanism introduces a principled approach to robot-gated interactive imitation learning that successfully bridges the gap between human-gated effectiveness and robot-gated efficiency. By learning to predict when human intervention would be most valuable and adapting these predictions as the agent improves, AIM makes interactive learning more practical and scalable.

The experimental results demonstrate that AIM achieves superior learning efficiency compared to existing methods while collecting expert demonstrations of comparable quality to human-gated approaches. The method's ability to automatically adapt intervention rates without manual tuning represents a significant step toward deploying interactive learning systems in real-world applications.

Future work could explore extending AIM to handle multiple human supervisors with different expertise levels, incorporating uncertainty quantification for additional safety guarantees, and validating the approach with real human subjects in practical deployment scenarios.
