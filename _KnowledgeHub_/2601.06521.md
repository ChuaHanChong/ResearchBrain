# BabyVision: Visual Reasoning Beyond Language

> [!summary] Summary
> BabyVision introduces a benchmark evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) on foundational visual reasoning tasks, revealing that even state-of-the-art models significantly underperform human children on basic visual perception, often due to a "verbalization bottleneck" that limits true visual understanding.

## Problem

- Existing Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) demonstrate an "inverted profile of competence," excelling at complex, knowledge-intensive tasks but failing at basic, pre-linguistic visual perception.
- Current benchmarks primarily evaluate MLLMs on high-level, language-dependent visual reasoning, overlooking foundational visual skills acquired by young children.
- MLLMs frequently rely on linguistic priors or exhibit "language leakage," rather than true visual understanding, during visual reasoning.

## Method

- Developed ==BABYVISION==, a benchmark with **388** questions across **22** subtypes, specifically designed to test MLLMs' foundational visual understanding akin to human children (3-12 years old).
- Introduced ==BABYVISION-GEN==, a generative benchmark (280 questions) where models provide visual annotations as answers, addressing tasks more naturally solved through visual actions.
- Established human performance baselines using children and adults, and implemented an LLM-as-judge evaluation for model outputs, validated by human experts.

## Results

- The best MLLM, Gemini3-Pro-Preview, scored **49.7%** on BABYVISION, demonstrating a **44.4%** absolute performance gap compared to adult humans (**94.1%**) and lagging behind average 6-year-old children.
- MLLMs showed consistent deficits across all four early-vision categories (Fine-grained Discrimination, Visual Tracking, Spatial Perception, Visual Pattern Recognition), with "Count 3D Blocks" (max **20.5%**) being especially challenging.
- Generative models achieved an average accuracy of **18.3%** on BABYVISION-GEN, with visual tracking tasks remaining particularly difficult (**6.7%** for the best model), suggesting limitations in visual reasoning through direct generation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The "verbalization bottleneck" is a core reason for MLLMs' failures, as encoding visual information into language for reasoning leads to loss of fine-grained detail, spatial coherence, and continuous identity.
> - MLLMs exhibit systemic failures in tasks requiring precise fine-grained discrimination, visual tracking, 3D spatial imagination, and abstract visual pattern induction.
> - Achieving human-level visual intelligence requires moving beyond current vision-language encoders towards natively multimodal architectures that can reason directly in visual space.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has been a rapidly advancing field, yielding models capable of impressive feats in complex reasoning that integrate language and vision. Existing benchmarks like MMMU, HLE, MathVista, MathVision, MME, MMEvalPro, and DrVD-Bench demonstrate MLLMs' proficiency in high-level, knowledge-intensive tasks requiring substantial education or domain expertise, often achieving near-human or expert-level performance. This indicates a strong capability in combining visual inputs with extensive textual knowledge and performing multi-step logical deductions in various specialized domains.

However, despite these successes, a recurring observation in the research landscape is an "inverted profile of competence" in MLLMs. Several recent studies, such as BLINK, MMStar, and MMVP, have begun to expose significant gaps in MLLMs' foundational visual understanding. These benchmarks reveal that models, while excelling at expert-level tasks, often struggle with basic perceptual problems that require no specialized knowledge, sometimes over-relying on linguistic priors or exhibiting "language leakage" rather than true visual perception. This suggests a fundamental disconnect: MLLMs might be strong at semantic reasoning *about* visual content but weak at core visual *perception* itself.

"BabyVision" directly addresses this critical gap. It carves out a unique niche by focusing specifically on "early-vision abilities"—the foundational visual skills that humans, particularly young children (ages 3–12), acquire naturally and pre-linguistically. Unlike existing benchmarks that predominantly draw from adult-level, semantic-driven, or knowledge-intensive tasks, BabyVision systematically probes visual understanding independent of linguistic knowledge. By comparing MLLM performance to that of children at various developmental stages, the work aims to uncover whether current models possess the "atomic visual competencies" that underpin human vision from its earliest stages. This perspective is deeply rooted in developmental psychology, drawing on concepts like object permanence, visual tracking, and spatial understanding that emerge long before formal language acquisition. By doing so, "BabyVision" seeks to shift the focus from "what MLLMs know about the world" to "how MLLMs *see* the world," fundamentally re-evaluating their visual grounding.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of the "BabyVision" research is to systematically investigate and quantify a critical deficiency in contemporary Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs): their lack of robust, fundamental visual understanding that operates independently of linguistic knowledge. The authors pinpoint a crucial, yet overlooked, fact: state-of-the-art MLLMs consistently fail at basic visual tasks that human children, even as young as three years old, can effortlessly solve.

This observation serves as the primary motivation for introducing **BABYVISION**, a novel benchmark explicitly designed to assess "core visual abilities independent of linguistic knowledge" for MLLMs. The authors hypothesize that despite MLLMs' impressive performance on knowledge-heavy and language-intensive evaluations, they still lack the "fundamental visual primitives" that form the bedrock of human visual perception and reasoning. The benchmark is intended to expose this gap, which current evaluations, focused on expert-level or semantic reasoning, often fail to capture.

Beyond merely identifying these shortcomings, the paper also aims to explore alternative evaluation paradigms. Recognizing that many visual reasoning tasks are more naturally solved through visual actions (like tracing a path or completing a pattern) rather than verbal descriptions, the authors introduce **BABYVISION-GEN**. This generative counterpart evaluates visual reasoning through visual outputs, offering a pathway to assess visual competence that might be otherwise underestimated due to the "verbalization bottleneck" inherent in language-only responses.

Ultimately, the key objectives are multifaceted:
1. **Systematically Benchmark Early Vision:** Create a rigorous, developmentally-inspired benchmark (BABYVISION) to probe foundational, pre-linguistic visual skills across a wide range of tasks.
2. **Quantify the Human-Model Gap:** Empirically demonstrate the significant discrepancy between MLLM performance and human (child and adult) capabilities on these basic visual tasks.
3. **Uncover Failure Modes:** Identify and characterize the specific types of visual reasoning failures prevalent in current MLLMs.
4. **Propose Generative Evaluation:** Introduce BABYVISION-GEN and its accompanying automatic evaluation toolkit as a complementary method to assess visual reasoning through visual generation.
5. **Drive Future Research:** Motivate the development of new architectural and training paradigms that can overcome the identified limitations and advance MLLMs towards truly human-level visual perception and reasoning.

The underlying motivation is to push the boundaries of MLLM development beyond superficial visual pattern matching or language-aided inference towards genuinely grounded visual intelligence that mirrors early human cognitive development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in "BabyVision" is meticulous, encompassing the creation of two distinct benchmarks, a rigorous data curation pipeline, and innovative evaluation strategies for both language-based and generation-based models.

#### 3.1. BABYVISION: Benchmarking Visual Reasoning Beyond Language

The core of the methodology lies in the design and construction of BABYVISION, a benchmark specifically targeting early-vision capabilities.
- **Scope and Categories:** The benchmark is grounded in developmental psychology, focusing on visual skills acquired by humans before or alongside language. It is structured into four core categories: Fine-grained Discrimination (detecting subtle visual differences), Visual Tracking (following paths and trajectories), Spatial Perception (understanding 3D structures and relationships), and Visual Pattern Recognition (identifying logical and geometric patterns). These categories further subdivide into 22 specific subtypes, covering a broad spectrum of fundamental visual competencies.
- **Data Curation Pipeline:** A three-stage process ensures high quality and validity:
 1. **Taxonomy Definition and Seed Collection:** Researchers consulted developmental psychology textbooks and standardized visual tests to define the "early vision" scope. Four top-level categories and 22 subtypes were established. Approximately 100 "seed images" (4-5 per subtype) were manually selected as prototypes.
 2. **Data Collection and Filtering:** Using seed images, the dataset was expanded via reverse image search and keyword-based retrieval, yielding around 4,000 candidate images. Strict filtering removed copyrighted, text-heavy, culturally specific, or inappropriate content.
 3. **Annotation and Quality Assurance:** Trained annotators created questions, ground-truth answers, and detailed "solution processes" for valid images. Crucially, all annotations underwent a double-blind expert review, requiring agreement from two independent experts that answers were unambiguous and derivable primarily from visual analysis. This iterative process resulted in 388 high-quality questions.
- **Dataset Statistics:** The final benchmark comprises 388 unique questions, including 135 multiple-choice (34.8%) and 253 fill-in-the-blank (65.2%). Questions are concise (avg. 25.9 words) to minimize linguistic shortcuts. An additional 1,400 training examples were collected for future research into model training.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** MLLMs are evaluated using a unified prompt template encouraging explicit reasoning ("Think about the question and give your final answer..."). An LLM-as-judge approach (Qwen3-Max) determines semantic equivalence between model outputs and ground-truth answers, achieving 100% consistency with human evaluators.
- **Human Evaluation:** A crucial component involves a comparative study with human participants. For a smaller subset (BabyVision-Mini), 20 individuals from each age group (3, 6, 10, 12 years old) were tested. For the full 388-question benchmark, 16 adult participants (bachelor's degree or higher) completed the tasks, establishing a comprehensive human baseline.

#### 3.2. BABYVISION-GEN: Evaluating Visual Reasoning with Generation Models

To address the observation that many visual reasoning tasks are more naturally solved visually, the authors introduced BABYVISION-GEN.
- **Concept:** This extension evaluates visual reasoning through image (or video) generation, allowing models to demonstrate solutions by annotating the input image (e.g., tracing paths, completing patterns, marking relationships).
- **Dataset:** BABYVISION-GEN includes 280 questions across 21 subtypes adapted from BABYVISION for visual output. Each instance includes the original image, a generation prompt, and a reference solution image reflecting human annotator consensus.
- **Evaluation:** Models are instructed to annotate the original image with minimal overlays. Automatic evaluation is performed using Gemini-3-Flash as a judge, comparing the model's generated annotation with the human ground truth. Type-specific evaluation criteria are designed for precise assessment. A human evaluation on NanoBanana-Pro outputs confirmed the reliability of the automatic judge, showing a 96.1% agreement rate (F1=0.924).

#### 3.3. RLVR Study

An initial study was conducted to investigate the impact of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) on visual reasoning. Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking was fine-tuned using GRPO on a custom dataset of 1,400 BabyVision-style examples, using an LLM judge for reward signals. This explored whether "thinking-token" reasoning could be enhanced to improve visual abilities.

This multi-faceted methodology, combining rigorous benchmark construction, diverse evaluation strategies (textual and generative), and human baselines, provides a comprehensive framework for probing the foundational visual intelligence of MLLMs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields striking findings that highlight a significant chasm between state-of-the-art MLLMs and human visual reasoning capabilities, particularly at foundational, pre-linguistic levels.

#### 4.1. Performance on BABYVISION (Textual Output)

- **Overall Human-Model Gap:** The most pronounced finding is the substantial performance gap. Adult human testers achieved an average score of 94.1% on BABYVISION. In stark contrast, the best-performing MLLM, **Gemini3-Pro-Preview**, scored only **49.7%**, demonstrating a 44.4% absolute gap.
- **Comparison with Young Humans:** The gap becomes even more stark when compared to children. Gemini3-Pro-Preview lags approximately 20% behind average 6-year-old children. Most other frontier MLLMs perform even worse, often below the average 3-year-old, despite their proficiency on complex, language-heavy benchmarks. This underscores that current MLLMs struggle with basic visual primitives, not just "hard problems."
- **Proprietary Model Performance:**
 - **Gemini3-Pro-Preview (49.7%)** leads significantly.
 - **GPT-5.2 (34.4%)** and **Doubao-1.8 (30.2%)** follow.
 - Other proprietary models like Qwen3-VL-Plus (19.2%), Grok-4 (16.2%), and Claude-4.5-Opus (14.2%) show markedly lower performance.
 - The consistent deficits across all four task families (Fine-grained Discrimination, Visual Tracking, Spatial Perception, Visual Pattern Recognition) suggest a systemic limitation rather than isolated weaknesses.
 - **Challenging Subtypes:** "Count 3D Blocks" (best: 20.5%) and "Find the Same" (best: 26.5%) proved particularly difficult, indicating deficiencies in structured scene representations, object permanence, and depth-aware composition.
- **Open-Source Model Performance:**
 - **Qwen3VL-235B-Thinking (22.2%)** was the top open-source model, still far behind proprietary leaders and human baselines. Most other open-source models ranged from 12% to 19%.
 - **Impact of "Thinking" Mode:** Explicit intermediate reasoning, enabled by "Thinking" variants, consistently improved performance over "Instruct" variants (e.g., 22.2% vs. 19.5% for Qwen3VL-235B).
 - **Model Scaling:** Performance generally improved with model scale, but not monotonically (e.g., 4B-Thinking slightly outperformed 8B-Thinking), suggesting that simply increasing parameters may not resolve foundational perceptual issues.
- **Fine-Grained Analysis of Failure Patterns:**
 - **Fine-grained Discrimination:** Models struggle with subtle visual differences (e.g., "Find the Same," "Find the Different"), indicating issues with high-fidelity perceptual encoding.
 - **Visual Tracking:** A fundamental weakness in maintaining "manifold identity" (continuous identity of a line/path) through interactions like intersections, leading to "track-switching" errors (e.g., "Lines Observation," "Maze").
 - **Spatial Perception:** "Count 3D Blocks" highlights an inability to infer hidden volumes and maintain coherent 3D mental models. Tasks requiring 3D transformations are challenging.
 - **Visual Pattern Recognition:** While models perform better on discrete, rule-based transformations (e.g., rotation), they struggle with continuous spatial transformations and confounding visual attributes with abstract rules (e.g., "Overlay Patterns," "Mirroring Patterns").

#### 4.2. Performance on BABYVISION-GEN (Visual Output)

- **Generative Model Performance:** Evaluating three frontier visual generation models, **NanoBanana-Pro (18.3%)** achieved the highest overall accuracy, outperforming GPT-Image-1.5 (9.8%) and Qwen-Image-Edit (4.8%).
- **Category-Specific Strengths:** NanoBanana-Pro showed relatively stronger results in "Fine-grained Discrimination" (24.5%) and "Visual Pattern Recognition" (22.8%).
- **Persistent Challenges:** "Visual Tracking" remained extremely challenging (6.7% for the best model), with 0% accuracy on critical tasks like "Maze" and "Connect the Lines." This indicates that current generative models struggle to maintain spatial coherence over extended generation sequences.
- **Qualitative Observations:** While generation models like Sora-2 and NanoBanana-Pro show "human-like visual thinking" by explicitly drawing trajectories, their solutions still contain noticeable errors, implying that robust visual understanding is still lacking to guide the generation process accurately.

#### 4.3. RLVR Study Results

- An initial study on Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking showed a **+4.8% overall accuracy improvement** on BabyVision after RLVR fine-tuning.
- Gains were observed across most task subtypes, particularly in "Fine-grained Discrimination" and "Spatial Perception."
- However, RLVR yielded little to negative improvement in "Visual Tracking," suggesting that tasks least amenable to verbalization benefit less from RLVR's emphasis on "thinking-token" reasoning.

These findings collectively present a compelling picture of MLLMs' foundational visual limitations, calling for a re-evaluation of current architectural and training paradigms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "BabyVision" paper delivers a critical and timely diagnosis of a fundamental weakness in state-of-the-art Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), despite their impressive achievements in other complex domains. The core significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

#### 5.1. The Verbalization Bottleneck

The most profound insight from this work is the identification of the **"verbalization bottleneck"** as a unifying cause for MLLMs' visual failures. Current MLLMs predominantly process visual inputs by first translating them into linguistic representations, then performing reasoning in language space, and finally generating a verbal output. While this leverages the powerful reasoning capabilities of LLMs, it fundamentally limits their visual understanding. Visual information that cannot be faithfully and comprehensively expressed in language—such as precise geometric relationships, subtle curvatures, or continuous spatial trajectories—is inevitably lost during this compression, leading to a "resolution gap."

This bottleneck explains the four systematic failure modes characterized in the paper:
1. **Loss of Fine-Grained Detail:** MLLMs struggle with subtle visual cues because implicit semantic compression reduces high-fidelity perceptual encoding to lossy linguistic tokens, making micro-differences indistinguishable.
2. **Loss of Manifold Identity:** The inability to maintain the identity of continuous visual elements (like lines in a maze) through interactions is attributed to mapping 1D manifolds into discrete instruction sequences rather than using continuous perceptual tracking.
3. **Failure of Spatial Imagination:** Approximating 3D mental models via descriptive logic rather than non-verbal mental simulation leads to errors in affine transformations, as language is an insufficient coordinate system for volumetric constraints.
4. **Failure of Visual Pattern Induction:** MLLMs often conflate abstract relational structures with specific visual attributes because they approach induction through attribute enumeration rather than true relational mapping, missing compositional generalization.

#### 5.2. Reorienting Research Priorities

"BabyVision" forces a re-evaluation of what constitutes "visual intelligence" in AI. It demonstrates unequivocally that excelling on knowledge-heavy, language-intensive benchmarks does not equate to robust foundational visual competence. This necessitates a shift in research priorities, encouraging the community to focus on developing models that genuinely "see" and "perceive" the world, rather than merely reasoning about language-mediated visual descriptions. The benchmark serves as a crucial diagnostic tool for measuring progress toward truly grounded visual intelligence.

#### 5.3. Driving Architectural Innovations

The findings strongly suggest that incremental scaling of current MLLM architectures may not be sufficient to overcome these foundational visual limitations. Instead, progress will likely demand **architectural innovations** that preserve visual information throughout the entire reasoning process, bypassing the verbalization bottleneck. This means moving beyond vision-language encoders that primarily convert pixels to tokens, towards models that can inherently maintain and manipulate visual representations in their native format.

#### 5.4. The Promise of Visual Externalization and Natively Multimodal Models

The introduction of **BABYVISION-GEN** opens up an exciting new research direction: **visual externalization**. By showing that humans often solve visual reasoning tasks by drawing or marking directly on images, the paper motivates the development of models that can "reason in visual space" and output visual solutions. The preliminary results with generative models, despite their current limitations, reveal glimpses of "human-like visual thinking processes" through explicit drawing and tracing.

This points towards the potential of **natively multimodal architectures** (e.g., Bagel, Sora 2, Veo 3). These models are designed to natively integrate visual understanding and generation, maintaining rich visual representations throughout reasoning. If models can learn to directly manipulate images—tracing paths, completing patterns, transforming shapes—they could entirely bypass the verbalization bottleneck and achieve a more profound form of visual intelligence, leveraging the generative process itself as a form of visual reasoning. The RLVR study, while showing limited gains for highly perceptual tasks, still offers a pathway to enhance "thinking" in MLLMs where language plays a role, suggesting that hybrid approaches might be beneficial.

In conclusion, "BabyVision" is a seminal work that not only exposes critical blind spots in contemporary MLLMs but also provides a concrete framework and a clear roadmap for advancing multimodal AI towards genuinely human-like perception and reasoning. Its impact will likely be felt across benchmark design, architectural research, and training methodologies for the next generation of visual AI systems.
