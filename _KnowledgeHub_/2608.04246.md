# SAFECAST: Robust Failure Detection for VLA Policies with Contrast-Set Training and Calibration

> [!summary] Summary
> SAFECAST enhances robust failure detection for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies by augmenting failure probe training and calibration with contrast-set trajectories. This framework consistently improves detection reliability under multimodal distribution shifts in both simulated and real-world robotic tasks, demonstrating effective sim-to-real transfer for probe training.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, despite generalization capabilities, frequently fail in real-world deployment due to multimodal distribution shifts (visual and linguistic changes).
- Existing runtime failure detection methods, such as SAFE, rely on the assumption that calibration data is representative of deployment conditions, which often does not hold under distribution shifts.
- This mismatch in calibration leads to unreliable time-varying failure thresholds, resulting in delayed or missed failure warnings in critical robotic applications.

## Method

- **SAFECAST** augments both the failure probe training and ==functional conformal calibration== datasets with ==contrast-set trajectories== to account for expected deployment-time distribution shifts.
- Contrast-set rollouts are generated by applying controlled visual (e.g., distractors, clutter) and/or language (e.g., paraphrases) perturbations to source data and re-executing the VLA policy.
- A ==Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)== filtering procedure is used to select diverse and distinct contrast-set trajectories, preventing redundancy in the augmented datasets.

## Results

- SAFECAST configurations consistently improved failure detection F1-score and ROC-AUC compared to the SAFE baseline across both real-world DROID robot experiments and LIBERO simulation tasks.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that combining visual and language contrast sets led to the strongest out-of-distribution failure detection robustness for policies like OpenVLA and π0 (e.g., OpenVLA's F1 increased from **0.922** with visual-only to **0.959** with multimodal contrast sets).
- Training the SAFECAST probe entirely in simulation and then calibrating it on real-world contrast-set trajectories (sim-training to real-contrast-set-calibration) outperformed training and calibrating solely on a smaller set of real-world data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating contrast-set-perturbed trajectories during both probe training and threshold calibration is essential for building failure detection systems robust to multimodal distribution shifts.
> - Joint visual and language contrast sets are generally more effective in enhancing out-of-distribution failure detection robustness compared to perturbations in a single modality.
> - It is possible to train failure probes efficiently in simulation and then calibrate them effectively with a smaller set of real-world contrast-set trajectories, offering a cost-effective sim-to-real transfer approach.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies has enabled robots to execute complex manipulation tasks based on natural language instructions. Models such as OpenVLA and π0 have demonstrated generalization capabilities across diverse tasks, objects, and environments. However, a significant challenge in deploying these policies in real-world settings is their susceptibility to distribution shifts. These shifts can manifest as changes in visual observations (e.g., clutter, distractor objects, lighting variations, novel objects, altered initial states) or linguistic instructions (e.g., reworded instructions), leading to policy failures during deployment. Such failures are particularly problematic in safety-critical applications like assistive robotics or manipulation of fragile items.

A growing area of research focuses on runtime failure prediction for robotic policies. Existing methods, often inspired by ScAlable Failure Estimation (SAFE) frameworks, utilize the hidden states of VLA policies to predict failure risk. These methods typically employ functional conformal prediction to establish temporally calibrated risk thresholds, aiming to flag unsafe rollouts before task failure occurs. This approach relies on the assumption that the data used for calibration accurately represents the conditions encountered during deployment.

This assumption, however, is frequently violated under multimodal deployment shifts (combinations of visual and language changes). When the distribution of hidden-state risk trajectories changes substantially due to these shifts, thresholds calibrated on source-distribution rollouts can become unreliable, leading to delayed or missed warnings.

Contrast sets have emerged as a tool in natural language processing (NLP), vision-language reasoning, and robotics to evaluate model robustness by exposing brittleness through targeted perturbations. This work situates itself within this landscape by exploring whether contrast-set perturbations can be leveraged not just for evaluation but to enhance the reliability of runtime failure detection systems for VLA policies when confronted with deployment-time distribution shifts. It builds directly on the SAFE framework, addressing its limitations regarding calibration robustness under out-of-distribution conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enhances the robustness of hidden-state-based failure detection for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, particularly when confronted with multimodal deployment-time distribution shifts. The proposed framework, named SAFECAST (ScAlable Failure Estimation with Contrast-set Augmentation for Safety Tracking), aims to provide more reliable failure warnings under conditions that differ significantly from a policy’s original training and calibration environment.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical limitations observed in the current state of VLA policy deployment and failure detection:
1. **Vulnerability to Distribution Shifts:** Despite advancements in VLA policies, they often exhibit degraded performance and failures when deployed in real-world settings that introduce variations not encountered during training. These variations include visual shifts (e.g., clutter, novel objects, lighting changes, altered initial states) and language shifts (e.g., reworded instructions). In safety-critical applications, such failures are unacceptable.
2. **Calibration Mismatch in Existing Methods:** Current state-of-the-art failure detection methods, such as SAFE, rely on probing internal policy representations and calibrating time-varying failure thresholds using functional conformal prediction. A core assumption of these methods is that the calibration trajectories are representative of the deployment conditions. When deployment conditions deviate significantly from the calibration data, the distribution of hidden-state risk trajectories can change. This mismatch renders the calibrated thresholds unreliable, leading to late or missed failure warnings.
3. **Need for Robust Calibration:** To ensure timely and accurate failure detection, the calibration process must account for the types of distribution shifts expected during deployment. Simply using source-distribution data for calibration is insufficient when the operational environment is diverse and dynamic.
4. **Leveraging Contrast Sets:** The researchers are motivated by the observation that contrast sets have been effective in revealing model brittleness. They hypothesize that by incorporating contrast-set-perturbed trajectories into both the training of failure probes and the calibration of risk thresholds, the failure detection system can become more resilient to these deployment shifts.

Specifically, the paper sets out to investigate the following:
- Whether integrating visual and language contrast-set trajectories during probe training and conformal calibration can improve the reliability of failure detection during distribution shifts compared to methods calibrated only on source-distribution data.
- Whether joint visual-language contrast sets offer greater robustness benefits than perturbations in a single modality (visual-only or language-only).
- Whether a SAFECAST probe trained in simulation can be effectively calibrated on physical rollouts to achieve better performance than more expensive real-world probe training, thereby offering a more cost-effective approach to deployment.

By addressing these questions, the research aims to contribute to the development of more trustworthy and safely deployable robotic manipulation systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SAFECAST framework builds upon the existing ScAlable Failure Estimation (SAFE) methodology, which predicts runtime failures in VLA policies by probing their hidden states. The core innovation of SAFECAST lies in its strategic use of contrast-set-augmented data during both the training of the failure probe and the calibration of the failure detection thresholds.

#### 3.1. Background on SAFE

SAFE operates by extracting pre-final-layer hidden representations (h_t) from a frozen VLA policy at each timestep (t) of a rollout. A lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) probe (f_phi) is then trained to map these hidden representations to a scalar failure score (r_t), which indicates the estimated risk of the rollout eventually failing. Functional conformal prediction is subsequently applied to sequences of these risk scores (r_1:T) from calibration rollouts to compute a time-varying failure threshold (δ_t) at a specified significance level (α), which controls the operational risk tolerance. A rollout is flagged as failing if r_t > δ_t.

#### 3.2. SAFECAST's Core Intervention: Contrast Set Augmentation

SAFECAST addresses the limitation of SAFE’s reliance on source-distribution calibration data by augmenting the probe training (D_train_phi) and calibration (D_cal_phi) datasets with contrast-set trajectories. A contrast-set rollout (τ_CS) is generated by applying controlled perturbations to the visual (˜o_t) and/or language (˜l) inputs of a source-distribution rollout (τ_src), and crucially, re-executing the *frozen* VLA policy under these perturbed conditions. This re-execution means that the resulting action sequence (˜a_1:T) and hidden-state trajectory are not assumed to be relabeled copies but potentially distinct outcomes, capturing how the policy behaves under shift. The augmented rollout pool is denoted as D_aug = D_src ∪ D_CS.

#### 3.3. Contrast Set Construction

The paper specifies different types of contrast-set perturbations:
- **Visual Contrast Sets:**
 - **In Simulation (LIBERO):** Distractor objects are added to LIBERO-Spatial scenes. These distractors are chosen not to alter the task's intent, are not referenced in the instruction, and do not obstruct the nominal rollout. This ensures the target object, action, and goal remain consistent while the visual context changes.
 - **In Real-World (DROID):** Visual perturbations include distractor objects, cluttered backgrounds, novel objects, and altered object configurations. These perturbations directly lead to novel rollouts.
- **Language Contrast Sets:**
 - **In Simulation (LIBERO):** For each LIBERO-Spatial task, three semantically equivalent paraphrases are generated using a ChatGPT 5.1 Instant model. These paraphrases preserve the target object, action, and goal location, testing robustness to linguistic form changes. To manage collection cost, each paraphrase is evaluated on a subset of episodes.
 - **In Real-World (DROID):** Language perturbations include paraphrased instructions and, where applicable, negated or distractor phrasing, also generated by ChatGPT 5.1.
- **Joint Visual-Language Contrast Sets:** These combine both visual perturbations (e.g., clutter, novel objects) and language perturbations (e.g., paraphrased instructions) to induce multimodal deployment shifts, which are particularly relevant in real-world DROID experiments.

#### 3.4. Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) Filtering

To prevent redundancy and increase diversity within the augmented probe training set (D_train_phi,CS), an active rejection procedure based on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) is applied. Candidate contrast-set rollouts are represented by translation-normalized 3D end-effector position time series. Their DTW distance to already-selected rollouts of the same task and outcome class is computed. If this normalized DTW distance falls below a fixed threshold (τ_DTW = 0.02), the candidate rollout is rejected. This filtering ensures that the augmented dataset contains sufficiently distinct trajectories. Calibration subsets (D_cal_phi,CS) are also sampled with controlled success and failure counts to avoid threshold estimation being skewed by a single outcome class.

#### 3.5. SAFECAST Configurations

The study evaluates four configurations to isolate the effects of contrast-set augmentation:
- **SAFE (Baseline):** Probe trained and calibrated solely on source-distribution data.
- **SAFECAST_TRAINAUG:** Probe trained on augmented data (D_train_phi,aug), but calibrated only on source-distribution data (D_cal_phi,src).
- **SAFECAST_CALAUG:** Probe trained only on source-distribution data (D_train_phi,src), but calibrated on augmented data (D_cal_phi,aug).
- **SAFECAST (Full):** Probe trained on augmented data (D_train_phi,aug) and calibrated on augmented data (D_cal_phi,aug).

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

Experiments are conducted on:
- **Physical Robot:** Franka/DROID setup using π0 and π0-FAST VLA policies. π0 is LoRA-finetuned on 20 teleoperated spatial pick-and-place demonstrations due to zero-shot performance, while π0-FAST is evaluated without additional finetuning. Evaluation uses deployment rollouts (D_real_eval) with strong shifts like novel objects, new tasks, clutter, lighting variations, and altered initial states.
- **Simulation Environment:** LIBERO-Spatial for source data and LIBERO-Plus for evaluation, using OpenVLA and π0 policies. The study distinguishes source, calibration, and deployment distributions to examine robustness under controlled shifts.

#### 3.7. Metrics

Failure detectors are evaluated using F1-score and ROC-AUC across a sweep of conformal significance levels (α). F1-score measures the balance between precision and recall for rollout-level failure detection, while ROC-AUC quantifies the separability between successful and failed rollouts. Performance is often reported as α-marginalized (average over the α sweep) to assess robustness across different risk tolerances. Statistical significance is assessed using seed-paired t-tests on full-curve functional CP ROC-AUC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across both simulated and real-world robotic environments, demonstrating the efficacy of the SAFECAST framework.

#### 4.1. SAFECAST Improves Failure Detection During Distribution Shifts

The results indicate that SAFECAST configurations consistently enhance failure detection reliability compared to the baseline SAFE method.
- **Real-World DROID Experiments:** SAFECAST significantly improved deployment robustness for both π0 and π0-FAST policies (Table 1, Figure 2). For π0, augmented probe training (SAFECAST_TRAINAUG) yielded the largest gains, suggesting that exposing the probe to perturbed trajectories during learning improves its ability to differentiate failure. Conversely, π0-FAST benefited more from augmented calibration (SAFECAST_CALAUG), which implies that for stronger pretrained policies, calibration mismatch under deployment shifts is a more critical bottleneck. Overall, the full SAFECAST configuration, combining both augmented training and calibration, either achieved the best performance or closely matched the strongest individual augmentation strategy, demonstrating consistent robustness.
- **Simulation (LIBERO) Experiments:** Similar trends were observed in simulation (Table 1, Figure 3). Contrast-set-aware configurations consistently outperformed SAFE. OpenVLA showed the most substantial benefits from augmented probe training, while π0 achieved the strongest robustness under full SAFECAST. These findings collectively support that SAFECAST strengthens failure detection under various deployment conditions and risk tolerances.

#### 4.2. Visual + Language Contrast Sets Improve Failure Detection Robustness

Ablation studies examining the modality of contrast sets revealed that jointly incorporating both visual and language perturbations generally leads to the strongest out-of-distribution (OOD) failure detection robustness.
- **Simulation Ablations:** When evaluating visual-only, language-only, and joint visual-language contrast sets, the multimodal approach consistently produced the best performance for both OpenVLA and π0 (Figure 4). For instance, on OpenVLA, multimodal contrast sets improved F1 from 0.922 (visual-only) to 0.959. While single-modality contrast sets did improve robustness, the optimal performance was achieved when both modalities were considered together. This suggests that calibrating failure detectors for deployment benefits from covering the joint perturbation space rather than modeling visual or language shifts independently.

#### 4.3. SAFECAST Probe Training in Simulation Calibrates DROID Failure Detection

A significant finding relates to the potential for sim-to-real transfer.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The study demonstrated that training a failure probe entirely in simulation (using LIBERO-Spatial data) and then performing functional conformal calibration using a smaller dataset of real-world contrast-set trajectories (collected on the DROID setup) yielded improved failure detection robustness. This "sim-training to real-contrast-set-calibration" approach outperformed the more expensive strategy of training and calibrating a probe solely on a smaller set of real-world data (Figure 5). This result suggests a pathway for reducing the significant data collection costs associated with real-world robot experiments by leveraging simulation for initial probe training.

#### 4.4. Statistical Significance

The observed improvements in ROC-AUC were subjected to statistical testing using seed-paired t-tests over the range of α values. The results, as detailed in Appendix C (Table 6), indicate that SAFECAST configurations, particularly SAFECAST_CALAUG and the full SAFECAST, showed statistically significant improvements in ROC-AUC compared to the SAFE baseline across most policies and settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SAFECAST framework addresses a fundamental challenge in the deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies: maintaining reliability and safety under real-world distribution shifts. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Robotic System Reliability and Safety:** By providing a robust method for runtime failure detection, SAFECAST contributes directly to the safer deployment of VLA-controlled robots. In applications ranging from assistive robotics to industrial manipulation, timely and accurate failure warnings can prevent undesirable outcomes, equipment damage, or harm to humans. This is particularly crucial as VLA policies are increasingly adopted in environments with unpredictable conditions.

2. **Addressing a Critical Gap in Conformal Prediction for Robotics:** The work highlights and mitigates a key limitation of existing functional conformal prediction methods in robotics – the assumption of exchangeability between calibration and deployment data. SAFECAST demonstrates that actively incorporating diverse, perturbed trajectories via contrast sets during calibration can bridge this distribution mismatch, making conformal guarantees more relevant and effective in dynamic, real-world scenarios.

3. **Improved Generalization under Distribution Shift:** SAFECAST's ability to improve failure detection across various types of visual and language shifts—including clutter, novel objects, lighting changes, reworded instructions, and their combinations—suggests a pathway for developing more generalizable and resilient robotic perception and control systems. The finding that joint visual-language perturbations are most effective underscores the importance of considering multimodal shifts comprehensively.

4. **Cost-Effective Deployment through Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The demonstration of successful sim-to-real transfer for failure probe training presents a significant practical impact. Training failure probes predominantly in simulation, followed by calibration with a smaller set of real-world contrast-set trajectories, offers a cost-effective alternative to extensive real-world data collection. This could accelerate the development and deployment cycles for new robotic applications, making advanced VLA policies more accessible.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** SAFECAST opens avenues for future work, including:
 - **Adaptive Contrast-Set Generation:** Developing methods to automatically generate new perturbations online based on observed detector uncertainty or failure modes could lead to continuously improving and self-adapting failure detection systems.
 - **Human-in-the-Loop Integration:** Calibrated risk estimates from SAFECAST could be used to trigger human intervention, allowing supervisors to halt unsafe operations, provide corrective actions, or teleoperate the robot through challenging situations. This integration could facilitate a move from passive failure detection to interactive policy improvement, where human feedback enriches policy training and failure calibration datasets.
 - **Benchmarking and Overfitting Detection:** The concept of anti-goal perturbations, explored in the appendix, while not directly improving failure detection in this study, could serve as a useful diagnostic tool for benchmarking VLA policies and detecting overfitting in future research.

In summary, SAFECAST represents a notable contribution towards making VLA policies more robust and trustworthy for real-world robotic applications by systematically addressing the challenge of reliable failure detection under deployment-time distribution shifts.
