# Reward Design with Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Reward Design with Language Models prompts GPT-3 as a proxy reward function: a user writes one prompt containing a task description, few-shot examples or a zero-shot objective description, a parsed episode outcome, and a question; the LLM's yes/no answer is parsed into a binary reward that any RL algorithm consumes. Agents trained this way align with user objectives roughly 35% better than supervised-learned rewards.

## Problem

- Hand-designing reward functions for fuzzy human notions like a versatile negotiator is unintuitive, and balancing competing objectives leaves agents open to reward hacking.
- Learning rewards from labeled examples needs large, costly datasets; a single example cannot capture the nuance of an individual user's preferences.
- Neither approach transfers to new users: each new objective forces re-engineering the reward or re-collecting data, and RLHF finetuning still demands large user-feedback corpora.

## Method

- ==Proxy reward via prompting==: a four-part prompt (task description, user objective examples or description, parsed episode string, and a yes/no question) is fed to GPT-3, whose textual answer becomes the reward.
- Two handcrafted task-specific parsers: f maps states and actions into the episode string, g maps the LLM's output to a binary reward, keeping the framework agnostic to the RL algorithm (DQN, on-policy RL).
- Prompt regimes: ==few-shot== examples with short natural-language explanations for hard-to-define objectives, and ==zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting== that asks the model to define a known solution concept before judging outcomes.

## Results

- Across three tasks the LLM reward trained objective-aligned agents about **35%** more accurately than baselines; on DEALORNODEAL RL agent accuracy beat supervised learning by **46%**, within **4%** of true reward.
- Zero-shot matrix games: labeling accuracy rose **48%** over a no-objective prompt (**36%** with scrambled payoffs); agents hit perfect accuracy on Total Welfare and Equality, **75%** on the other objectives.
- Ablations: dropping explanations from a single-example prompt cut labeling accuracy **31.67%** and agent accuracy **28.8%**; supervised learning needed hundreds more labels to match; **10** pilot users rated aligned agents **3.72** vs **1.56** (p<0.001).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explanations, not example count, carry the signal: one example plus a rationale beats ten bare labels, because in-context learners exploit reasons a supervised model cannot read.
> - A frozen foundation model can serve as a cheap, swappable reward oracle; changing user objectives means editing a prompt rather than retraining a reward model.
> - Caution: zero-shot accuracy leans on memorized problem structure, since scrambling familiar payoff associations degrades labels; binary rewards and handcrafted parsers limit reach to text-summarizable episodes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Designing reward functions for reinforcement learning (RL) systems can be frustratingly challenging. Translating human preferences into mathematical formulations often requires significant expertise, is prone to errors, and can lead to reward hacking where agents exploit loopholes in imperfectly designed reward functions. The paper "Reward Design with Language Models" presents an innovative solution to this problem by leveraging the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) to serve as reward functions for reinforcement learning agents.

This framework, authored by Minae Kwon, Sang Michael Xie, Kalesha Bullard, and Dorsa Sadigh from Stanford University and DeepMind, allows users to specify their objectives in natural language, requiring only a few examples rather than extensive datasets or complex reward engineering. By bridging the gap between human intent and machine understanding, this approach makes RL more accessible and intuitive, while potentially reducing the risk of misaligned AI systems.

### 2. Background and Motivation

Reinforcement learning has shown impressive results across various domains, but the challenge of reward specification remains a significant hurdle to its broader application. Traditional approaches to this problem include:

1. **Manual reward engineering** - Requires expertise and is often unintuitive
2. **Learning from demonstrations** - Requires large datasets and may not capture all aspects of the desired behavior
3. **Learning from preferences** - Typically needs numerous comparisons between behaviors
4. **Reward shaping** - Can lead to behavior that optimizes shaped rewards rather than true objectives

The motivation behind using LLMs as reward functions stems from their ability to understand and process natural language, combined with their capacity for in-context learning. This approach offers several potential advantages:

- **Intuitive specification** - Users can express objectives in natural language
- **Data efficiency** - Requires only a few examples rather than large datasets
- **Flexibility** - Can accommodate diverse objectives without redesigning reward functions
- **Generalizability** - May transfer more easily to new environments or tasks

### 3. Methodology

The proposed framework integrates an LLM as a reward function within a standard RL training loop. The process works as follows:

1. **User Input**: The user provides a natural language description of their objective, potentially with a few examples (few-shot) or just a description (zero-shot).

2. **Prompt Construction**: During RL training, a prompt is constructed by combining:
 - A task description
 - The user's objective (examples or description)
 - A textual description of the agent's behavior in a recent episode
 - A question asking whether the behavior satisfies the objective

3. **LLM Evaluation**: The LLM processes this prompt and outputs a judgment (e.g., "Yes"/"No") on whether the behavior aligns with the objective.

4. **Reward Generation**: The LLM's output is parsed into a numerical reward signal (typically binary).

5. **Policy Update**: This reward signal is used to update the RL agent's policy.

6. **Iteration**: The process repeats with the agent generating new behaviors and receiving feedback through the LLM.

The framework is designed to be algorithm-agnostic, meaning it can be incorporated into various RL training methods. Importantly, it does not require fine-tuning the LLM, instead relying on its in-context learning capabilities.

### 4. Experimental Tasks

The authors evaluated their approach on three distinct tasks that represent different challenges in reward specification:

#### 4.1. Ultimatum Game

A two-player game where one player proposes how to split a sum of money, and the second player can accept or reject the offer. If rejected, both players receive nothing. The objective was to reward desirable outcomes where unfair offers are rejected.

The authors tested both few-shot prompting (using 10 examples without explanations) and a single example with an explanation. They compared their approach to supervised learning baselines.

#### 4.2. Matrix Games

Classic two-player games including versions of the Prisoner's Dilemma. The researchers tested zero-shot prompting with well-known objectives like total welfare, equal rewards, Rawlsian fairness, and Pareto optimality.

#### 4.3. DEALORNODEAL Negotiation

A more complex task where agents negotiate over items with different values. The authors defined four negotiation styles (versatile, push-over, competitive, and stubborn) and evaluated whether the LLM could guide agents to adopt these styles.

### 5. Key Findings

The research yielded several significant findings:

#### 5.1. Effectiveness of Few-Shot Prompting

The LLM maintained high accuracy in the Ultimatum Game even with just a single example and explanation. In contrast, supervised learning models required significantly more data to achieve comparable performance.

#### 5.2. Zero-Shot Capability

In Matrix Games, the LLM successfully identified well-known objectives without examples, demonstrating its ability to understand complex concepts from natural language descriptions alone.

#### 5.3. Handling Complex Behaviors

In the negotiation task, the LLM effectively guided agents to adopt different negotiation styles, outperforming supervised learning baselines in labeling accuracy and producing agents that qualitatively differed in their negotiation approaches.

### 6. Practical Implications

This research has several practical implications for AI development and deployment:

1. **Accessibility**: By allowing users to specify objectives in natural language with minimal examples, the framework makes RL more accessible to non-experts.

2. **Customization**: The approach enables easier customization of AI behaviors to individual user preferences without requiring extensive retraining.

3. **Alignment**: The method provides a new approach for aligning AI systems with human values and intentions, potentially reducing the risk of misaligned AI.

4. **Efficiency**: The data efficiency of the approach makes it practical to implement in scenarios where collecting large datasets is challenging or expensive.

### 7. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its promising results, the approach has several limitations that point to directions for future research:

1. **Binary Rewards**: The current implementation typically uses binary rewards, which may limit the nuanced guidance that can be provided to the agent. Future work could explore using the likelihood of LLM outputs as continuous rewards.

2. **Computational Cost**: Querying an LLM for each reward signal is computationally expensive, particularly for complex environments that require many training steps.

3. **Environment Representation**: The current approach relies on textual descriptions of environments, which may not capture all relevant aspects of complex scenarios. Extending to multimodal LLMs could address this limitation.

4. **Prompt Engineering**: While the approach showed robustness to prompt variations, designing effective prompts still requires some expertise. Automating prompt design could further improve accessibility.

5. **Evaluation at Scale**: The current evaluation focused on relatively simple environments. Testing in more complex, real-world scenarios is an important next step.

The authors suggest several promising directions for future work, including larger user studies, integrating multimodal foundation models, exploring more complex prompting strategies, and scaling to more challenging tasks.

### 8. Conclusion

The paper "Reward Design with Language Models" presents a significant advancement in reinforcement learning by leveraging LLMs as reward functions. This approach addresses one of the fundamental challenges in RL - specifying what we want agents to do - by allowing users to communicate objectives in natural language with minimal examples.

By combining the powerful natural language understanding of LLMs with the optimization capabilities of RL, this framework offers a new paradigm for creating AI systems that better align with human intentions. The approach demonstrates impressive data efficiency, robustness to prompt variations, and effectiveness across a range of tasks.

As AI systems become more capable and widespread, ensuring they act in accordance with human values and intentions grows increasingly important. This research provides a practical step toward more intuitive and aligned AI development, potentially making reinforcement learning more accessible while reducing the risks associated with misspecified rewards.
