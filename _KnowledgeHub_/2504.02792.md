# Unified World Models: Coupling Video and Action Diffusion for Pretraining on Large Robotic Datasets

> [!summary] Summary
> Unified World Models (UWM), developed by researchers at the University of Washington and Toyota Research Institute, presents a diffusion-based framework that unifies robot policy learning and world modeling within a single transformer, enabling it to learn from both action-labeled robotic data and action-free video datasets. The model demonstrates improved task success rates and robustness on real-world robot manipulation tasks, outperforming existing baselines.

## Problem

- Scaling imitation learning (IL) for generalist robots is bottlenecked by the high cost and scarcity of high-quality, action-annotated robot demonstration data.
- World modeling approaches, while leveraging abundant action-free video data, struggle to effectively translate temporal dynamics knowledge into robust, generalizable robotic controllers for action generation.
- The fields of imitation learning (action generation) and world modeling (observation prediction) have largely developed disparately, missing opportunities for joint learning and mutual benefit from shared features.

## Method

![[2504.02792-method.png]]
*UWM's unified architecture enables multiple inference modes from a single trained model. By manipulating independent diffusion timesteps for actions and observations, the same model can serve as a policy (predicting actions), forward dynamics model (predicting future observations), inverse dynamics model (inferring actions from state transitions), or video prediction model.*

- Introduces **UWM** (Unified World Models), a ==diffusion-based framework== that integrates an ==action diffusion process== and an ==image (video) diffusion process== into a single ==Diffusion Transformer== architecture.
- Employs ==independent diffusion timesteps== for actions and next observations, allowing for flexible inference modes (policy, forward dynamics, inverse dynamics, video prediction) by manipulating these timesteps at inference time.
- Trains the model on multitask robotic datasets with action labels and co-trains on action-free video data by fixing the action timestep to a fully noised state, effectively masking missing actions.

## Results

- **UWM** achieved the highest success rates (up to **20%** higher than baselines) on real-world manipulation tasks in the DROID dataset, showing strong performance in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings.
- Co-training UWM with action-free videos consistently improved its performance, boosting generalization and robustness (e.g., from **0.86** to **0.92** in Stack-Bowls in-distribution and **0.76** to **0.84** in out-of-distribution scenarios).
- UWM accurately predicted future image observations, demonstrated effective inverse dynamics by generating actions that adhered more closely to reference trajectories, and scaled more effectively with large pretraining datasets than other methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Maintaining independent diffusion timesteps for actions and future observations enables a single model to flexibly infer policies, forward dynamics, inverse dynamics, and perform video prediction from the same trained model.
> - Co-training with action-free video data, by effectively masking out actions through diffusion timesteps, enhances model generalization and robustness to out-of-distribution conditions without diluting the action learning signal.
> - The use of 'register tokens' in the Diffusion Transformer architecture improves performance, hypothesized to facilitate information exchange between distinct modalities (actions and latent image patches).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical bottleneck in the pursuit of generalist robots: the scarcity of high-quality, action-annotated robot data needed for imitation learning (IL), contrasting with the abundance of unannotated video data.

**Imitation Learning (IL):** Imitation learning, particularly behavior cloning (BC), has emerged as a promising paradigm for teaching robots complex behaviors from expert demonstrations. Recent advancements in multimodal generative models, like diffusion models, have significantly enhanced BC's ability to model complex action distributions. However, a fundamental limitation persists: BC's reliance on large volumes of *high-quality, on-robot demonstrations with precise action labels* makes scaling it prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. While methods like Diffusion Policy have shown success, they still fundamentally operate within the confines of action-labeled data.

**World Modeling:** An alternative paradigm, world modeling, focuses on learning approximate models of how the environment changes over time (i.e., predicting future observations). World models can be trained from much larger and more diverse datasets, including uncurated "play" data or even action-free videos. Video diffusion models, for instance, excel at generating realistic future frames. However, the challenge lies in effectively translating the temporal dynamics knowledge learned by world models into robust, generalizable robotic controllers for action generation. The fields of imitation learning (action generation) and world modeling (observation prediction) have largely developed disparately.

**Bridging the Gap:** The paper identifies this gap as a central problem. Existing approaches often either:
- Rely purely on action data, limiting scalability (e.g., traditional Diffusion Policy).
- Attempt to leverage videos but struggle with the "embodiment gap" or require complex intermediate representations (e.g., keypoint mapping, tracking human hand trajectories).
- Use two-stage processes (pre-train vision, fine-tune actions) that may not fully leverage feature sharing.
- Jointly model actions and observations but with coupled mechanisms that limit flexible inference (e.g., PAD, which uses a shared diffusion timestep and simple conditioning).

Unified World Models (UWM) directly aims to bridge this gap by offering a single, unified framework that can learn from both action-labeled robotic datasets and action-free video data. It proposes a novel way to integrate action and video diffusion processes, allowing for rich feature sharing and flexible inference modes, thereby addressing the scalability and generalization challenges faced by current robot learning methods. This work contributes to the broader trend of developing "robot foundation models" that can leverage massive, heterogeneous datasets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind developing Unified World Models (UWM) are:

1. **Scaling Imitation Learning for Generalist Robots:** The authors aim to overcome the data bottleneck in imitation learning, which typically requires expensive, high-quality, action-annotated robot demonstrations. The goal is to enable the development of more generalist robot policies capable of handling diverse tasks and environments.
2. **Leveraging Abundant Action-Free Video Data:** Recognizing the vast availability of video data depicting real-world dynamics and agent-environment interactions (even without explicit action labels), a key objective is to effectively harness this data source to improve robot policy learning.
3. **Unifying Policy Learning and World Modeling:** The paper seeks to integrate the traditionally disparate paradigms of imitation learning (predicting actions) and world modeling (predicting future states) into a single, cohesive framework. The motivation is that a unified model, by learning joint distributions and causal relationships between actions and observations, can benefit from shared features and mutual supervision, leading to more robust and generalizable policies.
4. **Enabling Flexible Inference:** By integrating these paradigms, UWM aims to create a model that is not only capable of generating actions (as a policy) but can also serve as a forward dynamics model (predicting future observations from actions), an inverse dynamics model (inferring actions from desired state transitions), and a general video prediction model. This multi-functionality aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the robot's environment and its interactions.

In essence, UWM is motivated by the vision of training large robot foundation models that can leverage the best of both action-centric and observation-centric data, leading to policies that are both effective and robust to out-of-distribution conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

UWM proposes a novel diffusion-based learning framework that unifies imitation learning and world modeling by integrating an action diffusion process and an image (video) diffusion process into a single transformer architecture. The core innovation lies in using **independent diffusion timesteps** for each modality (actions and future observations).

**Core Idea: Decoupled Diffusion Timesteps:**
- A standard diffusion model progressively adds noise to data (forward process) and learns to reverse this noise (denoising process) to generate samples.
- UWM extends this to a joint distribution of actions ($a$) and next observations ($o'$), conditioned on the current observation ($o$).
- Instead of a single, coupled diffusion timestep for both $a$ and $o'$ (as in some prior work like PAD), UWM maintains separate timesteps: $t_a$ for actions and $t_{o'}$ for next observations.
- During training, $t_a$ and $t_{o'}$ are sampled independently and randomly, exposing the model to all combinations of noise levels for both modalities. This trains a coupled noise prediction network $s_\theta(o, a_{t_a}, o'_{t_{o'}}, t_a, t_{o'})$ to predict the noise $\epsilon_a, \epsilon_{o'}$ on actions and next observations.
- The key insight is that by manipulating these independent timesteps at inference time, one can control conditioning and marginalization.
 - **Marginalization:** Setting a timestep to $T$ (fully noised) effectively "masks out" or marginalizes that variable, as the model learns to predict noise from purely Gaussian input.
 - **Conditioning:** Setting a timestep to $0$ (unnoised) effectively "conditions" on the clean input variable.

**Flexible Inference Modes:**
This decoupled timestep mechanism allows UWM to flexibly sample from various distributions using the *same trained model*:
- **Policy ($p(a|o)$):** Set $t_{o'} = T$ (marginalize out next observation) and diffuse actions from $a_T \sim N(0,I)$.
- **Forward Dynamics ($p(o'|o,a)$):** Set $t_a = 0$ (condition on action $a_0 = a$) and diffuse next observations from $o'_T \sim N(0,I)$.
- **Inverse Dynamics ($p(a|o,o')$):** Set $t_{o'} = 0$ (condition on next observation $o'_0 = o'$) and diffuse actions from $a_T \sim N(0,I)$.
- **Video Prediction Model ($p(o'|o)$):** Set $t_a = T$ (marginalize out action) and diffuse next observations from $o'_T \sim N(0,I)$.

**Training Objective:**
The model is trained using a simple modification of the standard denoising score matching objective, minimizing the weighted sum of squared differences between predicted noise and actual noise for both actions and next observations:
$ \ell(\theta) = E_{(o,a,o') \sim D, t_a,t_{o'} \sim U(0,T), \epsilon_a,\epsilon_{o'} \sim N(0,1)} [w_a \|\epsilon^\theta_a - \epsilon_a\|^2_2 + w_{o'} \|\epsilon^\theta_{o'} - \epsilon_{o'}\|^2_2 ] $
where $a_{t_a}$ and $o'_{t_{o'}}$ are noisy versions of $a$ and $o'$, and $\epsilon^\theta_a, \epsilon^\theta_{o'}$ are the noise predictions from the model.

**Architecture:**
UWM is implemented as a **Diffusion Transformer**.
- **Inputs:** Current observations ($o$), noisy actions ($a_{t_a}$), noisy next observations ($o'_{t_{o'}}$), action timestep ($t_a$), and observation timestep ($t_{o'}$).
- **Observation Encoding:** Each frame from multiple camera views is encoded using a ResNet-18 encoder.
- **Timestep Encoding:** Timesteps ($t_a, t_{o'}$) are encoded using a shared sinusoidal timestep encoder.
- **Conditioning:** The combined image features and timestep embeddings condition the transformer via **Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN)**.
- **Input to Transformer:** The core context consists of:
 - Action embeddings: Obtained by passing action chunks through a shallow MLP.
 - Image embeddings: Raw images are encoded into latent space using a frozen SDXL VAE, then patchified using a 3D convolution layer. Noising and denoising occur in this latent space.
 - **Registers:** Empirically, the authors found that adding randomly initialized "register tokens" (similar to) improves performance. They hypothesize these registers act as an intermediary medium for information exchange between distinct modalities (actions and pixels).
- **Outputs:** The transformer outputs tokens that are then decoded (MLP for actions, DiT decoder for image patches) to predict the noise for both modalities.

**Training Paradigms:**
- **Pretraining on Robot Data:** UWM is trained on datasets of $(o, a, o')$ tuples from large multitask robotic datasets.
- **Co-training on Action-Free Video Data:** For video data without action annotations ($D_{af} = \{(o_i, o_{i+1})\}$), the action timestep $t_a$ is fixed to $T$ (effectively masking actions), and missing actions are imputed with random noise. The same loss function is optimized, allowing the model to learn from vision-only data while still maintaining its action prediction capabilities for the robot data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments on both real-world robotic manipulation tasks (using the DROID dataset) and simulated environments (LIBERO benchmark), comparing UWM against Diffusion Policy (DP), PAD, and GR1.

**Real Robot Experiments (DROID Dataset):**
- **Pretraining Effectiveness (In-Distribution - ID):** UWM consistently achieved the highest success rates across all five diverse manipulation tasks (Stack-Bowls, Block-Cabinet, Paper-Towel, Hang-Towel, Rice-Cooker). It outperformed the best baseline by up to 20%. This highlights UWM's ability to absorb rich dynamic information and causal relationships from multitask datasets due to its unified action-video diffusion and independent timesteps.
- **Robustness to Distribution Shifts (Out-of-Distribution - OOD):** While all models experienced performance drops in OOD settings (e.g., visual distractions, opened cabinets), UWM maintained superior performance, demonstrating strong robustness.
- **Benefit of Co-training with Action-Free Videos:** Co-training UWM with additional action-free videos consistently improved its performance, further boosting generalization and robustness (e.g., from 0.86 to 0.92 in Stack-Bowls ID, and 0.76 to 0.84 in OOD). This validates the effectiveness of using diffusion timesteps for masking/unmasking modalities. In contrast, GR1 showed mixed results with co-training, sometimes performing worse, suggesting that its masking mechanism might dilute the action learning signal. PAD showed weak positive transfer but started from a suboptimal baseline.
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - **DP:** Failed to leverage rich pixel information from pretraining, performing significantly worse, indicating that pure behavior cloning on diverse multitask trajectories is less efficient.
 - **PAD:** Achieved the lowest success rates, attributed partly to its conditioning method (concatenating raw pixels) which limited its ability to capture conditional action distributions without increased model capacity.
 - **GR1:** Consistently delivered the second-best results, showing strong baseline performance for deterministic regressive models, but lagged behind UWM.

**Simulated Experiments (LIBERO Benchmark):**
- **Validation of Findings:** UWM also achieved the highest success rates across LIBERO evaluation tasks in OOD settings, confirming the real-world findings in a controlled benchmark.
- **Impact of Auxiliary Signal:** The results imply that UWM effectively learns from large robotic datasets due to pixel reconstruction as an auxiliary signal and the causal understanding instilled by independent diffusion timesteps.

**Analysis and Ablation Experiments:**
- **Forward Dynamics:** UWM accurately predicted future image observations conditioned on actions, closely resembling ground truth, demonstrating its effective world modeling capabilities.
- **Inverse Dynamics:** In trajectory tracking tasks, the inverse dynamics mode of UWM generated actions that adhered more closely to reference trajectories, achieving higher success rates than the policy mode when limited to trajectory length.
- **Categorized OOD Experiments:** Further OOD tests (varied lighting, backgrounds, clutter) on Stack-Bowls and Block-Cabinet showed that UWM co-trained with videos was significantly more robust than UWM pretrained on robot data alone and DP.
- **Real-World Learning from Scratch:** When trained from scratch on task-specific data, UWM and DP performed similarly. However, UWM scaled much more effectively when pretrained on large datasets, highlighting the value of its pretraining paradigm.
- **Effect of Registers:** Ablations showed that adding "registers" (redundant tokens) significantly improved UWM's performance, supporting the hypothesis that they facilitate information exchange between distinct modalities (actions and latent image patches).
- **Learning Objectives:** Reconstructing *future* observations (dynamics prediction) proved more advantageous than reconstructing *current* observations or no reconstruction (as in DP), underscoring the benefit of learning temporal dynamics.
- **Learning from Internet Videos:** Co-training UWM with Kinetics-400 and Something-Something-v2 (human activity videos) showed some positive transfer, though less than using in-domain robot videos, suggesting potential for leveraging broader internet-scale data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Unified World Models (UWM) represents a significant step forward in scalable robot learning, bridging a critical gap between imitation learning and world modeling paradigms. Its potential impact is multifaceted:

1. **Enabling Scalable Generalist Robot Policies:** By demonstrating how to effectively leverage both action-labeled and action-free video data, UWM provides a pathway towards training more robust and generalizable robot policies. This capability is crucial for developing "robot foundation models" that can adapt to novel tasks and environments without requiring exhaustive, expensive human demonstrations for every new scenario.
2. **More Data-Efficient Robot Learning:** The ability to co-train with action-free videos significantly expands the usable data pool for robot learning. This reduces the reliance on meticulously collected, action-annotated datasets, potentially lowering the barrier to entry for developing complex robotic behaviors.
3. **Deeper Causal Understanding:** By training a unified model to capture all combinations of marginal and conditional distributions (policy, dynamics, inverse dynamics, video prediction), UWM is instilled with a more profound understanding of the causal relationships between actions and visual observations. This deep understanding contributes to its superior robustness and generalization compared to methods focused solely on action prediction.
4. **Versatile Robotic Models:** The framework yields a single model capable of multiple inference modes. This versatility is valuable for debugging, planning, and control, as the same underlying representation can be interrogated for different purposes. For instance, the inverse dynamics capability could be leveraged for goal-conditioned planning, while forward dynamics aids in simulation and model-predictive control.
5. **Conceptual Unification:** UWM offers a simple yet powerful unification between two previously separate fields in robot learning. This conceptual clarity and practical implementation could inspire further research at the intersection of generative models, control, and embodied AI.
6. **Foundation for Future Work:** While the paper acknowledges limitations (e.g., bridging the human embodiment gap, artifact reduction in forward dynamics), UWM lays a strong foundation for future advancements. Its modularity and strong empirical results suggest that incorporating more advanced generative models or denser video prediction techniques could further amplify its capabilities, potentially enabling robots to learn from a much wider variety of internet-scale video data.

In summary, UWM proposes an elegant and empirically effective solution to a core challenge in robotics, offering a promising path towards more autonomous, adaptable, and data-efficient generalist robots.
