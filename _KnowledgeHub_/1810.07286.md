# At Human Speed: Deep Reinforcement Learning with Action Delay

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper handicaps game-playing RL agents with human-level reaction time by inserting a fixed action-delay queue, then restores performance with a learned action-conditional predictive model that rolls the current state forward to the moment the action actually executes. Combined with IMPALA and self-play in Super Smash Bros. Melee, predictive agents beat non-predictive ones head to head and traded games with a top-ranked professional.

## Problem

- Game-playing agents beat humans partly through superhuman speed and precision rather than intelligence, so head-to-head comparisons of information processing are unfair.
- Deep RL had not been deliberately studied under action delay; standard methods simply degrade sharply as reaction time approaches the human 250ms range.
- Classical constant-delay MDP work relies on state augmentation, which blows up exponentially in action count, and was tested only on simple single-agent grid and mountain-car tasks.

## Method

- Delay is imposed by a length-d ==action queue==, with the queue appended to the observation, giving the ==augmented-state formulation== as the baseline agent design.
- A ==residual predictive model== gates between a ==delta network== adjusting the prior state and a ==new-state network== via a learned ==forget weight==, over a shared ==GRU== core, unrolled p steps.
- Training regresses predicted states to true states (L2 on continuous, cross-entropy on discrete), shifts the policy return to start at t+d, feeds the critic the true delayed state, and uses ==V-trace==.

## Results

- IMPALA on six Atari games at **200M** frames with delays of **0-5** steps (**0-333ms**) shows final score inversely correlated with delay on all games except mixed results on Ms. Pacman.
- In self-play among delayed SSBM agents, the fully predictive **(4,4)** agent beat the partially predictive **(4,2)** and non-predictive **(4,0)** agents by a wide reward margin, indicating p=d is needed.
- Against Professor Pro (UK top, world **41st**), the non-predictive delay-6 agent went **0-6**, while predictive agents scored **3-5** at delay 6 and **2-5** at delay 7.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting the present is the right inductive bias for delay: modeling the state where the action lands beats merely handing the policy the pending action queue.
> - Partial compensation is not enough; prediction horizon should match the delay exactly, and value estimation can exploit privileged true future states during training.
> - Model unrolling costs real-time compute, capping usable delay, and treating a learning opponent as part of the environment remains an unmodeled approximation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of deep reinforcement learning has witnessed an "explosion in capabilities" over recent years, with algorithms achieving superhuman performance across a wide array of tasks. This work directly addresses a fundamental, yet often unacknowledged, aspect of these successes: the inherent advantage of AI agents in terms of speed and precision.

Historically, DRL agents have conquered various game categories:
- **Perfect information games:** AlphaGo and AlphaZero by DeepMind demonstrated mastery in Go and other board games, leveraging DRL with Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to surpass human world champions.
- **Imperfect information games:** Systems like DeepStack have achieved expert-level performance in No-Limit Poker, navigating hidden information and probabilistic reasoning.
- **Video games:** Beginning with Atari 2600 titles (pioneered by DQN), DRL has continuously pushed boundaries, extending to more complex modern games like StarCraft II (DeepMind's AlphaStar) and Dota 2 (OpenAI Five).

While these achievements are undeniably impressive, the authors argue that "AI agents are often winning on more than intelligence alone – they possess superhuman speed and precision by default." For example, an agent acting at every frame of a 60Hz game essentially has a 17ms reaction time, far beyond human capabilities. This superhuman performance can obscure a true comparison of "intelligence" – the information processing abilities – between machines and humans.

This paper positions itself as a critical step towards a "more principled way to compare" AI and human intelligence. By deliberately introducing human-level reaction time constraints, it seeks to:
- **Level the playing field:** Ensure that AI's success is attributed to sophisticated strategy and learning, rather than merely faster processing.
- **Foster relatable AI strategies:** Agents operating under human constraints might develop strategies that are more understandable, interpretable, and aesthetically pleasing to human observers, potentially leading to more engaging human-AI interactions.
- **Enhance DRL robustness for real-world applications:** Many real-world control problems (e.g., robotics, autonomous driving, networked systems) inherently involve delays. Developing DRL methods that cope with action delay is crucial for transitioning from simulated perfect environments to messy, real-world scenarios.

The work builds upon prior theoretical foundations for Delayed Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), specifically Constant-Delay MDPs (CDMDPs) as studied by Walsh et al. (2008). However, it extends this to the challenging realm of deep reinforcement learning, complex game environments (especially multi-agent ones like Super Smash Bros. Melee), and leverages neural network architectures for predictive modeling, a technique inspired by how human perception anticipates the future. Thus, it bridges theoretical computer science, practical DRL, and insights from cognitive psychology to address a fundamental challenge in AI development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate deep reinforcement learning agents that can effectively operate and achieve high performance in environments that impose human-like action delays. This involves investigating the impact of such delays on standard DRL algorithms and proposing novel architectural solutions to mitigate the observed performance degradation.

The core motivations driving this objective are multifaceted:

1. **Fairer Comparison of AI and Human Intelligence:** A central motivation is to establish a more equitable basis for comparing the "intelligence" of AI systems with that of humans. The authors highlight that current DRL successes in games often stem not solely from superior strategic reasoning, but also from the agents' default superhuman speed and precision. By artificially imposing human-like reaction time delays, the researchers aim to isolate and evaluate the information processing capabilities of AI, rather than its purely mechanical advantages. This allows for a deeper understanding of whether AI truly "understands" and strategizes as effectively as humans, or if it merely reacts faster.

2. **Developing More Relatable and Interesting AI Strategies:** When AI agents are unconstrained by human reaction times, they can exhibit behaviors that are optimal but alien, relying on split-second maneuvers that humans simply cannot execute. By introducing human constraints, the expectation is that AI agents will be forced to adopt strategies that are more similar to human play, potentially making them more engaging opponents, better teammates, or more understandable subjects for study. This aligns with broader goals in AI research focused on explainability and human-compatible AI.

3. **Addressing a Neglected Problem in DRL:** The authors explicitly state, "To our knowledge, deep reinforcement learning methods have not been deliberately applied to environments with action delay." This highlights a gap in the existing DRL literature, where most benchmarks and algorithms assume instantaneous action execution. The paper aims to empirically investigate this problem, quantify its impact, and propose a viable solution, thereby enriching the DRL paradigm for more realistic scenarios.

4. **Inspiration from Human Cognition:** A key motivation for the proposed solution stems from observations in human perception and motor control. Humans constantly anticipate future states to compensate for neural processing and motor control delays (e.g., the Flash-Lag Effect). This cognitive insight provides a powerful inductive bias for designing an AI architecture that can similarly "undo" the environmental delay, making the approach biologically plausible and potentially more robust.

5. **Enhancing Real-World Applicability:** Beyond game playing, many real-world control systems (e.g., robotics operating in dynamic environments, control systems over unreliable networks) inherently suffer from actuation or observation delays. Developing DRL agents capable of handling action delay is a crucial step towards deploying DRL in these practical, safety-critical applications, where robustness to latency is paramount.

In essence, the paper seeks to bridge the gap between AI's raw computational power and the nuanced, constrained intelligence that defines human performance, pushing DRL towards a more sophisticated and universally applicable form of artificial intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a structured approach to first identify the problem of action delay in DRL and then propose and validate a novel solution inspired by human cognition.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup and Initial Investigation

The authors introduce **fixed action delay** into standard DRL environments. This is implemented by augmenting the environment with a queue of length *d* (representing *d* delayed steps). When the agent selects an action, it is pushed onto this queue. The action at the front of the queue (chosen *d* steps ago) is then executed in the environment. Crucially, the action queue itself is provided to the agent as part of the augmented state, giving it, in principle, perfect information about its pending actions.

**Test Environments:**
- **Atari 2600 Games:** A suite of six classic Atari games (e.g., Ms. Pacman) served as initial benchmarks. Agents were trained for 200 million frames using a frame skip of 4 and varying delays from 0 to 5 agent steps.
- **Super Smash Bros. Melee (SSBM):** This fast-paced, multi-player fighting game was selected for its high reliance on reaction time, active professional scene, and previous success with DRL agents. The interface used a discrete action set and a structured state space with both discrete (e.g., character animation state) and continuous (e.g., position, velocity) components. Agents were trained against the in-game AI at its hardest difficulty, using a frame skip of 3.

**Baseline DRL Method:** The **IMPALA** (Importance Weighted Actor-Learner Architectures) algorithm, a scalable distributed DRL method known for its efficiency and strong performance, was used as the base agent architecture for all experiments.

#### 3.2. Why Delay is Difficult for Standard DRL

The initial empirical investigation confirmed that standard IMPALA agents "drastically drop in performance" as delay increases in both Atari and SSBM. The authors explain this difficulty intuitively: with delay, the agent cannot know the exact state in which its chosen action will ultimately be executed. This uncertainty is particularly problematic for:
- **Discrete state components:** Changes in these (e.g., character animation states in SSBM) can fundamentally alter transition dynamics and optimal policy.
- **Continuous state components:** Small changes in position can lead to sharp discontinuities (e.g., an attack hitting or missing).
Theoretically, adding delay inflates the state space by a factor of $|A|^d$ (where $|A|$ is the action space size), making the augmented MDP intractable for traditional methods.

#### 3.3. Proposed Solution: Neural Predictive Model

Inspired by human perception (where the brain constantly anticipates the near future to compensate for neural delays, as seen in the Flash-Lag Effect) and prior work on Model-Based Simulation (MBS) for Delayed MDPs, the authors propose endowing agents with a **neural predictive model of the environment**. This model's purpose is to "undo" the action delay by predicting the future state.

**Predictive Architecture:**
- **Action-Conditional Transition Model (P):** A neural network `P(s, a)` is trained to predict the next state `s'` given the current state `s` and an action `a`.
- **Residual-style Architecture:** `P(s, a) = F(s, a) × (s + D(s, a)) + (1 − F(s, a)) × N(s, a)`
 - `D` (Delta Network): Additively adjusts continuous components of the previous state.
 - `N` (New Network): Constructs a new state, particularly useful for discrete components.
 - `F` (Forget Network): Outputs weights in `` to smoothly interpolate between the adjusted (`s + D`) and new (`N`) states.
 - All three are feed-forward networks, and operations are component-wise.
- **Recurrent Core:** The networks sit on top of a shared Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) core. The GRU maintains hidden states (`h`) that process `s_t,i` to `s_t,i+1`, enabling the model to account for temporal dependencies.
- **Predictive Process:** For a `(d, p)` agent (d frames delay, p prediction steps), given current state `s_t` and a queue of past actions `a_t-d, ..., a_t-1`, the model generates predicted states `s_t,i+1 = P(s_t,i, a_t-d+i)` for `i = 0` to `p-1`. The agent's policy network then receives the predicted state `s_t,p` as input, allowing it to act based on an estimate of the future state where its action will be executed.

#### 3.4. Training with Delay

- **Predictive Model Training:** The model `P` is trained via regression. The distance between predicted states `s_t,i` and their true future counterparts `s_t+i` is minimized using L2 loss for continuous components and cross-entropy for discrete components.
- **Agent Training (Policy and Critic):**
 - **Returns:** For a delayed action `a_t` executed at `s_t+d`, the return `R_t+d` is used for reinforcement, rather than immediate rewards.
 - **Critic:** Although the policy uses the predicted state `s_t,d`, the critic, which is only used during training, leverages the true future state `s_t+d` for a more accurate value estimate.
 - **Policy Gradient:** V-trace, an off-policy correction algorithm, was found to be critical due to the increased sensitivity of the policy (with its *p* prediction steps) to parameter changes.

#### 3.5. Experiments and Validation

The predictive architecture was evaluated through a series of experiments in SSBM:
- **Against in-game AI:** Comparing (4,0,3), (4,2,3), and (4,4,3) agents.
- **Self-play:** A population of agents playing against each other, comparing (4,0), (4,2), and (4,4) agents.
- **Against Human Professional:** A (6,6,2) agent (trained for 3 days) and a (7,7,2) agent (trained for 1 week) were tested against "Professor Pro," a top-ranked SSBM player. A non-predictive (6,0,2) agent was also trained for comparison.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a clear demonstration of the problem, the theoretical inspiration for the solution, the detailed architectural implementation, and its empirical validation against both AI and human opponents.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings that underscore the challenge of action delay in DRL and the efficacy of the proposed predictive modeling solution.

1. **Significant Performance Drop of Standard DRL with Delay:**
 - **Atari Games:** Experiments on six Atari 2600 games (e.g., Space Invaders, Freeway, Ms. Pacman) using IMPALA agents showed a clear inverse correlation between increasing action delay (from 0 to 5 agent steps, or 0ms to 333ms) and final scores. Performance "drastically falls" for almost all games, demonstrating that standard DRL methods are "ill-equipped" to handle action delay.
 - **Super Smash Bros. Melee (SSBM):** When IMPALA agents were trained against the in-game AI, increasing delay (from 0 to 5 agent steps, each representing 50ms) led to "dramatically lowered performance" in terms of both learning speed and final rewards. This confirmed the widespread nature of the problem across different game types.

2. **Predictive Model Efficacy: Mixed Results Against Weak AI, Strong Against Difficult Policies:**
 - **Against In-Game AI:** Initially, when predictive agents (e.g., a (4,4,3) agent, meaning 4 frames delay, 4 prediction steps, 3 frame skip) were trained against the in-game SSBM AI, they performed only *slightly worse* than non-predictive agents (e.g., (4,0,3)). The authors attribute this to the in-game AI's deterministic and exploitable nature, coupled with the increased computational overhead of the predictive model. Against such a weak opponent, the faster, simpler, non-predictive agents could sometimes achieve better wall-clock time performance.
 - **In Self-Play (Against Learning Opponents):** The true strength of the predictive model became evident in self-play. A population of agents, including non-predictive (4,0), partially predictive (4,2), and fully predictive (4,4) agents, played against each other. The (4,4) agent "outperform[ed] the (4,2) in green and (4,0) in blue by a wide margin." This suggests that the predictive model is **necessary for learning more difficult and sophisticated policies** that emerge in competitive, adaptive multi-agent environments. Furthermore, the results indicated that **fully predicting through the delay (p=d) is crucial** for optimal performance, as partial prediction (p<d) was insufficient.

3. **Near Human-Level Performance Against a World-Class Professional Player:**
 - The ultimate test involved pitting the predictive agents against "Professor Pro," ranked 41st internationally in SSBM. A (6,6,2) agent (6 frames delay, 6 prediction steps, 2 frame skip) was trained for three days, and a (7,7,2) agent for one week. These agents were constrained to human-plausible reaction times (e.g., 7 frames delay at 60Hz is approximately 117ms, close to human reaction times around 250ms but still faster than average).
 - While the predictive agents were "not ultimately victorious," they "did come close to even against a very skilled human opponent." This is a highly significant result, demonstrating that DRL agents, when equipped with a predictive model, can operate effectively under human-like reaction time constraints and challenge top human professionals. In contrast, a non-predictive (6,0,2) agent trained for a week performed very poorly (0 wins, 6 losses against Professor Pro), highlighting the essential role of the predictive architecture.

In summary, the research clearly establishes action delay as a significant impediment to standard DRL performance. It then successfully demonstrates that a neural predictive model, inspired by human cognition, provides the necessary inductive bias to overcome this challenge, enabling DRL agents to learn complex strategies and achieve near-human-level performance even when constrained by realistic reaction times.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant implications across several dimensions of artificial intelligence, particularly in the fields of deep reinforcement learning, human-AI interaction, and cognitive science-inspired AI design.

1. **Re-evaluating AI "Intelligence" and Fair Comparison:** The most profound impact lies in providing a "more principled way to compare the intelligence – that is, information processing – abilities of machines and people." By leveling the playing field regarding reaction time, this work shifts the focus from AI's raw speed advantage to its strategic depth and adaptive capabilities. This is crucial for truly understanding how AI's "intelligence" stacks up against human cognition, moving beyond mere task accomplishment to a more nuanced assessment of cognitive prowess. It encourages the DRL community to consider human constraints not as limitations, but as essential parameters for meaningful evaluation.

2. **Developing More Relatable and Human-Like AI:** By forcing agents to operate within human-like temporal constraints, the research implicitly encourages the development of strategies that are more akin to human play. These strategies might be more predictable, understandable, and even aesthetically pleasing to human observers. This has positive implications for human-AI interaction, making AI opponents more engaging, teammates more collaborative, and general AI behavior more intuitive. This moves DRL closer to creating agents that are not just superhuman, but also "human-compatible" or "human-centric."

3. **Enhancing Robustness and Real-World Applicability of DRL:** Many real-world applications of DRL inherently involve delays, whether in robotics (actuation lag, sensor latency), autonomous systems (communication delays), or even human-computer interfaces. Standard DRL algorithms, which often assume instantaneous action, perform poorly in such delayed environments. This paper's demonstration of a robust solution significantly enhances DRL's potential for deployment in these complex, real-world scenarios, where dealing with latency is a fundamental requirement. It suggests a pathway for building more resilient and dependable AI controllers.

4. **Bridging Cognitive Science and Deep Learning:** The inspiration drawn from human perception, particularly the brain's predictive mechanisms to compensate for sensory and motor delays (e.g., Flash-Lag Effect), highlights the value of cross-disciplinary insights. This work exemplifies how studying human cognition can provide powerful inductive biases and architectural designs for artificial intelligence, leading to more effective and biologically plausible solutions to AI challenges. It encourages further exploration of cognitive principles to inform AI development.

5. **Foundational Work for Future DRL Research:** The paper opens several promising avenues for future research:
 - **Model-Based Planning:** Extending the predictive model beyond the immediate delay window to enable explicit, long-term planning, similar to AlphaGo's success. This could lead to more sophisticated and forward-thinking AI agents, though challenges like error compounding and computational cost need to be addressed.
 - **Opponent Modeling:** Enhancing the predictive model to explicitly account for and predict the adversary's actions. This would be crucial for complex multi-agent games with imperfect information, moving towards game theory-informed DRL.
 - **Other Temporal Action Spaces:** Investigating variable action delays and constraints on actions per minute (APM), which are also human limitations. This could lead to more efficient and adaptable AI.
 - **Improved Exploration:** Utilizing the predictive model for curiosity-driven exploration, potentially making learning more sample-efficient.

In conclusion, "At Human Speed" is a significant contribution that challenges the prevailing narrative of DRL's superhuman achievements by introducing crucial human-level constraints. Its proposed predictive modeling solution is not only empirically effective but also conceptually elegant, drawing inspiration from how humans navigate a delayed world. This work paves the way for building more intelligent, robust, and relatable AI systems, fostering a deeper understanding of intelligence itself.
