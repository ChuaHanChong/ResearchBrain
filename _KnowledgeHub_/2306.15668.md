# Physion++: Evaluating Physical Scene Understanding that Requires Online Inference of Different Physical Properties

> [!summary] Summary
> Physion++ is a new benchmark and dataset that evaluates AI systems' visual physical prediction by requiring online inference of latent mechanical properties from dynamic object interactions. Experiments reveal that current state-of-the-art AI models largely fail to infer and leverage these properties, performing significantly below human levels and exhibiting different reasoning patterns.

## Problem

- Existing physical reasoning benchmarks often focus on observable cues or limited scenarios, overlooking the critical need for AI to infer unobservable latent physical properties (e.g., mass, friction, elasticity).
- AI models currently lack the human-like ability to infer these hidden mechanical properties from observed dynamic interactions, limiting their generalizability and robustness in complex environments.
- Previous benchmarks, including the original Physion, kept mechanical properties uniform, thus not requiring models to deduce differences in material composition or internal object states.

## Method

- Physion++ introduces nine physical scenarios across four fundamental mechanical properties (mass, friction, elasticity, deformability) within the **ThreeDWorld** simulator.
- Each video stimulus includes an "inference phase" where properties are revealed through interaction, followed by a "prediction phase" requiring an Object Contact Prediction (OCP) task.
- "Paired trials" are designed to be visually identical in the prediction phase but diverge in outcome due to differing latent properties, forcing models to utilize the inference phase.

## Results

- All evaluated AI models perform only slightly above chance (around 50-55% accuracy) on Physion++, falling well below human accuracy (60%) across all mechanical properties.
- Most models show no significant performance improvement when an inference phase (with property cues) is present versus absent, indicating an inability to infer or utilize latent mechanical properties.
- Model outputs exhibit poor Pearson correlation with average human responses (e.g., best model r=0.12 compared to human split-half r=0.37), suggesting divergent computational strategies between AI and humans.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current state-of-the-art AI models, including pixel-wise prediction and object-centric representation models, generally fail to effectively infer and leverage latent mechanical properties from visual observations.
> - AI models' reasoning strategies for physical understanding differ substantially from human cognition, demonstrated by low correlations between model outputs and average human responses, and contrasting performance on easy versus hard trials.
> - While models encoding "objectness" perform better, architectural limitations rather than just data quantity appear to be a primary bottleneck for learning complex physical inferences, and unified dynamics models struggle across diverse properties.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence (AI) has made remarkable strides in areas like computer vision and natural language processing. However, endowing AI systems with a robust understanding of the physical world, akin to human intuitive physics, remains a significant challenge. This work directly addresses this challenge by focusing on a critical, yet often overlooked, aspect: the inference of latent physical properties.

Existing benchmarks for physical reasoning (e.g., KITTI, Human3.6M, CLEVRER, IntPhys, PHYRE, CRAFT, CoPhy) have pushed the boundaries of AI models, evaluating their ability to localize objects, recognize events, or predict dynamics based on observable cues. However, as the authors rightly point out, many of these benchmarks either:
1. Are unrealistic or focus on single, limited scenarios.
2. Do not contain diverse dynamics or physical properties.
3. Fail to necessitate the understanding that objects possess *individuated latent properties* (e.g., mass, friction, elasticity, deformability) that are not directly visible but profoundly influence physical outcomes. Instead, models might rely on visual appearance alone, which can be ambiguous in real-world scenarios.

The original Physion benchmark (Bear et al., 2021) was a crucial step forward, offering a suite of stimuli generated from realistic simulations across a wide range of dynamic scenes. While Physion effectively evaluated physical prediction based on object shapes and arrangements, it deliberately kept mechanical properties uniform across objects. This meant models weren't required to infer differences in material composition or internal object states.

"Physion++" emerges precisely to fill this gap. It explicitly extends the Physion framework by introducing scenarios where accurate physical prediction *mandates* the efficient inference of latent mechanical properties from observed interactions. This is a crucial distinction, as humans constantly infer such properties – observing a box sink into a couch cushion allows us to infer it's heavy, influencing our subsequent actions. Without this capability, AI agents cannot achieve truly general physical scene understanding, limiting their ability to interact intelligently and safely in complex, dynamic environments (e.g., in robotics or autonomous systems).

Therefore, Physion++ represents a significant evolution in the benchmarking of intuitive physics in AI. It moves beyond superficial visual cues to probe deeper, more human-like reasoning about the underlying physics, pushing the field towards developing models that can learn and leverage these crucial, hidden attributes of objects. This work aligns with the broader goal of building AI systems with common sense and a robust understanding of causality, fundamental for advanced intelligence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and validate **Physion++**, a novel dataset and benchmark designed to rigorously evaluate artificial systems' visual physical prediction capabilities under conditions where accurate predictions *require online inference of latent physical properties*.

The core motivations behind this work stem from a recognized deficiency in current AI models and existing benchmarks for physical scene understanding:
1. **Addressing the Latent Property Inference Gap:** While significant progress has been made in visual and video prediction, current benchmarks often do not demand that models infer unobservable, latent physical properties (such as mass, friction, elasticity, or deformability) from observed object interactions. Models might succeed by memorizing visual patterns or relying on directly observable features, rather than understanding the underlying physics.
2. **Mimicking Human Intuition:** Humans effortlessly infer these latent properties by observing how objects move and interact with their environment or other objects (e.g., a rolling ball's trajectory suggests its bounciness, or a fluid's flow indicates its viscosity). An artificial system aiming for general physical scene understanding must possess a similar capability to interpret these dynamic cues.
3. **Enhancing Generalizability and Robustness:** Without the ability to infer and leverage latent properties, AI models are brittle; they may fail when confronted with novel objects or scenarios where superficial visual cues are misleading or insufficient. Developing this inference capability is crucial for creating more generalizable and robust AI systems applicable in diverse real-world settings, such as robotic manipulation, autonomous driving, or even virtual reality.
4. **Moving Beyond Direct Observables:** Previous benchmarks, including the original Physion, largely focused on properties that are either directly visible (shape, size, color) or implicitly derivable from arrangements. Physion++ specifically targets properties that *must be inferred* from dynamic interactions, pushing models to a deeper level of physical comprehension.
5. **Comparing AI to Human Cognition:** A key motivation is to not only test models against a challenging new benchmark but also to compare their performance and reasoning strategies against human participants. This allows researchers to ascertain not just *if* models can make predictions, but *how* their predictions align with human intuitive physics, revealing whether they are learning human-like competencies or relying on distinct, non-human-like mechanisms.

In essence, the paper aims to provide a more comprehensive and challenging testbed for intuitive physics, driving the development of AI models that can infer the "hidden mechanics" of the world, thereby moving closer to human-level physical intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves the creation of a novel dataset, Physion++, comprehensive evaluation of state-of-the-art AI models, and a comparative study with human performance.

#### 3.1. Dataset Design and Generation (Physion++)

The Physion++ dataset is built upon the ThreeDWorld (TDW) simulator, a Unity3D-based environment known for generating realistic physical simulations. The dataset features:
- **Nine Scenarios, Four Mechanical Properties:** The dataset focuses on four fundamental mechanical properties—mass, friction, elasticity, and deformability—across nine distinct physical scenarios (e.g., Mass-Dominoes, Mass-Waterpush, Elasticity-Wall, Friction-Slide, Deform-Roll). These scenarios are designed to necessitate property inference for accurate prediction.
- **Video Stimuli Structure:** Each stimulus is a single video composed of two main phases:
 - **Inference Phase:** An initial segment where objects interact in a way that provides visual cues about their latent mechanical properties. For example, observing how a domino reacts to being hit reveals its mass.
 - **Prediction Phase:** Following the inference phase, the scene is often reconfigured, and models are tasked with an Object Contact Prediction (OCP) task: predicting whether a cued "red" object will contact a "yellow" object after the observed video ends.
- **Transition Phase:** For scenarios where the inference event irrevocably alters the scene's configuration, a "transition phase" is introduced. A curtain briefly occludes the scene, objects are rearranged for the prediction phase while hidden, and then the curtain reopens. This ensures the prediction task starts from a fresh configuration while preserving the inferred properties.
- **"Paired Trials" Design:** To prevent models from relying on superficial visual cues, Physion++ includes "paired trials." These are visually identical in their initial prediction phase frame but diverge in outcome due to differing latent physical properties, thus forcing models to infer the properties from the preceding inference phase.
- **Dataset Splits:** The dataset is divided into three sets:
 - **Dynamics Training Set:** 2,000 trials per mechanical property, without OCP labels, used for pretraining models to learn general environment dynamics (e.g., future frame prediction).
 - **Readout Fitting Set:** 192 trials per mechanical property, with OCP labels, used to train a simple classifier (logistic regression on learned representations) for the final YES/NO contact prediction.
 - **Testing Set:** 192 trials per mechanical property, with OCP labels, used for final evaluation of models and human participants.

#### 3.2. Model Evaluation

A diverse set of state-of-the-art video prediction and physical reasoning models were evaluated, categorized by their architectural principles:
1. **Pixel-wise Prediction Models:** MCVD (Masked Conditional Video Diffusion), which learns visual encoders and physics prediction through pixel-level output.
2. **Supervised Pretraining + MLPs:** pRESNET-mlp, pVGG-mlp, and pDEIT-mlp, which use encoders pretrained on ImageNet, followed by MLPs trained on the benchmark data.
3. **Object-Centric Representation Models:** SlotFormer and ALOE, which learn physics prediction on top of self-supervised object-centric representations.
4. **3D Particle-based Graph Neural Network (Oracle Model):** DPI-Net, which takes ground-truth 3D state and latent properties as input. This model serves as an upper bound, representing a system with perfect observability and explicit physical knowledge, without a visual encoder.

**Training and Readout Protocols:**
- **Training Dynamics:** Models were trained with both 'separate' (dynamics learned for each property scenario) and 'unified' (joint dynamics for all scenarios) protocols.
- **Testing Conditions:** Models were evaluated under three conditions to isolate the role of property inference:
 - **'w/ property':** Standard videos including the inference phase.
 - **'w/o property':** Videos truncated before the critical inference interactions, making property inference impossible.
 - **'fully observed':** Videos extended to include the ground-truth outcome, testing the quality of learned representations.

**Evaluation Pipeline:** Models first learned dynamics on the training set. Then, a readout model (logistic regression) was trained on features extracted from model rollouts on the readout fitting set to perform the OCP task. Finally, performance was assessed on the testing set.

#### 3.3. Human Experiments

- **Participants:** 200 participants (50 per mechanical property) were recruited from Prolific.
- **Task:** Participants viewed videos from the testing set and predicted (YES/NO) whether the red object would contact the yellow object.
- **Metrics:** Overall accuracy and Pearson correlation between model outputs and average human responses, including analysis on 'easy' (human accuracy > 66.7%) and 'hard' (human accuracy < 33.3%) trials.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments on Physion++ revealed significant insights into the capabilities and limitations of current AI models in physical scene understanding, particularly concerning latent property inference, and highlighted a substantial gap between AI and human performance.

1. **General Model Performance is Sub-human:**
 - All video prediction models (MCVD, pDEIT-mlp, pRESNET-mlp, pVGG-mlp, ALOE, SlotFormer) consistently failed to achieve human-level prediction accuracy across all four mechanical properties. Most models performed only slightly above chance (around 50-55%), with overall accuracy for humans at 60%.
 - The particle-based DPI-Net, which operates on ground-truth 3D states and properties (acting as an oracle), achieved the best overall performance (60.2% accuracy) when trained separately with property inference, performing comparably to humans. This suggests that robust representations of 3D states and explicit property knowledge are crucial.

2. **Failure to Utilize Latent Property Inference:**
 - A critical finding was that most models, when presented with the 'w/ property' videos (including the inference phase), performed similarly to when presented with 'w/o property' videos (where the inference phase was absent). This indicates that these models are generally *not effectively inferring or leveraging the latent mechanical properties* from the dynamic cues provided in the initial video segments.
 - The only exception was DPI-Net in the mass scenario, where performance significantly improved from 60.1% (w/o property) to 75.9% (w/ property), suggesting some implicit learning of mass inference within its explicit 3D framework.
 - An ablation study further confirmed this: adding explicit property estimation as an auxiliary task during training did not improve OCP performance, reinforcing that models lack access to these properties even when prompted.

3. **Architectural Insights:**
 - Models that encode "objectness" (e.g., SlotFormer, ALOE) generally performed better than those not explicitly trained on object-relevant tasks. This aligns with prior findings that object-centric representations aid physical reasoning.
 - Training dynamics separately for each mechanical property ('separate' protocol) typically led to better performance than learning a unified dynamics model for all scenarios ('unified' protocol), suggesting that current models struggle to generalize a single physical understanding across diverse properties.

4. **Dissimilarity in Human-Model Reasoning:**
 - **Low Correlation:** A stark result was the *poor Pearson correlation* between model outputs and average human responses (e.g., SlotFormer, the best correlating model, achieved r=0.12 compared to a human split-half correlation of r=0.37). This indicates that even when models achieve similar accuracy to humans, they are not making predictions in a human-like way; they are likely succeeding or failing on different sets of trials.
 - **Performance on Easy vs. Hard Trials:** Models consistently underperformed humans on "easy" trials (where human accuracy was high) but *outperformed humans on "hard" trials* (where human accuracy was low). This further supports the conclusion that models are relying on different computational strategies than humans. Humans might be better at discerning subtle cues for intuitive physics, while models might be leveraging statistical regularities that are not necessarily aligned with human perception.
 - **Property-Specific Discrepancies:** Models performed better than humans in the deformability category, but worse in mass, friction, and elasticity. This suggests potential differences in how humans and models prioritize or process information related to various physical properties.

5. **Impact of Training Data Size:** Performance of tested models generally improved with more training data (from 200 to 2000 trials per property), but eventually saturated, implying that architectural limitations, rather than just data quantity, are a primary bottleneck for learning these complex physical inferences.

In summary, Physion++ unequivocally demonstrates that current state-of-the-art AI models largely fail to infer and leverage latent physical properties from visual observations, exhibiting significantly different reasoning patterns compared to humans.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Physion++ is highly significant for the field of artificial intelligence and cognitive science, carrying several potential impacts:

1. **Elevating the Benchmark for Intuitive Physics:** Physion++ provides a crucial and more challenging benchmark for evaluating physical scene understanding in AI. By explicitly requiring the inference of latent mechanical properties (mass, friction, elasticity, deformability), it pushes beyond previous benchmarks that primarily focused on observable features or direct dynamics. This shifts the research focus towards a deeper, more sophisticated form of physical intelligence.

2. **Uncovering Critical AI Limitations:** The findings unequivocally highlight a major limitation of current state-of-the-art video prediction and object-centric models: their inability to reliably infer and utilize latent physical properties from dynamic visual cues. This demonstrates that despite impressive progress in general video tasks, these models still lack a fundamental aspect of human-like intuitive physics. This revelation is crucial for guiding future research efforts.

3. **Guiding Future Model Development:**
 - The observed low correlation between model and human predictions, even on similarly performing trials, suggests that current AI models are not developing human-like reasoning strategies for physical understanding. Future work needs to focus not just on accuracy, but on *how* models arrive at their predictions, perhaps by incorporating more cognitive science-inspired inductive biases or explicit modules for property inference.
 - The superior performance of the ground-truth-aware DPI-Net underscores the importance of robust 3D representations and explicit physical states. This implies that future visual models may benefit from integrating more explicit physics engines or learning highly granular 3D representations that facilitate property estimation.
 - The struggle of unified models compared to separately trained ones suggests that current architectures might lack the flexibility to learn a single, generalized physics model for diverse properties simultaneously. This calls for more compositional or hierarchical learning approaches.

4. **Advancing Robotics and Embodied AI:** The ability to infer latent physical properties is paramount for robotics. A robot must understand if an object is heavy, slippery, or deformable to safely and effectively interact with it, whether picking it up, pushing it, or predicting its future motion. Physion++ directly addresses this need, offering a benchmark that can accelerate the development of more intelligent and adaptable robotic agents.

5. **Fostering Interdisciplinary Research:** The collaboration between cognitive scientists and AI researchers, as evidenced by the author list and the comparative human study, is a strong example of how interdisciplinary work can drive significant progress. Physion++ offers a common ground for both fields to understand the mechanisms of physical reasoning in both natural and artificial intelligence.

6. **Open Science and Reproducibility:** The public release of the Physion++ dataset, including training, readout, and testing sets, along with human data, is a significant contribution. This promotes transparency, reproducibility, and collaborative development within the research community, allowing other researchers to benchmark their models against a common, challenging standard.

In conclusion, Physion++ is not just another dataset; it's a diagnostic tool that reveals a profound gap in current AI capabilities regarding intuitive physics. By meticulously designing scenarios that demand latent property inference, the authors have provided a critical compass for navigating the next generation of AI research towards truly robust and human-like physical intelligence.
