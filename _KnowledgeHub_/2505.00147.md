# AdaptMI: Adaptive Skill-based In-context Math Instruction for Small Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Princeton Language and Intelligence developed AdaptMI and AdaptMI+, adaptive methods that enhance Small Language Models' in-context learning for mathematical reasoning by dynamically selecting skill-based examples to prevent cognitive overload. The approach improves accuracy by up to 6% on math benchmarks compared to non-adaptive skill-based strategies.

## Problem

- Small Language Models (SLMs) exhibit a significant performance gap in in-context learning (ICL) compared to larger models, particularly in complex reasoning tasks like mathematics.
- Skill-based prompting, effective for Large Language Models (LLMs), often yields minimal or even negative impacts on SLM performance due to "cognitive overload" on simpler problems.
- SLMs frequently produce unnecessarily long and erroneous responses when presented with inappropriate or excessive skill-based instructions for questions they could otherwise solve easily.

## Method

- A two-stage adaptive framework, ==AdaptMI==, classifies questions as "easy" or "difficult" for a specific SLM using a ==process reward model (PRM)== to analyze step-by-step reasoning.
- For "difficult" questions, AdaptMI applies ==skill-based in-context examples==, while for "easy" questions, it reverts to fixed, simpler examples.
- ==AdaptMI+== further refines this by using a large LLM to analyze initial failed responses on difficult questions, identifying specific *missing* skills, and then selecting in-context examples only for those targeted skills.

## Results

- AdaptMI and AdaptMI+ consistently surpassed non-adaptive baselines, with AdaptMI+ achieving accuracy improvements of up to **6%** over naive skill-based strategies on math benchmarks.
- Performance gains were most pronounced for smaller models (e.g., Qwen2.5-**1.5B**, Llama-3.2-**1B**), highlighting the methods' effectiveness in enhancing resource-constrained SLMs.
- An iterative version of AdaptMI+ demonstrated continuous accuracy improvements, boosting performance by up to **7.2%** for the Qwen2.5-1.5B model on the MATH dataset.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Small Language Models (SLMs) experience "cognitive overload," where providing excessive or inappropriate skill-based information for easy problems degrades performance, leading to longer and erroneous responses.
> - Adaptive selection of in-context examples, rather than uniform application, is critical for optimizing SLM performance, mirroring principles from human adaptive teaching.
> - Process reward models, which evaluate step-by-step reasoning, are more effective than outcome reward models for accurately identifying reasoning flaws and classifying question difficulty for SLMs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of in-context learning (ICL), small language model (SLM) optimization, and the application of cognitive science principles to artificial intelligence. It addresses a critical challenge in the field: enabling smaller, more efficient language models to achieve performance levels closer to their much larger counterparts, especially in complex reasoning tasks like mathematics.

In-context learning (ICL), pioneered by models like GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), has revolutionized how language models adapt to new tasks without gradient updates, by simply conditioning on demonstrations within the prompt. However, ICL is recognized as an "emergent property" (Wei et al., 2022), with its effectiveness strongly correlating with model scale. This has left Small Language Models (SLMs) struggling to generalize effectively from in-context examples, often exhibiting high sensitivity to prompt construction. The current paper directly tackles this "performance gap" for SLMs.

A recent line of work (Didolkar et al., 2024a;b) demonstrated that ICL performance could be significantly enhanced for *large language models (LLMs)* by leveraging their "metacognitive abilities" to predict required skills for a problem and then selecting in-context examples tailored to these skills. This "skill-based prompting" approach aligns with human pedagogical strategies that emphasize structured, skill-oriented guidance (Kirschner et al., 2006; Sweller, 2011). However, the authors note that the gains from this strategy on SLMs have been minimal, sometimes even detrimental.

This paper critically investigates *why* skill-based prompting fails for SLMs and proposes an adaptive solution. By identifying "cognitive overload" in SLMs—where providing too much or inappropriate skill-based information on easy problems degrades performance—the authors draw a direct parallel to human learning theories (Diaconis & Mazur, 2003; Liu et al., 2024b). This interdisciplinary connection is a key aspect, bringing insights from educational psychology (e.g., Adaptive Teaching, Randi, 2022; Cognitive Load Theory, Sweller, 2011) to AI system design.

Furthermore, the methodology integrates the use of reward models (Xiong et al., 2024) for assessing problem-solving quality, a technique commonly employed in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and advanced model evaluation. By building upon these established techniques—ICL, skill-based prompting, reward modeling, and cognitive theory—the paper offers a nuanced and innovative approach to optimize SLM performance, moving beyond simple scaling laws to more intelligent, context-aware instruction strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **significantly improve the in-context learning (ICL) performance of Small Language Models (SLMs)**, particularly for mathematical reasoning tasks. The authors identify a crucial limitation: while skill-based in-context example selection has proven effective for larger language models (LLMs), its benefits for SLMs are surprisingly negligible, often leading to minimal gains or even performance degradation.

This observation serves as the primary motivation. The authors aim to understand the root cause of this disparity. Their investigation reveals that providing skill-based instructions indiscriminately to SLMs can be counterproductive for "easy" questions—problems the SLM could solve with simpler prompts. This leads to what they term "cognitive overload," where the SLM generates unnecessarily long and erroneous responses, akin to a human student "overthinking" a simple problem when presented with overly complex guidance.

Motivated by this insight and drawing inspiration from human adaptive teaching strategies, the paper sets out to develop a more intelligent and selective approach to skill-based instruction. The specific objectives are:
- To empirically demonstrate and analyze why existing skill-based prompting methods are ineffective or detrimental for SLMs on easy questions.
- To introduce **AdaptMI**, a two-stage adaptive method that applies skill-based in-context examples only to questions identified as "difficult" for the SLM, while retaining simpler instructions for "easy" questions.
- To further develop **AdaptMI+**, a refined version of AdaptMI that specifically targets *missing* skills in the SLM's initial failed responses for difficult questions, mirroring how humans benefit most from feedback on specific errors.
- To evaluate the efficacy of AdaptMI and AdaptMI+ across various SLMs and math benchmarks, demonstrating consistent and substantial performance gains.
- To explore the potential of an iterative version of AdaptMI+ for progressively guiding SLMs to tackle increasingly challenging problems.

Ultimately, the motivation is to bridge the ICL performance gap between LLMs and SLMs, making SLMs more robust, efficient, and capable problem-solvers by optimizing how they receive and process in-context information, thereby unlocking their full reasoning potential.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the AdaptMI methodology is a two-stage adaptive approach to selecting in-context examples for Small Language Models (SLMs), designed to address the observed "cognitive overload" issue.

#### 3.1. Preliminary Concepts

The work operates within the k-shot in-context learning (ICL) paradigm, where *k* examples are provided in the prompt. Baselines include:
- **Fixed k-shot examples:** A static set of *k* examples used for all queries.
- **Random k-shot examples:** *k* randomly selected examples for each query.
- **Skill-based k-shot examples:** Builds on Didolkar et al. (2024a). A frontier LLM (GPT-4o-mini) is used to predict the high-level skills required for a given question. An "Example-Bank" maps skills to annotated examples. For each query, *k* examples are chosen by randomly picking one example for each predicted skill.

#### 3.2. Stage 1: Detection of Easy and Difficult Questions via Reward Filtering

This crucial stage classifies each evaluation question `q` as "easy" or "difficult" for a specific SLM. Instead of relying on ground truth (which would limit applicability), the method employs a **process reward model (PRM)**.
- **Process Reward Model:** The PRM (specifically, RLHFlow/Llama3.1-8B-PRM-Mistral-Data) scores the SLM's step-by-step response to a question, initially prompted with fixed k-shot examples. This yields a set of reward scores for each step `{r_q,1..., r_q,t}`.
- **Threshold-based Filtering:** Questions are classified as difficult if:
 - The final step has a low reward (`r_q,t ≤ τ1`).
 - The average reward across all steps is low (`(1/t) Σ r_q,i ≤ τ1`).
 - Any intermediate step has a low reward (`∃i < t s.t. r_q,i ≤ τ2`).
 - Otherwise, the question is classified as easy. This allows detection of flawed reasoning processes even if the final answer might coincidentally be correct.

#### 3.3. Stage 2: Skill-based Selection of In-Context Examples

Based on the classification from Stage 1, different prompting strategies are applied:
- **AdaptMI:**
 - For **difficult questions (Q_difficult)**: Uses skill-based k-shot examples.
 - For **easy questions (Q_easy)**: Uses fixed k-shot examples.
- **AdaptMI+:** This is a more targeted version, drawing inspiration from focused feedback in human learning.
 - For **difficult questions (Q_difficult)**: A large LLM (GPT-4o-mini) analyzes the SLM's initial (failed) response and the problem's required skills (from `Skill-Map(q)`) to identify the *specific skills missing* in the SLM's solution. Then, in-context examples are selected *only for these identified missing skills*.
 - For **easy questions (Q_easy)**: Uses fixed k-shot examples.

**Iterative AdaptMI+:** The paper also extends AdaptMI+ into an iterative loop. In each iteration, the SLM makes an inference, its responses are analyzed to detect difficult questions, and GPT-4o-mini selects new, targeted skill-based examples for these still-difficult questions. This process repeats, progressively pushing the SLM to solve harder problems.

**Experimental Settings:**
- **Datasets:** MATH (7.5k training, 5k test) and GSM8K (7.4k training, 1.3k test), common benchmarks for mathematical reasoning. GPT-4o-mini is used for skill annotation of both training and test sets.
- **Models:** Five instruction-tuned SLMs: Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.2-1B, and Llama-3.2-3B.
- **Evaluation:** 5-shot ICL performance, measured by Pass@1 accuracy (temperature 0.0). Comparison includes Consistency@5 voting (Wang et al., 2022) as a strong baseline.
- **Reward Model & LLM:** RLHFlow/Llama3.1-8B-PRM-Mistral-Data for reward modeling (τ1=0.85, τ2=0.7). GPT-4o-mini for skill annotation and identifying missing skills in AdaptMI+.

**Ablation Studies:** Extensive ablations are performed to understand the impact of:
- Different combinations of in-context examples for easy vs. difficult questions.
- The choice of threshold values (τ1, τ2) for the reward model.
- Comparison of process reward models vs. outcome reward models and simple heuristic filtering methods.
- Effectiveness of natural language instructions from LLMs compared to few-shot examples.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yields several significant findings that underscore the efficacy and rationale behind the AdaptMI and AdaptMI+ methods:

1. **The "Cognitive Overload" Phenomenon in SLMs:** A key discovery is that while skill-based in-context example selection benefits LLMs, it can **harm SLM performance on easy questions**. The authors observe an average 4% performance drop on easy questions (across 5 SLMs on MATH) when using skill-based selection compared to non-skill-based strategies. Fine-grained analysis reveals that skill-based examples lead SLMs to produce **longer, often erroneous responses** for easy questions, mimicking "overthinking" in humans. Conversely, skill-based examples *do* boost performance on genuinely difficult questions.

2. **Superiority of Adaptive Methods (AdaptMI and AdaptMI+):**
 - **Consistent Accuracy Gains:** AdaptMI and AdaptMI+ consistently outperform all non-adaptive Pass@1 baselines (fixed, random, and naive skill-based examples) across all five tested SLMs and both MATH and GSM8K datasets.
 - **Significant Improvement:** AdaptMI+ achieves particularly notable improvements, boosting accuracy by **up to 6%** over naive skill-based strategies. AdaptMI also yields substantial gains, up to 3.6%.
 - **Strong Baselines:** These adaptive methods often perform on par with or even surpass Consistency@5 voting, a more robust baseline that involves multiple generations and majority voting.
 - **Impact on Smaller Models:** The performance gains are most pronounced for the smallest models (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct), highlighting their effectiveness in enhancing the capabilities of resource-constrained models.
 - **Targeted Skill Identification for Stronger Models:** For higher-performing SLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct), AdaptMI+ (which identifies *missing* skills) shows greater effectiveness than AdaptMI (which applies skill-based examples for all difficult questions), suggesting that more intelligent and targeted instruction is crucial for models with higher inherent capabilities.

3. **Effectiveness of Iterative AdaptMI+:** Extending AdaptMI+ into an iterative loop demonstrates its potential for **continuous and progressive accuracy gains**. The iterative approach consistently improves reasoning performance on MATH for all three Qwen SLMs, showing gains of up to 7.2% for the 1.5B model. This suggests a powerful mechanism for gradually guiding SLMs to tackle increasingly complex problem sets.

4. **Ablation Study Insights:**
 - **Optimal Example Combination:** The ablation studies confirm that the chosen combination of "difficult+skill-based; easy+fixed" examples is consistently the best-performing configuration, highlighting the importance of adaptive example selection for SLMs.
 - **Sensitivity to Thresholds:** The performance of AdaptMI is sensitive to the reward model's filtering thresholds (τ1, τ2). The chosen values (τ1=0.85, τ2=0.7) are shown to be optimal for classification accuracy and downstream AdaptMI performance.
 - **Process vs. Outcome Reward Models:** Process reward models (scoring step-by-step reasoning) are found to be superior to outcome reward models (scoring only the final answer) for classifying question difficulty.
 - **Limitations of Natural Language Instructions:** Unstructured natural language feedback from LLMs (as an alternative to few-shot examples for difficult questions) did not provide any benefit, suggesting that SLMs tend to ignore such long, unstructured contextual information. This underscores the value of structured, example-based instruction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AdaptMI and AdaptMI+ methods represent a significant step forward in optimizing Small Language Models (SLMs) for complex reasoning tasks, with broad implications for both research and practical applications.

1. **Enhanced SLM Capabilities and Accessibility:** By demonstrating that SLMs can achieve substantial performance gains in mathematical reasoning without additional fine-tuning, this work makes high-quality AI reasoning more accessible. It enables SLMs to perform tasks previously thought to be exclusive to much larger, computationally expensive models, facilitating their deployment in resource-constrained environments such as edge devices, mobile applications, or scenarios where rapid, cost-effective inference is critical. This democratization of advanced AI capabilities could drive innovation across various industries.

2. **Deeper Understanding of ICL and Model Cognition:** The paper offers novel empirical evidence for "cognitive overload" in SLMs, drawing a compelling analogy to human learning. This finding not only explains a crucial limitation of current skill-based prompting for smaller models but also deepens our theoretical understanding of how language models process and learn from in-context information. It suggests that simply providing "more" information, even if relevant, isn't always better, and that the *adaptive delivery* of information is key for optimal learning in AI systems, much like in human pedagogy.

3. **Practical and Cost-Effective Solution:** AdaptMI and AdaptMI+ provide a readily implementable, two-stage framework that leverages existing frontier LLMs (like GPT-4o-mini) for "metacognitive" tasks (skill prediction, missing skill identification) and reward models for difficulty assessment. This external, "teacher-like" guidance allows SLMs to improve without requiring expensive retraining or fine-tuning, thus offering a practical and cost-efficient solution for practitioners.

4. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens several promising avenues for future research:
 - **Adaptive Training of SLMs:** The findings on adaptive instruction could be extended to guide the *training* process of SLMs, potentially leading to more efficient distillation techniques, curriculum learning approaches, or self-supervised learning strategies where SLMs learn to adaptively select their own learning materials. This could significantly impact the development of next-generation SLMs.
 - **Generalization to Other Domains:** While demonstrated on math, the principles of adaptive instruction and mitigating cognitive overload are likely applicable to other complex reasoning tasks and even general NLP benchmarks.
 - **Refining Difficulty Detection:** Further research into more robust, efficient, and model-agnostic methods for detecting question difficulty (e.g., improved heuristics or lightweight proxy models) could enhance the broad applicability of AdaptMI.
 - **Multi-Modal AdaptMI:** Extending adaptive instruction to multi-modal SLMs could unlock their potential in domains requiring reasoning across text, images, and other data types.

In conclusion, "AdaptMI" not only provides a highly effective method for boosting SLM performance but also contributes to our fundamental understanding of in-context learning, offering insights that bridge artificial intelligence and cognitive science. Its impact will likely be felt in the development of more intelligent, efficient, and accessible AI systems.
