# Improving Robot-Centric Learning from Demonstration via Personalized Embeddings

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Tech developed MIND MELD, a framework that improves robot-centric Learning from Demonstration by meta-learning personalized embeddings to correct suboptimal and varied human feedback. The system improved human-provided corrective labels by up to 63% and captured individual demonstrator styles, correlating with measurable stylistic tendencies.

## Problem

- Robot-centric Learning from Demonstration (LfD) methods, such as DAgger, are susceptible to suboptimal and inconsistent feedback provided by human teachers.
- Human demonstrators exhibit heterogeneous teaching styles, making it difficult for robots to generalize or consistently learn from diverse forms of corrective input.
- Existing LfD approaches often treat human feedback generically, failing to model or adapt to specific stylistic tendencies of individual demonstrators.

## Method

- The Mutual Information-driven Meta-learning from Demonstration (MIND MELD) framework uses a neural network with a Bidirectional LSTM Encoder, a Prediction Subnetwork, and a Mutual Information Subnetwork.
- It learns a d-dimensional personalized embedding for each demonstrator, capturing their unique corrective style by maximizing mutual information between the embedding and the sequence of corrective feedback.
- The framework trains network parameters and personalized embeddings simultaneously using a combined loss, enabling it to predict and apply corrections to human-provided labels, thus approximating ground truth.

## Results

- MIND MELD improved the quality of human-provided corrective labels by **63%** on a hold-out calibration task in human-subjects experiments.
- The learned personalized embeddings significantly correlated with objective measures of human demonstrator stylistic tendencies, including over/under-correction and delayed/anticipatory feedback (p < **0.001** for both).
- Synthetic experiments demonstrated a **61%** improvement in label quality during calibration and **55%** on unseen test tasks, with embeddings clustering distinctively based on simulated styles.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Suboptimal human corrective feedback can be systematically identified, modeled, and corrected by learning personalized representations of demonstrator styles.
> - Maximizing mutual information is an effective strategy to ensure that personalized embeddings meaningfully encapsulate individual teaching tendencies, such as over/under-correction or delayed feedback.
> - Personalized embeddings derived from human interaction can correlate with quantitative measures of demonstrator style and demographic factors, providing insights into human teaching behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of Learning from Demonstration (LfD) for robots, which aims to enable robots to acquire novel tasks by observing human examples. LfD approaches are broadly categorized into human-centric and robot-centric paradigms. In human-centric LfD, the human demonstrator directly provides task trajectories, as seen in methods like Behavioral Cloning (BC). While BC is straightforward, it is known to suffer from issues such as covariate shift and poor performance in stochastic environments, where the robot encounters states not seen during training.

To address the limitations of human-centric approaches, robot-centric LfD methods were developed. Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) is a prominent robot-centric algorithm where an initial policy is learned from demonstrations, and then the robot executes the policy. A human demonstrator observes the robot's performance and provides real-time corrective feedback, which is then aggregated with previous data to refine the policy. Prior work established that DAgger can outperform human-centric LfD when the demonstrator is an "oracle" providing consistently high-quality, optimal labels.

However, the efficacy of DAgger in real-world scenarios with human demonstrators has been questioned. Studies have shown that human demonstrators often provide suboptimal or inconsistent feedback, leading to degraded robot performance, sometimes even worse than that achieved with simpler BC methods. This suboptimality is compounded by heterogeneity among human demonstrators, as individuals vary in their teaching styles, leading to diverse forms of corrective feedback. Challenges associated with DAgger also include the high workload placed on the human demonstrator, which can lead to fatigue, and the practical difficulties of providing precise real-time corrections.

Existing research has attempted to mitigate these issues by reducing the demonstrator's burden or by learning from heterogeneous demonstrators. For example, some approaches allow humans to control when to intervene (e.g., HG-DAgger, Expert Intervention Learning) to simplify the interaction. However, a gap remains in addressing the core problem of demonstrator suboptimality and heterogeneity simultaneously by inferring and accounting for individual demonstrator styles to improve the quality of the corrective labels themselves. This paper aims to fill this gap by developing a framework that learns personalized representations of human feedback styles to map suboptimal human corrections to a more accurate approximation of ground truth.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the effectiveness of robot-centric Learning from Demonstration (LfD) techniques, specifically those based on Dataset Aggregation (DAgger), when human users serve as teachers. The authors aim to improve robot learning outcomes by addressing the inherent challenges posed by human demonstrators: the provision of suboptimal and heterogeneous corrective feedback.

The motivation for this work stems from observed limitations in current robot-centric LfD:
1. **Suboptimal Human Feedback:** Human teachers, unlike theoretical "oracle" experts, frequently provide corrective labels that are not perfectly optimal. These inaccuracies can be due to various factors, including perceptual limitations, delayed reactions, or an incomplete understanding of the robot's internal state. When DAgger relies on such low-quality input, the learned robot policy can suffer, sometimes performing worse than basic human-centric methods.
2. **Heterogeneity in Demonstration Styles:** Humans exhibit diverse teaching styles. One demonstrator might consistently over-correct, while another might under-correct or provide feedback with a consistent delay or anticipation. These individual differences, if not accounted for, can make it difficult for a robot to generalize across different teachers or even to consistently learn from a single teacher whose feedback style might subtly vary.
3. **Lack of Personalized Correction:** Prior LfD approaches have largely treated human feedback as a generic input, without explicitly modeling or adapting to the specific stylistic tendencies of individual demonstrators. The authors hypothesize that understanding and modeling these personalized styles can facilitate a more effective transformation of suboptimal human input into high-quality training data for the robot.

Therefore, the key objectives are:
- To develop a novel LfD framework, named Mutual Information-driven Meta-learning from Demonstration (MIND MELD), that can meta-learn a mapping function. This function would transform suboptimal human-provided corrective labels into labels that more closely approximate the ground truth.
- To learn a meaningful, personalized embedding for each human demonstrator. This embedding is intended to encapsulate information about an individual's unique tendencies and corrective style, allowing the system to adapt its label correction process to that specific person.
- To demonstrate that the proposed framework can significantly improve the quality of human-provided corrective labels in robot-centric LfD tasks.
- To investigate whether the learned personalized embeddings genuinely capture salient aspects of demonstrator style, by correlating them with measured stylistic tendencies, personality traits, and experience metrics.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to create more robust and adaptable LfD systems that can effectively learn from the diverse and imperfect feedback characteristic of human teachers in real-world human-robot interaction settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce Mutual Information-driven Meta-learning from Demonstration (MIND MELD), a framework designed to improve robot-centric LfD by inferring and leveraging personalized demonstrator styles. The methodology operates under three core assumptions: (1) humans provide suboptimal corrective feedback, (2) these suboptimal strategies are identifiable and representable as distinct "styles" via learned embeddings, and (3) humans provide corrective feedback in a predictable manner across different tasks.

**Architecture:**
MIND MELD employs a neural network architecture comprising three main components:
1. **Bidirectional LSTM Encoder (Eφ'):** This component processes a sequence of corrective feedback provided by a demonstrator (a^(p)_(t:t+Δt)) and extracts a fixed-dimensional encoding (~z). The use of a bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network allows the system to capture temporal dependencies and contextual information within the sequence of actions, which is relevant for understanding corrective styles.
2. **Prediction Subnetwork (fθ):** This subnetwork takes the extracted encoding (~z) and a personalized embedding (~w^(p)) as input. Its function is to predict the difference (d^(p)_t') between the ground truth optimal action (o_t') and the human demonstrator's provided action (a^(p)_t'). This predicted difference is then used to adjust the human's label towards the ground truth. The subnetwork has a hidden layer with 200 neurons.
3. **Mutual Information Subnetwork (gφ):** This subnetwork maps the encoding (~z) and the predicted difference (d^(p)_t') to a multivariate normal distribution. This distribution serves as the approximate posterior for the demonstrator's personalized embedding (~w^(p)). This component is crucial for ensuring that the personalized embeddings are informative and capture salient aspects of the demonstrator's style. It features two hidden layers with 64 and 32 neurons, respectively.

**Personalized Embeddings and Variational Inference:**
A central element of MIND MELD is the personalized embedding (~w^(p)), a d-dimensional vector unique to each demonstrator 'p'. This embedding is intended to capture the individual's corrective style. To ensure these embeddings are meaningful, the framework maximizes the mutual information I(~w^(p); ~z, d^(p)_t') between the learned embedding, the encoding of demonstrator labels, and the corrective mapping. Maximizing mutual information implies that observing informative corrective feedback should reduce uncertainty about the learned embedding, indicating the embedding effectively characterizes the demonstrator's style.

Since directly maximizing mutual information often involves an intractable posterior distribution, the authors utilize variational inference. Specifically, they employ the evidence lower bound (ELBO) to approximate and maximize this mutual information, guiding the learning process to ensure ~w^(p) encapsulates relevant stylistic information. The embedding for an individual is initialized from a standard normal prior (N(0,1)) and then sampled from the approximate posterior distribution learned by gφ.

**Loss Functions:**
The network is trained using a combined loss function that minimizes two mean squared errors:
1. **Embedding Loss:** The squared difference between the sampled embedding approximation (ˆ~w^(p)) and the current personalized embedding (~w^(p)). This term drives the embedding to converge to a stable representation of the individual's style.
2. **Label Correction Loss:** The squared difference between the predicted difference (d^(p)_t') and the true difference between the ground truth label (o_t') and the human's label (a^(p)_t'). This term ensures the network accurately predicts how to correct human labels.
These combined losses are backpropagated through the network, including the input embedding, allowing both the network parameters (θ, φ, φ') and the personalized embeddings (~w^(p)) to be learned and updated during training.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology involves two phases of experimentation:
1. **Synthetic Experiment:**
 - **Purpose:** To validate the network architecture and its ability to correct suboptimal labels in a controlled environment.
 - **Task:** A simulated driving task where an agent learns to navigate to a goal.
 - **Data Generation:** Artificial DAgger-like rollouts were created. Ground truth labels were derived from the optimal steering angle required to reach the goal. Artificial human demonstrators were simulated by systematically introducing "styles" (delayed/anticipatory, over-/under-correcting) to the ground truth labels, generating suboptimal feedback.
 - **Evaluation:** Analysis of learned embedding clusters and quantitative improvement in label quality.
2. **Human-Subjects Experiment:**
 - **Purpose:** To evaluate MIND MELD with actual human demonstrators and determine if learned embeddings correlate with real human characteristics.
 - **Domain:** A driving simulation using AirSim (Unreal Engine) with an Xbox steering wheel. The task involved driving to an orange ball, with obstacles present.
 - **Data Collection:** 34 participants provided corrective feedback for 12 pre-recorded "calibration rollouts." These rollouts were designed to be representative of DAgger scenarios.
 - **Ground Truth:** For the calibration tasks, optimal paths were generated using RRT* (Rapidly-exploring Random Tree*) and optimal control actions were derived using a Stanley controller.
 - **Metrics:** Participants completed pre-study questionnaires assessing:
 - **Big Five Personality Traits** (Mini-IPIP).
 - **Prior Experience** with video games, physical driving, and virtual driving (Likert scales).
 - **Trust in Automation** (survey by Adams et al.).
 - **Stylistic Tendencies:** Calculated using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) to quantify the degree of over/under-correction and delayed/anticipatory feedback by comparing human labels to ground truth.
 - **Training:** MIND MELD was trained on the human participant data using PyTorch with a learning rate of 0.001.
 - **Hypotheses:** The study formulated hypotheses regarding MIND MELD's ability to improve labels and the correlation of learned embeddings with personality, experience, and stylistic tendencies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented findings from both synthetic and human-subjects experiments to evaluate the MIND MELD framework.

**Synthetic Experiment Results:**
The synthetic experiment served as a proof-of-concept for the MIND MELD architecture.
- **Meaningful Embeddings:** The learned personalized embeddings in the latent space clustered distinctively based on the artificially assigned demonstrator styles. Over-correctors and under-correctors separated along one axis, while delayed and anticipatory feedback styles separated along another. This demonstrated that the embeddings successfully captured the intended stylistic variations.
- **Label Improvement:** MIND MELD achieved a 61% improvement in the quality of labels during calibration tasks. For unseen test tasks, which were not used for training the network, a 55% improvement in label quality was observed after mapping. These results indicated that the architecture could effectively correct suboptimal feedback.

**Human-Subjects Experiment Results:**
A study with 34 human participants was conducted to assess MIND MELD's performance with real human data.
- **Improvement in Corrective Labels (Hypothesis 1):** MIND MELD improved human-provided corrective labels by 63% on a hold-out calibration task. This finding supports the hypothesis that the framework can effectively learn and correct for suboptimal human feedback.
- **Correlation with Stylistic Tendencies (Hypotheses 3a and 3b):** The learned personalized embeddings significantly correlated with two key stylistic tendencies derived from participants' demonstrations:
 - Tendency to over- or under-correct (p < 0.001).
 - Tendency to provide delayed or anticipatory feedback (p < 0.001).
 These significant correlations indicate that MIND MELD's embeddings effectively capture and represent individual demonstration styles.
- **Correlation with Demographic, Personality, and Experience Metrics (Hypotheses 2a, 2b, 2c):**
 - **Gender:** A significant correlation was found between demonstrator gender and the learned embeddings (p = 0.0015). The paper notes that further exploration into the meaning of this correlation is outside its scope.
 - **Experience (Virtual Cars, Physical Cars):** While not statistically significant at the α = 0.05 level, experience with virtual cars (p = 0.18) and physical cars (p = 0.15) showed trends towards significance. This suggests a potential relationship between driving experience and feedback style that warrants further investigation.
 - **Neuroticism:** The personality trait of neuroticism also trended towards significance (p = 0.16), suggesting that how individuals manage stress might influence their feedback style.
 - Other personality traits (Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Openness), general video game experience, and trust in automation did not show significant correlations with the learned embeddings.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that MIND MELD effectively improves the quality of human-provided corrective labels by a substantial margin and that its learned personalized embeddings are meaningful representations of individual demonstration styles, correlating significantly with quantitative measures of these styles and with gender.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Learning from Demonstration (LfD) by directly addressing a critical limitation of robot-centric approaches like DAgger: their susceptibility to suboptimal and heterogeneous feedback from human teachers. The introduction of MIND MELD represents a step toward making LfD more robust and practical for real-world human-robot interaction.

The **significance** of this work is multifaceted:
- **Improved LfD Performance:** By demonstrating a 63% improvement in the quality of human-provided corrective labels, MIND MELD suggests a pathway to more reliable and efficient robot learning from human input. This enhanced label quality could lead to the development of more accurate and robust robot policies, reducing the need for extensive, often impractical, human oversight or high-fidelity "oracle" demonstrations.
- **Personalized Robot Learning:** The framework's ability to learn personalized embeddings that capture individual demonstrator styles (e.g., tendencies to over-correct, under-correct, or provide delayed/anticipatory feedback) is a key advancement. This enables robots to adapt their learning process to the specific idiosyncrasies of each human teacher, rather than treating all human feedback uniformly. This personalization could make human-robot teaching interactions more intuitive and less frustrating for users, as the robot becomes more attuned to the human's unique teaching approach.
- **Understanding Human Teaching Behavior:** The observed correlations between learned embeddings and quantifiable stylistic tendencies provide empirical evidence that individual differences in teaching can be computationally modeled. Furthermore, the correlation with gender and the trending significance with certain experience metrics and personality traits (neuroticism) suggest that these embeddings could serve as a valuable tool for future research aiming to understand the underlying psychological and behavioral factors influencing human-robot teaching dynamics.
- **Bridging the Gap to Real-World Applications:** By overcoming some of the challenges associated with human suboptimality and variability, MIND MELD moves LfD closer to real-world applications where human teachers are not perfect oracles. This could facilitate the deployment of robots in a wider range of settings, from industrial tasks to assistive robotics, where novice users need to teach robots novel skills.

**Potential Impact:**
The potential impact of this research extends to:
- **More Efficient Robot Training:** Robots could learn tasks with fewer demonstrations and less effort from human teachers, as the system effectively "cleans" and enhances the provided feedback.
- **Broader Accessibility of Robotics:** Novice users or those without specialized robotics knowledge could more easily teach robots, lowering the barrier to entry for programming and customizing robotic systems.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration:** A robot that better understands and adapts to its human partner's teaching style could foster more effective and harmonious collaborative relationships.
- **Informed HRI Design:** The insights gained from how human styles are captured in embeddings could inform the design of future human-robot interaction interfaces, making them more adaptive and user-centric.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- MIND MELD requires ground truth labels for calibration tasks and a set of training participants to learn personalized embeddings, which adds an initial data collection burden.
- The current framework assumes a single optimal solution to a task and does not account for scenarios where different demonstrators might have valid, diverse preferences for task solutions (e.g., scenic vs. fastest route in driving).
- The interpretation of some correlations, such as the one with gender, requires further investigation using methods like explainable AI.

Future work includes conducting a follow-up human-subjects study to directly compare the policies learned by a MIND MELD-trained agent against those learned via standard DAgger and BC, using the improved labels to train the LfD agent. Additionally, applying MIND MELD to more complex robotic tasks, such as pick-and-place with higher degrees of freedom, and exploring ways to account for heterogeneity in both feedback style and task solution preferences are planned next steps. These future directions aim to further validate the framework's generalizability and practical utility.
