# A Benchmark of Dexterity for Anthropomorphic Robotic Hands

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces POMDAR (Performance-based Outcome Measures of Dexterity for Anthropomorphic Robotic Hands), an open-source benchmark with 18 tasks to systematically evaluate anthropomorphic robotic hand dexterity, grounded in human manipulation taxonomies. It successfully differentiated performance across various robotic hand embodiments, revealing that increased kinematic capabilities generally improve task performance and grasp stability, particularly for complex manipulation and smaller object grasping.

## Problem

- The research community lacked a standardized framework and consistent definition for evaluating anthropomorphic robotic hand dexterity, hindering systematic comparisons and objective assessment of design improvements.
- Existing evaluation methods relied on either proxy metrics (inferring capability from design parameters) or task-based benchmarks that often lacked reproducibility, a robust theoretical foundation, or a unified scoring system.
- Challenges existed in designing evaluation tasks that directly observe standardized motions, suppress compensatory strategies (e.g., arm/wrist movement), and provide quantitative, objective throughput-based metrics.

## Method

- The authors developed **POMDAR**, a comprehensive, performance-based benchmark consisting of ==18 tasks== derived from established ==human manipulation and grasp taxonomies== to formalize dexterity as measurable task performance.
- The benchmark tasks feature open-source, ==3D-printable physical components== and a high-fidelity ==MuJoCo simulation==, designed with ==mechanical scaffolding== to constrain motion to intended degrees of freedom and prevent compensatory strategies.
- A quantitative ==throughput-based scoring metric== was introduced, combining task correctness (weighted **80%**) and execution speed (weighted **20%**) relative to human baselines, providing objective and continuous evaluation.

## Results

- A human user study established baseline completion times and validated that participants adopted motion and grasp patterns consistent with the underlying taxonomies, confirming the effectiveness of the scaffolded task design.
- Teleoperation experiments with **four ORCA hand embodiments** demonstrated that aggregated dexterity scores generally increased with the number of DoFs, with performance differences most pronounced in tasks requiring coordinated multi-finger interactions and finger abduction.
- An ablation study comparing teleoperation methods showed consistently lower scores with Apple Vision Pro compared to Rokoko motion-capture gloves, primarily due to egocentric perception occlusions limiting accurate finger tracking during manipulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The scaffolded design of POMDAR tasks effectively constrains robotic hand interactions to specific manipulation primitives, allowing for direct and objective assessment of a hand's intrinsic dexterity without external compensation.
> - Dexterity gains are strongly task-dependent; while increased kinematic complexity (e.g., more DoFs, presence of abduction) generally improves performance, specific kinematic capabilities offer more pronounced benefits in certain manipulation and grasping tasks.
> - The benchmark's ability to differentiate performance across varying robotic hand embodiments and highlight specific task challenges provides empirical data for informing and optimizing future anthropomorphic hand designs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterity is a fundamental concept in the design and assessment of anthropomorphic robotic hands, distinguishing them from simpler grippers. However, the research landscape has been characterized by an inconsistent definition of dexterity and a lack of a standardized framework for its evaluation. This has impeded systematic comparison across different hand designs, hindered the assessment of design improvements, and complicated the selection of appropriate hands for specific manipulation tasks.

Current evaluation practices broadly fall into two categories:
- **Parameter-based (proxy) evaluation:** This approach infers dexterity from design parameters such as degrees of freedom (DoF), joint ranges, actuator counts, speed, or kinematic indices (e.g., manipulability). While these measures are quick to compute, they reflect a system's *potential* capability rather than its *realized* capability in contact-rich manipulation tasks. They do not directly evaluate grasp stability, contact transitions, or control in physical interaction.
- **Task-performance-based evaluation (PBOMD-style):** Widely adopted in clinical settings for human assessment, this paradigm evaluates dexterity by measuring how effectively a subject performs physical tasks. In robotics, existing PBOMD-style benchmarks include:
 - **The Elliott & Connolly Benchmark:** Focuses on in-hand dexterity, isolating within-hand object motions using digit motion alone. It utilizes objects from the YCB set but relies on visual tracking, which can be prone to occlusion and adds instrumentation burden, and does not include pure grasping tasks.
 - **50 Hand Dexterity Benchmarks (HD-marks):** Offers a broad checklist of 50 tasks covering grasping, thumb dexterity, and in-hand manipulation, drawing from the GRASP taxonomy and Kapandji test. Its strength is breadth, but evaluation is largely binary (success/failure), and objects are not standardized across tasks, limiting comparability.
 - **Dexterity Test Board:** A modular board with 24 tasks across various categories. While open and modular, its tasks are not consistently mapped to formal taxonomies, making its theoretical foundation less robust.

This body of work identifies a critical gap: the absence of a unified, performance-based definition of dexterity coupled with a standardized, reproducible, and taxonomy-grounded framework for objective evaluation that allows for consistent comparison across different anthropomorphic robot hand designs. This paper aims to address this gap by introducing a new benchmark.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce POMDAR (Performance-based Outcome Measures of Dexterity for Anthropomorphic Robotic Hands), a comprehensive and systematic dexterity benchmark. This benchmark aims to formalize dexterity as task performance across a structured set of manipulation and grasping motions.

The motivation for developing POMDAR stems from several identified challenges and research gaps in the field:
- **Addressing the ambiguity of "dexterity":** The term is often used inconsistently, making meaningful comparisons difficult. The work seeks to provide a unified, performance-based definition grounded in measurable outcomes rather than subjective interpretations or proxy metrics.
- **Bridging the evaluation gap:** Current evaluation practices for anthropomorphic hands often rely on ad-hoc measures or limited common metrics, such as finger reachable workspace, as highlighted by examples from recent literature. This lack of a standardized framework impedes the ability to objectively compare different designs.
- **Enabling systematic design and optimization:** Without a reliable method to compare dexterity across hand designs, researchers and developers face challenges in assessing design improvements and matching robotic hands to specific task requirements. A standardized benchmark would facilitate a more systematic advancement of dexterous manipulation platforms.
- **Ensuring representativeness and grounding in human motor control:** Dexterity needs to be evaluated through a representative set of tasks informed by established studies of human hand motion, grasping, and manipulation primitives, rather than arbitrary tests.
- **Promoting reproducibility and accessibility:** The community requires a benchmark that is easily replicable across laboratories, without requiring specialized equipment or proprietary components. This includes both physical and simulated implementations.
- **Enforcing standardized motions and suppressing compensatory strategies:** To truly assess a hand's intrinsic dexterity, tasks need to be designed to constrain motion to the intended degrees of freedom, preventing external assistance (e.g., arm/wrist movement, gravity) or other compensatory behaviors that might mask a hand's limitations.
- **Developing a quantitative and objective scoring metric:** A unified scoring system that combines task correctness and execution speed is needed to provide objective, continuous, and easily comparable evaluations across diverse hand designs. This metric should effectively measure "throughput" of dexterous tasks.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to provide an open-source, standardized, and taxonomy-grounded benchmark to facilitate consistent comparison and evaluation of anthropomorphic robot hands.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of POMDAR involved a systematic approach to define, design, implement, and score dexterous tasks.

**A. Definition of Dexterity and Task Classification:**
The work defines dexterity as coordinated voluntary movement for functional object manipulation, emphasizing speed and task completion, and acknowledging its dynamic interaction with environmental constraints.
Tasks were constructed based on established taxonomies from human motor control and robotics:
- **Manipulation:** 14 patterns derived from Elliott and Connolly's taxonomy (13 patterns, with "Rock" split into two variants) and Ma and Dollar's extension (Finger Pivoting/Tracking).
- **Grasping:** 33 grasp types from Feix et al.'s GRASP Taxonomy.
An initial analysis revealed that 16 of the 33 grasp types are inherently contained within the manipulation patterns, allowing for consolidation. This led to a dual-structured task categorization:
- **Manipulation Tasks (12 tasks):** Primarily motion-based tasks assessing in-hand dexterity, covering patterns like rolling, sliding, and sequential repositioning. These tasks simultaneously evaluate grasping and in-hand manipulation.
- **Pure Grasping Tasks (6 tasks):** Specifically evaluate grasp types not covered by manipulation tasks, focusing on the hand’s ability to form and maintain stable grasp configurations.
This structure aligns with medical distinctions between "Fine Dexterity" (inter-digit coordination, assessed by manipulation tasks) and "Gross Dexterity" (coordinated action of fingers and palm, assessed by pure grasping tasks).

**B. Consolidation of Tasks:**
- **Manipulation Tasks:** Prismatic 2–4 finger precision grasping patterns (F6 to F8) were consolidated into one task (V2: Stick). Grasp patterns F19 (Distal Type, renamed H1: Scissors) and F21 (Tripod Variation, integrated into H2: Chopsticks) were incorporated due to their functional within-hand motions. EC12 was deemed redundant. EC4, EC7, and EC8 (small object rotation) were combined into C1: Thread. EC9 (full roll) was grouped into V3: Sphere to maximize component reuse. This resulted in 12 manipulation tasks.
- **Grasping Tasks:** Grasps were clustered based on manipulated object geometry to ensure fairness to hands with fewer fingers. Cylindrical (F2, F3, F4, F15, F17, F1, F31, F5), spherical (F26, F28), and disk-shaped (F18, F22, F30) objects were grouped, leading to 6 distinct grasping tasks.

**C. Design of the POMDAR Benchmark:**
The benchmark tasks were designed according to four principles:
1. **Representative:** Each task is traceable to existing manipulation or grasp taxonomies.
2. **Reproducible:** Open-source, fully 3D-printable components, and available in simulation.
3. **Directly observable, standardized motions:** Mechanical scaffolding constrains task motion to intended DoFs, suppressing compensatory strategies (e.g., gravity assistance, palm support, arm/wrist involvement) and enabling objective measurement.
4. **Quantitative, throughput-based evaluation:** A unified score combines task success and execution speed.

The physical tasks are organized into four configurations:
- **Vertical (scaffolded) configuration:** Hand grasps an object and pulls a rod upward through a sequence of notches, requiring coordinated in-hand adjustments (e.g., V1: Wheel, V2: Stick, V3: Sphere).
- **Continuous Rotation configuration:** Hand continuously rotates an object using stepping sequences or geared transmissions, often employing a gravity-based clutch to maintain suspension (e.g., C1: Thread, C2: Stick, C3: Wheel, C4: Fidget).
- **Horizontal (scaffolded) configuration:** Object is manipulated in-hand while translated horizontally along a curved rail with progressively increasing curvature (e.g., H1: Scissors, H2: Chopsticks, H3: Squeeze, H4: Palmar, H5: Pinch).
- **Pure Grasping tasks (G):** Objects are picked up from inserts and relocated in free space without external constraints, assessing grasp robustness and quality (e.g., G1: Wheel, G2: Sphere, G3: Disk, G4: Cylinder\_Small, G5: Cylinder, G6: Cylinder\_Large).
Many objects are reused across configurations to enhance consistency and reduce redundancy. The setup is compact, entirely 3D-printable, and requires no additional components beyond printing material.

**D. Benchmark Protocol and Scoring:**
Dexterity is quantified as throughput, combining correctness and speed into a unified score:
`Score = 0.8 * Correctness + 0.2 * Speed`
- **Correctness (0-1):** For scaffolded tasks, it's the fraction of progress achieved (e.g., notches traversed, curves cleared, normalized rotation). For pure grasping, it's discrete: 0 (not lifted), 0.5 (lifted but dropped), 1 (successfully relocated).
- **Speed (unbounded):** Ratio of human baseline completion time (from user study) to the evaluated system's completion time. The weighting prioritizes task completion (0.8) over speed (0.2), reflecting current challenges in robotic manipulation.

**E. Real-World and Simulation Benchmark Design:**
- **Real-World:** All components are consumer-grade 3D-printable, open-source, and designed for repeatable mounting and easy replacement using clamps and retention mechanisms.
- **Simulation:** Implemented in MuJoCo, using a task-oriented modeling strategy with simplified geometries for efficiency. Complex non-convex components were preprocessed using CoACD for convex decomposition. Task-specific workarounds (e.g., manually enforced gear couplings, feedforward PID for screw motion) were used. The simulation supports teleoperation via motion capture gloves and VR, providing a platform for design optimization and learning-based approaches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. User Study:**
A user study with six participants established human baseline performance and validated the task design.
- Participants performed all 18 tasks (three trials per task, totaling 18 trajectories).
- Motion-capture gloves tracked 22 hand keypoints, which were analyzed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA).
- The analysis confirmed that participants adopted motion and grasp patterns consistent with the underlying taxonomies, suggesting that the scaffolded design effectively constrained interactions to the intended motion primitives.
- Baseline completion times, derived from this study, were used for the speed component of the benchmark score.

**B. Benchmark Results (Robot Teleoperation):**
POMDAR was evaluated using teleoperation experiments with four embodiments of the ORCA hand, progressively increasing in dexterity:
1. 2-finger setup (thumb and index, no abduction, 5 DoF).
2. 3-finger setup (no abduction).
3. 5-finger setup (no abduction, 11 DoF).
4. Full 5-finger configuration (16 DoF, including abduction).
The hand was mounted on a 7-DoF Franka Emika arm, and a single operator used Rokoko motion-capture gloves for teleoperation. Each task and embodiment involved 5 practice trials and 20 recorded trials, totaling 1140 trajectories and approximately 25 hours of testing.
- **General Performance Trend:** The aggregated scores generally increased with the number of DoF, demonstrating the benchmark's sensitivity to variations in embodiment complexity.
- **Manipulation Tasks:**
 - Performance differences were most pronounced in tasks requiring coordinated multi-finger interactions and finger abduction (e.g., H2: Chopsticks, H3: Squeeze, H4: Palmar). These tasks showed clear improvements with higher DoF configurations.
 - Conversely, some tasks (e.g., H1: Scissors, C1: Thread, H5: Pinch) exhibited comparable performance across embodiments, suggesting that their execution strategies were less sensitive to the added DoFs.
 - Continuous rotation tasks, particularly those involving the gravity-based clutch, were generally the most challenging and sometimes unreliable with the tested system. Vertical tasks showed higher variance due to their fine-grained metrics.
- **Grasping Tasks:**
 - All embodiments were capable of grasping the objects, but increased dexterity consistently improved both grasp stability and execution speed.
 - This effect was more significant for smaller objects, where additional fingers provided enhanced robustness. For larger objects, the primary benefit was faster relocation.
 - A substantial performance gain in grasp stability was observed when moving from the 2-finger to the 3-finger embodiment. However, the improvement from 3-finger to 5-finger (without abduction) was limited, indicating that specific kinematic capabilities (like abduction) or optimized finger configurations contribute more significantly than merely increasing finger count for some tasks.
- **Task-Dependency:** The results confirmed that dexterity gains are strongly task-dependent, with different tasks benefiting from different kinematic capabilities.

**C. Ablation Study (Teleoperation Method Comparison):**
An ablation study compared teleoperation using Rokoko motion-capture gloves versus Apple Vision Pro (AVP).
- Scores obtained with AVP were consistently lower across embodiments and representative tasks (V1: Wheel, V2: Stick).
- This performance reduction was primarily attributed to occlusions in egocentric perception inherent to AVP, which limited accurate tracking of finger motions during manipulation, particularly in vertical configurations where the thumb can obscure other finger movements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The POMDAR benchmark offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of anthropomorphic robotic hand research and development:

- **Standardization of Dexterity Evaluation:** By introducing a taxonomy-grounded, structured, and quantitative benchmark, POMDAR provides a unified framework for defining and measuring dexterity. This standardization is critical for enabling consistent and meaningful comparisons of anthropomorphic robot hands across different research groups and designs, addressing a long-standing challenge in the field.
- **Enhanced Reproducibility and Accessibility:** The open-source nature of the benchmark, coupled with its fully 3D-printable physical components and detailed simulation implementation, significantly lowers the barrier to entry for researchers. This accessibility promotes widespread adoption and ensures that evaluation results are reproducible across laboratories, fostering collaborative progress.
- **Objective and Interpretable Performance Metrics:** The performance-based scoring metric, which combines task correctness and execution speed relative to human baselines, moves beyond subjective assessments or proxy measures. This provides objective, continuous, and interpretable insights into the realized capabilities of robotic hands, highlighting both success rates and efficiency.
- **Guidance for Hand Design and Optimization:** The benchmark's ability to differentiate performance across varying robotic hand embodiments (e.g., different DoF counts, presence of abduction) provides empirical data that can inform future hand designs. Researchers can identify which kinematic capabilities are most critical for specific manipulation and grasping tasks, leading to more targeted and effective design choices.
- **Foundation for Advanced Control and Learning:** The availability of POMDAR in a high-fidelity simulation environment (MuJoCo) establishes a robust platform for developing and evaluating autonomous control policies. This enables rapid iteration and optimization of robot hand controllers and learning-based manipulation strategies, decoupling performance from human teleoperation skill.
- **Identification of Research Frontiers:** The benchmark highlights areas where robotic hand performance still significantly lags human capabilities, such as complex continuous rotation tasks. It also identifies the limitations of current teleoperation interfaces, providing direction for further research in these areas.
- **Systematic Advancement of Dexterous Manipulation:** Ultimately, POMDAR is designed to facilitate a systematic and data-driven advancement of dexterous manipulation platforms. By providing a common yardstick, it helps the robotics community track progress, benchmark new technologies, and accelerate the development of more capable and adaptable anthropomorphic robotic hands for a wider range of real-world applications.
