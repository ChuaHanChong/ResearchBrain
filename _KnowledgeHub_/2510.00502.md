# Diffusion Alignment as Variational Expectation-Maximization

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called Diffusion Alignment as Variational Expectation-Maximization (DAV) fine-tunes diffusion models to optimize for external rewards while preserving sample diversity and naturalness. The method achieved an aesthetic reward of 8.04 in text-to-image synthesis and 7.71 predicted activity in DNA sequence design, consistently mitigating reward over-optimization and mode collapse.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning-based diffusion alignment methods often lead to "mode collapse," reducing the diversity of generated samples.
- Direct backpropagation approaches for diffusion fine-tuning can result in "reward over-optimization," producing unnatural or degenerate samples.
- Prior fine-tuning strategies are frequently limited to continuous diffusion models or require differentiable reward functions, restricting their general applicability.

## Method

- The research formulates diffusion alignment as an iterative variational ==Expectation-Maximization== (EM) process with alternating exploration (E-step) and amortization (M-step).
- In the E-step, a two-stage local search mechanism with importance sampling identifies diverse, high-reward trajectories from a reward-tilted variational posterior.
- The M-step updates diffusion model parameters by distilling the knowledge from collected trajectories via forward KL divergence minimization, optionally with a KL regularization term (DAV-KL).

## Results

- In text-to-image synthesis, DAV achieved an aesthetic reward of 8.04, outperforming DDPO (6.83) and DRaFT (7.22), and preserved superior sample diversity.
- For DNA sequence design, DAV balanced reward (7.71 Pred-Activity) with biological validity (ATAC-Acc **0.887**) and naturalness (3-mer Correlation **0.397**), surpassing existing fine-tuning baselines.
- The test-time "posterior" variants of DAV achieved the highest reward scores in both continuous (9.18 aesthetic) and discrete (9.24 Pred-Activity) domains, while maintaining substantial diversity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reformulating diffusion alignment as a variational Expectation-Maximization process can effectively mitigate both reward over-optimization and mode collapse.
> - An iterative cycle of explicit test-time search for diverse, high-reward samples (E-step) and subsequent distillation into the model (M-step) is crucial for balanced optimization.
> - The framework is versatile, operating on both continuous and discrete diffusion models and accommodating non-differentiable reward functions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Diffusion models have demonstrated capabilities in generating high-fidelity samples across various domains, including image synthesis, robotics, and computational biology. However, many real-world applications require samples that are not just high-likelihood but also optimized for specific external criteria, such as aesthetic quality or biological activity. This necessity has driven the development of diffusion model fine-tuning methods for "diffusion alignment."

Existing alignment approaches typically fall into two categories:
1. **Reinforcement Learning (RL)-based fine-tuning:** These methods optimize diffusion model parameters using a reverse-KL objective with on-policy data. While successful in maximizing rewards, they are prone to "mode-seeking" behavior, which can lead to premature convergence, "mode collapse," and a reduction in sample quality and diversity.
2. **Direct backpropagation:** This approach propagates gradient signals from differentiable reward functions through the diffusion denoising chain, offering higher sample efficiency. However, it relies on potentially sharp or brittle gradient signals, which can result in "reward over-optimization" where the model sacrifices broader qualities for narrow reward maximization.

A third category, **test-time inference approaches**, focuses on allocating additional computation during generation to find aligned outputs without altering model weights. These include guidance-based methods and search-based algorithms. While effective at bypassing post-training phases, guidance methods can suffer from under-optimization, and search-based methods can incur substantial computational overhead, limiting practical application.

The present work, Diffusion Alignment as Variational Expectation-Maximization (DAV), addresses a recognized gap in the field: the need for a fine-tuning framework that effectively maximizes rewards while simultaneously preserving the diversity and naturalness of samples generated by a pretrained diffusion model. It integrates strengths from both fine-tuning and test-time inference paradigms by using test-time search to gather diverse, high-reward samples, which are then distilled back into the model parameters using a principled Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. Unlike some recent methods focused solely on continuous diffusion models or differentiable rewards, DAV is designed to be applicable to both continuous and discrete diffusion models, and it operates without requiring the differentiability of the reward function. It also draws inspiration from the self-training paradigm, where an "expert rollout" (E-step) generates data for subsequent model "distillation" (M-step).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust fine-tuning framework for diffusion models that can optimize samples for specific external reward functions while simultaneously mitigating two common failure modes observed in prior work: "reward over-optimization" and "mode collapse." The authors aimed for a framework that could preserve the diversity and naturalness inherent in the pretrained diffusion model.

The motivation for this objective stems from the limitations of existing diffusion alignment strategies:
- **Mode-Seeking Behavior:** RL-based methods, by optimizing a reverse-KL objective, tend to concentrate on a single dominant mode of high reward, thereby reducing the diversity of generated samples. This leads to mode collapse, where the model produces highly similar, albeit high-reward, outputs.
- **Over-Optimization and Brittle Gradients:** Direct backpropagation methods, while efficient, can over-optimize for the reward function due to reliance on potentially noisy or sharp gradient signals from learned reward models. This can lead to unnatural or degenerate samples that score high on the reward function but lack generalizability or fidelity.
- **Computational and Generality Constraints:** Test-time inference approaches can be computationally intensive at inference time. Furthermore, many recent fine-tuning methods are specifically tailored for continuous diffusion models and necessitate differentiable reward functions, limiting their applicability to discrete domains or black-box reward scenarios.

To overcome these issues, the authors propose DAV, an iterative framework inspired by the variational Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. This approach is motivated by the idea that an alternating process of explicit exploration (E-step) and subsequent model refinement (M-step) can lead to a more balanced optimization. The E-step is designed to proactively discover diverse, high-reward samples from a broad variational posterior, rather than relying solely on on-policy data which can be mode-seeking. The M-step then refines the diffusion model using a mode-covering objective (forward-KL divergence), ensuring that the model learns to generate a diverse set of reward-aligned samples, thereby preventing mode collapse. This iterative "synergetic cycle" is hypothesized to lead to a multi-modal aligned distribution.

A key motivation was to demonstrate the framework's versatility across different data modalities and reward types. The chosen tasks—text-to-image synthesis (a continuous domain) and DNA sequence design (a discrete domain), involving both differentiable and non-differentiable rewards—were selected to empirically validate this broad applicability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Diffusion Alignment as Variational Expectation-Maximization (DAV) formulates diffusion alignment as an iterative process that alternates between an E-step (Exploration) and an M-step (Amortization), inspired by variational EM algorithms. The framework aims to maximize the likelihood of a binary optimality variable, $O=1$, where $p_\theta(O=1|\tau) \propto \exp(\sum_{t=1}^T r(x_t, x_{t-1})/\alpha)$, with $\tau$ representing a diffusion trajectory. An evidence lower bound (ELBO), $J_{\alpha,\gamma}(\eta, p_\theta)$, is introduced, incorporating a discount factor $\gamma$ for credit assignment.

The iterative process proceeds as follows for the $k$-th iteration:

**E-step: Test-Time Search for Posterior Inference**
The objective of the E-step is to determine and sample trajectories from the optimal variational posterior distribution, $\eta^*_k(\tau) = \text{arg max}_\eta J_{\alpha,\gamma}(\eta, p_{\theta_k})$. This optimal posterior corresponds to a soft optimal policy, $\eta^*_k(x_{t-1}|x_t)$, which takes the form of a reward-tilted distribution:
$\eta^*_k(x_{t-1}|x_t) \propto p_{\theta_k}(x_{t-1}|x_t) \exp(Q^*_{soft,\theta_k}(x_t, x_{t-1})/\alpha)$.
Here, $Q^*_{soft,\theta_k}$ is the soft Q-function, which is approximated using a recursive soft Bellman equation and Tweedie's formula, yielding $Q^*_{soft}(x_t, x_{t-1}) \approx \gamma^{t-1}r(\hat{x}_0(x_{t-1}))$.

Direct sampling from this optimal policy is generally intractable. To address this, the E-step employs a two-stage local search mechanism:
1. **Proposal Construction via Taylor Expansion:** If the reward function is differentiable, a proposal distribution, $\hat{\eta}_k$, is constructed.
 - **Continuous Diffusion:** For continuous latent diffusion models (e.g., Stable Diffusion), the soft Q-function is approximated by the reward function. A first-order Taylor expansion of $r(\hat{x}_0(x_{t-1}))$ around the mean of the prior distribution, $\mu_{\theta_k}(x_t, t)$, is used. This effectively incorporates the linearized reward gradient as a guidance term, shifting the mean of the Gaussian prior distribution. The proposal policy $\hat{\eta}_k$ becomes a Gaussian with this modified mean.
 - **Discrete Diffusion:** For discrete masked diffusion models (e.g., for DNA sequences), a similar first-order Taylor expansion is applied to $r(\hat{x}_0(x_{t-1}))$. This results in a categorical proposal distribution where the log-probabilities of the prior policy $p_{\theta_k}$ are shifted by a linear guidance term derived from the reward gradient.
2. **Importance Sampling Correction:** To account for potential mismatches between the constructed proposal distribution $\hat{\eta}_k$ and the true optimal policy $\eta^*_k$, a set of $M$ candidate particles are sampled from $\hat{\eta}_k$. Each particle is then assigned an importance weight, $w^m_{t-1} = \eta^*(x^m_{t-1}|x_t) / \hat{\eta}(x^m_{t-1}|x_t)$, which approximates the ratio of the true optimal policy to the proposal. A corrected sample for $x_{t-1}$ is then obtained by resampling from these particles based on their normalized weights.

This modular design allows DAV to integrate any advancements in test-time search algorithms.

**M-step: Amortizing Test-Time Search into Diffusion Models**
In the M-step, the diffusion model's parameters, $\theta$, are updated by distilling the knowledge from the high-reward, diverse trajectories collected during the E-step. This corresponds to maximizing the ELBO by projecting $\eta^*_k$ onto the diffusion model $p_\theta$ via forward KL minimization: $\theta_{k+1} = \text{arg max}_\theta J_{\alpha,\gamma}(\eta^*_k(\tau), \theta) = \text{arg min}_\theta D_{KL}(\eta^*_k(\tau)||p_\theta(\tau))$.
Minimizing this forward KL divergence is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood of the trajectories sampled from $\eta^*_k$. The training objective for DAV is thus $L_{DAV}(\theta) = E_{\tau \sim \eta^*_k}[- \log p_\theta(\tau)]$.

To prevent excessive deviation from the pretrained model's capabilities, a variant called **DAV-KL** is introduced. This adds a KL-divergence regularization term to the objective, penalizing deviations from the initial pretrained policy $p_{\theta_0}$: $L_{DAV-KL}(\theta) = L_{DAV}(\theta) + \lambda D_{KL}(p_\theta(x_{t-1}|x_t)||p_{\theta_0}(x_{t-1}|x_t))$, where $\lambda$ controls the trade-off.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of DAV was empirically evaluated across two distinct domains: continuous diffusion for text-to-image synthesis and discrete diffusion for DNA sequence design.

#### 4.1. Continuous Diffusion: Text-to-Image Synthesis

- **Setup:** Stable Diffusion v1.5 was fine-tuned with LoRA using a set of 40 animal prompts. The primary reward was the differentiable LAION aesthetic score.
- **Metrics:** Aesthetic score (reward), CLIPScore and ImageReward (prompt alignment, over-optimization detection), LPIPS-A and LPIPS-P (sample diversity).
- **Baselines:** DDPO (RL-based), DRaFT (direct backpropagation), TDPO (gradient-free RL), and DAS (test-time search).
- **Key Findings:**
 - **Over-optimization and Diversity:** DAV and DAV-KL demonstrated superior ability to maintain high alignment scores (Aesthetic, ImageReward) and preserve sample diversity (LPIPS-A, LPIPS-P) throughout the fine-tuning process, in contrast to baselines which often showed degradation (Figure 2, Table 1).
 - **Reward Optimization:** DAV achieved a high aesthetic reward (8.04), surpassing DDPO (6.83) and DRaFT (7.22), while maintaining a competitive ImageReward (0.95). DAV-KL, with its KL-regularization, traded off some reward (6.99) for improved diversity (LPIPS-A 0.58) and ImageReward (1.13).
 - **Qualitative Results:** DAV and DAV-KL generated well-aligned images without the repetitive backgrounds indicative of mode collapse observed in DDPO and DRaFT (Figure 3).
 - **Test-Time Search Superiority:** The "posterior" variants (DAV Posterior, DAV-KL Posterior), which are generated solely via test-time inference from the amortized policy, achieved the highest aesthetic scores (9.18 and 8.66 respectively) among all methods, including the pure test-time search method DAS (7.22).
 - **Importance of E-step:** Ablation studies confirmed that the test-time search in the E-step is critical for optimizing the ELBO and achieving high aesthetic scores. Simpler variants like "Search and distill" (no posterior update) or "Reweight" (no test-time search) failed to improve performance (Figure 4).
 - **Non-differentiable Rewards:** DAV-KL effectively optimized for non-differentiable rewards such as image compressibility and incompressibility, demonstrating the framework's versatility beyond differentiable objectives (Appendix J, Figures 10 & 11).

#### 4.2. Discrete Diffusion: DNA Sequence Design

- **Setup:** A pretrained masked discrete diffusion model was fine-tuned for designing DNA enhancers.
- **Metrics:** Pred-Activity (target reward from a held-out Enformer network), ATAC-Acc (biological validity), 3-mer Pearson Correlation (naturalness), and Levenshtein distance (diversity).
- **Baselines:** DRAKES (direct backpropagation), DDPO (RL-based), and VIDD (RL-based) for discrete models, along with test-time search methods SVDD and SGDD.
- **Key Findings:**
 - **Balanced Optimization:** DAV achieved a superior balance between reward optimization, diversity, and naturalness compared to baselines. While RL-based methods like DDPO and VIDD could reach high rewards, they suffered from significant drops in diversity and ATAC-Acc (biological validity), indicating over-optimization.
 - **High Reward and Naturalness:** DAV (7.71 Pred-Activity, 0.397 3-mer Correlation, 87.91 Levenshtein Diversity) outperformed fine-tuning baselines, maintaining high naturalness and diversity.
 - **Peak Performance with Posterior:** DAV Posterior achieved the highest scores in both target reward (9.24 Pred-Activity) and validity (0.920 ATAC-Acc) while preserving substantial diversity, outperforming all other methods, including strong test-time search baselines like SGDD which showed lower validity despite high diversity and reward (Figure 5, Table 2).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Diffusion Alignment as Variational Expectation-Maximization (DAV), a novel and general framework for fine-tuning diffusion models. Its primary significance lies in effectively addressing two prevalent challenges in diffusion alignment: reward over-optimization and mode collapse. By formulating alignment as an iterative variational EM process that alternates between explicit exploration (E-step) and principled distillation (M-step), DAV demonstrably achieves robust reward maximization while preserving the inherent diversity and naturalness of generated samples.

The demonstrated versatility of DAV across both continuous (text-to-image synthesis) and discrete (DNA sequence design) data modalities, and its ability to handle non-differentiable reward functions, underscores its broad applicability. This makes DAV a valuable tool for various real-world applications where generating diverse, high-quality, and functionally optimized samples is critical, such as drug discovery, materials design, and creative content generation. The modularity of its E-step, which allows for the integration of various test-time search algorithms, positions DAV to benefit directly from future advancements in efficient sampling techniques.

Potential impacts of this work include:
- **Improved Generative Models:** Providing a method for developing diffusion models that are not only high-fidelity but also precisely aligned with complex, often multi-objective, external criteria without sacrificing the richness of their generative capabilities.
- **Advancements in Scientific Design:** For domains like computational biology, DAV offers a method to design novel sequences (e.g., DNA enhancers) that are both functionally active and biologically plausible, accelerating discovery and engineering efforts.
- **More Robust Alignment Techniques:** By mitigating over-optimization and mode collapse, DAV contributes to more reliable and generalizable fine-tuning strategies, reducing the risk of generating biased or low-quality outputs in downstream tasks.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges two primary areas for future research:
1. **Computational Overhead of E-step:** The test-time search in the E-step can be computationally intensive. Future work aims to integrate more efficient search algorithms to mitigate this bottleneck.
2. **Approximation Errors in Soft Q-function:** The reliance on Tweedie's formula for approximating the soft Q-function, particularly at high noise levels, can introduce inaccuracies. Future directions could explore distillation techniques to improve the accuracy of posterior mean approximation, especially at larger timesteps, thereby yielding more reliable Q-function estimations and enhancing the framework's overall performance.
