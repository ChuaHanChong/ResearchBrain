# Reflex: Real-Time VLA Control through Streaming Inference

> [!summary] Summary
> Reflex, a streaming inference system, enables real-time, continuous control for Flow Matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by re-architecting their serving loop, achieving a 2.58x inference speedup and reducing reaction latency by up to 54% for stable 50Hz operation.

## Problem

- Iterative denoising in Flow Matching VLA models necessitates blocking execution, causing high reaction latency and unsuitability for dynamic robotic tasks.
- Standard Key-Value caching is ineffective for Flow Matching VLAs due to global timestep injections, requiring costly re-computation.
- Continuous, high-frequency inference in mixed-precision settings frequently leads to numerical instabilities and model collapse due to high-variance initialization noise.

## Method

- Introduces **Reflex**, a ==streaming inference== system that re-architects the VLA serving loop to parallelize vision and action generation.
- Employs a ==Partitioned Attention== mechanism that exploits the timestep-invariance of perception encoders for efficient O(1) incremental cache updates.
- Integrates ==AdaRMSNorm== for numerical stability during continuous ==mixed-precision inference== and an asynchronous pipeline for throughput with future-state prediction.

## Results

- Achieved a **2.58x** inference speedup and up to **54%** reduction in end-to-end reaction latency, enabling continuous **50Hz** control with a **0%** stall rate.
- Demonstrated numerical stability for over **2,000 steps** in mixed-precision streaming, preventing model collapse observed in baselines.
- Improved task success rates on dynamic benchmarks like Kinetix (**+6.7-7.4%**) and real-robot tasks (**+11-17 pp**) while maintaining performance on static tasks like LIBERO.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The timestep-invariance of VLA perception encoders allows for semantic partitioning of attention context, enabling efficient caching even with global timestep injection.
> - Achieving long-horizon numerical stability in mixed-precision requires dynamic, precision-aware normalization to mitigate high-variance noise and prevent model collapse.
> - Decoupling vision and policy streams asynchronously, coupled with future-state prediction, effectively hides vision latency and ensures accurate state-conditioned action generation for continuous control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an advancing area in embodied intelligence, enabling robots to interpret natural language instructions and execute continuous manipulation tasks. Recent developments in Flow Matching architectures have enhanced this capability by modeling high-dimensional action manifolds with increased precision compared to discrete tokenization methods. However, these models generate actions through an iterative denoising process, which typically necessitates blocking execution. This synchronous operational paradigm means a robot must complete an entire inference cycle before acting, leading to significant reaction latency in dynamic environments where targets may move.

Existing efforts to address real-time robotic control often focus on accelerating individual inference steps, employing strategies like action chunking or asynchronous inference. Action chunking generates multiple future actions per inference, but this can lead to actions being executed based on outdated observations as the control loop progresses. Asynchronous inference pipelines computation with execution by predicting future robot states to compensate for latency. While this removes the blocking gap, it does not intrinsically reduce the computational expense of the flow matching model itself, which still requires full forward passes through its backbone for each denoising step.

A standard optimization for Transformers, Key-Value (KV) caching, is crucial for reducing complexity from O(N^2) to O(1) for subsequent tokens. However, this technique is not directly applicable to Flow Matching VLAs. The time-dependent signal (timestep `t`) injected into every layer of these models causes their internal state to shift continuously during generation. This makes cached features immediately obsolete, forcing a choice between computationally expensive re-computation for correctness or potentially incorrect cache reuse. Furthermore, continuous, high-frequency inference in mixed-precision environments (e.g., BFloat16) exposes models to high-variance initialization noise more frequently than during training, posing challenges for numerical stability that are often overlooked in serving contexts.

This research addresses these limitations by re-architecting the VLA serving loop. It introduces a system-level approach that exploits the structural and temporal properties of VLA models, particularly the timestep-invariance of perception encoders. By co-designing caching mechanisms, numerical stability measures, and an asynchronous execution pipeline, this work aims to enable real-time, continuous control that overcomes the traditional bottlenecks of flow matching VLAs without compromising computational correctness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this work is to develop a streaming inference system that enables real-time continuous control for Flow Matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, bridging the gap between their computational demands and the high-frequency requirements of robotic manipulation.

The motivation stems from the inherent incompatibility between the iterative denoising process of flow matching models and the need for low-latency, responsive control in robotics. While flow matching offers precise continuous action generation, its iterative nature requires a blocking "stop-think-act" cycle. This results in significant inference latency, which can cause robots to react to outdated information, leading to failures in dynamic or interactive tasks, such as grasping a moving object. The authors argue that merely accelerating individual computation steps is insufficient; the primary bottleneck is the synchronous waiting that halts execution, rather than solely the duration of computation. A fundamental solution requires an asynchronous architecture that allows the robot to act concurrently with ongoing inference.

Specifically, the authors define three design goals (G1, G2, G3) for their streaming inference system:
- **(G1) Correctness**: The system's attention mechanism, particularly when employing caching, must produce outputs identical to full-batch attention for a fixed observation window and fixed inputs. This is a critical challenge because the global timestep injection in flow matching models typically invalidates standard KV-cache reuse, as the internal state continuously changes. Any caching strategy must account for this timestep dependency while preserving semantic correctness.
- **(G2) Stability**: The system must exhibit numerical stability during infinite-horizon mixed-precision deployment. Continuous operation at high frequencies (e.g., 50Hz) exposes the model to high-variance initialization noise far more frequently than during offline training. This elevated exposure can lead to activation spikes and numerical underflow, particularly in BFloat16 precision, risking long-term operational instability and system crashes.
- **(G3) Throughput**: The system must minimize reaction latency and maximize hardware utilization. This involves hiding the computational cost of heavy vision encoders (which can take 50-100ms) through asynchronous pipelining, while also optimizing performance at the kernel level through operator fusion to reduce overhead. The goal is to ensure actions are generated and ready for execution with minimal delay, enabling smooth, continuous control.

These objectives collectively aim to resolve the fundamental issues preventing the efficient real-world deployment of advanced flow matching VLA models for robotic control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Reflex framework is designed to facilitate continuous, real-time streaming inference for Flow Matching VLA models by parallelizing vision and action generation. The methodology is structured around three core components: partitioned attention for correctness, AdaRMSNorm for numerical stability, and an asynchronous pipeline for throughput, complemented by low-level system optimizations.

#### 3.1. Stream Correctness: Partitioned Attention

Reflex addresses the challenge of KV-cache invalidation in flow matching models by exploiting the **Timestep-Invariance Property** of VLA perception encoders, which states that visual encoders are functionally independent of the denoising timestep (`∂Enc/∂t_k = 0`). This insight allows for a structured partitioning of the model's attention context into three semantic regions:
- **Static Prefix**: Contains language tokens and system instructions. These features are computed once at the start of a task and are permanently cached, remaining invariant throughout the control sequence.
- **Sliding History**: Comprises a fixed-size (e.g., N=10 frames) FIFO queue of the most recent visual observations (`o_t-N:t`). As new frames arrive, the oldest tokens are evicted, maintaining constant memory usage and providing a sliding temporal context.
- **Dynamic Suffix**: Holds the transient flow state (`x^(k)`) and timestep embeddings, which are specific to the current denoising step. This region is recomputed for each denoising cycle.

This context factorization enables a modified attention mechanism (Eq. 1) where queries attend to concatenated keys and values from these partitioned regions. Crucially, only the dynamic suffix requires recomputation for each denoising step, allowing for **O(1) incremental cache updates** for fixed inputs and observation window while preserving outputs identical to full-batch attention for the same inputs. This approach is applicable to VLA architectures where the perception encoder does not receive the denoising timestep.

To further optimize efficiency and memory management, Reflex implements **Incremental Prefill**, which encodes only the newest observation frame at each step. Additionally, **Manual Cache Merging** is employed: before the iterative flow matching denoising loop, the static prefix and sliding history are pre-merged into a single contiguous memory buffer. The dynamic suffix is managed in a separate, pre-allocated tensor that is reset per inference cycle. This avoids frequent memory allocations and `torch.cat` operations, ensuring consistent latency during high-frequency denoising.

#### 3.2. Stream Stability: AdaRMSNorm

To counter numerical instability in long-horizon, mixed-precision streaming, Reflex introduces **AdaRMSNorm**. In continuous 50Hz operation, the model is frequently exposed to high-variance flow initialization noise (e.g., `x ~ N(0, I)` at `t = 1`), which can lead to activation spikes and BFloat16 underflow in standard normalization layers like RMSNorm.

AdaRMSNorm dynamically modulates the activation distribution by incorporating a precision-aware gating mechanism (Eq. 2): `AdaRMSNorm(x, c) = (x / RMS(x)) * γ(c)`, where `γ(c) = 1 + MLP(c)`. The conditioning vector `c` concatenates the sinusoidal timestep embedding (`t_k`) and the proprioceptive robot state (`s_t`). Critical to its stability, the Root Mean Square (RMS) calculation (`RMS(x) = sqrt(1/d * sum(x_i^2) + epsilon)`) is performed in FP32 to prevent underflow, while the gating MLP operates in BFloat16 for memory efficiency. A **Robust Dtype Inference** mechanism automatically detects the backbone's true compute precision at runtime, ensuring correct alignment and preventing type mismatch errors.

#### 3.3. Stream Throughput: Asynchronous Pipeline

To overcome the compute latency of heavy vision backbones, Reflex decouples visual perception and action generation into separate, asynchronously communicating threads (Figure 5).
- **Vision Stream (Thread A)**: Acts as a "producer," continuously encoding new camera frames and pushing the resulting KV pairs to a shared cache. This typically operates at a lower frequency (10-30Hz) due to visual processing demands.
- **Policy Stream (Thread B)**: Functions as a "consumer," querying the latest available cached state to generate action chunks through the flow matching policy. This operates at a higher frequency (e.g., 50Hz) to meet control loop requirements.

This parallel execution allows for overlapping vision encoding with action generation, effectively hiding the vision processing latency from the control loop.

A key challenge with asynchronous execution is **state alignment**, where the robot's state may have changed by the time an action is computed. Reflex addresses this via **Future-Conditional State Prediction**. Instead of conditioning the policy on the stale sensor state (`s_t`), it conditions on a predicted future state (`ŝ_t+Δ ≈ a_cmd_t`), where `a_cmd_t` is the last action commanded by the system. This lightweight heuristic assumes the robot's low-level controller accurately tracks commanded actions, providing a first-order approximation that enables the policy to generate smoother, more continuous trajectories. An **Adaptive Overlap Scheduling** mechanism (Algorithm 1) dynamically adjusts the lookahead based on real-time inference latency measurements, ensuring the next action chunk is ready precisely when the current one is exhausted.

#### 3.4. System Optimizations

Reflex incorporates low-level optimizations to ensure deterministic low latency and efficient memory usage:
- **Operator Fusion**: Multiple element-wise operations and logical projections are fused into single CUDA kernels. For instance, Query, Key, and Value projections (`W_QKV = [W_Q; W_K; W_V]`) and the Gate and Up projections in SwiGLU blocks are combined. This reduces kernel launch overhead by 50% per layer, yielding a 15-20% wall-clock speedup for single-stream inference.
- **Static Ring Buffer Architecture**: Dynamic memory allocations, which can lead to heap fragmentation and unpredictable latency spikes in long-duration operation, are replaced by a pre-allocated monolithic tensor. Custom pointer arithmetic is used to circularly index this buffer, guaranteeing O(1) memory access times and zero dynamic allocations during the control loop, ensuring consistent responsiveness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper evaluates Reflex on two VLA models (Pi0.5, 2.3B parameters, and Pi0, 3.1B parameters) across two benchmarks (LIBERO for systematic manipulation and Kinetix for dynamic, physics-rich tasks), along with real-robot deployment.

#### 4.1. System Efficiency

- **Inference Speedup**: Reflex achieves a 2.58× inference speedup on LIBERO with Pi0.5, reducing latency from 135.2ms to 52.4ms. On the larger Pi0 model, the speedup is 2.73×, indicating scale invariance. A detailed latency breakdown reveals that Reflex reduces vision encoder prefill time by 81% and denoising loop time by 52%.
- **Memory Efficiency**: Reflex maintains a flat memory footprint, reducing peak VRAM usage by 27% on LIBERO and 24% on Kinetix compared to standard inference.
- **Partitioned Attention Exactness**: Ablation studies confirm that Partitioned Attention yields an MSE of 0.00 compared to the full-batch attention oracle, maintaining exactness for fixed inputs and observation windows. In contrast, naive KV-caching results in an MSE > 1.0 and catastrophic task failure.
- **Operator Fusion**: Custom fused CUDA kernels (e.g., FlashNorm, FusedAdaLN) reduce the action expert's forward pass latency by 18%, saving 4.2ms.
- **Hardware Portability**: Evaluations on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 show that Reflex maintains a consistent speedup ratio (2.47×) compared to the RTX 4090 (2.58×), demonstrating hardware-agnostic benefits.

#### 4.2. Control Responsiveness and Stability

- **Reaction Latency**: Reflex reduces end-to-end reaction latency by up to 54% (e.g., from 156.5ms to 82.5ms for Pi0.5 on LIBERO average). This reduction is particularly pronounced in multi-step tasks like LIBERO-Long.
- **Stall Rate**: The system achieves a 0% stall rate, eliminating the blocking execution inherent in synchronous baselines (which exhibit 100% stall rates). This enables smooth, continuous control at 50Hz.
- **Stream Stability**: AdaRMSNorm significantly enhances numerical stability. While a BFloat16 baseline collapses after 120-220 steps due to NaN/Inf events, AdaRMSNorm enables stable streaming for over 2,000 steps without observed numerical issues. This is attributed to computing RMS statistics in FP32.
- **Future-State Predictor Robustness**: An ablation study on Kinetix shows that the future-state predictor improves success rates. At four delay steps, Reflex with the predictor achieves an 11 percentage point (pp) improvement over Reflex without the predictor and 22 pp over an Async-Naive baseline.

#### 4.3. Task Performance

- **LIBERO Benchmark**: Reflex maintains performance parity with synchronous baselines across LIBERO subtasks. Notable improvements are observed in LIBERO-Long tasks, with a +3.6% (Pi0.5) and +4.0% (Pi0) increase in success rate.
- **Kinetix Benchmark**: On the dynamic Kinetix benchmark, Reflex shows more pronounced improvements, with success rates increasing by +7.4% for Pi0.5 and +6.7% for Pi0. This indicates the benefits of reduced latency in reactive, physics-intensive environments.
- **Real-Robot Deployment**: On an AgileX PiPer robot, Reflex improves success rates by +11 pp on Pick-Place, +14 pp on Articulated, and +17 pp on Dynamic Recovery tasks, while maintaining 0% stall and reaction latencies between 101-110ms.
- **Context Window Size**: An ablation study on context window size (K) identifies K=10 frames as an optimal balance, yielding a 2.58× speedup with near-maximum accuracy. Very small windows degrade accuracy, while very large ones increase latency without significant accuracy gains.
- **Generalization to SmolVLA**: Reflex demonstrates applicability to the lightweight SmolVLA model (500M parameters), achieving a 2.29× speedup and an average +1.4% success rate on LIBERO subtasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a notable advancement in enabling real-time control for robotic systems utilizing complex Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, particularly those based on iterative denoising flow matching architectures. The primary significance of Reflex lies in its ability to bridge the fundamental incompatibility between the computational demands of these high-capacity models and the low-latency requirements of responsive robotic control.

The research demonstrates that the traditional "stop-think-act" paradigm, a major impediment to real-time VLA deployment, is not an inherent limitation of the models themselves but rather a consequence of system design choices. By introducing a co-designed algorithmic and runtime optimization framework, Reflex achieves stable 50Hz streaming, a 2.58× acceleration in inference speed, and a reduction in reaction latency by up to 54%. These performance gains are critical for tasks requiring dynamic interaction and precise timing, moving beyond quasi-static manipulation to more agile and responsive behaviors.

The methodological contributions are multi-faceted:
- **Timestep-Invariant Partitioned Attention**: This novel caching strategy for flow-matching models addresses the challenge of global timestep injection by maintaining O(1) cache updates while preserving computational correctness. This is a crucial step for efficient inference in models where standard KV-caching is typically invalidated.
- **AdaRMSNorm**: This adaptive normalization layer tackles the often-overlooked issue of numerical instability in mixed-precision, long-horizon streaming. By dynamically adjusting to variance shifts and ensuring critical computations are performed with appropriate precision, it prevents model collapse, allowing for prolonged, robust operation.
- **Asynchronous Pipelining with Future-Conditional State Prediction**: This architectural approach effectively hides the latency of computationally intensive vision encoders, aligning the policy's conditioning with the robot's future state at execution time. This minimizes end-to-end reaction delays, a key factor for dynamic task success.
- **Low-Level System Optimizations**: Operator fusion and a static ring buffer architecture contribute to predictable low latency and efficient memory utilization, essential for deploying large models on resource-constrained robotic hardware.

The consistent performance improvements observed across different VLA model scales (Pi0.5, Pi0, SmolVLA), diverse benchmarks (LIBERO, Kinetix), and validated on a physical robot (AgileX PiPer), suggest broad applicability and robustness of the Reflex framework.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. By enabling high-frequency, stable, and responsive control for advanced VLA models, Reflex could accelerate the deployment of autonomous robots in real-world applications such as manufacturing, healthcare, and service industries. Increased automation capabilities may have societal implications, including potential effects on employment in certain sectors. The authors acknowledge these aspects and advocate for responsible deployment with appropriate human oversight, while noting that the methods do not introduce new concerns regarding data privacy or security beyond those inherent in existing vision-language models.
