# GRL-SNAM: Geometric Reinforcement Learning with Path Differential Hamiltonians for Simultaneous Navigation and Mapping in Unknown Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> The GRL-SNAM framework introduces a geometric reinforcement learning approach that leverages Hamiltonian mechanics for Simultaneous Navigation and Mapping (SNAM) in unknown environments. It achieves efficient, safe, and robust navigation for agents, including deformable robots, by learning adaptive energy landscapes from local observations, with minimal mapping requirements.

## Problem

- Traditional deep reinforcement learning (RL) methods for Simultaneous Navigation and Mapping (SNAM) suffer from sample inefficiency and poor generalization to new environments, particularly in continuous control and long-horizon tasks.
- Existing SNAM systems often prioritize building comprehensive global metric maps, which can be computationally intensive and unnecessary for task completion, especially for deformable robots.
- Integrating safety-critical constraints directly into navigation policies without leading to conservative or myopic behavior, while also managing multi-scale decision-making (e.g., obstacle avoidance, global goal pursuit), remains a challenge.

## Method

- **GRL-SNAM** formulates SNAM as a sequential ==energy landscape== exploration problem, where navigation is guided by a learnable, context-conditioned Hamiltonian that integrates goal attraction, obstacle barriers, and object deformation costs.
- A modular, three-sub-policy architecture (sensing, frame-level motion, and shape control) is employed, with each policy induced by a module-specific Hamiltonian score field and coordinated via shared, adaptive dual weights.
- The framework utilizes a hybrid offline-online adaptation scheme: offline training learns reference Hamiltonians, and online adaptation performs geometric alignment by dynamically adjusting the Hamiltonian's input-source terms using secant Jacobian estimation to maintain stable, low-variance performance.

## Results

- GRL-SNAM achieved high path efficiency (SPL ≈ **0.95**, detour ≈ 1.09) with significantly lower mapping ratios (10-**11%** of workspace) compared to deep RL baselines which consumed more map area but performed worse.
- The framework demonstrated superior safety and success rates on challenging in-distribution and out-of-distribution environments, maintaining strictly positive clearance and effectively navigating narrow passages with a deformable robot.
- In dungeon navigation, GRL-SNAM achieved an **87.5%** success rate with approximately 500k gradient steps, outperforming full-episode deep RL methods (which had <**8%** success) and stagewise deep RL variants trained on identical data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Embedding geometric and physics-grounded Hamiltonian dynamics directly into reinforcement learning policies significantly enhances sample efficiency, stability, and generalization compared to structureless RL optimization.
> - Learning an adaptive internal 'energy terrain' from local sensory observations allows for efficient navigation with minimal mapping, where low-energy valleys guide the agent's movement and paths are refined differentially as new information emerges.
> - The coordination of multi-scale policies (sensing, motion, shape) through a shared, learnable Hamiltonian provides a principled mechanism for coherent, safe, and adaptable behavior without requiring hand-engineered hierarchical reward structures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses Simultaneous Navigation and Mapping (SNAM) in unknown environments, a challenging problem in robotics and artificial intelligence. This field requires an agent to move towards a goal while concurrently acquiring and updating a spatial understanding of its surroundings. The paper positions its contribution against several existing research paradigms, highlighting their limitations in the context of continuous, real-world, and often deformable robot navigation.

Current deep reinforcement learning (RL) methods, such as SAC and PPO, are noted for their sample inefficiency, requiring millions of interactions, and their limited generalization capabilities, with performance degrading under mild distribution shifts. These issues are amplified in SNAM due to the need for long-horizon reasoning, multi-scale decision-making, and safe, sample-efficient adaptation. Specifically, three structural limitations of many RL approaches in navigation are identified: sample efficiency in continuous control, generalization to new environments, and effective temporal scale separation between fast obstacle avoidance, mid-range planning, and global goal pursuit.

Existing SNAM systems, including SGoLAM, CMP, and CL-SLAM, typically prioritize the construction of detailed global metric maps, which are then used by learned or differentiable planners. While effective, these methods implicitly optimize for better maps rather than minimal, task-focused exploration. The presented work, GRL-SNAM, aims to achieve navigation with minimal mapping, refining paths and safety margins locally as new information emerges, rather than globally rebuilding plans.

The paper differentiates itself from prior geometric RL and Hamiltonian learning approaches, which have largely been restricted to simpler control or manipulation tasks and have not addressed deformable-body navigation or SNAM. GRL-SNAM employs a Hamiltonian formulation directly for navigation, where sensing, path planning, and deformation evolve on a shared energy manifold with differential policy optimization. This contrasts with traditional RL methods that rely on value-based objectives and Bellman recursions.

Safety-critical control techniques like Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) are often treated as external projection layers, which can lead to conservative or myopic behavior. GRL-SNAM integrates safety directly within the Hamiltonian energy through barrier potentials, co-optimizing safety and optimality.

In deformable and soft robot navigation, existing methods often use pre-programmed deformation sequences or offline-optimized actuation patterns, lacking online adaptation. GRL-SNAM addresses this by treating the deformable robot as an energy-carrying body, where deformation penalties and contact forces are encoded in a Hamiltonian that drives both motion and shape control based on local observations.

While neural scene representations (e.g., NeRF-based SLAM, Gaussian splatting) provide dense geometric information, they generally aim for accurate map reconstruction, with the control policy often being a separate layer. GRL-SNAM is complementary, as it can convert these representations into potentials for its energy terms, but its objective is to minimize mapping effort by refining a task-encoding Hamiltonian using only local observations.

Finally, the work contrasts with hierarchical RL and imitation learning methods, which often rely on hand-specified hierarchies or reward structures. GRL-SNAM achieves multi-scale coordination through coupled sensing, path, and deformation policies operating within a shared Hamiltonian framework with differential updates.

In summary, GRL-SNAM distinguishes itself by being one of the first frameworks to formulate deformable-body SNAM using coupled Hamiltonian dynamics on a shared energy manifold under strict local sensing, integrating safety and navigation objectives into the energy landscape, and coordinating multi-policy actions via differential updates. This approach aims to provide stable, sample-efficient, and robust navigation with minimal mapping.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a geometric reinforcement learning framework, GRL-SNAM, that enables Simultaneous Navigation and Mapping (SNAM) in unknown environments. This is motivated by several limitations observed in current reinforcement learning (RL) and robotics navigation paradigms when applied to complex, dynamic, and partially observable scenarios.

The core problem addressed is the difficulty of designing policies for robotic agents to move towards a goal in an environment where a complete map is unavailable a priori. The agent must acquire environmental information through sensors along its path and integrate this information into its navigation strategy.

The specific motivations for developing GRL-SNAM are:

- **Addressing Limitations of Traditional RL in SNAM:**
 - **Sample Efficiency:** Conventional deep RL methods typically demand a vast number of interactions to learn robust continuous control policies, which is impractical for real-world robotics and challenging in mapless environments.
 - **Generalization:** Black-box RL policies often memorize training environments, leading to brittle performance when encountering new obstacle layouts or sensing conditions, which is critical in unknown SNAM settings.
 - **Temporal Scale Separation:** Navigation tasks involve decisions at various timescales (e.g., fast obstacle avoidance, mid-range local planning, long-term goal pursuit). Current hierarchical RL approaches often rely on hand-engineered reward decompositions that can be difficult to manage.
- **Minimizing Mapping Effort for Task Completion:** Unlike systems that prioritize building comprehensive global maps, GRL-SNAM's objective is to reach the goal along high-quality paths while mapping as little of the unknown environment as possible. The aim is to differentially refine the path and safety margins as new local information is revealed, rather than repeatedly rebuilding global plans.
- **Leveraging Geometric and Physics-Grounded Formulations:** The research is motivated by the hypothesis that navigation policies designed with explicit consideration of geometric and physical constraints (conservation laws, kinematics, contact constraints) offer distinct advantages:
 - **Physical Realizability:** Ensures that learned dynamics are compatible with real actuators and physical constraints.
 - **Stability:** Symplectic structures, inherent in Hamiltonian dynamics, can preserve invariants (e.g., energy, momentum) and mitigate long-horizon drift in control.
 - **Compositionality:** Allows complex behaviors to emerge from the composition of interpretable energy terms (e.g., goal attraction, collision barriers, deformation costs) which can be reweighted and adapted without requiring full policy relearning.

To achieve these objectives, the research makes several contributions:

1. **Geometric RL Framework via Hamiltonians:** Formulating navigation and mapping as a sequential control optimization problem, mapping environment contexts to a reduced Hamiltonian landscape. This framework produces differentiable updates of the Hamiltonian, where differentiation in phase space corresponds to policy updates, and variation across environments induces meta-level adaptation.
2. **Multi-scale Geometric Coordination via Multi-policy Energies:** Introducing a three-submodular-policy architecture (for sensing, frame-level motion, and shape control) that composes policies through induced Hamiltonian flow maps. This structure is intended to achieve temporal scale separation without reliance on hand-designed hierarchies.
3. **Physics-Grounded Offline-Online Adaptation:** Learning Hamiltonians using a hybrid offline-online scheme. Offline training identifies admissible control objectives, while online adaptation performs geometric alignment by adapting input-source terms in the Hamiltonian dynamics, ensuring stable, low-variance adaptation.
4. **Empirical Validation on Deformable Navigation:** Demonstrating the framework's effectiveness on challenging tasks, such as a hyperelastic robot navigating narrow passages, showing higher success rates, better safety margins, and superior sample efficiency compared to various baselines.

In essence, the motivation is to move beyond structureless optimization in RL for navigation by integrating geometric and Hamiltonian principles, thereby enabling robots to navigate unknown environments efficiently, safely, and robustly with minimal prior knowledge and mapping effort.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GRL-SNAM formulates Simultaneous Navigation and Mapping (SNAM) as a sequential energy landscape exploration and mapping process, subject to dynamic and geometric constraints. The central idea involves progressively shaping an internal "energy terrain" from local sensory observations, whose low-energy valleys guide the agent's movement.

The methodology can be broken down into several interconnected components:

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation as Hamiltonian Optimization

- **Stagewise Constrained Optimal Control Problem (OCP):** The navigation of a (deformable) robot in a 2D unknown environment is formulated as a constrained optimal control problem at each stage (Equation 2). The robot's state `q = (y, c, ψ)` includes sensor configuration `y`, robot pose `c`, and deformation/object configuration `ψ`. The objective is to minimize a Lagrangian `L(q, u, t; E)` subject to robot dynamics, local collision avoidance constraints `d_i(q; E) ≥ 0`, and boundary conditions (initial state `q_0` and local target `q_target(E)`).
- **Reduction to Hamiltonian System:** Under assumptions such as control-affine dynamics and separable running costs, the necessary conditions for optimality in the OCP can be expressed via the Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP) as a Hamiltonian system (Lemma 3.1). The reduced PMP Hamiltonian `H_PMP(q, p; E)` combines the Lagrangian, state dynamics, and costate `p`.
- **Barrier Relaxation and Learnable Hamiltonian:** To enable differentiable rollout and learning, the hard state constraints `d_i(q; E) ≥ 0` are relaxed using smooth barrier potentials `b_i(d_i(q; E))` with environment-conditioned weights `α_i(E)`. This transforms the constrained OCP into an unconstrained one (Proposition 3.2), leading to a mechanical Hamiltonian structure `H(q, p; E) = 1/2 p^T M^{-1}p - V(q; E)`, where `V(q; E)` includes the running cost and barrier terms (Equation 6). The paper utilizes an IPC barrier function (Equation 25) which is shown to converge to hard constraints under specific conditions (Corollary A.4).
- **Geometric Reinforcement Learning Framework:** GRL-SNAM learns a scenario-to-energy map `E ↦ H(·; E)`. The resulting phase-space integrator `x_{t+1} = Φ_{∆t}^η(x_t; E)` (Equation 1) produces a feasible rollout using only forward passes. The proposed generalized Hamiltonian for GRL-SNAM is `H(q, p; E) = 1/2 p^T M^{-1}p + R(q; E)`, where `R(q; E)` is a potential term encompassing sensor cost (`E_sensor`), goal attraction (`E_goal`), object deformation energy (`E_obj`), and collision barriers (Equation 8, Figure 5). The sign of the potential term `R` is reversed to align with feedforward dynamics, as explained in the appendix.

#### 3.2. Navigator Score Fields and Context-Conditioned Policies

- **Modular Decomposition:** The robot's control is decomposed into three sub-modular policies operating at different timescales:
 - `π_y`: **Sensing policy** for updating sensor configuration and acquiring environment representation (`Q_y`).
 - `π_f`: **Frame policy** for short-horizon motion planning towards local "stage" goals (`Q_f`).
 - `π_o`: **Shape policy** for modulating robot morphology to navigate passages (`Q_o`).
- **Policy as Flow Map:** Each policy is defined as a time-`∆t` flow map `Φ_{∆t}^k(·; E)` induced by a module-specific Hamiltonian score field `s_{θ_k,ξ_k}^k(z_k, t; E)` (Equations 10-11). These score fields can include dissipation `Γ_{ξ_k}` and nonconservative input `G_{ξ_k} u_{ξ_k}`. The sensor and shape modules are purely symplectic (Γ=0, u=0), while the frame module includes damping and an additional force term (Equation 17).
- **Shared Dual Weights for Coordination:** Modules are coupled through a shared mechanical energy family and context-conditioned potential reshaping. A meta-policy `g_ξ` outputs nonnegative dual weights `η(E) = (β(E), λ(E), {α_i(E, t)})` (Equation 9) which modulate the contribution of `E_sensor`, `E_goal`, `E_obj`, and collision barriers in each module's potential `R_k` (Equations 13-15).

#### 3.3. GRL-SNAM Offline Training and Online Adaptation

- **Meta-Policy Learning `g_ξ(E)`:** The `g_ξ` network maps an environment context `E` to the meta-parameters `[η_ξ(E), μ_ξ(E), u_ξ,f]` (Equation 18), defining the potential and non-conservative forces for the frame module.
- **Offline Training (Algorithm 2):** This phase learns reference Hamiltonians. A supervised loss function `L(η_ξ, μ_ξ)` (Equation 20) is minimized using trajectory data. This loss includes terms for position matching, velocity matching, friction alignment, and a multi-start robustness penalty `L_multi` to discourage brittle behavior near obstacles.
- **Online Adaptation (Algorithm 1, Figure 8):** In unseen environments, GRL-SNAM performs online geometric alignment by adapting the meta-parameters.
 - **Observables:** The system monitors an observable measurement vector `y_t = [-clr_t, dist_t, -speed_t]^T` (clearance, goal distance, speed) and compares it to a desired target `y^*_t`.
 - **Secant Jacobian Estimation:** Smoothed secant Jacobians `J_t = ∂y/∂ζ` and `P_t = ∂y/∂u_f` (Equations 82, 83) are estimated online to relate changes in parameters `ζ_t = [η_t, μ_t]` and port input `u_f` to changes in observables.
 - **Tikhonov-Regularized Least Squares:** Parameter updates `∆ζ_t` are computed by solving a Tikhonov-regularized least squares problem `arg min_{∆ζ} ||J_t ∆ζ - ∆y_{des,t}||^2_2 + λ||∆ζ||^2_2` (Equation 84). The parameters are then projected to ensure non-negativity.
 - **Port Correction:** Any residual error `r_t = ∆y_{des,t} - J_t ∆ζ_t` is used to compute a nonconservative port correction term `u_f` via another least-squares problem (Equation 86), primarily for regulating speed or addressing unmodeled dynamics.
- **Multi-Scale Temporal Coordination (Figure 9):** The policies operate at natural temporal hierarchies (sensor updates at low frequency, path planning at medium frequency, shape control at high frequency), enabling stable coordination by allowing faster dynamics to equilibrate within the timescales of slower ones.

#### 3.4. Hyperelastic Ring Robot Model (for specific tasks)

- **Deformable Body Model:** For the deformable navigation task, the framework is instantiated with a planar hyperelastic ring robot. Its boundary is represented by a periodic cubic B-spline (Equation 21).
- **Shape State:** The shape policy acts on a low-dimensional shape state `z_o(t) = (s(t), p_s(t))`, where `s(t)` is a uniform scale and `o(t)` is the ring center (Figure 10).
- **Specialized Hamiltonians:** The generic Hamiltonian (Equation 12) is specialized for the ring, incorporating a mass term for scale (`M_s`) and deformation energy `E_obj`.
- **Barrier Potentials:** The collision barriers use an Incremental Potential Contact (IPC) formulation (Equation 25) which provides smooth contact forces and ensures physical behavior (Equation 26).
- **Object Potential:** The `E_obj` term (Equation 28) includes a bulk stiffness that encourages compression in tight passages and expansion in free space, based on minimal clearance.

This comprehensive methodology allows GRL-SNAM to learn and adapt local energy landscapes, enabling navigation without global maps, while adhering to physical constraints and coordinating multi-modal robotic behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GRL-SNAM was conducted on two distinct 2D navigation tasks: a deformable hyperelastic ring robot navigating cluttered workspaces and a point-agent in dungeon layouts. Both tasks shared a stagewise, local-sensing interface. The results were assessed based on navigation performance, safety, mapping/sensing budget, and learning efficiency.

#### 4.1. Effective Path Planning Under Minimal Sensing

- GRL-SNAM achieved high path efficiency comparable to Control Barrier Functions (CBF) (SPL ≈ 0.95, detour ≈ 1.09) while utilizing a minimal mapping ratio (∼10-11% of the workspace area). This mapping budget was similar to Potential Fields (PF), which yielded substantially longer paths (detour 1.42).
- Deep RL baselines (PPO, TRPO, SAC), even when using matched stagewise sensing and short-rollout distributions, consumed more map area (14-18%) but performed considerably worse in terms of SPL and minimum clearance. This indicates that the advantage of GRL-SNAM stems from its Hamiltonian-structured supervision rather than simply local sensing.

#### 4.2. Comprehensive Navigation Comparison

- **Performance across ID and OOD Environments (Figure 13):** GRL-SNAM demonstrated near-perfect success rates on both in-distribution (Test-ID) and out-of-distribution (Test-OOD) test sets. It achieved the highest Success-weighted Path Length (SPL) with low variance, indicating efficient paths.
- **Safety and Clearance:** GRL-SNAM consistently maintained strictly positive clearance, with a low grazing rate (P(clr_min ≤ d_thr)). In contrast, kinematic/dynamic RL baselines often showed lower clearance and increased collision incidents.
- **Qualitative Behavior (Figure 12):** On a representative Test-OOD bottleneck, GRL-SNAM executed a smooth "squeeze-and-recover" maneuver, navigating a narrow gap and re-centering with minimal detour, achieving perfect success and SPL. Deep RL variants (PPO, TRPO, SAC across Kin, Dyn, Coef parameterizations) frequently failed in such bottlenecks, either stalling, taking unproductive detours, or succeeding by unsafely hugging obstacle boundaries. Global planners (Rigid A*, Deform A*) succeeded but produced conservative, jagged paths with larger stand-off distances. Classical local controllers (PF, CBF, DWA) also exhibited reactive artifacts, such as local minima (PF) or oscillatory movements (CBF, DWA), resulting in less direct routes.

#### 4.3. Learning Strategies in Dungeon Navigation: Deep RL vs. Hamiltonian Supervision

- **Full-episode Deep RL Limitations (Table 3):** Standard end-to-end PPO, TRPO, and SAC methods on the long-horizon dungeon task, despite 5-8 million environment transitions, showed very low success rates (<8%) and high collision rates (∼25%), highlighting the challenges of long-horizon credit assignment under partial observability.
- **Stagewise Short-Rollout RL:** When the same deep RL algorithms were trained on the identical stagewise short-rollout distribution and structured observations as GRL-SNAM, their performance improved (18-26% success) but remained significantly below GRL-SNAM.
- **Hamiltonian Supervision Advantage (Table 4):** GRL-SNAM achieved 87.5% success with substantially lower state error and goal distance, requiring only approximately 500k gradient steps. This directly demonstrated that the structured Hamiltonian supervision, which learns an energy model encoding goal attraction, barrier avoidance, and dissipation, provided a significant advantage over optimizing a sparse scalar reward, even when controlling the same underlying dynamics and using the same observation interface.

#### 4.4. Hamiltonian Field and Constraint Learning Analysis

- **Force-Field Decomposition (Figure 14):** GRL-SNAM learned a coherent force field that balanced goal attraction (`F_g`) and obstacle barriers (`F_bs`). The learned, context-dependent coefficients (`β`, `γ`) adaptively modulated the relative strength of these components, enabling smooth, clearance-preserving trajectories.
- **Online Coefficient Adaptation (Figure 15):** During navigation, coefficients (`β`, `γ`, `α`) adapted smoothly, rebalancing goal and barrier terms as the robot approached and exited cluttered areas, leading to changes in the magnitudes of `F_g` and `F_bs`.
- **Empirical Barrier Learning (Figure 16):** The learned obstacle barrier energy closely matched the simulator's analytic reference, demonstrating that the system could capture and adapt safety-critical potentials from interaction data, with slight softening at intermediate distances and steepening near contact.
- **Replay Buffer Thriftiness (Figure 17):** The replay buffer predominantly stored local transitions (∼80%) and compact constraint/contact summaries (∼15%), indicating memory efficiency by not caching full maps or dense cost volumes.

#### 4.5. Ablations and Robustness

- **Effect of Loss Components (Table 5):**
 - Without the friction loss (`L_friction`) or multi-start robustness penalty (`L_multi`), the learned field resulted in oscillations, barrier violations, and penetrations.
 - `L_multi` alone enforced safety but led to excessively slow movement.
 - `L_friction` alone was sufficient to eliminate penetrations and produce smooth, fast trajectories.
 - Combining both terms yielded the best trade-off, with stable motion and robust behavior.
- **Robustness to Perturbations (Table 6):** GRL-SNAM maintained high success rates (≥ 87%) and positive clearance even under severe sensing noise (position jitter, radius errors, obstacle dropping/hallucination) and dynamics perturbations (damping coefficient, applied velocities). Performance degraded gracefully rather than catastrophically, as the learned Hamiltonian field re-weighted terms in response to distorted observations.

Overall, the findings indicate that GRL-SNAM's geometric reinforcement learning approach, leveraging Hamiltonian structure and adaptive energy landscapes, enables superior performance in SNAM tasks compared to various deep RL and classical planning baselines, particularly in terms of efficiency, safety, sample efficiency, and robustness to unknown environments and perturbations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GRL-SNAM framework introduces a geometric reinforcement learning approach that leverages Hamiltonian mechanics to address the complex problem of Simultaneous Navigation and Mapping (SNAM) in unknown environments. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

#### 5.1. Fundamental Contributions to Reinforcement Learning and Robotics

- **Integration of Geometric Priors:** The work demonstrates the advantages of embedding explicit geometric and physics-grounded structures (Hamiltonian dynamics) directly into reinforcement learning policies. This contrasts with many model-free RL methods that treat control as a structureless optimization problem, often struggling with stability, physical realizability, and interpretability.
- **Sample Efficiency and Generalization:** By formulating policies through adaptive energy landscapes rather than high-dimensional, black-box function approximations, GRL-SNAM exhibits substantially improved sample efficiency and better generalization to unseen and out-of-distribution environments. This is a critical factor for deploying learning-based agents in real-world scenarios.
- **Minimal Mapping Effort:** The framework enables effective navigation with minimal local sensing and mapping, refining paths based on immediate observations rather than relying on or building extensive global maps. This approach can reduce computational load and memory requirements associated with map management.
- **Unified Multi-Policy Coordination:** The decomposition into sensor, frame, and shape policies, coordinated through a shared, learnable Hamiltonian, provides a principled way to manage multi-scale decision-making without relying on hand-designed hierarchical structures or reward functions. This allows for coherent, coordinated behavior even with varying temporal dynamics.
- **Inherent Safety and Interpretability:** Integrating safety constraints directly into the Hamiltonian's energy terms (e.g., barrier potentials) ensures that collision avoidance is co-optimized with task goals. The interpretable nature of energy fields (goal attraction, obstacle repulsion) also provides a clearer understanding of the agent's decision-making process compared to arbitrary policy outputs.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact on Real-World Robotic Systems

- **Deformable Robot Navigation:** The successful application to a hyperelastic ring robot demonstrates a path toward enabling soft and deformable robots to navigate complex, tight, and cluttered environments that would be inaccessible to rigid counterparts. This could accelerate the development and deployment of robots in unstructured and human-centric spaces.
- **Autonomous Exploration and Search:** For tasks requiring autonomous exploration in unknown or partially known environments, the ability to navigate effectively with minimal mapping effort is highly valuable. This could benefit applications in planetary exploration, disaster response, and industrial inspection.
- **Robustness to Uncertainty:** The demonstrated robustness to sensing noise and dynamics perturbations indicates that GRL-SNAM could offer more reliable performance in real-world settings where perfect sensing and modeling are unattainable. This adaptability is crucial for long-duration autonomy.
- **Efficient Resource Utilization:** The memory-efficient replay buffer, which stores local transitions and compact constraint summaries, suggests that the framework is suitable for resource-constrained robotic platforms.

#### 5.3. Future Directions

The paper concludes by indicating future work will extend the approach to richer sensing modalities and more complex environments, with the ultimate goal of validating its scalability to real robotic systems. This implies that the current demonstrations, primarily in 2D simulated environments, lay the groundwork for more advanced applications.

In conclusion, GRL-SNAM represents a step toward developing robust, efficient, and interpretable learning-based navigation systems by grounding policy design in geometric and physical principles. Its potential impact lies in advancing the capabilities of autonomous agents, particularly deformable robots, to operate intelligently and safely in unknown and challenging real-world environments.
