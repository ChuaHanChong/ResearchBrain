# In-Context World Modeling for Robotic Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Fudan University and Shanghai Innovation Institute developed In-Context World Modeling (ICWM), enabling Vision-Language-Action policies to adapt to novel robot configurations by implicitly inferring system dynamics from self-generated, task-agnostic interactions. This approach improved average success rates by 13.0% on out-of-domain viewpoints in simulation and achieved 71% higher success on real-world novel viewpoints compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models struggle to generalize to novel system configurations (e.g., camera viewpoints, robot morphology) due to an implicit assumption of a fixed operational context during training.
- Performance significantly degrades when deployment environments deviate from training data, necessitating costly and data-intensive scene-specific fine-tuning for each new setup.
- Existing in-context learning methods primarily focus on 'what to do' (behavior specification), often requiring human demonstrations, while explicit world modeling approaches introduce dedicated parameters and computational overhead.

## Method

- **ICWM** reformulates VLA policies to condition actions on an implicitly inferred representation of world dynamics, derived from a short history of self-generated, task-agnostic interaction clips.
- At test time, the robot first enters an '==Active Probing Phase==' where it performs random, fixed-step movements to collect observation-action-observation tuples as interaction context.
- This interaction context is prepended to the task query, allowing a Transformer backbone (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) to implicitly identify the system configuration and adapt its action generation without parameter updates or prior calibration.

## Results

- In simulation, ICWM improved the average success rate by **13.0%** on out-of-domain viewpoints and by **9.5%** over baselines provided with explicit configuration data.
- On a real UR5e robot, ICWM achieved an average success rate **71%** higher than the Multi-View baseline when deployed under novel camera viewpoints.
- ICWM demonstrated robust generalization to semantic scene perturbations (e.g., distractor objects, novel textures) and maintained performance under robot morphological changes (e.g., gripper offsets, varied link lengths) where baselines significantly failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA generalization can be reframed as a test-time implicit system identification problem, where understanding 'how the system operates' (system configuration) is crucial for adaptation.
> - Even task-agnostic, self-generated random movements can provide sufficient interaction context for a sequence model to implicitly infer complex underlying system configurations.
> - Visual outcomes within the interaction context are essential for the model to understand the consequences of its actions and actively conditions on this information, exhibiting negative transfer with misaligned contexts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models is advancing rapidly, with models increasingly capable of interpreting complex instructions and executing robotic tasks. However, a persistent challenge for these models is their ability to generalize to novel system configurations, such as changes in camera viewpoints or robot morphology, that deviate from their training environments. Current VLA models are typically conditioned only on the immediate observation and language instruction, implicitly assuming a fixed operational context established during training. This assumption often necessitates extensive data collection and fine-tuning for each new deployment scenario, impeding the development of truly generalist robotic systems.

Existing approaches to enhance robot adaptation and generalization can be broadly categorized into several areas:
- **In-Context Learning (ICL) for Robotics:** This paradigm leverages large pre-trained models by providing expert trajectories as context, allowing the model to perform "next-token prediction" for skill acquisition. Methods like in-context imitation or cross-embodiment transfer using human play videos fall into this category. However, these methods primarily treat context as a "behavior specification" (what task to perform) and typically require human-provided demonstrations at test time.
- **Meta-Learning:** Gradient-based meta-learning seeks to optimize a model initialization that allows for rapid fine-tuning on new tasks or environments. Recurrent meta-Reinforcement Learning (RL) encodes interaction history to infer the task, often relying on reward signals that are not available prior to task execution. These approaches involve parameter updates or reward signals, which can limit their applicability in real-world, zero-shot deployment settings.
- **World Modeling for Robotic Control:** This area focuses on explicitly learning environment dynamics to predict future observations (forward models) or infer actions from visual changes (inverse dynamics models). Such approaches typically introduce dedicated parameters and training objectives for the world model. While effective in capturing dynamics, they add complexity and computational overhead.

This work, In-Context World Modeling (ICWM), positions itself differently by reframing VLA generalization as a test-time system identification problem. Instead of specifying *what* to do (like traditional ICL) or explicitly learning a world model with dedicated parameters, ICWM utilizes the context window to implicitly infer *how* the system operates from self-generated, task-agnostic interactions. This approach distinguishes itself by requiring no human-provided demonstrations, no reward signals, and no parameter updates at test time, offering a more practical and less data-intensive solution for adaptation to novel configurations. It attempts to achieve implicit world modeling as an emergent inference capability of sequence models, contrasting with methods that rely on explicit world model architectures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the In-Context World Modeling (ICWM) framework are detailed as follows:

**Problem Identification:**
The authors identify a critical limitation in current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models: their inability to generalize to novel deployment setups, such as altered camera viewpoints, sensor configurations, or robot morphologies. Standard VLA policies, represented as $\pi_{\theta}(a_t | o_t, l)$, condition actions solely on the current observation ($o_t$) and language instruction ($l$). This formulation implicitly embeds an assumption that the underlying system configuration ($\psi$, encompassing factors like camera viewpoints and robot kinematics) is fixed and known, with its properties absorbed into the model's parameters during training.

**Consequences of the Limitation:**
When the deployment environment deviates from the training context (e.g., a new camera angle is used), the model lacks the necessary information to correctly interpret observation-action correspondences. This leads to a significant degradation in performance. The prevailing remedy—scene-specific fine-tuning—is resource-intensive, requiring human intervention and data collection for every new configuration, which contradicts the goal of developing generalist, autonomously adaptable robotic systems.

**Conceptual Analogy and Inspiration:**
The research draws motivation from human motor adaptation. When a human operates a robot with an unfamiliar control mapping (e.g., a joystick), their initial instinct is to explore: perform random movements, observe the robot's response, and infer the input-output relationship. This brief, self-generated "calibration phase" allows the human to build an internal model of the system dynamics, enabling a transition from exploratory movements to goal-directed control without prior task knowledge or external guidance. The authors aim to imbue robotic policies with a similar capability.

**Theoretical Framing:**
The authors formalize this generalization failure as a "system identification problem." An ideal policy should explicitly condition on the system configuration, $\pi^{*}_{\theta}(a_t | o_t, l, \psi)$. Without $\psi$, the trained model effectively marginalizes over all configurations in its training dataset, resulting in an averaged policy that lacks specificity for any particular test-time configuration. The core motivation is, therefore, to enable the explicit recovery of $\psi$ at test time.

**Novel Approach to System Identification:**
Given the limitations of existing In-Context Learning (ICL) methods (which focus on behavior specification and often require human demonstrations) and dedicated world modeling approaches (which introduce additional parameters), the authors propose repurposing the Transformer's context window for system identification. Their key insight is that even task-agnostic, self-generated random movements can provide sufficient interaction context to implicitly recover the system configuration. This eliminates the need for task-specific demonstrations, external guidance, or parameter updates at test time.

**Specific Contributions:**
The paper outlines three main contributions:
1. **Reframe VLA generalization:** Identifying the absence of explicit $\psi$ conditioning as an underexplored failure mode.
2. **Propose In-Context World Modeling (ICWM):** A framework that implicitly identifies system configuration from self-generated, task-agnostic exploratory transitions prepended as context, allowing test-time adaptation without parameter updates or task-specific demonstrations.
3. **Validate ICWM:** Demonstrating its effectiveness on both simulation and real-robot platforms, showing significant improvements on novel camera viewpoints and generalization to semantic scene variations and robot morphological changes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

In-Context World Modeling (ICWM) is designed to enable VLA policies to adapt to novel system configurations by implicitly inferring world dynamics from self-generated interactions. The methodology involves a reformulated policy, a specific training procedure, and a two-phase inference protocol.

#### 3.1. Policy Formulation

The standard VLA policy $\pi_{\theta}(a_t | o_t, l)$ is enhanced by conditioning on an inferred representation of world dynamics. Given a short history of task-agnostic interaction clips, $T = \{(o^s_i, a_i, o^e_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, the system configuration $\psi$ is implicitly inferred by a function $\Psi(T)$. The policy is then reformulated as:
$a_t \sim \pi_{\theta}(a_t | \Psi(T), o_t, l)$
Here, $\Psi(T)$ serves as a representation of the world dynamics under the current configuration $\psi$, allowing the model to interpret $o_t$ and $l$ within the current operational context. This interaction-centric conditioning enables the policy to adapt its action selection without altering its parameters.

#### 3.2. Interaction Context (T)

The interaction context $T$ is defined as a sequence of observation-action-observation tuples. A theoretical proposition (Proposition 1, Appendix A) supports the use of interaction context, stating that under partial observability and information-preserving transitions, the interaction context $(o_{0:t}, a_{1:t})$ carries strictly more information about the time-invariant system configuration ($\psi$) than any single observation ($o_0$). Crucially, this holds for any action distribution, implying that even task-agnostic random movements can enrich the information about $\psi$.

#### 3.3. In-Context Training

To implement $\Psi(T)$, ICWM adopts a parameter-efficient design where $\Psi$ shares its parameters with the VLA backbone $\pi_{\theta}$. This is motivated by the understanding that both action prediction and configuration inference require understanding observation-action correspondences.
- **Data Structure:** Each training sample is constructed by prepending $N$ task-agnostic interaction clips to the task query. These clips are randomly sampled from a pool of interaction segments collected across all training trajectories and viewpoints. This ensures diversity in the interaction context $T$, which naturally varies with the underlying system configuration $\psi$.
- **Learning Signal:** The model is trained to predict task actions. The variation in $T$ during training implicitly incentivizes the model to learn to extract and utilize the dynamics information embedded within $T$. This process facilitates an implicit understanding of the action-to-observation mapping under different system setups.
- **Loss Function:** The model is optimized using a standard negative log-likelihood loss:
 $L = -\log \pi_{\theta}(a_t | \Psi(T), o_t, l)$

#### 3.4. Test-Time Active Probing and Inference

At deployment, ICWM employs a two-phase inference protocol that requires no gradient updates, prior calibration, or prior knowledge of the target environment.
- **Active Probing Phase:**
 - Before task execution, the robot collects the interaction context $T = \{(o^s_i, a_i, o^e_i)\}_{i=1}^N$.
 - For each step $i \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$, a random target pose is sampled within the robot’s safe workspace. The robot executes a fixed-step action $a_i$ to move towards this target. The resulting transition, consisting of the start observation ($o^s_i$), executed action ($a_i$), and end observation ($o^e_i$), is recorded.
 - These probing movements are designed to be spatially diverse to cover a sufficient portion of the local dynamics manifold. The probing workspace is defined to avoid contact with task-relevant objects, preserving the task's initial state.
 - For real-world deployment, the workspace bounding box is derived automatically from the robot's forward kinematics model, requiring no manual specification for viewpoint or scene changes. This probing phase is performed once per deployment, with the collected context reused throughout task execution.
- **In-Context Execution Phase:**
 - Once $T$ is collected, the configuration inference function $\Psi$ processes this interaction context to implicitly recover the latent system configuration.
 - Conditioned on the inferred hidden representation $\Psi(T)$, the current observation $o_t$, and the language instruction $l$, the policy generates the task action $a_t \sim \pi_{\theta}(a_t | o_t, l, \Psi(T))$.
 - Since $\Psi$ shares parameters with the VLA backbone (a Transformer architecture), this is implemented as a single forward pass. The Transformer first attends to the tokens representing $T$, building configuration-aware hidden states, before processing the task query $(o_t, l)$ to produce actions aligned with the current physical setup.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

The model utilizes Qwen2.5-VL-3B as its backbone and FAST as the action tokenizer. Actions are chunked with a size of 5, and $N=5$ context clips are used during inference. The system is implemented in PyTorch and trained on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate peaking at $5 \times 10^{-5}$ after a 50k-step warmup, followed by cosine decay.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ICWM's effectiveness in adapting to novel system configurations in both simulated and real-world environments. The findings address generalization to unseen viewpoints, the nature of implicit world modeling, and versatility across different perturbations.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results (LIBERO Benchmark)

Experiments were conducted on the LIBERO benchmark, utilizing a cross-view protocol with 8 in-domain (seen) and 6 out-of-domain (unseen) azimuthal camera viewpoints.
- **Generalization to Unseen Viewpoints:** ICWM consistently outperformed all baselines (Multi-View BC, Explicit Configuration, and pretrained models like NORA, $\pi$-FAST, $\pi_{0.5}$) across both seen and unseen viewpoints. On out-of-domain (OOD) viewpoints, ICWM improved the average success rate by 13.0% over the Multi-View BC baseline, indicating stronger resilience to geometric extrapolation.
- **Implicit vs. Explicit Configuration:** ICWM achieved a 9.5% higher OOD success rate compared to the Explicit Configuration baseline, which was provided with ground-truth camera angles. This suggests that ICWM's implicit system identification, derived from interaction context, provides a deeper understanding of system dynamics than explicit metadata alone.
- **Performance on Long-Horizon Tasks:** ICWM demonstrated the most significant gains on LIBERO-Long tasks, outperforming Multi-View BC by 29.9% on seen and 26.3% on unseen viewpoints. This is attributed to ICWM's ability to mitigate small spatial errors, which are amplified in multi-stage tasks, by continuously grounding actions in inferred system dynamics.

#### 4.2. Real-World Results (UR5e Robot)

Evaluation on a UR5e manipulator with a 12-camera multi-view system across 4 manipulation tasks showed:
- Standard VLA policies experienced a sharp performance drop from 68% to 17% when deployed under novel viewpoints, confirming the challenge of generalization.
- ICWM substantially mitigated this degradation, achieving an average success rate of 71% higher than the Multi-View baseline on novel viewpoints.
- Qualitative analysis indicated that ICWM-enhanced policies corrected end-effector drift and premature gripper closures that were common in baseline VLA models operating under spatial misalignment caused by viewpoint shifts.

#### 4.3. Analysis: What Does the Interaction Context Contribute?

Ablation studies on interaction context components in simulation provided insights into ICWM's mechanism:
- **Necessity of Visual Outcomes:** Removing images from the context clips ("w/o images") resulted in the largest performance collapse (average -56.4%), suggesting that visual outcomes are crucial for the model to understand the consequences of its actions and not merely mimic exploratory movements.
- **Importance of Paired Actions:** Removing actions ("w/o actions") led to a moderate degradation, indicating that while visual flow provides a coarse spatial anchor, the full (observation-action-observation) tuple is essential for comprehensive calibration.
- **Genuine Conditioning:** Providing "false context" (clips from a 180°-offset viewpoint) resulted in worse performance than providing no context at all (18.9% vs. 22.0% average success). This negative transfer, symmetric in magnitude to the gains from correct context, indicates that the model actively conditions on the context content for configuration inference rather than passively ignoring misaligned information.
- **Specialized Training:** A behavior cloning (BC) policy trained without in-context supervision showed near-zero performance when context tokens were prepended, confirming that ICWM's capability is not an emergent property of standard sequence modeling but requires explicit incentive during training.
- **Identifiable Representations:** t-SNE visualization of the learned hidden representations $\Psi(T)$ revealed tight within-viewpoint clustering and clear between-viewpoint separation, demonstrating that the model forms stable and identifiable representations of system configurations.

#### 4.4. Analysis: What Probing Strategy is Effective?

Evaluation of four probing strategies (Random, XY-only, Z-only, R-only) showed:
- All four strategies consistently outperformed the multi-view baseline (15-27% higher average success), indicating that the benefit of ICWM stems from the interaction format itself rather than a specific movement pattern.
- Performance differences across strategies suggested that various axes expose different aspects of local dynamics, with no single strategy dominating all viewpoints.

#### 4.5. Generalization Beyond Camera Shifts?

ICWM's robustness was stress-tested against semantic scene variations and robot morphological changes:
- **Semantic Perturbations:** ICWM maintained a consistent performance margin over the Multi-View baseline when faced with distractor objects (35.0% vs. 27.5% success) and novel table textures (41.2% vs. 37.5%).
- **Morphological Generalization (UR5e):** By attaching rigid spacers to the gripper (altering forward kinematics), ICWM consistently outperformed MV across all offsets (e.g., +60% margin). At a significant offset (ΔL = 80 mm), ICWM retained non-trivial success (14.4%) while MV largely failed (5.6%).
- **Morphological Generalization (WindowX):** On a platform with systematically varied link lengths, ICWM degraded more gracefully (77% → 62%) than MV (57% → 28%) as kinematic uncertainty increased (from 10% to 20% link-length offset). This further supports that ICWM infers altered kinematics from the probing context.

#### 4.6. Inference Latency Analysis

The computational overhead of ICWM was evaluated on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU:
- Baseline VLA: 0.112s per inference step.
- ICWM with N=3 context clips: 0.165s per step.
- ICWM with N=5 context clips: 0.185s per step.
This additional latency is attributed to processing extra image and action tokens. However, since the interaction context is static for a fixed configuration, its hidden states can be pre-computed and cached (KV caching), effectively reducing per-step inference cost to near baseline levels after the initial probing phase. The initial probing phase (20 actions) takes approximately 5-6 seconds, a small fraction of total deployment time for multi-step tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The In-Context World Modeling (ICWM) framework addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic control: the generalization gap of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to novel and unseen system configurations. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** ICWM significantly improves the ability of VLA models to adapt to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, particularly novel camera viewpoints, semantic scene changes, and robot morphological alterations. This translates to more robust and reliable robotic systems capable of operating in diverse and dynamic environments without extensive reconfiguration.
- **Reduced Data Dependency and Operational Costs:** By enabling test-time adaptation through self-generated, task-agnostic interactions, ICWM eliminates the need for data-intensive fine-tuning or human-provided demonstrations for every new deployment setup. This can substantially reduce the engineering effort, time, and computational resources associated with deploying robots in varied contexts.
- **Novel Paradigm for System Identification:** The work reframes VLA generalization as an implicit system identification problem, demonstrating that a model can infer "how the system operates" by processing short, exploratory interaction histories within its context window. This contrasts with traditional In-Context Learning (ICL) that focuses on "what to do" and explicit world modeling that relies on dedicated architectures, offering a more integrated and parameter-efficient approach.
- **Increased Autonomy for Robotic Systems:** The ability for robots to autonomously calibrate and understand their current sensory-motor relationship, akin to how humans adapt to new tools or controls, represents a step towards more autonomous and intelligent embodied AI. This self-calibration capability can facilitate faster deployment and greater flexibility in unstructured or frequently changing environments.
- **Implications for Foundation Models in Robotics:** By showing that implicit world modeling can emerge from a standard sequence modeling architecture when appropriately trained, ICWM provides insights into leveraging the inherent capabilities of large foundation models for robotics. It suggests that such models can infer complex dynamics without necessarily having specialized "world model" components, provided they are given the right interaction context.
- **Practical Deployability:** The minimal inference latency overhead and the one-time nature of the active probing phase make ICWM a practical solution for real-world robotic applications. The ability to pre-compute and cache context representations further minimizes the runtime impact, preserving control loop stability.

In summary, ICWM contributes to bridging the gap between highly capable but context-specific VLA models and the demand for generalist robots. Its approach to implicit system identification opens new avenues for research into adaptable and autonomous robotic control, potentially accelerating the deployment of advanced robotic technologies across various industries and applications.
