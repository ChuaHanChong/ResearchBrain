# IRIS: An Immersive Robot Interaction System

> [!summary] Summary
> IRIS, an open-source Immersive Robot Interaction System, enables efficient data collection and interaction for robots across diverse simulators, real-world environments, and XR hardware. User studies revealed IRIS interfaces achieved over 90% task success rates and significantly faster completion times compared to traditional methods, with policies trained on IRIS data matching or outperforming baselines in various simulation and real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Existing Extended Reality (XR) systems for robot interaction and data collection suffer from limited asset diversity, platform dependency, and specific XR device compatibility.
- These limitations hinder the reproducibility and reusability of XR-based methods, making it difficult for researchers to adapt systems to new or varied contexts.
- The inability to collect diverse and complex datasets efficiently undermines the training of robust and generalizable robot learning models.

## Method

- Developed **IRIS**, a modular, general, and extensible framework with a lightweight ==ZeroMQ== communication protocol, supporting multi-user interaction and broad compatibility across simulators and XR devices.
- Introduced a ==Unified Scene Representation (USR)== that is simulator-agnostic, allowing the visualization and interaction with arbitrary rigid and deformable objects and various robot embodiments.
- Implemented real-time ==point cloud visualization== from depth cameras for immersive real-world teleoperation and data collection, enhancing spatial awareness over traditional video streaming.

## Results

- IRIS XR interfaces (Kinesthetic Teaching and Motion Controller) achieved success rates exceeding **90%** and significantly lower task completion times (**p < 0.05**) in user studies compared to keyboard and 3D mouse baselines.
- Policies trained with IRIS-collected data in simulation demonstrated performance on par with or superior to those trained on original benchmark datasets for general manipulation and achieved high success rates for deformable object tasks (e.g., Fold Cloth **0.97**).
- In real-world evaluations, IRIS facilitated higher data collection success rates and led to more effective imitation learning policies than a monitor-based teleoperation baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A simulator-agnostic Unified Scene Representation (USR) is essential for developing highly general and extensible XR systems that can interact with diverse assets and robot types.
> - Decoupling the communication protocol from ROS (while maintaining compatibility) and using ZeroMQ enhances the cross-platform and multi-user capabilities of robot interaction systems.
> - Immersive real-time point cloud visualization significantly improves spatial awareness and interactivity for real-world robot teleoperation and data collection compared to 2D video feeds.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is reliant on the availability of diverse and high-quality data to enable robots to acquire complex behaviors. Extended Reality (XR) technologies have emerged as a method for efficient and intuitive large-scale data collection in both simulated and real-world robotic environments. XR offers immersive perspectives and interaction paradigms that can facilitate human-robot interaction and demonstration collection.

However, existing XR-based systems for robot interaction and data collection often face limitations that hinder their reproducibility and reusability. These limitations include:
- **Limited asset diversity**: Many current approaches depend on predefined sets of objects and robot models, restricting their applicability to novel scenarios.
- **Platform dependency**: A significant number of existing methods are tailored to specific simulators or real-world conditions, which complicates their adaptation to different platforms and reduces reusability.
- **XR device compatibility**: Previous frameworks tend to be optimized for particular XR headset versions, leading to issues with broader device compatibility.

These constraints collectively limit the widespread adoption and generalizability of XR-based data collection and robot interaction methodologies within the robotics research community. IRIS is presented as an effort to address these challenges by providing a more general, extensible, and compatible framework that supports a wider array of simulators, real-world environments, and XR hardware. This positions IRIS as a contribution towards enhancing the fundamental infrastructure for data-driven robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing IRIS stems from the observed challenges in existing XR-based robot interaction systems, specifically their lack of reproducibility and reusability. While current XR solutions facilitate efficient data collection, their specificity to particular robots, objects, simulators, and environments creates barriers for researchers aiming to deploy these systems in new or varied contexts. The authors highlight the importance of diverse and complex datasets for training robust and generalizable robot learning models, a goal often undermined by the limitations of current XR tools.

The key objectives of the IRIS system are detailed as follows:
- **To develop a highly general and extensible framework**: IRIS aims to support a wide range of simulators, real-world environments, and XR headsets, moving beyond the specific configurations of existing systems.
- **To facilitate cross-scenario interaction and data collection**: The system is designed to allow immersive interaction and data collection across diverse settings, encompassing arbitrary rigid and deformable objects, various robot embodiments, and multiple simulation engines.
- **To integrate real-time sensor data for real-world applications**: A specific objective is to incorporate real-time point cloud visualization generated from depth cameras, enabling immersive data collection in physical environments.
- **To enhance collaborative capabilities**: IRIS seeks to support multi-user interaction within the same virtual or mixed reality scene, allowing for coordinated tasks and collective data collection.
- **To address specific technical limitations**: This includes achieving:
 - **Cross-Scene** capability, allowing interaction with arbitrary simulated objects beyond predefined models.
 - **Cross-Embodiment** support for diverse robot types without specialized configurations.
 - **Cross-Simulator** compatibility, demonstrated by supporting multiple simulation engines (e.g., MuJoCo, IsaacSim, CoppeliaSim, Genesis).
 - **Cross-Reality** operation, enabling seamless transitions between simulated and real-world environments.
 - **Cross-Platform** compatibility across different XR devices (e.g., Meta Quest 3, HoloLens 2).
 - **Cross-User** functionality, supporting simultaneous multi-user collaboration.

By achieving these objectives, IRIS intends to provide a foundational tool that streamlines data acquisition for robot learning, thereby accelerating research in areas requiring extensive and varied datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

IRIS is designed as an Immersive Robot Interaction System leveraging Extended Reality (XR) to facilitate versatile data collection and robot interaction. Its methodology encompasses a modular system architecture, a unified scene representation, and support for diverse simulation and real-world scenarios.

#### 3.1. System Architecture

The system operates across simulation/sensor-processing computers and XR headsets, connected via a network.

#### 3.2. Node Communication Protocol

IRIS employs a lightweight communication protocol based on ZeroMQ (ZMQ), decoupled from the Robot Operating System (ROS) to ensure broader compatibility, while maintaining ROS compatibility via an interface. The protocol follows a master-node architecture: a simulation PC acts as the master, broadcasting UDP messages for automatic node discovery (e.g., by XR headsets). Upon discovery, XR nodes establish a stable ZMQ connection for data transmission, supporting both Request-Response and Publish-Subscribe patterns. This design facilitates multi-user interaction (Cross-User) and robust communication with automatic reconnection capabilities.

#### 3.3. Unified Scene Representation (USR)

To overcome the limitations of predefined models in XR systems, IRIS introduces a Unified Scene Representation (USR). This simulator-agnostic specification captures all objects with their geometry, meshes, materials, and textures, organizing them in a kinematic tree structure. A custom Python library, SimPublisher, automatically parses simulation data into this USR, which is then serialized (JSON for structure, compressed byte format for large meshes and textures) and transmitted to XR headsets. The Unity-based XR application reconstructs the scene natively. This approach enables Cross-Scene and Cross-Embodiment capabilities by allowing visualization of arbitrary objects and robots from various simulators (MuJoCo, IsaacSim, CoppeliaSim, Genesis).

#### 3.4. Multiple Headsets Compatibility

The XR application is developed using the Unity framework, which supports deployment across various XR devices. This modular design separates visualization and interaction logic, enabling developers to adapt the system to new XR headsets by implementing device-specific input handling while reusing visualization modules. IRIS has been tested on Meta Quest 3 and HoloLens 2, demonstrating Cross-Platform compatibility.

#### 3.5. Intuitive Robot Control Interface

IRIS provides Kinesthetic Teaching (KT) and Motion Controller (MC) as default robot control interfaces, based on prior research identifying them as intuitive. The flexible framework allows for customization and implementation of additional control methods, such as hand tracking or custom motion tracking systems.

#### 3.6. System Applications

IRIS's design supports a range of applications within robotics research:

#### 3.7. General Manipulation Data Collection

The flexible framework enables data collection across various robot manipulation benchmarks in different simulators, including MuJoCo-based environments (MetaWorld, LIBERO, RoboCasa, robosuite, Fancy Gym) and CoppeliaSim-based benchmarks (PyRep, Colosseum).

#### 3.8. Deformable Object Manipulation

IRIS supports the manipulation of deformable objects by dynamically updating their mesh data in real time, a feature specifically implemented with IsaacSim. This functionality facilitates data collection and algorithm testing for tasks involving soft bodies.

#### 3.9. Collaborative Manipulation

The communication protocol allows multiple users to interact simultaneously within shared virtual or physical environments. This enables collaborative data collection and coordinated robot control, expanding on systems typically limited to single-user operation.

#### 3.10. High-Dynamic Task Data Collection and Interaction

Utilizing motion controllers, IRIS can capture precise user movements for dynamic tasks, such as a simulated table tennis game, allowing for the collection of data for training policies that require timely and accurate actions.

#### 3.11. Real World Teleoperation and Data Collection

For real-world applications, IRIS employs point cloud visualization generated from RGB-D cameras rather than traditional video streaming. The system transforms camera coordinates to the robot's base frame, processes point clouds (cropping, filtering, GPU-accelerated voxel downsampling), and transmits them to XR headsets for immersive, real-time 3D scene reconstruction. This enhances spatial awareness and interactivity compared to fixed-view video feeds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness and scalability of IRIS were evaluated through experiments addressing user experience, policy training with collected data in simulation, and real-world data collection.

#### 4.1. User Experience Evaluation

A pilot study was conducted involving eight participants without prior IRIS or XR experience, focusing on four tasks from the LIBERO benchmark. The study compared IRIS's Kinesthetic Teaching (KT) and Motion Controller (MC) interfaces against LIBERO's Keyboard (KB) and 3D Mouse (3M).

#### 4.2. Objective Metrics

- **Success Rate**: IRIS interfaces (KT and MC) achieved success rates exceeding 90% across all four tasks. In contrast, non-XR interfaces (KB and 3M) frequently resulted in failures, with 3M achieving only 37.5% success on one complex task. Statistical analysis indicated significantly higher success rates for IRIS interfaces (p < 0.05) in most conditions.
- **Task Completion Time**: KT and MC consistently demonstrated lower task completion times compared to KB and 3M (p < 0.05), particularly for more complex tasks, suggesting higher efficiency.

#### 4.3. Subjective Metrics

A UMUX framework-based questionnaire, utilizing a 7-point Likert scale, assessed Experience, Usefulness, Intuitiveness, and Efficiency. IRIS's KT and MC interfaces received significantly higher scores (p < 0.05) across all criteria compared to KB and 3M, indicating positive user perception and ease of use.

These results indicate that IRIS offers a more intuitive and efficient approach for data collection than the compared baseline methods.

#### 4.4. General Manipulation

To assess the quality of data collected by IRIS, two imitation learning algorithms (BC-Transformer and BESO) were trained on datasets from LIBERO tasks collected using IRIS's MC interface and compared against policies trained on the original LIBERO dataset. The policies trained on IRIS-collected data demonstrated performance on par with those trained on the original LIBERO dataset.

#### 4.5. Deformable Objects

IRIS's capability for deformable object manipulation was evaluated using three tasks (Fold Cloth, Lift Teddy, Stow Teddy) within IsaacSim. Policies trained with a U-Net diffusion model on IRIS-collected data achieved high success rates: Fold Cloth (0.97 ± 0.018), Lift Teddy (0.90 ± 0.035), and Stow Teddy (0.85 ± 0.053).

#### 4.6. Dynamic Task Data Collection

For high-dynamic tasks, IRIS was used to collect data for a table tennis task in a Fancy Gym environment. Policies trained with imitation learning models (MoDE, BEAST) on this data demonstrated effective performance in ball interception and successful return rates, indicating the quality of data collected for dynamic movements.

These experiments collectively validate that data collected using IRIS is of comparable quality to that from traditional methods and can be effectively used for training robot learning policies across various task types, including general manipulation, deformable object interaction, and dynamic tasks.

#### 4.7. Real World Evaluation

IRIS was evaluated for real-world data collection through two tasks: Cup Inserting and Picking Up Lego. It was compared against "Tele-Op," a baseline method where users observe the scene via a monitor.

- **Data Collection Success Rate**: IRIS achieved a higher success rate during the data collection phase compared to Tele-Op.
- **Policy Success Rate**: Policies trained with IRIS-collected data exhibited better performance in executing the tasks than those trained with Tele-Op data.

These findings suggest that IRIS facilitates the collection of higher-quality real-world data, leading to more effective imitation learning outcomes.

#### 4.8. System Performance Analysis

- **Network Latency**: The system exhibits an average network latency of approximately 20-30 ms for asynchronous bidirectional data transfer.
- **Scene Loading**: Large scenes (e.g., RoboCasa scenes with over 200 MB of compressed assets and >300 objects) can be transferred and generated within 5 seconds.
- **Point Cloud Transmission**: For real robot teleoperation, the system efficiently handles 10,000 points at 60 Hz, surpassing typical camera frame rates.
- **Headset FPS**: On Meta Quest 3, a scene with one robot runs at approximately 70 FPS; on HoloLens 2, it runs around 40 FPS. Performance decreases with increased scene complexity, with Meta Quest 3 experiencing lag around 200 objects. For real-world point cloud visualization, approximately 10,000 points maintain a stable 40 FPS.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

IRIS represents a contribution to the field of robot learning by addressing critical limitations in existing XR-based systems for human-robot interaction and data collection. The system's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Reproducibility and Reusability**: By introducing a unified scene specification and supporting a diverse range of simulators, robot embodiments, and XR platforms, IRIS directly tackles the challenges of reproducibility and reusability that hinder broader adoption of XR in robotics research. This design allows researchers to deploy and adapt the system to new scenarios with reduced effort.
- **Efficient and Intuitive Data Collection**: The user studies demonstrated that IRIS's XR-based interfaces (Kinesthetic Teaching and Motion Controller) offer a more intuitive and efficient experience for data collection compared to traditional methods. This efficiency can accelerate the acquisition of high-quality, diverse datasets essential for advanced robot learning algorithms.
- **Expanded Capabilities for Robot Learning**: IRIS's support for novel functionalities, such as deformable object manipulation in XR and collaborative multi-user interaction, extends the scope of tasks that can be effectively addressed through human demonstrations. Its ability to collect data for high-dynamic tasks further broadens its applicability.
- **Bridging Simulation and Reality**: The system's Cross-Reality capability, particularly its point cloud-based visualization for real-world teleoperation, offers a more immersive and spatially aware interaction experience than video streaming. This can lead to more effective and safer data collection in physical environments, closing the sim-to-real gap for demonstration-based learning.
- **Facilitating Community-Wide Research**: As an open-source project, IRIS has the potential to become a foundational tool for the robotics research community. Its flexible and extensible codebase can encourage further development, adaptation to new hardware, and integration into diverse use cases, thereby fostering collaborative innovation in robot learning.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as basic RGB texture visualization, initial deformable object support limited to IsaacSim, and testing on a finite number of XR devices, the core architectural advancements present a step towards more generalized and accessible XR frameworks for robotics. IRIS aims to position itself as a promising solution for future data collection pipelines, supporting the development of more capable and generalist robots.
