# Adaptation of Agentic AI

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and other leading institutions introduce a comprehensive 2x2 taxonomy for adaptation strategies in agentic AI. This framework categorizes methods based on whether the agent or its tools are optimized and how the supervision signal is obtained, clarifying the research landscape and highlighting fundamental trade-offs.

## Problem

- Agentic AI systems frequently encounter challenges such as unreliable tool use, limited long-horizon planning, and poor generalization across diverse tasks.
- The rapidly expanding field of agentic AI lacked a systematic and unifying framework to categorize and understand its diverse adaptation strategies.
- Existing surveys on AI agents primarily focused on architectures or capabilities, without deeply analyzing the mechanisms by which these systems adapt and improve.

## Method

- A novel ==2x2 conceptual framework== was developed, classifying adaptation strategies based on what is being optimized (the agent or the tool) and how the adaptation signal is obtained (tool execution outcomes or agent's final outputs).
- This framework established four distinct paradigms: ==Tool Execution Signaled Agent Adaptation (A1)==, ==Agent Output Signaled Agent Adaptation (A2)==, Agent-Agnostic Tool Adaptation (T1), and Agent-Supervised Tool Adaptation (T2).
- Detailed technical reviews were conducted for numerous representative methods within each paradigm, analyzing their objectives, architectural choices, and empirical characteristics.

## Results

- The proposed 2x2 taxonomy successfully unified diverse adaptation strategies, providing a clear conceptual foundation and common language for understanding adaptation in agentic AI.
- Analysis revealed that T2 (Agent-Supervised Tool Adaptation) methods demonstrated significantly higher data efficiency, requiring up to **7**0x** less data** compared to some A2 approaches for specific subagent training.
- Four critical future research directions were identified: co-adaptation (joint optimization), continual adaptation (learning over time), safe adaptation (mitigating risks), and efficient adaptation (resource-constrained settings).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adaptation is identified as a central mechanism critical for the continued growth, reliability, and real-world applicability of agentic AI systems.
> - The concept of 'Symbiotic Inversion' (T2 paradigm) allows powerful, frozen agents to efficiently supervise the adaptation of smaller, specialized tools, thereby decoupling skill acquisition from general reasoning.
> - Agents successfully trained under A1 or A2 paradigms can be 'graduated' to become highly capable, modular T1 tools, showcasing a lifecycle of expertise within agentic systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language model (LLM) agents has progressed beyond simple prompting to encompass more complex behaviors such as tool use, persistent memory, and reusable skills. Prior research has demonstrated capabilities ranging from general-purpose LLM assistants like ChatGPT, to systems leveraging on-policy reinforcement learning for reasoning and tool use (DeepSeek), and agents that accumulate persistent memory and reusable skills (OpenClaw).

Despite these advancements, the existing research landscape on LLM agents is characterized by fragmentation. Various mechanisms for improving agents—including post-training methods, retrieval systems, memory architectures, and skill libraries—have often been studied in isolation. Existing surveys on AI agents typically provide broad overviews of agentic systems without a specific focus on adaptation mechanisms.

This survey addresses this fragmentation by introducing a unified framework that organizes these disparate developments under the singular concept of "adaptation." Adaptation is defined as any process that improves an agent, its tools, or their interaction subsequent to pretraining. The work positions itself as distinct from prior surveys by specifically focusing on the mechanisms through which agentic AI systems evolve and specialize. It aims to provide a structured understanding of how post-training, memory systems, and skills interact within this adaptation framework, thereby offering a cohesive perspective on agent evolution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to create a unified conceptual framework for understanding and categorizing the diverse approaches to adaptation in agentic AI systems. This framework aims to provide a structured lens through which current and future developments can be analyzed and compared.

The motivation for this work arises from several observed limitations in current agentic AI systems:
- **Unreliable Tool Use**: Agents often struggle with consistent and effective invocation of external tools.
- **Shallow Long-Horizon Planning**: The ability of agents to formulate and execute multi-step plans over extended timeframes remains limited.
- **Domain-Specific Reasoning Gaps**: General-purpose LLMs frequently exhibit shortcomings in specialized domains, requiring further customization.
- **Poor Generalization**: Agents may perform inadequately in environments or tasks that significantly deviate from their training distributions.

These limitations indicate that even highly capable foundation models necessitate additional adaptation to specialize for particular tasks or deployment scenarios. The authors identify three core mechanisms driving this adaptation:
1. **Post-training**: Methods such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), and preference optimization (PO) that modify the agent's parameters.
2. **Memory Systems**: Architectures like episodic buffers, reflective databases, and structured knowledge graphs that enable agents to retain and build upon past experiences without full model retraining.
3. **Skills**: Reusable units of procedural knowledge that accumulate through various mechanisms, from internalized tool-use procedures via post-training to external skill libraries.

The paper aims to map how these three mechanisms interact within a shared design space, providing a systematic treatment that exposes trade-offs and guides the selection of adaptation strategies based on supervision signals, task requirements, and system-level constraints.

Specific contributions highlighted include:
- Establishing a 2x2 framework for decomposing agentic adaptation, comparing adaptation locus (agent vs. tool) and signal source (execution-grounded vs. output-evaluated).
- Systematically analyzing agentic memory and skills within this framework.
- Proposing a multi-dimensional evaluation framework to assess adaptation across different paradigms and identify evaluation gaps.
- Mapping adaptation paradigms to real-world applications in diverse domains.
- Identifying open research challenges and future opportunities, such as co-adaptation, continual learning, safety, and efficient deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The survey constructs a unified framework for agentic AI adaptation, classifying existing studies based on two primary dimensions: (1) what component is optimized (the agent or the tool), and (2) how the adaptation signal is obtained. This yields four distinct paradigms:

**A. Agent Adaptation**: Focuses on modifying the agent's internal parameters, representations, or behavioral policies.
- **A1: Tool Execution Signaled Agent Adaptation**: The agent is optimized using feedback signals derived from the verifiable execution results of external tools it invokes. The signal captures immediate outcomes such as code sandbox results, retrieval relevance scores, or API call outcomes. This typically involves supervised fine-tuning (SFT) based on successful tool-call trajectories or reinforcement learning (RL) using tool-execution outcomes as rewards.
- **A2: Agent Output Signaled Agent Adaptation**: The agent is optimized using evaluations of its own final outputs, such as comprehensive answers, plans, or reasoning traces, potentially after integrating tool results. This includes both tool-free outcome-based learning and tool-augmented adaptation driven by answer correctness or preference scores. Similar to A1, optimization can be through SFT (combining tool-call imitation and final-output supervision) or RL, where rewards are based on the quality of the agent's final output.

**B. Tool Adaptation**: Focuses on optimizing the external callable components (tools) that extend the agent's capabilities, while keeping the core agent fixed.
- **T1: Agent-Agnostic Tool Adaptation**: Tools are trained independently of any specific agent. These are typically pre-trained modules such as retrievers, domain-specific models, or simulators that can be integrated as plug-and-play components. Their optimization is guided by intrinsic tool performance metrics (e.g., retrieval accuracy, simulation fidelity).
- **T2: Agent-Supervised Tool Adaptation**: Tools are adapted using signals derived from the outputs of a fixed (frozen) agent. This paradigm aims to construct or refine tools to better complement the frozen agent's capabilities. Adaptation can occur via supervised learning, where the agent's final output quality (e.g., accuracy, preference score) provides a quality-weighted signal for tool improvement, or via reinforcement learning, where the tool is updated based on the reward assigned to the agent's final output.

The framework further contextualizes these paradigms by discussing their instantiation through three core mechanisms:
1. **Post-training**: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Preference Optimization (PO), and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).
2. **Memory Systems**: Episodic buffers, reflective databases, and structured knowledge graphs, largely classified as T2 when updated by a frozen agent, or T1 when pre-trained.
3. **Skills**: Reusable procedural knowledge, which can be acquired through A1/A2 post-training or managed as T1/T2 tool ecosystems.

The survey's methodology involved reviewing literature published through early 2026, primarily from top-tier machine learning and natural language processing conferences (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL, EMNLP, NAACL) and high-impact arXiv preprints. Methods were categorized based on their dominant optimization locus and signal source. The authors acknowledge that quantitative cross-paradigm comparisons in the literature may be subject to confounding factors due to differences in experimental setups.

Illustrative examples are provided across retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and code-execution-based tasks to demonstrate how different methods map to the A1, A2, and T2 paradigms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey's analysis yielded several key findings regarding the characteristics and trade-offs of the four adaptation paradigms:

**A. Comparison of Agent Adaptation Paradigms (A1 vs. A2):**
- **A1 (Tool Execution Signaled)**: Methods in this paradigm optimize the mechanics of individual tool calls using dense, verifiable, and causally grounded feedback (e.g., test-case pass rates for code, retrieval recall for search). This leads to strong, specialized performance in domains with deterministic tool outcomes. Examples include DeepRetrieval for search query reformulation and RLEF/Code-R1 for code synthesis. This approach can be highly data-efficient due to precise feedback.
- **A2 (Agent Output Signaled)**: This paradigm focuses on optimizing the agent’s overall strategy for tool use and holistic reasoning, using sparse, high-level rewards based on the final output (e.g., final answer correctness in QA). It enables agents to learn complex strategic behaviors like when to invoke a tool, what to search for, and how to integrate results. Examples include Search-R1 and ReSearch for RAG. A2 methods can lead to emergent behaviors like self-correction but often require more data due to the sparsity of the reward signal and the difficulty of credit assignment.
- **Trade-off**: A1 offers mechanistic precision for tool mastery, while A2 provides strategic flexibility for end-to-end task optimization.

**B. Comparison of Tool Adaptation Paradigms (T1 vs. T2):**
- **T1 (Agent-Agnostic)**: These are pre-trained, plug-and-play components (e.g., CLIP, SAM for vision; AlphaFold2 for protein structure prediction). Notably, agents initially trained via A1 or A2 methods can be "graduated" by freezing their parameters and redeploying them as T1 tools (e.g., DeepRetrieval as a query rewriter). T1 offers high system-level flexibility and reusability across different agents but may not be optimally tailored to a specific agent's needs.
- **T2 (Agent-Supervised)**: This paradigm inverts the adaptation target, optimizing external tools or lightweight subagents (e.g., 7B models) to better serve a fixed, frozen foundation model (e.g., GPT, Claude). The frozen agent provides high-level reasoning and reward signals, while the tool learns narrow procedural skills (e.g., adaptive search, memory curation, planning). Examples include s3 for agentic searchers, Mem-α for memory construction, and AgentFlow for meta-cognitive planners.
- **Key Findings on T2**: T2 methods demonstrate substantial data efficiency advantages. For instance, s3 achieved comparable accuracy to A2-style end-to-end agent training (Search-R1) with approximately 70 times fewer training examples in retrieval-augmented QA. This efficiency stems from the specialized nature of the learning task for the subagent, which benefits from the frozen agent's inherent knowledge and reasoning abilities. T2 also offers architectural modularity, allowing tools to be updated or swapped without risking catastrophic forgetting in the core agent.

**C. Cross-Paradigm Insights:**
- **Cost and Flexibility**: A1/A2 methods are computationally expensive, requiring retraining large models, but offer broad parametric flexibility. T1/T2 methods are generally cheaper, optimizing smaller external components, and provide high system-level flexibility through modularity.
- **Data Efficiency**: T2 methods can significantly reduce data requirements for learning new skills compared to A2, particularly when the core agent's knowledge can be leveraged.
- **Modularity and System Evolution**: T1 and T2 foster modular system design, allowing for incremental updates and specialization without destabilizing the core agent, contrasting with the monolithic nature of A1/A2 updates.
- **Memory and Skills as Adaptation Mechanisms**: The survey systematically treats memory modules and skill libraries as forms of adaptive tools, primarily under the T2 paradigm, highlighting their role in enabling long-term learning and efficient task execution for frozen agents.

**D. Evaluation Framework Insights:**
- The survey emphasizes the inadequacy of static, single-score benchmarks for evaluating agentic adaptation.
- It distinguishes between **Verifiable Execution Metrics** (aligned with A1/T1, e.g., code pass@k, retrieval recall), which provide dense, causal feedback for tool mechanics, and **Holistic Utility Metrics** (aligned with A2/T2, e.g., final-answer EM, LLM-as-judge), which assess end-to-end task performance. Both are necessary for comprehensive evaluation.
- A significant gap exists in standardized **T2-specific benchmarks** and controlled **cross-paradigm comparisons**, making it difficult to definitively compare the efficiency and effectiveness of different adaptation strategies under matched conditions.
- The importance of **dynamics-aware evaluation** is highlighted, including tracking sample efficiency, generalization across tasks/agents, and continual adaptation stability (e.g., catastrophic forgetting).
- **Systemic requirements** such as cost, safety (unsafe exploration, reward hacking), and alignment (human-agent, agent-tool) are recognized as critical for deployed systems and require dedicated evaluation.

**E. Application Mapping**: The survey maps the dominant adaptation paradigms across domains like deep research, software development, computer use, and drug discovery, illustrating how factors like feedback availability and retraining costs influence the choice of adaptation strategy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey contributes to the field of agentic AI by providing a structured and comprehensive framework for understanding adaptation. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

**A. Conceptual Clarity and Research Guidance:**
- **Unified Understanding**: The proposed 2x2 framework (A1/A2/T1/T2) offers a systematic lens to categorize and analyze diverse adaptation methods, which were previously fragmented. This clarity can help researchers position their work and identify underexplored areas.
- **Informed Design Choices**: By detailing the trade-offs in cost, flexibility, data efficiency, generalization, and modularity for each paradigm, the survey provides practical guidance for practitioners and researchers in selecting appropriate adaptation strategies for specific tasks and constraints. It outlines strategic recommendations, such as using A1 for local mechanistic mastery, A2 for global strategic coordination, T1 for horizontal reusability, and T2 for efficient specialization around a fixed agent.
- **Architectural Shifts**: The analysis highlights an emerging trend toward modular, tool-centric architectures (T1/T2), where frozen foundation models serve as stable cognitive cores, supported by an evolving ecosystem of specialized, adaptive subagents. This shift suggests a move away from monolithic, costly retraining toward more flexible and scalable system designs.

**B. Advancing Evaluation Practices:**
- **Multi-Dimensional Evaluation**: The proposed evaluation framework moves beyond single-score metrics to advocate for a multi-dimensional assessment that includes signal quality, adaptation dynamics (efficiency, generalization, stability), and systemic concerns (cost, safety, alignment). This encourages a more holistic and diagnostically informative evaluation of agentic systems.
- **Identifying Gaps**: The survey explicitly points out critical gaps in current evaluation practices, particularly the lack of standardized T2 benchmarks and controlled cross-paradigm comparisons. This call for more rigorous benchmarking is crucial for validating the claims of different adaptation approaches and fostering cumulative research.
- **Dynamic Assessment**: Emphasizing dynamics-aware evaluation (e.g., learning curves, catastrophic forgetting, safety trajectories) underscores the importance of understanding the *process* of adaptation, not just the endpoint performance.

**C. Shaping Future Research Directions:**
The survey identifies four key opportunities for future research that emerge directly from its taxonomy:
- **Co-Adaptation**: Developing frameworks to jointly optimize both agents and tools simultaneously, addressing complex credit assignment and stability challenges in bi-level learning systems. This represents a significant move beyond the current practice of freezing one component while adapting the other.
- **Continual Adaptation**: Addressing the challenge of non-stationary task distributions by enabling agents to continuously learn new tasks and adapt to evolving environments without catastrophic forgetting. This involves integrating techniques from continual learning, parameter-efficient updates, and external memory mechanisms.
- **Safe Adaptation**: Mitigating dynamic safety risks inherent in adaptive agentic systems, such as unsafe exploration (e.g., during on-policy RL) and parasitic adaptation (e.g., reward hacking, adversarial tooling). This calls for robust safety guardrails and evaluation methods that account for autonomous risk-taking behaviors.
- **Efficient Adaptation**: Exploring methods to reduce the computational and memory costs of adaptation (e.g., parameter-efficient adaptation like LoRA, quantized adaptation, and on-device/personalized adaptation) to enable wider deployment on resource-constrained devices.

In conclusion, this survey provides a timely and essential consolidation of the rapidly expanding field of agentic AI adaptation. By offering a cohesive framework, detailing trade-offs, and highlighting critical research gaps and opportunities, it aims to guide the development of more capable, robust, efficient, and continually evolving AI agents.
