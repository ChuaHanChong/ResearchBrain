# One Policy to Control Them All: Shared Modular Policies for Agent-Agnostic Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley, Google, and Carnegie Mellon University developed Shared Modular Policies (SMP) that enable a single control policy to generalize across diverse robot morphologies with varying state and action space dimensions. This modular approach, particularly with two-way message passing, allows for zero-shot generalization to unseen agents and facilitates the emergence of complex, coordinated locomotion gaits.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning typically requires training a separate control policy for each distinct robot morphology, necessitating extensive re-training and hyperparameter tuning.
- Creating general-purpose controllers is challenging due to the inherent variability in robotic agents, including differing numbers of limbs, sensor configurations, and action space dimensionalities.
- Unlike other machine learning domains, robotics lacks robust methods for generalizable pre-trained models that can transfer knowledge across diverse robot structures.

## Method

- Introduced **Shared Modular Policies (SMP)**, an architecture where a global control policy is composed of identical, interacting neural network modules.
- Represented each agent morphology as an ==undirected graph==, with limbs as nodes and joints as edges, allowing for a structured representation of diverse body plans.
- Implemented modular communication via learned ==message passing== (both-way, top-down, or bottom-up) between neighboring limbs, conditioning local policy actions on received messages.

## Results

- Shared Modular Policies with both-way message passing consistently achieved higher average rewards compared to a monolithic multi-task reinforcement learning baseline.
- Demonstrated zero-shot generalization by successfully controlling **20%** of unseen agent morphologies with high rewards without further fine-tuning.
- Message vector analysis showed cyclic patterns in root node messages, indicating the capture of contextual information essential for rhythmic and coordinated locomotion gaits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Message passing between modular policy components is critical for achieving coordinated behaviors and learning diverse locomotion gaits across heterogeneous agents.
> - Both-way message passing enables the emergence of a centralized coordination mechanism, where a root node aggregates information and disseminates control signals.
> - The shared modular policy architecture inherently supports zero-shot generalization, allowing control over robot morphologies not encountered during training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Current methodologies in deep reinforcement learning (RL) typically involve training a control policy specifically for a single robot morphology. This approach often necessitates extensive hyperparameter tuning and re-training from scratch for each new agent, regardless of how similar its task or structure might be to previously encountered agents. This agent-specific paradigm contrasts with the development of generalizable pre-trained models observed in other machine learning domains, such as image classification or natural language processing, where models can transfer knowledge across diverse inputs.

The challenge of creating general-purpose controllers for robots is compounded by the inherent variability in robotic agents. Different morphologies can possess varying numbers of limbs, unique sensor configurations, distinct motor command spaces, and require diverse control behaviors. This heterogeneity makes direct multi-task learning difficult, as a single monolithic policy struggles to adapt to changes in state and action space dimensionality.

Biological systems, in contrast, often exhibit modularity and reuse in their sensorimotor control, allowing for efficient adaptation and coordinated movements across complex bodies. This biological inspiration suggests that a modular approach could offer a pathway toward more generalizable robotic control. Existing works have explored modular control for reconfigurable robots or meta-learning for parameter variations, and dynamic graph networks have investigated modularity for self-assembling agents. However, these approaches often allow for morphology adaptation or focus on fixed-parameter variations. This paper specifically addresses the challenge of learning a single policy that performs across *diverse static morphologies* that necessitate *different locomotion gaits*, aiming for pre-trainable priors akin to those in other AI fields.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the feasibility of developing a single, global control policy capable of generalizing to and controlling a wide variety of agent morphologies. This includes agents where the dimensionality of the state and action spaces changes significantly due to differing skeletal structures (e.g., monopod hoppers, quadrupeds, bipeds).

The motivations for this objective are multifaceted:
- **Overcoming Agent-Specific Training Limitations**: Current state-of-the-art RL methods require training a distinct policy for each robot morphology. This process is resource-intensive, requiring considerable computational power and manual hyperparameter tuning for each new agent. The authors aim to move beyond this limitation by developing a policy that can control multiple agents simultaneously with shared parameters.
- **Enabling General Pre-trained Priors**: Analogous to pre-trained models in computer vision or natural language processing, the research seeks to establish a foundation for pre-trainable priors in robot control. Such priors could enable new robots to acquire complex behaviors with significantly less data or training time.
- **Addressing Morphological Diversity**: The work is specifically motivated by the challenge posed by agents with drastically different physical structures and kinematic properties. A general controller must not only adapt to varying limb counts and geometries but also learn to coordinate these diverse structures to achieve goal-directed behaviors, which may involve entirely different locomotion gaits (e.g., hopping for a monopod versus walking for a biped).
- **Leveraging Biological Modularity**: The natural world provides examples of modularity and reuse in sensorimotor systems, where local units contribute to global coordination (e.g., muscle groups, central pattern generators). The authors are motivated to explore if similar principles can be applied to artificial control policies to manage complexity and facilitate generalization.
- **Facilitating Emergent Coordination**: A significant motivation is to investigate whether complex, globally coordinated behaviors can emerge purely from a reinforcement learning objective, driven by decentralized modules that communicate locally, rather than being explicitly programmed or centrally orchestrated.

In essence, the paper aims to develop a robust, scalable, and generalizable RL framework that can control an array of robotic agents without the need for agent-specific policy design or extensive re-training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology is the introduction of **Shared Modular Policies (SMP)**, an architecture that expresses a global policy as a collection of identical, interacting neural network modules.

#### 3.1. Agent Representation as Graphs

Each agent morphology is represented as an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$, where each node $v_i \in V$ corresponds to a limb (actuator) and an edge $(v_i, v_j) \in E$ indicates a joint connection between limbs. For the purpose of message passing, these graphs are typically considered connected and acyclic (trees), with one limb designated as the root (e.g., the torso).

#### 3.2. Sensorimotor Modules

The control policy, $\pi_\theta(\cdot)$, is composed of identical modules. The parameters $\theta$ of this module are shared across all limbs of all agents being controlled. At each timestep $t$, each module instance receives a local sensory state $s_k^t$ for its corresponding $k$-th limb and outputs torque values $a_k^t$ for that limb's actuator. The local state $s_k^t$ includes global positions, positional and rotational velocities, 3D rotations (represented via exponential map), and the range of movement of the limb's joint. The torso limb, though without an actuator, still has a module for message passing.

#### 3.3. Modular Policy Optimization

The objective is to maximize the cumulative discounted reward across all $N$ agents simultaneously:
$$ \max_{\theta} E_{\pi_{\theta}} \left[ \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r_n^t(\{s_k^{t+1}\}_{k=1}^{K_n}, \{a_k^t\}_{k=1}^{K_n}) \right] $$
where $a_k^t = \pi_\theta(s_k^t, \text{messages})$ and $\gamma$ is the discount factor. The policy is optimized using a standard actor-critic setup, specifically the TD3 (Twin-Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient) algorithm, without significant modifications to its core implementation.

#### 3.4. Modular Communication via Message Passing

To enable coordination between limbs and facilitate diverse gaits, each limb's policy module $\pi_\theta$ is conditioned not only on its local state but also on a learned message vector generated by its neighboring limbs. Messages are 32-dimensional vectors. Three message passing schemes are investigated:

- **Bottom-Up Message Passing**: Messages flow from leaf nodes towards the root node. The action $a_k^t$ and message $m_{k \to p(k)}^t$ (from limb $k$ to its parent $p(k)$) are generated based on the local state $s_k^t$ and an aggregated input from messages received from its children, $f(\{m_{i \to k}^t\}_{i \in C(k)})$. The aggregator function $f(\cdot)$ combines messages from child nodes into a fixed-dimension output.
- **Top-Down Message Passing**: Messages flow from the root node towards the leaf nodes. The action $a_k^t$ and message $m_{k \to c_k}^t$ (from limb $k$ to its children $c_k$) are generated based on the local state $s_k^t$ and the message received from its parent, $m_{p(k) \to k}^t$.
- **Both-Way Message Passing**: This scheme combines both directions, first performing a bottom-up pass to aggregate information towards the root, and then a top-down pass from the root to the leaves to generate actions. This is implemented by dividing the modular policy into two sub-policies, $\pi_{\theta_1}$ for the upwards pass (outputting messages) and $\pi_{\theta_2}$ for the downwards pass (outputting actions and messages). This structure is designed to allow the root node to emerge as a centralized information aggregator and disseminator, similar to a central nervous system.

#### 3.5. Handling Different Numbers of Children Nodes

To accommodate varying numbers of children per parent node across diverse morphologies, a "caching trick" is employed. In the top-down pass, a parent module outputs a fixed maximum number of messages (equal to the maximum number of children any joint has across all agents). If a joint has fewer children, the relevant messages are used, and the others are ignored. Similarly, for bottom-up aggregation, if a node has fewer children than the maximum, zero vectors are appended to compensate, ensuring a consistent input dimension for the aggregation function.

#### 3.6. Experiment Setup

- **Environments**: Standard Gym MuJoCo locomotion tasks are used, including 'Walker2D-v2', 'Humanoid-v2', 'Hopper-v2', and 'HalfCheetah-v2'. The 3D humanoid is constrained to 2D.
- **Agent Variants**: To test generalization, multiple variants of each agent type are created by generating subsets of limbs from the original morphology, ensuring connectivity and inclusion of the torso. These variants result in collections like 'walker++', 'humanoid++', 'cheetah++', 'walker-hopper++', and 'walker-hopper-humanoid++', ranging from 2 to 18 distinct agents per collection. 80% of variants are used for training, and 20% are held out for zero-shot generalization testing.
- **Neural Network Architecture**: The internal modules (for bottom-up and top-down passes) are 4-layered fully-connected neural networks with ReLU and tanh non-linearities.
- **Training Protocol**: Experiments are run in parallel across environments with independent replay buffers. Actions are initially sampled randomly, then guided by the policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the efficacy and properties of Shared Modular Policies.

#### 4.1. Comparison with Multi-Task RL Baseline

The Shared Modular Policies (SMP) with both-way message passing consistently outperformed a standard monolithic multi-task RL baseline. The baseline, which uses a single centralized policy with zero-padded state and action spaces to handle varying dimensions across agents, largely failed to achieve significant rewards across diverse environments (Figure 3). This indicates that simply increasing the capacity of a centralized network is insufficient to handle the morphological and behavioral diversity addressed in this work. In contrast, the modular approach, particularly with both-way communication, successfully learned to control a wide array of agents.

#### 4.2. Role of Message Passing

Message passing was found to be crucial for successful learning, with different schemes exhibiting varying degrees of effectiveness.
- **Both-Way Superiority**: The both-way message passing scheme consistently achieved higher average rewards across all tested environments compared to decentralized schemes (top-down only, bottom-up only) and the "no messages" setting (Figure 3).
- **Enabling Diverse Gaits**: For agents requiring distinct locomotion strategies (e.g., a two-limb walker that hops versus a four-limb walker that walks), both-way message passing enabled the policy to model and execute these diverse gaits simultaneously (Figure 4). Decentralized schemes often failed to learn meaningful behaviors for agents with highly varied gaits, especially those with fewer limbs.
- **Emergent Centralization**: The effectiveness of both-way message passing suggests that a form of centralized coordination emerged, with the root node (e.g., the torso) acting as an aggregator of global information during the bottom-up pass and a disseminator of control signals during the top-down pass.

#### 4.3. Zero-Shot Generalization

A key finding is the ability of SMP with both-way message passing to generalize to unseen agent morphologies in a zero-shot manner (Figure 5). The policy, trained on 80% of variants, achieved high rewards on the remaining 20% held-out variants without any further fine-tuning. This demonstrates that the learned modular policy acquired a set of general principles for locomotion that are transferable across novel morphological structures. Decentralized message passing schemes showed limited or no zero-shot generalization capabilities.

#### 4.4. Root Node Agnosticism

The choice of the root node in the agent's graph representation did not significantly impact performance. Experiments showed that defining a non-torso limb (e.g., a left foot) as the root node yielded comparable or even slightly better training rewards than the default torso-root setup, indicating robustness to this structural choice.

#### 4.5. Analysis of Message Content

Analysis of the message vectors revealed that they capture contextual information essential for coordinated behaviors. Specifically, the t-SNE projection of the top-down root node messages over an episode exhibited cyclic patterns, which correlated with the alternating gaits observed in agents like walkers and hoppers (Figure 6). This suggests that the messages convey information about the agent's overall pose and contribute to rhythmic, coordinated movements, further supporting the idea of emergent centralized control from decentralized modules. Message range analysis also indicated that messages convey meaningful contextual information, with closer messages being more correlated with local states and actions.

#### 4.6. Sanity Check on Single-Agent Environments

When tested on single-agent environments, SMP with both-way message passing achieved performance comparable to monolithic policies trained specifically for those individual agents (Figure 8). This indicates that the modular architecture, despite the overhead of learning message passing for coordination, does not suffer a performance penalty when applied to simpler, single-agent control tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes contributions to the field of reinforcement learning and robotics, with several potential impacts:

- **Foundation for General-Purpose Robot Control**: By demonstrating that a single, shared modular policy can effectively control a wide array of diverse robot morphologies, including those with varying state and action space dimensions, the research establishes a foundation for truly general-purpose robot controllers. This moves beyond the paradigm of designing and training individual policies for each specific robot.
- **Enabling Pre-trained Priors for Robotics**: The ability of the Shared Modular Policies to generalize to unseen morphologies in a zero-shot manner suggests the possibility of creating "pre-trained priors" for sensorimotor control. This could significantly reduce the data and training requirements for deploying new robots or teaching novel tasks, akin to the impact of large pre-trained models in computer vision and natural language processing.
- **Scalability and Efficiency in Multi-Robot Systems**: The modular architecture, with its shared parameters and local communication, offers potential benefits for scalability in scenarios involving multiple, heterogeneous robots. Instead of maintaining separate policies for each robot type, a single modular policy could govern an entire fleet.
- **Insights into Emergent Coordination**: The observation that complex, globally coordinated behaviors and even a form of centralized control can emerge from decentralized, locally communicating modules through a pure reinforcement learning objective provides valuable insights into the principles of sensorimotor organization, both artificial and biological. This could inform the design of more robust and adaptive control systems.
- **Robustness to Morphological Variations**: The demonstrated robustness to variations in morphology and even the choice of graph root node highlights the adaptive capabilities of the proposed architecture. This is critical for real-world applications where robots may experience slight structural differences or damage.

Overall, this research represents a step towards creating more adaptable, intelligent, and autonomous robotic systems that can operate effectively across a broad spectrum of physical forms without requiring extensive re-engineering for each new configuration.
