# Automated Weld Seam Recognition and 3D Mapping for Robotic Post Processing Using Photogrammetry and Semantic Segmentation

> [!summary] Summary
> An experimental vision pipeline localizes weld seams in 3D for robotic post-processing. Smartphone images from multiple viewpoints feed two branches: RealityScan photogrammetry reconstructs the AprilTag-scaled workpiece, while a SegFormer network segments weld masks that are thinned into centerline splines, projected via depth maps and camera poses, and fused across views. Reconstruction error was about 3 mm and weld localization RMSE 4.2 mm.

## Problem

- Laser and structured-light scanning of large welded workpieces is slow, limited in measurement volume, and produces large volumes of data irrelevant to post-processing.
- Photogrammetry is rarely explored for weld seam localization and reconstruction, and the few existing efforts are confined to small measurement volumes.
- Low-resolution depth cameras used as a coarse pre-scanning stage lack the spatial resolution to reliably detect narrow or geometrically complex weld seams.

## Method

- Photogrammetric branch: multi-view smartphone images with surrounding ==AprilTag markers== processed in **RealityScan** to yield a metrically scaled 3D model, camera poses, intrinsics, and depth maps.
- Segmentation branch: a **SegFormer** network trained on 388 images for binary weld/background masks, then noise filtering, ==connected-component analysis==, and ==PCA-guided slicing== to extract centerline spline points.
- Fusion: image-space spline points lifted to camera coordinates via depth maps, transformed into the global frame by camera pose, then de-duplicated and outlier-filtered across views.

## Results

- Photogrammetric reconstruction matched physical outer dimensions of the **40 x 10 x 5 cm** welded tube specimen to roughly **3 mm**, with only minor local surface deviations.
- Projected weld spline points compared against a manually annotated reference weld line gave a root mean square error of **4.2 mm**, confirming millimeter-level coarse localization.
- The SegFormer model segmented the seam in most of the **68** captured **1920 x 1080** images, with occasional misses from lighting and reflections plus some false weld regions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A coarse localization stage can restrict expensive high-precision scanning to seam regions, trading absolute accuracy for large reductions in acquisition effort.
> - Commodity smartphone imagery plus fiducial markers suffices for metrically scaled reconstruction, lowering hardware cost versus laser or structured-light scanning setups.
> - Segmentation robustness, not geometry, is the bottleneck: reflective metal surfaces and lighting variation drive both missed seams and false-positive spline points.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of automated robotic post-processing, encompassing operations such as grinding, smoothing, finishing, and inspection, increasingly relies on precise knowledge of workpiece geometry, particularly the location and form of weld seams. Historically, these tasks have been performed manually or with limited automation in many industrial settings. The broader research landscape has explored various methods for acquiring the necessary 3D geometric information.

A significant body of work has focused on identifying weld seams from 3D point clouds and meshes, typically captured using high-precision laser scanners or structured light sensors. While these techniques offer high accuracy, they often present limitations when applied to large workpieces. These limitations include the time-consuming nature of complete surface scanning, the generation of substantial amounts of irrelevant data, and their primary suitability for smaller components due to constraints in scanning speed and measurement volume. The substantial data generated can also lead to increased processing overheads, further hindering efficiency in industrial applications involving large parts.

An alternative approach for acquiring 3D data, photogrammetry, has been explored less extensively for weld seam localization and 3D reconstruction. Existing applications in this domain have primarily been limited to small measurement volumes, similar to the constraints faced by high-precision scanners. The potential of photogrammetry to provide 3D data for larger objects without the high cost or specialized hardware of laser scanners, but with its own set of challenges, represents an underexplored avenue.

For large workpieces, the inefficiency of complete high-resolution scanning has motivated research into preliminary stages that can guide subsequent high-precision measurements. Such preliminary stages aim to reduce overall scanning effort by focusing detailed measurements only on regions containing weld seams. Previous efforts in this direction have included the use of low-resolution depth cameras. However, these systems often suffer from limited spatial resolution, which can impede the reliable detection of narrow or geometrically complex weld structures, thereby restricting their utility in scenarios requiring moderate precision.

This current work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the need for an efficient first-stage localization process for weld seams on large workpieces. It seeks to overcome the limitations of high-precision scanners (time, data volume) and low-resolution depth cameras (spatial resolution) by proposing a vision-based pipeline that leverages the accessibility of standard cameras, the broad applicability of photogrammetry for 3D reconstruction, and the robustness of semantic segmentation for weld seam identification. By focusing on approximate localization as a preliminary step, the research aims to improve the efficiency of targeted high-precision scanning and subsequent robotic operations, thereby bridging a gap in current automated post-processing methodologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The fundamental motivation for this research stems from the ongoing industrial requirement for accurate weld seam geometry in 3D space to facilitate automated robotic post-processing operations. Tasks such as grinding, smoothing, finishing, and inspection are critical steps after welding, yet they frequently remain manual or rely on limited automation due to the complexities involved in precisely localizing and characterizing weld seams for robotic execution.

One primary challenge is the inefficiency inherent in current 3D data acquisition methods, particularly when dealing with large workpieces. High-precision laser scanners and structured light sensors, while capable of providing accurate geometric information, are often time-consuming for large areas and generate an excessive volume of data, much of which may not be relevant to the specific task of weld seam post-processing. This leads to a substantial increase in processing time and computational resources. The authors note that existing photogrammetry applications for weld seam localization have been limited to small measurement volumes, indicating a gap in its application for larger industrial components.

The paper highlights the deficiency of existing preliminary guidance systems, such as those employing low-resolution depth cameras. While these systems aim to reduce scanning effort by providing an initial, coarse localization, their limited spatial resolution often proves insufficient for reliably detecting the narrow or geometrically complex weld features commonly encountered in industrial environments. This inadequacy means they cannot consistently provide the necessary guidance for subsequent high-precision operations.

Motivated by these challenges, the key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:
- To investigate an alternative weld seam localization approach that is efficient for large workpieces, moving beyond the limitations of full high-resolution scanning.
- To develop a preliminary stage localization process that can guide subsequent high-precision measurements, thereby reducing overall scanning effort and improving data acquisition efficiency by focusing detailed measurements on regions containing weld seams.
- To establish a methodology that combines image-based weld detection with photogrammetric 3D reconstruction using readily available hardware, such as a standard smartphone camera. This aims to leverage the accessibility and cost-effectiveness of common imaging devices.
- To create a pipeline that generates an approximate spatial representation of weld seams, serving as a foundational input for targeted high-precision scanning and subsequent robotic post-processing operations. The emphasis is on providing "approximate localization" as a first step, rather than high-precision measurement, thereby optimizing the overall workflow.

In essence, the research is driven by the need for a scalable, efficient, and robust method for preliminary weld seam localization that can integrate seamlessly into robotic automation workflows for large industrial components, thereby addressing a critical bottleneck in the advancement of automated post-welding processes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a vision-based pipeline designed for the approximate localization and 3D mapping of weld seams. As depicted in Figure 1, the workflow proceeds through two parallel processing branches that ultimately merge to form a spatial representation of the weld seam.

#### 3.1. A. Data Acquisition and 3D Reconstruction

The initial phase involves acquiring a set of images and reconstructing the workpiece in 3D.
- **Workpiece Setup:** AprilTag markers are strategically placed around the welded workpiece. These markers serve as known geometric references, crucial for scaling the reconstructed 3D model and for aligning different frames of reference. Their known dimensions provide a basis for accurate spatial scaling.
- **Image Capture:** Multiple overlapping images of the welded workpiece are captured from various viewpoints. A standard smartphone camera is used for this purpose. The overlapping nature of the images is essential for the photogrammetric reconstruction process, allowing the software to identify common features across different views. The AprilTag markers are ensured to be visible in the captured images. The experimental setup involved a total of 68 images, each with a resolution of 1920x1080 pixels, collected to provide comprehensive visual coverage of both the workpiece geometry and the weld seam.
- **Photogrammetric Processing:** The acquired image set is processed using RealityScan, a photogrammetry-based software tool. This software performs the 3D reconstruction of the workpiece, generating a 3D representation. In addition to the 3D model, RealityScan also outputs critical camera parameters, including the camera poses (position and orientation) for each captured image, the intrinsic parameters of the camera (e.g., focal length, principal point), and depth maps corresponding to each image.

#### 3.2. B. Weld Seam Segmentation & Centerline Extraction

Simultaneously with 3D reconstruction, weld seams are identified and characterized in the 2D images.
- **Semantic Segmentation:** A SegFormer-based semantic segmentation network is employed for weld seam recognition. The task is formulated as a binary classification problem, distinguishing between "weld" and "background" pixels within an image.
- **Training Data:** The network was trained using a dataset comprising 388 images. These images were collected from various sources, including publicly available online datasets and custom captures from the surrounding environment, to enhance the model's generalization capabilities.
- **Output:** The trained network processes input images and generates binary masks, where pixels identified as belonging to a weld seam are marked.
- **Centerline Extraction:** The predicted binary weld masks undergo a series of post-processing steps to derive a thinner, spline-like representation of the seam:
 - **Filtering:** Small and noisy regions within the generated masks are filtered out to improve the robustness of detection.
 - **Component Identification:** Connected weld components within the mask are identified.
 - **Orientation Estimation:** For each connected weld region, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is applied to estimate its dominant orientation. This provides a local directional context for the weld.
 - **Slice Computation:** The mask is then divided into local slices along the estimated dominant direction.
 - **Center Point Calculation:** For each slice, a center point is computed. The sequential collection of these center points forms a spline-like representation of the weld seam in the 2D image space.

#### 3.3. C. 3D Projection and Multiview Fusion

The final stage involves projecting the 2D weld seam representations into the 3D reconstructed model and consolidating them.
- **3D Projection:** The extracted 2D spline points (center points of weld slices) are projected into 3D space. This is achieved by utilizing the depth maps and camera calibration data (intrinsic parameters and camera poses) obtained from the photogrammetric reconstruction process. Each 2D image point representing a weld spline is first converted into 3D camera coordinates using its corresponding depth value and intrinsic parameters. Subsequently, these camera coordinates are transformed into the global reconstruction coordinate system using the respective camera pose (position and orientation) for that image.
- **Multiview Fusion:** Since a single weld seam can be visible in multiple captured images, its 3D projection may result in several sets of reconstructed weld points. To obtain a coherent 3D representation, all these reconstructed weld points are transformed into a common coordinate frame and then merged. This merging process includes removing duplicate observations and applying outlier filtering techniques to refine the consolidated 3D weld seam representation, resulting in a single, robust spatial mapping of the weld seam on the reconstructed workpiece geometry.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presented an experimental investigation into the feasibility of an integrated pipeline for automated weld seam localization. The primary objective was to assess the overall viability of the proposed workflow as a proof of concept rather than to conduct a rigorous quantitative evaluation of individual processing stages.

#### 4.1. A. 3D Reconstruction Performance

- The photogrammetry-based reconstruction successfully generated a 3D point cloud of the test workpiece. The scale of this reconstructed model was consistent with the physical dimensions of the actual workpiece, which measured 40 cm × 10 cm × 5 cm.
- A dimensional error of approximately 3 mm was observed when comparing the outer dimensions of the reconstructed model to the actual physical dimensions of the workpiece.
- The overall geometry of the workpiece was reconstructed, though minor local deviations and surface mismatches were present. These inaccuracies were considered acceptable for the stated purpose, as the method is intended for approximate localization to guide subsequent high-precision measurements.

#### 4.2. B. Weld Seam Segmentation Performance

- The custom-trained SegFormer model demonstrated the ability to identify and segment the weld seam within the majority of the captured images. This indicates the model's effectiveness in discerning weld regions from the background under various conditions.
- However, the segmentation process was subject to certain limitations:
 - Missed detections occurred in a small number of instances, primarily attributed to variations in lighting conditions and surface reflections present during image acquisition.
 - Occasionally, regions without actual welds were misclassified as weld seams. This led to the generation of spurious weld spline points in areas where no physical weld existed.

#### 4.3. C. 3D Projection and Localization Accuracy

- The extracted 2D weld spline points were successfully projected onto the reconstructed 3D geometry of the workpiece using the associated depth maps and camera pose information. This process enabled the conversion of 2D weld masks into a 3D spatial representation.
- The resulting spatial representation provided an accurate indication of the approximate location and extent of the weld seam on the workpiece's 3D model.
- To quantitatively assess the localization performance, the projected 3D weld spline points were compared against a manually annotated reference weld line. This comparison yielded a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 4.2 mm. This millimeter-level accuracy suggests that the proposed method can provide sufficiently precise preliminary localization for industrial guidance applications.

#### 4.4. D. Overall Workflow Feasibility

- The experimental results collectively demonstrated the feasibility of integrating image-based weld seam detection with photogrammetric 3D reconstruction for coarse weld localization.
- Despite the noted minor inaccuracies in the 3D reconstruction and occasional errors in the segmentation stage, the workflow successfully produced a spatial map of the weld seam with an accuracy in the millimeter range. Such a map is considered suitable for guiding subsequent high-precision inspection or measurement procedures.
- The system's ability to generate this approximate map supports the objective of reducing overall scanning effort by delineating regions of interest for more detailed data acquisition.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an integrated experimental pipeline for automated weld seam localization, contributing to the field of robotic post-processing. The primary significance of this work lies in its approach to combine semantic segmentation for image-based weld detection with photogrammetric 3D reconstruction and subsequent 3D weld mapping.

The main contribution is the proposed integrated experimental pipeline itself, which systematically connects several processing stages: weld seam segmentation, mask-based centerline extraction, depth-assisted 3D projection, and multi-view weld point fusion. This comprehensive approach differentiates it from methods that focus solely on 2D weld detection by aiming to localize the weld seam directly onto the reconstructed 3D object geometry. This direct 3D localization is crucial for applications that require spatial understanding of the weld for robotic interaction.

The potential impact of this work is primarily in enhancing the efficiency and automation of industrial robotic post-processing operations.
- **Reduced Scanning Effort:** By providing an approximate 3D map of weld seams, the system enables more targeted high-precision scanning. Instead of scanning an entire large workpiece at high resolution, detailed measurements can be confined to the identified regions of interest, thereby significantly reducing overall data acquisition time and computational load. This efficiency gain is particularly beneficial for large-scale industrial components where complete high-resolution scanning is often impractical.
- **Foundation for Robotic Applications:** The generated approximate 3D weld path serves as a preliminary guide for subsequent, more precise robotic tasks. This makes it a suitable foundation for developing automated robot trajectories for operations such as grinding, finishing, and inspection of welded components. The long-term objective of generating executable robot trajectories directly supports the goal of full automation in these challenging industrial processes.
- **Accessibility and Cost-effectiveness:** The use of a standard smartphone camera for image acquisition and photogrammetry offers a potentially more accessible and cost-effective alternative to high-precision laser scanners or structured light systems, especially for initial localization tasks. This could lower the barrier to entry for smaller manufacturers or allow for more flexible deployment in diverse industrial environments.

However, the reported limitations also highlight areas for future development that are crucial for maximizing the impact of this research. Addressing the segmentation model's sensitivity to lighting, reflections, and image quality by expanding the training dataset and applying stronger augmentation strategies is necessary for robust industrial deployment. Improvements in 3D weld path refinement and the estimation of local surface normals and tool orientations are critical steps toward generating accurate and executable robot trajectories. Overcoming these limitations would solidify the proposed pipeline's role as a robust and practical solution for advanced robotic post-processing. KG (PFERD) further underscore its relevance and potential impact on industrial manufacturing processes.
