# PRISM: Projection-Integrated Sampling-Based MPC with Bayesian Cost Tuning for Bimanual Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> PRISM is a sampling-based MPC framework for bimanual manipulation that uses a GPU-accelerated MuJoCo simulator as an online world model. Sampled joint-velocity trajectories are projected by a custom ADMM quadratic-program solver onto the set satisfying position, velocity, acceleration and jerk bounds plus initial-velocity continuity, decoupling exploration from feasibility; Bayesian optimization tunes cost weights offline. Success reaches 80-100% across four tasks at 10-16 Hz, with dual-UR5e sim-to-real transfer.

## Problem

- End-to-end imitation and reinforcement policies for bimanual manipulation stay tied to their demonstration distribution, degrading on unseen obstacle layouts or task constraints without extra data collection or fine-tuning.
- Sampling-based MPC baselines such as MPPI and CEM yield non-smooth controls; post-hoc filters, spline parameterizations and low-pass sampling never enforce strict acceleration or jerk bounds.
- Cost-weight tuning for multi-phase, multi-objective bimanual planners remains manual and task-specific, while prior Bayesian-optimization work targeted controller gains rather than task-cost weights.

## Method

- **PRISM**'s ==QP-guided control sampling==: every sampled joint-velocity trajectory is projected onto the set meeting position, velocity, acceleration and jerk bounds plus ==MPC== boundary continuity before GPU-parallel ==MuJoCo== rollout evaluation.
- Custom ==ADMM==/==Bregman-splitting== QP solver exploiting joint-wise separability and reusable matrix factorizations, solving independent per-joint horizon-wise projections in parallel so projection cost stays negligible against rollouts.
- ==MPPI==-style importance-weighted mean update with ==CEM==-style adaptive diagonal covariance, plus offline ==Bayesian optimization== of task-cost weights over 1,000 randomized episodes per task.

## Results

- Against baselines, QP projection reaches **95%** ball lifting, **90%** handover and **95%** tray moving versus vanilla CEM's **70%**, **15%** and **30%**; low-pass filtering leads only on obstacle-free box lifting, **90%** versus **80%**.
- Bayesian-optimized weights raise success from **75%** to **100%** (ball), **60%** to **95%** (handover), **45%** to **90%** (tray) and **45%** to **80%** (box), with no added online computation.
- A single-joint diagnostic gives effective sampling deviation **0.4716** versus **0.0045** baseline and **0.0106** low-pass, roughly **100x** and **45x**; under obstacle shift PRISM succeeds **60%** versus **20%** for a learned policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Projecting samples onto the feasible set beats restricting the sampling distribution: any constrained sampling-based MPC can decouple exploration breadth from motion smoothness this way.
> - Feasibility enforced before rollout removes auxiliary machinery baselines need, collision-free inverse-kinematics warm-starts, smoothness penalties, filter cutoff hyperparameters, replacing them with physically meaningful actuator bounds.
> - Online physics-based planning complements learned policies under geometric distribution shift, but still needs an accurate simulator, hand-designed costs, and struggles with asymmetric objects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dual-arm manipulation is an active area in robotics research, driven by the potential for complex tasks such as assembly, cooperative object handling, and interaction with unstructured environments, capabilities that often exceed those of single manipulators.

Historically, end-to-end learning approaches, including imitation learning and reinforcement learning, have been a dominant paradigm. These methods have demonstrated success in learning high-dimensional policies by directly mapping perceptual input to motor commands and implicitly handling complex arm-object interactions. However, these learning-based approaches often require extensive and diverse training data. Their performance can be sensitive to distribution shifts, necessitating additional data or fine-tuning when encountering environmental configurations not well-represented in the training set, such as novel obstacle placements or modified task constraints.

This work positions itself as a complementary paradigm: real-time, model-based planning that utilizes a GPU-accelerated physics simulator as an online world model. This approach aims to address the limitations of learned policies by enabling direct reasoning over the current geometric and physical state of the scene, including previously unseen obstacle layouts, object poses, and contact interactions. This model-based strategy is particularly relevant for contact-rich bimanual manipulation, where collecting diverse expert demonstrations is challenging and online adaptation to evolving interaction dynamics is crucial for task success.

The paper connects to several specific areas of related work:
- **Sampling-based planning for manipulation:** This work builds on the flexibility of sampling-based optimization, which can evaluate forward rollouts of a world model with arbitrary task costs, unlike gradient-based methods that require explicit characterization of task geometry and contact structures. It leverages GPU-accelerated physics simulators for parallel trajectory evaluation, similar to approaches by Howell et al. and Pezzato et al. It addresses the challenge of exploring low-dimensional manifolds defined by coordination constraints in bimanual tasks, which are difficult for naive random sampling.
- **Smoothness in sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC):** A common challenge in sampling-based MPC, such as MPPI and CEM, is the generation of non-smooth control sequences, leading to oscillatory actuation and degraded performance. Previous strategies include post-hoc filtering, control-space lifting, frequency-domain shaping (e.g., LPF-MPPI), and polynomial/spline parameterization. This paper particularly draws inspiration from projection-based methods (e.g., π-MPPI) that minimally correct sampled controls to satisfy bounds, thereby decoupling exploration from feasibility.
- **Automatic cost function tuning:** The performance of sampling-based planners is sensitive to the weighting of competing objectives. Manual tuning is labor-intensive and task-specific. Bayesian optimization (BO) has been used for hyperparameter and reward tuning in robotics, and this work extends its application to task-specific cost weights in multi-phase, multi-objective bimanual manipulation.
- **End-to-end learning for bimanual manipulation:** While acknowledging the advances in imitation learning and reinforcement learning for bimanual tasks, the paper highlights their challenges, such as data collection, satisfying hard physical constraints, and inference costs. PRISM offers a model-based alternative that adapts online, explicitly reasons about physical interactions, and could potentially generate synthetic expert trajectories or serve as a planning component within model-based reinforcement learning frameworks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a robust, real-time, model-based planning framework for complex bimanual manipulation in cluttered, contact-rich environments. The framework, named PRISM (Projection-Integrated Sampling-Based MPC), aims to overcome key challenges in bimanual manipulation, specifically the requirement for coordinated motion generation, interaction-aware planning, and reliable execution under tight kinematic constraints.

The key motivations and detailed objectives are:

1. **Addressing Limitations of End-to-End Learning:** The paper explicitly acknowledges the success of imitation learning and reinforcement learning but highlights their dependency on extensive, diverse training data and sensitivity to distribution shifts (e.g., novel obstacles). PRISM is motivated by the need for an alternative approach that can adapt online to previously unseen configurations by directly reasoning over the current geometric and physical state of the scene.
2. **Enabling Feasible and Smooth Exploration:** A core objective is to allow broad exploration of coordinated bimanual behaviors while ensuring that all candidate motions are kinematically feasible and smooth. Traditional sampling-based methods struggle with a trade-off: large exploration noise leads to jerky, dynamically inconsistent trajectories, while conservative noise limits the search space. PRISM aims to decouple these concerns through a novel QP-guided control sampling strategy. This strategy projects sampled joint-velocity trajectories onto the set of motions satisfying joint position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk bounds, along with an initial-velocity boundary condition, *before* rollout evaluation.
3. **Achieving Efficient Online Execution:** To support real-time or near-real-time operation, the framework needs to perform computations efficiently. An objective is to develop a custom QP solver using ADMM/Bregman-splitting that exploits joint-wise separability and reusable matrix factorizations, thereby accelerating the projection step.
4. **Reducing Manual Parameter Tuning:** The performance of sampling-based planners is highly sensitive to the weighting of various cost components (e.g., collision avoidance, task progress, bimanual coordination, trajectory regularization). Manual tuning is labor-intensive and often task-specific. PRISM aims to automate this process by using Bayesian optimization to tune task-cost weights offline, thereby reducing human effort and improving reliability.
5. **Demonstrating Robustness and Generalization:** A practical objective is to evaluate PRISM on challenging bimanual manipulation tasks that involve significant coordination and contact interactions, potentially in cluttered environments. The goal is to demonstrate improved robustness and task success rates compared to existing sampling-based baselines, while also showcasing successful sim-to-real transfer to dual robotic manipulators. This includes adapting to geometric distribution shifts that might challenge learned policies.

In essence, the work seeks to provide a practical, model-based alternative to demonstration-heavy policy learning for contact-rich bimanual manipulation by integrating sophisticated sampling, constraint enforcement, and automated tuning techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PRISM framework integrates a GPU-accelerated physics simulator (MuJoCo) as an online world model within a Model Predictive Control (MPC) loop, featuring a novel projection-integrated sampling strategy and Bayesian cost tuning.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The bimanual planning problem is formulated as an optimization over a finite horizon $H$ to minimize a stage cost $c(\theta_k, \dot{\theta}_k, x_k)$, subject to system dynamics, kinematic constraints, and boundary conditions:
$$
\arg \min_{\dot{\theta}_{1:H}} \sum_{k=1}^H c(\theta_k, \dot{\theta}_k, x_k)
$$
subject to:
- $(\theta_{k+1}, x_{k+1}) = f(\theta_k, \dot{\theta}_k, x_k)$ (system dynamics, where $f$ is the MuJoCo simulator)
- $\theta^{(r)}_{\min} \le \theta^{(r)}_k \le \theta^{(r)}_{\max}$ for $r \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ (joint position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk bounds)
- $\theta_0 = \theta_{\text{init}}$, $\dot{\theta}_0 = \dot{\theta}_{\text{init}}$ (initial position and velocity boundary conditions)

The stage cost is a weighted sum of task-specific components, $c = \sum_i w_i c_i$, where $w_i$ are weights tuned by Bayesian optimization.

#### 3.2. Sampling-Based Optimization (Algorithm 1)

PRISM employs an optimizer combining elements of MPPI (importance-weighted mean update) and CEM (weighted diagonal covariance adaptation).
1. **Distribution Initialization**: A Gaussian distribution $N(\nu_m, \Sigma_m)$ over joint-velocity sequences $\dot{\theta}_{1:H}$ is initialized.
2. **Sampling and Projection**: At each iteration, $n$ candidate velocity sequences $\tilde{\dot{\theta}}_{1:H,j}$ are drawn from $N(\nu_m, \Sigma_m)$. Each sample is then *projected* onto the feasible set of kinematically bounded trajectories by solving a Quadratic Program (QP). This yields projected, feasible sequences $\dot{\theta}^*_{1:H,j}$.
3. **Parallel Rollouts and Cost Evaluation**: The projected velocity sequences $\dot{\theta}^*_{1:H,j}$ are then used in parallel MuJoCo physics rollouts to simulate the resulting manipulator and environment trajectories $(\theta_{k,j}, x_{k,j})$ over the horizon. The total trajectory cost $c_j$ for each rollout is evaluated.
4. **Importance Weighting and Distribution Update**: Importance weights $\omega_j$ are computed for each sample based on its cost using a temperature-scaled exponential. These weights are used to update the mean $\nu_{m+1}$ (MPPI-style) and diagonal covariance $\Sigma_{m+1}$ (CEM-style) of the Gaussian distribution, concentrating the search around high-quality solutions.
5. **Receding Horizon**: The process is repeated for $M$ iterations. The first few actions of the lowest-cost projected sequence are executed, and the planning problem is re-initialized at the next control step, warm-starting with the final distribution parameters from the previous cycle.

#### 3.3. MPC Planner (Figure 2)

The sampling-based optimizer is embedded within a receding-horizon MPC loop. At each control step, the current robot and environment state are observed to initialize the optimization. Only the first few computed controls are applied to the system, and the process repeats. The final distribution parameters $(\nu_M, \Sigma_M)$ from the previous step are time-shifted and reused to warm-start the next optimization, improving sample efficiency.

#### 3.4. Role of Quadratic Program (QP)

The QP (Eq. 3) is a central component that minimally corrects sampled velocity sequences to ensure kinematic feasibility:
$$
\dot{\theta}^*_{1:H} = \arg \min_{\dot{\theta}_{1:H}} \frac{1}{2} \|\dot{\theta}_{1:H} - \tilde{\dot{\theta}}_{1:H}\|^2_2
$$
subject to:
- Joint position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk bounds ($\theta^{(r)}_{\min} \le \theta^{(r)}_k \le \theta^{(r)}_{\max}$, for $r \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$).
- Initial position and velocity boundary conditions ($\theta_0 = \theta_{\text{init}}$, $\dot{\theta}_0 = \dot{\theta}_{\text{init}}$).

**Advantages of QP Projection:**
1. **Decoupling Exploration and Smoothness**: The QP allows the sampling distribution to have a large covariance for aggressive exploration without producing non-smooth, non-executable trajectories. Each sample is guaranteed to be feasible after projection.
2. **Stabilizing Warm-Start**: By enforcing derivative bounds, the QP ensures planned trajectories are inherently smooth. This prevents sharp oscillations between MPC steps, keeping successive solutions close and making warm-starting more effective.
3. **Empirical Validation**: A single-joint experiment demonstrated that QP projection enables an effective exploration radius approximately 100 times larger than baseline (uncorrelated Gaussian) and 45 times larger than LPF (low-pass filtered Gaussian) methods, while always satisfying kinematic bounds. This increased coverage of the feasible action space directly translates to a higher probability of discovering high-quality trajectories.

#### 3.5. Custom QP Solver

The projection QP is solved using an ADMM-inspired Bregman-splitting scheme.
1. **Formulation**: All derivative bounds (position, velocity, acceleration, jerk) are expressed as affine functions of the stacked joint-velocity trajectory $\dot{\Theta}^*$. These, along with the initial velocity boundary condition, are converted into a compact QP formulation with equality and inequality constraints.
2. **ADMM Iterations**: The solver iteratively updates:
 - **Slack variable**: Projection onto the non-negative orthant (element-wise clipping).
 - **Primal variable ($\dot{\Theta}^*$)**: Minimization of an augmented objective, which involves solving a linear system with a KKT matrix.
 - **Bregman multiplier ($\lambda$)**: Update based on the constraint residual.
3. **Computational Properties**: The solver is designed for efficiency:
 - **Pre-computable factorization**: The KKT matrix is constant and can be factorized once.
 - **Joint-wise separability**: The QP naturally separates across individual joints, allowing parallel solutions for $N_{dof}$ independent horizon-wise QPs.
 - **Batch parallelism**: All $n$ samples in a batch are processed in parallel on the GPU using batched operations.
 - **Boundary value update**: Only the right-hand side vectors of the QP need updating at each MPC step, not the KKT matrix.
 - **Convergence**: As a convex QP, the ADMM iterations converge to the global optimum.

#### 3.6. Bayesian Optimization for Cost Tuning (Algorithm 2)

Bayesian Optimization (BO) is used to automatically tune the task-cost weights offline.
1. **GP Surrogate Model**: A Gaussian Process (GP) surrogate model approximates the mapping from cost weights to task performance.
2. **Acquisition Function**: At each BO iteration, a candidate weight vector is sampled using the Expected Improvement (EI) acquisition function.
3. **Episode Evaluation**: The candidate weights are evaluated by executing a full task episode (up to $T$ MPC steps) in a randomized simulation environment (robot configuration, object pose, target pose). A dense cost (collision, coordination) is accumulated at each step, and a sparse terminal cost (task success/failure, final pose error) is computed upon episode completion.
4. **GP Update**: The observed (weights, total episode cost) pair is used to update the GP model, which refines the acquisition function.
5. **Optimal Weights**: After $B$ iterations, the weight vector with the lowest observed cost is returned. This offline tuning process aims to improve robustness and generalization without adding online computational overhead.

#### 3.7. Cost Function Design

The cost functions are crucial for guiding the optimizer. They include shared components and task-specific elements.
- **Shared Costs**:
 - **Collision Cost ($c_c$)**: Penalizes penetrations and uses a barrier function to prevent approaching infeasible regions.
 - **Joint Deviation Cost ($c_{\theta}$)**: Penalizes deviation from a home joint configuration.
 - **Relative Velocity Cost ($c_v$)**: Encourages coordinated motion by penalizing relative end-effector velocity components not perpendicular to their relative position, preventing stretching/compression.
- **Task-Specific Costs**: Detailed cost structures are designed for four challenging bimanual tasks:
 - **Moving Tray**: Includes end-effector vertical alignment, pick position/orientation, end-effector distance, tray position/orientation, and move position/orientation costs, structured into pick and move phases.
 - **Lifting and Transporting Ball**: Features phase-split collision cost (excluding ball-manipulator collisions during move), end-effector planar alignment, orientation, distance, end-effector-to-object alignment (pick), and object-to-target (move) costs.
 - **Hand-Over Task**: Consists of four phases (pick, pass, place, home) with costs for end-effector planar alignment (pass), phase-specific position and orientation (pick, pass, place), and home return costs.
 - **Lifting and Transporting Box**: A geometric variant of ball lifting, with box-specific end-effector-to-object alignment and an additional box orientation cost due to its asymmetry.

Phase transitions are determined dynamically at runtime based on the satisfaction of state-dependent cost thresholds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation and benchmarking of PRISM involved qualitative demonstrations, statistical evaluations, and comparisons against both sampling-based baselines and, qualitatively, learning-based approaches.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Results

- **Moving Tray (Figure 1)**: Demonstrated successful sim-to-real transfer on dual UR5e arms, with the system effectively transitioning from pick to move phases, maintaining a stable grasp, and navigating through cluttered obstacles to the target pose.
- **Lifting and Transporting Ball (Figure 6)**: Showed continuous, controlled contact maintenance by manipulators during lifting and transport in both simulation and real-world, clearing a central obstacle.
- **Hand-Over Task (Figure 7)**: Illustrated independent but synchronized manipulator roles, with the first arm retrieving a cube through obstacles, performing a precise mid-air exchange in the pass phase, and then proceeding to place and home phases.
- **Lifting and Transporting Box (Figure 8)**: Confirmed successful simulated execution of box lifting, with manipulators targeting opposing flat faces for a secure frictional grasp. The orientation costs effectively prevented unwanted rotations of the asymmetric payload during the move phase.

#### 4.2. Statistical Validation (Figure 9)

Experiments were conducted by randomizing goal poses, with statistics aggregated over 20 runs per setting and varying batch sizes (number of samples in Alg. 1). Metrics included success rate, mean/standard deviation of task time, and mean/standard deviation of computation time per MPC step.

- **Success Rate**:
 - Ball lifting: Strong dependence on batch size, improving from 65% at 500 samples to nearly 100% at $\ge 1250$ samples.
 - Tray moving: Consistently high performance (85-95%) across batch sizes.
 - Hand-over: Similar robustness, reaching up to 95% success at larger batch sizes.
 - Box lifting: More challenging, saturating around 55-60%, highlighting difficulty with asymmetric objects.
- **Computation Time**: Increased with batch size for all tasks. Ball lifting, tray moving, and hand-over tasks remained below $\approx 80$ ms (real-time). Box lifting incurred higher computational cost (up to 130 ms) due to increased task complexity requiring more optimization iterations.
- **Task Completion Time**: Generally decreased with increasing batch size for ball lifting and tray moving, indicating improved solution quality. Hand-over showed stable execution times ($\approx 85-95$ s). Box lifting had the lowest execution times ($\approx 8-15$ s) for successful episodes, but with a lower success rate.

**Summary**: Larger batch sizes generally improved success rates and task completion efficiency while maintaining real-time computation for most tasks. Asymmetric object manipulation (box lifting) remained challenging, with lower success rates and higher computational costs.

#### 4.3. Effect of Bayesian Optimization (Figure 10)

Bayesian Optimization (BO) was performed over 1,000 randomized episodes per task to tune cost weights offline.

- **Success Rate**: BO-optimized weights improved success rates across all four tasks:
 - Ball lifting: 75% to 100%
 - Handover: 60% to 95%
 - Tray moving: 45% to 90%
 - Box lifting: 45% to 80%
 These results indicate the planner's sensitivity to cost weight tuning and BO's effectiveness in finding more reliable configurations.
- **Computation Time**: Remained comparable between manually tuned and BO-optimized weights, confirming that offline tuning does not add online computational overhead.
- **Task Completion Time**: For successful episodes, completion times were either reduced or similar with BO-optimized weights. Notably, handover task completion time decreased significantly, indicating more efficient execution. This was a byproduct of improved task feasibility rather than explicit optimization for speed.

**Summary**: Bayesian Optimization systematically improved success rates without compromising online computational efficiency, enhancing the robustness and generalization of the sampling-based planner.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Existing Approaches (Figure 11)

PRISM with QP-based projection was compared against three baselines: Baseline-CEM (standard, unfiltered), Low-Pass Filter (LPF), and Savitzky–Golay Filter (SGF).

- **Practical Considerations for Baselines**: Baseline-CEM, LPF, and SGF required additional modifications (collision-free inverse kinematics, explicit smoothness cost) to achieve reliable task completion, which PRISM did not. LPF and SGF also introduced additional hyperparameters (cutoff frequency, polynomial order) that needed task-dependent tuning.
- **Success Rate**:
 - QP achieved the highest or joint-highest success rates in three of four tasks.
 - Ball lifting: QP (95%), LPF (95%), SGF (90%), Baseline-CEM (70%).
 - Handover: QP (90%), LPF (80%), SGF (75%), Baseline-CEM (15%).
 - Tray moving: QP (95%), SGF (70%), LPF (50%), Baseline-CEM (30%).
 - Box lifting: LPF (90%) slightly outperformed QP (80%), both superior to SGF (70%) and Baseline-CEM (40%).
 The poor performance of Baseline-CEM in coordination-heavy tasks was attributed to its inability to explore broadly without generating jerky motions, impacting MPC warm-start.
- **Computation Time**: QP's computation times were comparable to alternatives, confirming the specialized QP solver's efficiency. MuJoCo rollouts remained the dominant computational cost.
- **Task Completion Time**: QP achieved faster or comparable execution across all tasks. This was attributed to the QP allowing trajectories to exploit maximum permissible velocities and accelerations, unlike filtering methods that attenuate signals. LPF, while sometimes achieving high success (box lifting), often resulted in substantially longer task completion times due to velocity attenuation.

**Summary**: The QP-based projection consistently outperformed vanilla CEM, LPF, and SGF in success rate, particularly in tasks requiring sustained bimanual coordination in cluttered environments. This was linked to the QP's ability to decouple exploration from smoothness, allowing broader sampling while guaranteeing kinematically feasible and boundary-continuous trajectories.

#### 4.5. Comparison with Learning-Based Approaches (Figure 12)

A preliminary qualitative comparison was performed with a learning-based approach on a ball-lifting task with an added obstacle.

- **Scenario**: The task involved lifting a ball, with an introduced static obstacle outside the training distribution of the learned policy.
- **Results**:
 - The learned policy partially adapted to the obstacle during approach but often failed during the lift, struggling with a maneuver differing from its training distribution.
 - PRISM, by contrast, adapted to the novel obstacle configuration through online physics rollouts, discovering a feasible lifting strategy without retraining.
 - Over 20 trials, PRISM succeeded in 60% of cases, compared to 20% for the learned policy.
- **Conclusion**: This comparison highlights PRISM's complementary strength: its ability to adapt motion generation to previously unseen geometric constraints by directly reasoning over the current scene state, without the need for retraining a policy. The same optimization pipeline was also shown to be adaptable to different robot platforms (Franka-Panda) without changes to its core formulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PRISM framework represents a significant contribution to the field of bimanual manipulation, particularly in scenarios characterized by clutter and contact-rich interactions. Its key significance and potential impacts are:

1. **Advancement in Model-Based Planning for Bimanual Manipulation**: PRISM demonstrates a robust and effective model-based approach for complex bimanual tasks. By leveraging a GPU-accelerated physics simulator as an online world model, it allows for real-time interaction-aware planning, which is a crucial capability for robot deployment in dynamic and unstructured environments.

2. **Decoupling Exploration and Smoothness in Sampling-Based MPC**: The core algorithmic innovation of QP-integrated sampling is impactful. It addresses a fundamental limitation of traditional sampling-based optimizers, where the need for broad exploration often conflicts with the requirement for smooth, kinematically feasible trajectories. By projecting sampled trajectories onto the feasible set, PRISM enables aggressive exploration while guaranteeing executable motions, which is beneficial for robust control and real-world robot deployment. This decoupling principle has broader applicability to other sampling-based MPC pipelines operating under kinematic or dynamic constraints.

3. **Efficient and Reliable Constraint Enforcement**: The custom ADMM-based QP solver, designed to exploit joint-wise separability and pre-computable matrix factorizations, makes the projection step computationally efficient. This ensures that the benefits of strict constraint enforcement do not come at the cost of real-time performance, enabling PRISM to operate at 10-16 Hz on commodity hardware.

4. **Automated Cost Function Tuning for Enhanced Robustness**: The integration of Bayesian optimization for offline tuning of task-cost weights significantly enhances the framework's practical utility. This reduces the labor-intensive and non-systematic process of manual tuning, leading to substantial improvements in task success rates and generalization across randomized task instances without adding online computational overhead.

5. **Demonstrated Sim-to-Real Transfer and Adaptability**: Successful sim-to-real transfer on dual UR5e manipulators, including cluttered scenarios, underscores the practical potential of physics-based online planning. The ability to qualitatively adapt to geometric distribution shifts, even compared to some learning-based methods, highlights its flexibility in handling unforeseen environmental changes.

6. **Potential for Hybrid Approaches**: PRISM offers a complementary strength to end-to-end learning methods. Its generated trajectories can serve as expert demonstrations for training reactive policies, or it could be integrated into model-based reinforcement learning frameworks (e.g., TD-MPC) to combine online physical reasoning with learned long-horizon value information. This suggests a path towards hybrid systems that leverage the best of both model-based and data-driven paradigms.

While the framework currently relies on manually designed cost functions and an accurate physics model, future work could explore learning cost structures, extending to dynamic or deformable objects, and integrating neural warm-starting for further computational gains. Overall, PRISM provides a robust, adaptable, and computationally efficient framework that pushes the capabilities of model-based control for complex bimanual manipulation, with clear pathways for future integration with learning-based techniques.
