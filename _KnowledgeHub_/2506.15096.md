# DyNaVLM: Zero-Shot Vision-Language Navigation System with Dynamic Viewpoints and Self-Refining Graph Memory

> [!summary] Summary
> DyNaVLM, developed by researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, proposes a zero-shot vision-language navigation system for robots that utilizes dynamic viewpoints and a self-refining graph memory. The system demonstrates enhanced navigation flexibility and efficiency in both extensive simulations and real-world deployments on a quadrupedal robot.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) methods often restrict robotic agents to fixed, discrete action spaces, limiting navigation flexibility and efficiency in continuous real-world settings.
- Applying pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to embodied navigation typically requires extensive and costly task-specific fine-tuning, which can lead to catastrophic forgetting of broader semantic understanding.
- Current spatial memory systems for embodied agents frequently lack semantic richness or rely on prohibitive prior knowledge like pre-built 3D scene graphs, hindering adaptability in unexplored or dynamic environments.

## Method

- **DyNaVLM** introduces a dynamic action space formulation that allows the VLM to freely select continuous navigation targets by spatially sampling and safety-aware filtering potential points using real-time RGB-D data.
- It develops a self-refining graph memory that dynamically constructs and updates a knowledge graph of object instances and their topological relations during exploration, providing persistent semantic and spatial context.
- The system operates in a zero-shot, end-to-end manner by employing a multimodal prompt engineering strategy, feeding annotated images, task templates, and retrieved memory entries to a VLM for confidence-based action selection.

## Results

- DyNaVLM achieved a **45.0%** Success Rate (SR) and **0.232** Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) on the ObjectNav benchmark, outperforming prior VLM frameworks like VLMnav (**42.8%** SR, **0.218** SPL) and PIVOT.
- The system demonstrated **25.5%** SR and **0.102** SPL on the challenging GOAT-Bench, establishing the best performance among all VLM-based approaches, and competitive results against specialized non-zero-shot methods.
- In real-world deployment on a Unitree Go2 quadrupedal robot, DyNaVLM notably outperformed a VLMnav baseline in both success rate and travel distance across various indoor tasks, particularly in multi-target navigation scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can function as a zero-shot, end-to-end navigation policy for robots when integrated with dynamic action spaces and a self-refining memory system.
> - A dynamically constructed, self-refining graph memory significantly improves an agent's ability to retain and leverage spatial reasoning and contextual information over long navigation horizons.
> - Allowing VLMs to directly reason over dynamically sampled continuous target points, rather than predefined discrete actions, enhances navigation flexibility and efficiency in complex environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied AI, particularly Vision-Language Navigation (VLN), aims to enable robotic agents to understand and execute natural language instructions to navigate real-world environments. This paper, DyNaVLM, positions itself at the forefront of addressing several critical limitations within this landscape.

Historically, autonomous navigation systems have often relied on modular pipelines, separating perception, planning, and control. While effective for specific tasks, these traditional approaches struggle with generalization, scalability, and seamless real-world deployment due to their reliance on brittle, task-specific engineering.

The advent of large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has offered a promising paradigm shift, unifying perception and reasoning. However, their application in embodied navigation has been constrained by issues of spatial granularity and the difficulty of translating high-level linguistic understanding into precise, actionable robotic movements. Prior VLM-based VLN methods typically impose restrictive action spaces, such as fixed angular increments or cardinal directions, which oversimplify real-world complexities and lead to inefficient, unrealistic navigation (e.g., small, repeated steps). Furthermore, fine-tuning pre-trained VLMs for navigation tasks often risks "catastrophic forgetting" of their broader semantic understanding and necessitates prohibitively costly, large-scale dataset collection. While zero-shot VLM prompting has emerged to mitigate fine-tuning costs, these methods still largely adhere to rigid action spaces and often lack mechanisms to incorporate real-world constraints like obstacle avoidance or warning signs.

Another crucial aspect of embodied navigation is robust environmental memory. Traditional Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) provides geometric maps but lacks semantic context, limiting its utility in human-centric environments. Efforts to infuse semantic understanding into maps, often via human interaction or automated annotation, face challenges such as dependence on specialized sensors, computational intensity, or the requirement for extensive prior knowledge (e.g., pre-built 3D scene graphs or human-provided instructions).

DyNaVLM directly addresses these limitations. It distinguishes itself by demonstrating that VLMs can function as a zero-shot, end-to-end navigation policy without task-specific training or fine-tuning, thereby reducing deployment costs and enhancing generalization. Crucially, it liberates agents from fixed action spaces by allowing dynamic target selection through VLM-powered visual-language reasoning. Moreover, it introduces a novel, self-refining graph memory that is *dynamically built during exploration* (not requiring prior maps), enabling persistent spatial reasoning and even facilitating cross-robot memory sharing. This approach directly tackles the "semantic gap" in traditional mapping and the "fixed action space" inflexibility of existing VLM-based solutions, bridging the gap between idealized discrete VLN tasks and the continuous, dynamic realities of robot navigation. By integrating safety-aware filtering and leveraging VLM's inherent world knowledge, DyNaVLM marks a significant step towards more adaptable, practical, and truly autonomous embodied agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of DyNaVLM is to advance the state of embodied AI by enabling autonomous agents to navigate complex, unstructured environments with human-like flexibility and intelligence. The authors are motivated by several key challenges in current Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) systems:

1. **Overcoming Fixed Action Space Limitations:** Existing VLN methods often constrain robotic agents to a predefined, discrete set of actions (e.g., fixed angular turns or linear steps). This oversimplification severely limits navigation flexibility and efficiency in continuous real-world settings. A core motivation is to empower agents with a "dynamic action space formulation" where they can freely select navigation targets based on visual-language reasoning, mimicking human adaptability.

2. **Achieving Zero-Shot, End-to-End Navigation with VLMs:** While Vision-Language Models (VLMs) show immense promise for unifying perception and reasoning, their application in embodied navigation typically requires extensive, costly task-specific fine-tuning. The authors aim to demonstrate that VLMs can serve as a robust, training-free, end-to-end navigation policy, thereby significantly reducing the barriers to real-world deployment and enhancing generalization to unseen environments and tasks.

3. **Developing a Persistent and Adaptive Spatial Memory:** Effective long-horizon navigation necessitates the ability to build, retain, and leverage spatial knowledge over time. Current memory systems either lack semantic richness or require prohibitive prior knowledge. A key objective is to design a "self-refining graph memory" that dynamically constructs and updates a knowledge graph of object locations and topological relations, enabling robust contextual reasoning and memory sharing across multiple robots. This memory is crucial for overcoming the limitations of short-term VLM processing.

4. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap with Enhanced Robustness:** Real-world navigation demands adaptability to dynamic constraints (e.g., obstacles, warning signs) and the ability to maintain human-interpretable spatial representations. The motivation is to integrate safety-aware reasoning directly into the VLM's decision-making process, ensuring that dynamically selected navigation targets adhere to environmental constraints and commonsense understanding. This is vital for practical viability beyond simulation.

In essence, DyNaVLM seeks to establish a new paradigm for scalable embodied robots by creating a system that is flexible in its actions, independent of extensive task-specific training, capable of long-term spatial memory, and robust in real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DyNaVLM is conceptualized as an end-to-end vision-language navigation framework, fundamentally driven by a Vision-Language Model (VLM) augmented with a self-refining graph memory. The system processes a goal (natural language or image), real-time RGB-D observations, and the robot's pose to determine an appropriate action. Its core architecture comprises three interconnected components: Memory Management, Action Proposer, and Action Selector.

1. **Memory Management:**
 - This component serves as the central middleware, maintaining a **dynamic knowledge graph (Memory Bank)**, denoted as `G = (V, E)`.
 - **Vertices (V)** in the graph encode individual object instances, storing attributes such as `name`, `location`, and `visual features`.
 - **Edges (E)** represent spatial relationships and navigation accessibility between objects (e.g., "connected to," "above," "in front of").
 - Upon receiving real-time perceptual input from RGB-D sensors, the VLM actively performs two critical functions:
 - **Node embedding updates:** It generates and updates object descriptors for existing or newly identified objects.
 - **Edge addition:** It determines and encodes spatial relationships between objects, building the topological map dynamically during exploration. The memory updates through functions like `add_node(name, attributes)` and `add_edge(start, target, relation)`.
 - The Memory Manager provides a structured query interface (e.g., `spatial_query(semantic_filter)`, `path_inference(start, target)`) to the LLM (Large Language Model) controller, enabling efficient context propagation and handling ambiguous object references (e.g., "charging station near seating areas"). This graph can also be converted back to natural language to enrich VLM input. Crucially, this memory is self-constructed and supports distributed updates, allowing for potential cross-robot memory sharing.

2. **Action Proposer:**
 - This module addresses the challenge of enabling a VLM to directly output continuous navigation coordinates. It employs a candidate-based discretization strategy, simplifying the continuous action space into a finite set of potential navigation points.
 - **Spatial Sampling:** Given RGB-D data, it identifies the navigable ground boundary and uniformly samples a set of initial candidate points in polar coordinates `A_initial = {(αr, θ)}`. These points are filtered to ensure sufficient visual spacing (minimum angular distance `θ_δ`) and a safe margin (coefficient `α`). This process avoids prior environmental assumptions.
 - **Safety-Aware Filtering:** The VLM refines these initial candidate points. The coordinates of `A_initial`, along with natural language constraints `C` (e.g., "stay away from the oven"), are provided to the VLM via an annotated image (`I_hat`). The VLM then eliminates infeasible waypoints (e.g., near obstacles or non-traversable surfaces, potentially identified by ground recognition errors or warning signs). It can also geometrically adjust surviving candidates (fine-tuning positions or headings) based on scene semantics and commonsense understanding, without relying on specialized object detectors.

3. **Action Selector:**
 - This component synthesizes information from the proposed actions, current perception, and retrieved memory to make the final navigation decision.
 - **Multimodal Prompt Engineering:** A comprehensive multimodal prompt `P = [I_hat ⊕ T ⊕ M]` is constructed. This includes the annotated RGB image (`I_hat`), a task-specific template (`T`), and relevant structured memory entries (`M`) retrieved from the Memory Manager.
 - **Confidence-Based Execution:** The VLM evaluates each candidate action `a_i` from `A_final` based on the prompt `P`, generating a confidence score `s_i`. It also checks for a "stop" action (`s_stop`) to determine if the navigation goal has been reached. The final action `a*` is chosen as the one with the highest confidence, or "stop" if `s_stop` exceeds a threshold `τ_stop` for two consecutive steps.

The system utilizes Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite as its VLM, chosen for its multimodal capabilities. For real-world deployment, DyNaVLM was tested on a Unitree Go2 quadrupedal robot equipped with a RealSense D435 camera (RGB-D) and a Unitree L1 LiDAR for local odometry and obstacle detection. Auxiliary tools like FastSAM were used for ground segmentation in real-world scenarios. The entire framework operates in a zero-shot manner, meaning no task-specific training or fine-tuning is required, leveraging the VLM's inherent world knowledge and the dynamic memory system to adapt to novel situations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of DyNaVLM was rigorously evaluated through both extensive simulations and real-world robot deployment, demonstrating its superior performance compared to existing VLM-based navigation methods and competitive results against specialized approaches.

**A. Simulation Evaluation:**
The system was tested on two prominent embodied navigation benchmarks: ObjectNav and GOAT-Bench, utilizing the HM3D dataset. Performance was assessed using Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL).

1. **ObjectNav:** This benchmark evaluates an agent's ability to navigate to a specified object category in an unexplored environment.
 - DyNaVLM achieved the best performance with a **45.0% SR and 0.232 SPL**.
 - An ablation study, "Ours w/o Memory," showed a performance drop (43.1% SR, 0.187 SPL), confirming the significant contribution of the self-refining graph memory to both success and path efficiency.
 - DyNaVLM significantly outperformed prior VLM frameworks like VLMnav (42.8% SR, 0.218 SPL) and PIVOT (22.7% SR, 0.108 SPL).
 - The results highlight that restricting navigation point sampling to the ground plane, a feature of DyNaVLM's Action Proposer, is a crucial strategy for efficient exploration and goal localization.

2. **GOAT-Bench:** This is a more challenging benchmark involving multi-modal goal specifications (object names, images, detailed text descriptions) and multi-sub-task episodes, testing adaptability and memory usage in lifelong scenarios.
 - DyNaVLM achieved strong performance with **25.5% SR and 0.102 SPL**, the best among all VLM-based approaches.
 - It outperformed VLMnav (20.1% SR, 0.096 SPL) and PIVOT (10.2% SR, 0.055 SPL) by substantial margins, particularly in SR, underscoring the critical role of the memory module in longer and more complex tasks with potentially noisy goal specifications.
 - While specialized approaches like SenseAct-NN Skill Chain (29.5% SR, 0.113 SPL) and Modular GOAT (24.9% SR, 0.172 SPL) achieved slightly higher or comparable SPL (due to precise object localization models), DyNaVLM demonstrated competitive results despite being a zero-shot, training-free method, without relying on low-level policies or external object detection modules. This emphasizes its superior generalization capabilities.

**B. Real-World Evaluation:**
DyNaVLM was deployed on a Unitree Go2 quadrupedal robot in a large, diverse indoor environment (offices, meeting rooms, labs). Tasks included locating single objects or sequential dual targets. Metrics were SR and Average Cumulative Distance (ACD).

- DyNaVLM notably **outperformed the VLMnav baseline** in both success rate and travel distance across all real-world tasks.
- The improvement was particularly pronounced in **multi-target tasks** (e.g., sequentially finding a printer then a trash can), where memory is critical for retaining context across sub-goals. For instance, in a task to find a "Printer (2nd target)," DyNaVLM had a 1/3 SR with 76m ACD, while VLMnav achieved 0/3 SR.
- Qualitative analysis (e.g., Fig. 6) demonstrated that DyNaVLM's memory manager allowed the agent to reason about its current location (e.g., "laboratory space") and make intelligent decisions (e.g., "leave this site to find the water dispenser"), whereas the baseline VLMnav could get trapped in local loops.
- The real-world tests confirmed the framework's **robustness and generalization** across different starting locations and under practical constraints like dynamic obstacles, validating its practical viability beyond simulation.

Overall, the findings consistently support the effectiveness of DyNaVLM's innovative design, particularly the dynamic action space and the self-refining graph memory, in enhancing VLM-based embodied navigation in both simulated and real-world complex scenarios, without the need for task-specific training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DyNaVLM represents a significant leap forward in the field of embodied AI and robotics, establishing a new paradigm for scalable autonomous navigation. Its innovations address long-standing limitations, paving the way for more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems with substantial potential impact.

**1. Enabling Flexible and Human-Like Navigation:** The introduction of a dynamic action space formulation, where agents can freely select navigation targets through visual-language reasoning, is highly significant. This moves beyond the rigid, discrete action sets of prior methods, allowing for more natural, efficient, and human-like navigation in continuous environments. This flexibility is critical for robots operating in diverse, unstructured real-world settings where predefined movements are impractical.

**2. Reducing Development Costs and Enhancing Generalization through Zero-Shot Deployment:** By demonstrating high performance without task-specific training or fine-tuning, DyNaVLM dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for VLM-based navigation. The prohibitive costs and time associated with collecting vast amounts of navigation-specific data and retraining large models are eliminated. This "training-free" deployment enhances the system's generalization capabilities, allowing robots to adapt quickly to novel environments and tasks without prior exposure, which is a key requirement for truly autonomous and versatile robotic assistants.

**3. Advancing Spatial Reasoning with Self-Refining Graph Memory:** The self-refining graph memory architecture is a cornerstone innovation.
 - It allows for **persistent spatial reasoning**, enabling robots to build and retain a comprehensive understanding of their environment over long horizons.
 - Its ability to encode object locations as executable topological relations provides a rich, semantic understanding that goes beyond purely geometric maps, enhancing the VLM's decision-making through context-aware retrieval.
 - Crucially, this memory is **dynamically built during exploration**, eliminating the reliance on costly, pre-constructed 3D scene graphs or human annotations, making it highly scalable and practical for unexplored or changing environments.
 - The design also explicitly supports **cross-robot memory sharing**, opening avenues for collaborative multi-agent navigation systems where robots can collectively build and benefit from shared environmental knowledge, accelerating exploration and improving efficiency.

**4. Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Practical Applications:** The comprehensive validation, including successful real-world deployment on a quadrupedal robot, underscores the framework's robustness and practical viability. DyNaVLM's ability to operate under real-world constraints, such as dynamic obstacles and environmental variations, without task-specific training, demonstrates its potential for immediate application in logistics, service robotics, exploration, and other domains where autonomous navigation is essential. The qualitative example of the robot intelligently leaving a "laboratory space" to find a "water dispenser" showcases a higher level of commonsense reasoning embedded in the navigation policy, crucial for complex real-world scenarios.

**Potential Future Impact:**
DyNaVLM's contribution lays a strong foundation for the next generation of embodied AI. It pushes towards a future where robots are not just automated tools but intelligent, interactive, and autonomous agents capable of truly understanding and navigating their surroundings. Future research building upon this work could explore:
- Integrating even more sophisticated spatial priors and structured representations to further narrow the performance gap with highly specialized, non-zero-shot methods.
- Incorporating few-shot adaptation techniques to allow for incremental refinement of decision-making over extended operational periods without full retraining.
- Developing real-time learning mechanisms within the memory system to enable continuous improvement based on new experiences.
- Further exploring multi-agent collaboration and knowledge transfer to create more robust and efficient robotic teams.

In conclusion, DyNaVLM represents a significant step towards general-purpose, autonomous navigation agents. By empowering VLMs with dynamic action spaces and a self-refining, collaborative memory, it addresses critical challenges in robotic navigation, promising a future of more capable, flexible, and scalable embodied AI systems.
