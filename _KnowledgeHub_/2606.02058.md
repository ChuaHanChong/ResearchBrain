# TIDES: Time-Derivative Event Simulation via Deformable Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> A continuous-time event camera simulator, TIDES, uses dynamic Gaussian splatting and forward-mode automatic differentiation to precisely model per-pixel intensity changes, effectively eliminating "timestamp batching" issues in synthetic event streams. This approach yields event data with superior fidelity, enhancing the performance of trained event-based models in various downstream tasks, including achieving the highest PSNR in video reconstruction and lowest RMSE in depth estimation.

## Problem

- Existing event simulators relying on discrete frames suffer from "timestamp batching," causing inaccurate event timing under fast motion and occlusions.
- Finite-difference approximations of intensity change are unstable and inaccurate in dynamic scenes, leading to aliasing and mixing distinct visibility regimes.
- Current simulators lack explicit 3D scene understanding, preventing accurate modeling of continuous visibility changes and partial occlusions over time.

## Method

- Utilizes a dynamic 4D ==Gaussian splatting== scene representation for explicit 3D geometry and motion, providing continuous-time scene dynamics.
- Computes instantaneous, visibility-consistent time derivatives of log-luminance (L˙) via forward-mode automatic differentiation, avoiding finite-difference approximations.
- Employs risk-guided adaptive time-stepping to concentrate computation in complex regions and models finite sensor bandwidth with a derivative-conditioned readout arbiter.

## Results

- TIDES achieved the best fidelity (lower IG-NLL and Chamfer distance) across all evaluated datasets, substantially reducing timestamp batching artifacts (lower Same-ts, ISI spike ratio, Fano-factor error) compared to existing simulators.
- Models trained on TIDES-simulated events demonstrated superior transferability to real datasets, achieving the highest PSNR for video reconstruction and interpolation, lowest RMSE for depth estimation, and highest mIoU for semantic segmentation.
- Ablation studies confirmed that analytical visibility-consistent time-tangent derivatives, adaptive control, and multi-crossing event generation are critical components for maintaining high simulation fidelity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly deriving time derivatives of luminance from a dynamic 3D scene representation using forward-mode AD is crucial for accurate, continuous-time event simulation, surpassing frame-based limitations.
> - Visibility-consistent time-derivative computation is essential for correct event timing, especially near occlusions and disocclusions, preventing temporal aliasing.
> - Adaptive computational allocation based on scene complexity and activity, combined with realistic sensor readout modeling, significantly enhances simulation fidelity and the transferability of synthetic data to real-world tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Event cameras represent a distinct paradigm in computer vision, capturing asynchronous "events" in response to per-pixel log-intensity changes rather than traditional frame-based images. This sensing mechanism provides high temporal resolution and dynamic range, which are beneficial for applications involving fast motion or challenging illumination conditions. However, the specialized nature of event data makes large-scale real-world dataset collection and annotation costly and labor-intensive. Consequently, event camera simulation has become a critical tool for training and evaluating event-based algorithms.

Existing event simulators commonly operate by inferring event timestamps from sequences of discretely rendered frames. This approach inherently suffers from a limitation termed "timestamp batching," where numerous threshold crossings are forced to share a limited set of discrete timestamps determined by the rendering schedule. This issue becomes more pronounced under conditions of rapid motion or complex occlusions, as finite-difference approximations of intensity change can mix distinct visibility regimes, leading to inaccurate timing. While methods like ESIM have popularized this renderer-coupled design due to its simplicity, and others such as v2e and DVS-Voltmeter have introduced more realistic sensor models (e.g., photoreceptor dynamics, leak, threshold mismatch, stochastic processes, and readout effects), the fundamental reliance on discretely sampled video or renders means that timestamp batching persists. Even physically grounded analytical pipelines, while striving for realism, often face challenges in scalability and computational complexity for generating dense temporal queries.

Recent advancements in neural rendering, particularly Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) and its dynamic extension (4DGS), have provided mechanisms for real-time differentiable rendering with explicit visibility compositing using a scene representation composed of 3D primitives. Prior work in event-based neural rendering has predominantly focused on the inverse problem: reconstructing scene representations from event data. This paper, TIDES, represents a shift towards the forward problem, leveraging the continuous-time, explicit 3D scene and motion representation offered by dynamic Gaussian splatting to address the core problem of accurate, continuous-time event timestamp prediction, especially in scenarios involving occlusions and fast dynamics. By directly differentiating the rendering process, TIDES aims to overcome the timestamp batching limitations inherent in frame-based simulation and improve the fidelity of simulated event streams.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a continuous-time event camera simulator that generates high-fidelity event streams by directly modeling per-pixel intensity dynamics from an explicit 3D scene representation, thereby overcoming the limitations of existing frame-based simulation methods.

The motivation for TIDES stems from several key challenges identified in the current event simulation landscape:
- **Timestamp Batching:** Current simulators typically infer event timestamps by interpolating intensity changes between discretely rendered frames. This forces multiple threshold crossings to occur at the same or very close discrete times, particularly under fast motion and occlusions. This "timestamp batching" fundamentally misrepresents the asynchronous nature of real event cameras, which can detect changes at microsecond resolution.
- **Inaccuracy under Dynamic Conditions:** In highly dynamic scenes, especially with occlusions and disocclusions, the finite-difference estimates of intensity slope become unstable and inaccurate. This is because discrete samples can inadvertently mix different visibility regimes, making local brightness models unreliable precisely where accurate event timing is most critical. Existing methods, even those with advanced sensor models, still struggle when driven by undersampled intensity signals.
- **Lack of 3D Scene Understanding:** Most simulators lack an explicit understanding of the 3D scene geometry and object motion, resorting to 2D frame differences. This prevents them from accurately accounting for how objects partially occlude each other and how visibility changes continuously in time.
- **Computational Inefficiency for High Fidelity:** Mitigating timestamp batching in frame-based simulators would require rendering at extremely high frame rates, which is computationally prohibitive and can still introduce artifacts due to reliance on temporal video interpolation models.

To address these motivations, TIDES sets out to achieve the following specific objectives:
- **Continuous-Time Threshold-Crossing Prediction:** Develop a method to derive per-pixel intensity dynamics directly from the 3D scene, allowing for accurate prediction of threshold crossings in continuous time, including multiple crossings per rendering step, without relying on temporal upsampling or frame interpolation.
- **Visibility-Consistent Dynamics:** Ensure that the derived intensity dynamics and their derivatives correctly account for the visibility ordering of objects, especially near occlusions and disocclusions.
- **Adaptive Computational Allocation:** Guide adaptive time stepping to concentrate computational resources in regions where occlusion dynamics make simple brightness models unreliable, thereby optimizing efficiency without sacrificing accuracy in complex areas.
- **Realistic Sensor Artifacts:** Model finite sensor bandwidth using a tile-level arbiter that reproduces realistic sensor artifacts such as burst-induced timestamp spreading and event drops, conditioned on scene-derived burst and visibility cues.
- **Improved Transferability:** Generate simulated event streams that exhibit higher fidelity to real events and transfer more effectively to real-world downstream tasks (e.g., reconstruction, frame interpolation, depth estimation, segmentation) compared to existing simulators.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TIDES is designed as a continuous-time event simulator built upon a dynamic 4D Gaussian splatting scene representation. Its core principle is to derive event timestamps from an explicit understanding of the underlying 3D structure and motion rather than from finite differences between discretely rendered frames.

The methodology comprises several key components:

#### 3.1. Dynamic Gaussian Splatting Image Formation

TIDES utilizes a dynamic Gaussian splatting scene (e.g., 4DGS), which is typically fit from RGB videos. This scene representation consists of a collection of 3D Gaussians whose parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) are time-dependent, usually modeled by a time-conditioned MLP. The rendering process involves projecting these 3D Gaussians onto the 2D image plane, forming screen-space elliptical kernels. The final pixel intensity and opacity are then composited in a front-to-back order, where each Gaussian's contribution is weighted by its opacity and the transmittance of preceding Gaussians. The log-luminance, $L(u, t)$, is derived from the rendered luminance $Y(u,t)$. This explicit 3D model provides access to object geometry, motion, and visibility relationships.

#### 3.2. Forward-Mode Time-Derivative Gaussian Splatting

To achieve continuous-time event prediction, TIDES requires not only the log-luminance $L(u, t)$ but also its instantaneous time derivative, $\dot{L}(u, t)$. Instead of approximating $\dot{L}$ via finite differences of rendered frames, TIDES computes it analytically using forward-mode automatic differentiation (AD).
1. **Scene Parameter Derivatives:** The time-dependent Gaussian parameters $\Theta(t)$ are differentiated with respect to time, $\dot{\Theta}(t)$, via a Jacobian-vector product (JVP) along the time axis through the deformation network. This provides per-Gaussian time derivatives (e.g., $\dot{\mu}_k$, $\dot{\sigma}_k$, $\dot{q}_k$, $\dot{a}_k$).
2. **Propagation through Activations:** These raw derivatives are then analytically propagated through non-linear activations (e.g., `exp` for scale, `sigmoid` for opacity, quaternion normalization) to obtain consistent derivatives of the actual Gaussian parameters.
3. **Time-Tangent Renderer:** A custom time-tangent rasterizer computes screen-space primitives and their derivatives (e.g., $\dot{u}_k$, $\dot{Q}_k$, $\dot{c}_k$). Crucially, these derivatives are composited in the *same front-to-back order* as the primal image rendering. This ensures that the temporal derivatives are consistent with visibility decisions, such as early termination and occlusion ordering. This step provides visibility-consistent instantaneous derivatives of opacity ($\dot{\alpha}$) and luminance ($\dot{Y}$), which are then used to compute $\dot{L}(u, t)$. This approach provides stable per-pixel threshold-crossing prediction without relying on a discretized rendering schedule.

#### 3.3. Comparator and Sensor Model

TIDES employs a residual-state comparator model. Each pixel maintains a reference log-luminance $S_{\text{ref}}(u)$, which is updated upon event firing. An event is triggered when the current signal $S(u, t)$ (derived from $L(u,t)$) deviates from $S_{\text{ref}}(u)$ by a contrast threshold ($+C_u^+$ for positive events, $-C_u^-$ for negative events).
1. **Local Signal Approximation:** Within a small simulation window $(t_0, t_0 + \Delta t]$, the signal $S(u, t)$ is approximated using a first-order (linear) model based on $S_0(u)$ and $\dot{S}_0(u)$. Optionally, a quadratic model can be used if a second render $S_1 = S(\cdot, t_0 + \Delta t)$ is available, accounting for signal curvature.
2. **Root Finding:** The continuous-time threshold crossings are then found by solving for the roots of the linear or quadratic model within the interval $(0, \Delta t]$. TIDES supports multiple event crossings per pixel within a single time step.
3. **Sensor Variations:** The model optionally incorporates realistic sensor characteristics such as asymmetric thresholds, per-pixel threshold jitter (fixed once sampled), and comparator leak, where $S_{\text{ref}}$ slowly drifts.

#### 3.4. Adaptive Rendering

To optimize computational efficiency and accuracy, TIDES utilizes risk-guided adaptive time stepping. The 3D scene model inherently provides information about object overlap and evolving visibility.
1. **Risk Score Calculation:** Three diagnostic signals are extracted from the compositing loop at negligible cost: the transmittance rate $|\dot{\alpha}(u, t)|$ (indicating rapid visibility change), the contributor count $c(u, t)$ (indicating depth competition), and the coverage proxy $b(u, t)$ (accumulated blending mass). These are combined into a per-pixel risk score $r(u, t)$.
2. **Adaptive Step Size:** This risk score is used to dynamically shrink the simulation step size $\Delta t$ in regions where occlusion dynamics make a simple linear model unreliable. This concentrates computation only where it is most needed.
3. **Event Suppression:** Event emission can also be suppressed in regions where coverage $b$ is below a minimum threshold, indicating unreliable visibility.

#### 3.5. Derivative-Conditioned Finite-Bandwidth Readout

Real event sensors have finite readout bandwidth, which leads to queuing delays, jitter, and event drops during localized bursts. TIDES optionally models this by:
1. **Tile-Scanning Arbiter:** A tile-scanning arbiter manages event queues for different sensor tiles. Each tile can release a maximum number of events per tick ($K_m$) with a certain drop probability ($p_{\text{drop},m}$) and additive timestamp jitter ($\sigma_{t,m}$).
2. **Activity Score Conditioning:** These readout parameters ($K_m$, $p_{\text{drop},m}$, $\sigma_{t,m}$) are conditioned on a tile-level activity score $\psi_m$. This score aggregates renderer-derived cues like the event-rate proxy $(|\dot{L}|/C)$, the transmittance rate $(|\dot{\alpha}|)$, and the contributor count $(c)$. This mechanism realistically reproduces readout saturation, where high activity leads to reduced throughput, increased drops, and greater timestamp jitter.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TIDES involved comparing its simulated event streams against real events across several metrics and assessing its transferability to downstream tasks. The comparison included existing simulators such as ESIM, V2E, DVS-Voltmeter, and ICNS/IEBCS, using a shared dynamic Gaussian splatting scene as the continuous-time rendering source.

#### 4.1. Event Stream Fidelity and Batching Diagnostics

Experiments were conducted on four real datasets (EDS, DSEC, HS-ERGB, BS-ERGB) covering ego-motion and dynamic scenes.
- **Fidelity (Table 1):** TIDES consistently achieved the best fidelity across all four datasets, both with a fixed 10x query budget and its adaptive budget. Metrics like Inverse-Gaussian Negative Log-Likelihood (IG-NLL) and Chamfer distance (a motion-scaled spatiotemporal similarity metric) were used, with lower values indicating better agreement with real events. TIDES showed significant gains, particularly in scenarios with fast motion and occlusions, where frame-driven simulators tend to struggle due to aliasing and finite-difference inaccuracies. The adaptive rendering further improved IG-NLL and Chamfer distance, especially in datasets with sparse pose sampling and dynamic objects, by concentrating computation in mixed-visibility regions.
- **Batching Diagnostics (Table 2):** TIDES substantially reduced metrics associated with timestamp batching and burst distortion.
 - **Same-ts:** The fraction of events sharing identical timestamps within a window was consistently lower for TIDES, indicating reduced batching.
 - **ISI Spike Ratio:** The fraction of inter-spike intervals (ISIs) near zero or concentrated at discrete clock quanta was also lower, signifying more continuous and realistic event timing.
 - **Fano-factor Error:** TIDES demonstrated lower Fano-factor error, indicating a better match to the burst dispersion statistics of real events.
These improvements were attributed to TIDES's ability to render instantaneous, visibility-consistent $\dot{L}$ and allow for continuous-time threshold crossings, including multiple events per pixel per step, without arbitrary differencing intervals. Qualitatively, Figure 3 illustrates that TIDES produces smoother temporal dispersion along motion boundaries and fewer frame-aligned timestamp bands compared to baselines.

#### 4.2. Downstream Task Transfer (Table 3)

To assess the practical utility of TIDES-simulated events, models trained on simulated data were evaluated on real datasets for various downstream tasks:
- **Reconstruction (E2VID):** TIDES-trained models achieved the highest PSNR and lowest LPIPS on HS-ERGB for event-based video reconstruction.
- **Video Frame Interpolation (TimeLens-XL):** TIDES-trained models showed the best PSNR and lowest LPIPS on BS-ERGB, indicating superior performance in generating interpolated video frames.
- **Depth Estimation (Depth Any Event Stream):** TIDES-trained models yielded the lowest RMSE and RMSE log on DSEC, demonstrating improved depth estimation capabilities.
- **Segmentation (ESS):** TIDES-trained models achieved the highest mIoU on DSEC, indicating better semantic segmentation performance.
The results collectively suggest that events simulated by TIDES transfer more effectively to real-world tasks, enabling better performance of event-based vision algorithms trained on synthetic data. Qualitative examples in Figure 4 support these findings, showing that models trained on TIDES events produce higher quality outputs for tasks like reconstruction and depth estimation.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (Table 4)

An ablation study on EDS scenes quantified the contribution of each TIDES component:
- **Visibility-Consistent Time-Tangent Derivatives:** Replacing the analytical $\dot{L}$ with finite-difference approximations resulted in the largest degradation in fidelity (increased IG-NLL and Chamfer), confirming the critical role of continuous-time derivative computation.
- **Adaptive Control:** Disabling adaptive time stepping (fixed step size) or risk-gated refinement led to notable degradations, underscoring the importance of dynamic computational allocation in mixed-visibility regions.
- **Multi-Crossing and Mixed-Visibility Masking:** Disabling the ability to generate multiple events per step or masking events in unreliable mixed-visibility regions also decreased fidelity.
- **Readout Model:** While less impactful than the core continuous-time components, removing the readout model consistently resulted in a slight drop in fidelity, demonstrating its contribution to realistic artifact generation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TIDES represents a significant advancement in event camera simulation by addressing the long-standing problem of "timestamp batching" and providing a physically grounded, continuous-time event synthesis pipeline. By leveraging dynamic Gaussian splatting and forward-mode automatic differentiation, TIDES moves beyond frame-based approximations to directly model per-pixel intensity dynamics and their derivatives with visibility-consistent compositing.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:
- **Enhanced Realism and Fidelity:** TIDES generates event streams that more accurately reproduce the fine-grained timing and spatiotemporal characteristics of real event cameras, particularly in dynamic scenes with occlusions. This is evidenced by state-of-the-art results on standard fidelity metrics (IG-NLL, Chamfer) and significantly reduced batching artifacts (Same-ts, ISI spike ratio, Fano-factor error).
- **Improved Synthetic-to-Real Transfer:** The high fidelity of TIDES-simulated events translates directly to improved performance for downstream event-vision tasks. Models trained on TIDES-generated data demonstrate better transferability to real-world scenarios across tasks like video reconstruction, frame interpolation, depth estimation, and semantic segmentation. This is crucial for bridging the gap between simulated and real data, reducing the reliance on expensive real-world data collection and annotation.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** By providing a more realistic simulation environment, TIDES can accelerate research and development in event-based vision. Researchers can train and evaluate novel algorithms with greater confidence in the synthetic data's representativeness, fostering innovation in areas such as robust perception under fast motion and challenging illumination.
- **Novel Methodology:** The integration of forward-mode time-derivative rendering with dynamic Gaussian splatting for continuous-time event generation is a novel methodological contribution. The adaptive time-stepping guided by risk scores and the derivative-conditioned finite-bandwidth readout model further enhance the realism and efficiency of the simulation.
- **Scalability and Flexibility:** While current performance is tied to the underlying 4DGS reconstruction, the modular nature of TIDES allows it to benefit from ongoing improvements in 4DGS techniques. This makes the approach adaptable to future advancements in dynamic neural rendering.

The potential impact of TIDES includes enabling the development of more robust and accurate event-based computer vision systems for applications in robotics, autonomous driving, surveillance, and human-computer interaction, where high temporal resolution and dynamic range are critical. It democratizes access to realistic event data for algorithm development, circumventing the practical challenges of capturing and labeling real event datasets.
