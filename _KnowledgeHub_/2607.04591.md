# Simple-to-Complex Structured Demonstrations for Vision-Language-Action Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A Simple-to-Complex (S2C) structured demonstration strategy improves Vision-Language-Action (VLA) learning for robotic manipulation under limited data conditions. This method boosts the success rate for block grasping and sorting from 0% to 80.0% and for towel folding from 0% to 25.0% by progressively organizing demonstrations.

## Problem

- Existing VLA learning research predominantly focuses on model architectures and dataset scale, neglecting the systematic organization of robotic demonstrations.
- Acquiring large-scale robotic datasets is expensive and impractical for many research and real-world applications operating under limited-data conditions.
- Direct collection of end-to-end demonstrations for long-horizon tasks leads to unstable execution and poor generalization in VLA models due to coupled learning challenges.

## Method

- The research proposes a **Simple-to-Complex (S2C)** structured demonstration collection strategy that organizes data progressively from simple skills to complex task executions.
- S2C employs ==task decomposition== into progressive capability objectives (basic manipulation, object perception/state understanding, task execution).
- The strategy incorporates ==environment standardization== within stages to reduce irrelevant variations and ==progressive complexity scheduling==, introducing new difficulties only after prerequisite capabilities are established.

## Results

- For the block grasping and sorting task, S2C achieved an **80.0% success rate (4/5 trials)** with **300 demonstrations**, compared to **0% (0/5 trials)** for direct collection with **200 demonstrations**.
- For the towel folding task, S2C achieved a **25.0% success rate (7/28 trials)** with **300 demonstrations**, significantly outperforming direct collection which yielded **0% (0/28 trials)** with **200 demonstrations**.
- Remaining failures for S2C were primarily attributed to execution-level errors, hardware precision limitations, and intrinsic variability of deformable objects, rather than fundamental task-level misunderstandings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Demonstration organization is a critical, yet underexplored, factor that significantly impacts VLA learning efficiency and policy stability, especially under limited data.
> - Progressive decomposition of complex tasks into capability-oriented stages (S2C) reduces the cognitive load on VLA models, allowing sequential acquisition of low-level motor skills, perceptual reasoning, and high-level task sequencing.
> - The S2C approach can manage state variability for deformable objects through state normalization, which aids in learning long-horizon tasks by reducing error propagation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm in robotic manipulation, integrating visual perception, language understanding, and robot action generation. Existing research in VLA learning has predominantly concentrated on advancements in model architectures, optimization strategies, and the expansion of dataset scale. This includes the development of large-scale robotic datasets like RoboNet and Open X-Embodiment, which aggregate data across diverse platforms and embodiments to foster generalizable robotic policies. Concurrently, VLA systems such as OpenVLA, SmolVLA, and the $\pi$ series have leveraged these datasets to demonstrate manipulation capabilities and generalization across various tasks, underscoring the role of large-scale and diverse data in enhancing policy robustness and generalization. Studies have shown that increasing data diversity can lead to learning abstract shared features and improve generalization across tasks and environments.

Despite these advancements, the acquisition of large-scale robotic datasets typically requires substantial human effort, multiple robotic platforms, and extensive data collection infrastructure, rendering it an expensive and often impractical endeavor for many research settings or real-world applications that operate under limited-data conditions.

Within this landscape, curriculum learning and task decomposition have been explored in reinforcement learning and imitation learning to improve optimization by structuring training samples from simpler to more complex instances. However, these methods primarily focus on structuring the *training process* after the demonstration dataset has already been collected and fixed.

This paper identifies a gap in this research trajectory: the systematic investigation into how robotic demonstrations are *collected and organized*. While prior studies have noted that the structure and quality of demonstrations can significantly influence learning efficiency and policy performance, particularly for long-horizon tasks, systematic exploration of demonstration organization has been limited. This work positions itself as addressing this underexplored dimension, arguing that demonstration organization is a fundamental factor in VLA learning, especially under limited-data conditions where data efficiency is critical. It shifts the focus from merely scaling data or refining models to optimizing the *process of data collection itself* to facilitate more effective and stable policy learning for complex robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the overlooked aspect of demonstration organization in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) learning for robotic manipulation. The authors contend that while model architectures, training strategies, and dataset scale have received extensive attention, the method by which robotic demonstrations are collected and structured has been comparatively neglected.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several observations and identified challenges:

1. **Direct Impact on Policy Learning:** The authors emphasize that demonstrations define the supervision signal for imitation learning, directly influencing policy learning efficiency, convergence behavior, and final task performance. As robotic manipulation tasks become increasingly long-horizon and compositional, the quality and structure of this supervision become more critical.
2. **Suboptimal Supervision from Naive Collection:** Through preliminary experiments on a dual-arm robotic platform, the authors observed that different demonstration collection strategies yielded substantially different learning outcomes, even when using identical VLA models and training configurations. For instance, policies trained on full-task demonstrations for block grasping often failed to reliably execute intermediate grasping and sorting behaviors, exhibiting performance asymmetry between manipulators or failing to map visual cues to actions. Similarly, in towel folding, policies frequently exhibited execution failures or became stuck in intermediate states, suggesting that a naive collection of complete task demonstrations provides suboptimal supervision for complex, long-horizon manipulation.
3. **Difficulty of Coupled Learning under Limited Data:** Directly collecting end-to-end demonstrations for long-horizon tasks requires the VLA model to simultaneously acquire primitive manipulation skills, understand task-related perception, infer state transitions, and learn their temporal composition from a limited number of demonstrations. Under typical limited-data regimes, this coupled learning problem significantly increases optimization difficulty, leading to unstable execution and poor generalization as task complexity and state diversity increase. For the towel folding task, the large variation in initial towel configurations introduced substantial visual diversity, making limited demonstrations insufficient to cover the state space.
4. **Improving Data Efficiency and Learning Stability:** Given the expense and impracticality of collecting massive datasets for many real-world applications, data efficiency is a critical concern. The authors are motivated to explore structured demonstration collection as a means to improve data efficiency and learning stability under these limited-data conditions.

To address these issues, the paper proposes a simple-to-complex (S2C) structured demonstration collection strategy. The key objectives of this strategy are:
- To enable VLA models to first acquire fundamental manipulation skills.
- To progressively build upon these basic skills to learn increasingly complex task compositions.
- To facilitate more effective and stable learning of long-horizon manipulation tasks.

The work aims to demonstrate that demonstration organization is an important design dimension in VLA learning and to provide practical guidelines for robotic dataset construction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes and evaluates a simple-to-complex (S2C) structured demonstration collection strategy. This strategy is contrasted with a direct end-to-end demonstration collection baseline.

**Experimental Platform and Task Design:**
The experiments were conducted on a real-world dual-arm SO-101 robotic platform equipped with two manipulators and four synchronized RGB cameras for multi-view perception. Demonstrations were collected via human teleoperation. The underlying VLA policy model used was $\pi_{0.5}$, which was chosen based on prior benchmarking that showed its superior performance and robustness. The VLA model architecture, training procedure, hyperparameter settings, and evaluation protocol were kept constant across all experiments to isolate the effect of the demonstration collection strategy.

Two representative long-horizon manipulation tasks were selected for evaluation:
1. **Block Grasping and Sorting:** This task involves rigid-object manipulation, language-grounded localization, color-based reasoning, and sequential control. The robot must locate, grasp, transport, and place blocks according to color-based instructions.
2. **Towel Folding:** This task involves deformable object manipulation, bimanual coordination, and long-horizon execution. The robot must grasp, lift, and fold a towel neatly.

These tasks were chosen because they require the composition of multiple primitive manipulation skills into coherent long-horizon behaviors, presenting challenges under limited demonstration data.

**Direct Demonstration Collection (Baseline):**
In the baseline approach, each demonstration was recorded as a complete task trajectory, from the initial state to task completion, without any decomposition. For block grasping and sorting, a single trajectory covered object localization, grasping, transfer, and color-based placement. For towel folding, it captured the entire sequence, including grasping, lifting, folding, and final placement. This method aligns with conventional paradigms in imitation learning.

**Observed Challenges with Direct End-to-End Collection:**
Before proposing S2C, the authors observed specific failures with the direct collection method:
- **Block Grasping and Sorting (200 demos):** The trained policy showed severe performance asymmetry, with one arm able to perform coarse grasping but the other consistently failing to initiate grasps. It struggled to associate visual color cues with placement actions.
- **Towel Folding (200 demos):** The learned policy often failed, exhibiting prolonged hesitation and inability to progress. The large variation in initial towel configurations introduced excessive visual diversity, making the limited demonstrations insufficient.
These observations highlighted that direct collection forced the policy to jointly learn primitive skills and long-horizon composition from insufficient data, leading to instability.

**Simple-to-Complex (S2C) Structured Demonstration Strategy:**
The S2C strategy organizes demonstrations progressively from simple manipulation skills to complex task executions based on three principles:
1. **Task Decomposition:** A manipulation task $T$ is decomposed into progressive capability objectives:
 - $D_{manip}$: Basic manipulation (e.g., grasping, lifting, transferring).
 - $D_{state}$: Object perception and state understanding (e.g., recognizing colors, locating regions, state normalization).
 - $D_{exec}$: Task execution (combining previous capabilities to complete the full task).
 These capabilities form temporally ordered stages: $S_{manip}$, $S_{state}$, $S_{exec}$. This decomposition reduces the need for the policy to learn all capabilities simultaneously.
2. **Environment Standardization:** Within each stage, variations irrelevant to the current capability objective are reduced. For example, when learning basic grasping, object categories, colors, or spatial layouts might be simplified. This provides more focused supervision.
3. **Progressive Complexity Scheduling:** Task complexity is gradually increased across stages. New difficulties are introduced only after prerequisite capabilities are reliably established. This allows later stages to build upon stable foundations, improving learning stability and generalization.

**Instantiation of S2C for Block Grasping and Sorting (Total 300 demonstrations):**
- **Stage 1 ($S_1$): Basic Manipulation ($D_{block\_manip}$):** Focus on reliable block grasping and transfer. Single-color blocks are used, and demonstrations are collected independently for left and right manipulators (80 demonstrations across four colors, 10 per color per arm). Initial scene configuration is standardized.
- **Stage 2 ($S_2$): Object Perception and State Understanding ($D_{block\_state}$):** Introduce multiple colors, requiring association of visual cues with placement regions. Demonstrations are still collected independently for each manipulator (160 demonstrations, 20 per color per arm). Perceptual complexity increases while manipulation primitives remain consistent.
- **Stage 3 ($S_3$): Task Execution ($D_{block\_exec}$):** Focus on complete dual-arm sorting, including synchronized motion and spatial coordination within a shared workspace. Prerequisites from $S_1$ and $S_2$ are assumed (60 demonstrations).

**Instantiation of S2C for Towel Folding (Total 300 demonstrations):**
- **Stage 1 ($S_1$): Basic Manipulation / Initial-State Handling ($D_{towel\_manip}$):** Focus on recognizing and grasping towels from representative initial configurations (horizontal, vertical, slightly disordered). Limited initial state diversity helps acquire reliable grasping.
- **Stage 2 ($S_2$): Object Perception and State Understanding / State Normalization ($D_{towel\_state}$):** Transform diverse initial towel configurations into a standardized flat state ($o_{flat}$) via a unified unfolding operation. This reduces state variability for subsequent stages.
- **Stage 3 ($S_3$): Task Execution / Rule-Based Folding ($D_{towel\_exec}$):** Execute a standardized sequence of folding actions from the normalized flat state ($o_{flat}$) to the final folded state ($o_{final}$). Learning focuses on the sequential folding behaviors without direct influence from unpredictable initial deformable object configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results indicate consistent improvements in task success rate and training stability when utilizing the proposed Simple-to-Complex (S2C) structured demonstration collection strategy compared to direct end-to-end demonstration collection.

**Task Success Rate:**
The task success rate was defined as the ratio of successful trials to the total number of evaluation trials.

- **Block Grasping and Sorting:**
 - **Direct Collection (200 demonstrations):** Achieved a success rate of 0% (0 out of 5 trials). The policies exhibited severe performance asymmetry between the manipulators and failed to reliably map object colors to placement locations, consistent with initial observations.
 - **Proposed S2C Strategy (300 demonstrations):** Achieved a success rate of 80.0% (4 out of 5 trials). Both manipulators consistently performed object grasping and color-guided placement, indicating effective acquisition and integration of interdependent manipulation capabilities.
- **Towel Folding:**
 - **Direct Collection (200 demonstrations):** Achieved a success rate of 0% (0 out of 28 trials). The dominant failure mode was prolonged hesitation after grasping the towel, preventing progression to subsequent folding steps.
 - **Proposed S2C Strategy (300 demonstrations):** Achieved a success rate of 25.0% (7 out of 28 trials). While still modest, the policy was able to execute complete folding sequences in multiple trials, rather than consistently stalling early in the task, demonstrating that S2C enabled the learning of an executable long-horizon manipulation procedure that was not reliably obtained with direct collection.

Overall, S2C consistently outperformed direct collection across both rigid-object (block grasping) and deformable-object (towel folding) manipulation tasks, suggesting that demonstration organization is a critical factor for effective policy learning under limited-data conditions.

**Failure Analysis:**
A trial was considered successful only if the robot completed the entire task and reached the final goal. Failures were categorized into:
- **Grasp Failure:** Robot fails to stably grasp the object (empty grasps, unstable grasps, drops).
- **Wrong Action:** Robot performs an action not leading to the desired outcome (incorrect placement, incomplete folding).

- **Block Grasping and Sorting (S2C Strategy):**
 - Observed failures (2 total) were split evenly: 50% Grasp Failure (1 event) and 50% Wrong Action (1 event).
 - Grasp instability: The manipulator could localize the block and attempt grasping, but issues like unstable gripper closing positions, contact angles, or execution precision caused the block to slip or be pushed away. This indicated target localization and action initiation capabilities were present, but physical precision limited success.
 - Unstable or inaccurate placement: The robot successfully grasped the block but failed to place it stably or precisely in the target region. This was attributed to insufficient spatial precision and end-effector stability rather than a complete failure of color recognition.
- **Towel Folding (S2C Strategy):**
 - Observed failures (21 total) were predominantly Grasp Failure: 90.47% Grasp Failure (19 events) and 9.53% Wrong Action (2 events).
 - Initial grasp failure: The two-finger gripper of the SO-101 platform struggled to reliably grasp the thin towel lying close to the tabletop. This physical limitation often prevented the policy from proceeding to subsequent stages.
 - Unpredictable deformation during folding: Even with successful initial grasps, subsequent folding actions could lead to intermediate states that were rarely covered in the training data due to deviations in grasp points, gripper force, or towel slippage. These small errors amplified into significant shape changes, resulting in hesitation, stopping, or incomplete folding. This highlights accumulated errors over long-horizon, deformable-object manipulation.

The analysis indicated that remaining failures with S2C were primarily due to execution-level errors, hardware precision limitations, and the intrinsic variability of deformable objects, rather than fundamental misunderstandings of task-level semantics or a failure of the structured demonstration strategy itself.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research highlights that the organization of demonstrations is a critical, yet often underexplored, factor in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) learning, particularly under conditions of limited demonstration data. The findings suggest that merely increasing data quantity may be less effective than structuring the data collection process itself.

The **significance** of the proposed Simple-to-Complex (S2C) strategy lies in its ability to enhance learning efficiency and policy stability by explicitly aligning the demonstration collection process with the hierarchical dependency structure of manipulation capabilities. By progressively decomposing complex long-horizon tasks into capability-oriented learning stages (basic manipulation, object perception/state understanding, task execution), S2C reduces the cognitive load on the VLA model. This staged learning allows the model to acquire low-level motor skills, perceptual reasoning, and high-level task sequencing sequentially, minimizing the need to learn all interdependent components simultaneously from complex full-task demonstrations. The consistent performance improvements observed across both rigid-object (block grasping and sorting) and deformable-object (towel folding) tasks support the general applicability of this decomposition principle.

The **potential impact** of this work is multifaceted:
1. **Efficient Skill Acquisition:** S2C provides a practical framework for more efficient acquisition of complex robotic skills, particularly valuable in real-world scenarios where collecting vast quantities of high-quality data is resource-intensive.
2. **Scalable Dataset Construction:** The strategy offers guidelines for structuring robotic datasets. Instead of viewing demonstrations as independent trajectories, S2C proposes organizing them to facilitate incremental learning, which could lead to more robust and generalizable policies even with moderate dataset sizes.
3. **Improved Long-Horizon Manipulation:** For tasks requiring multiple sequential steps and intricate coordination, S2C's approach of building capabilities progressively (e.g., through state normalization in towel folding) helps to manage state variability and reduce error propagation, leading to more stable long-horizon execution.
4. **Foundation for Future Research:** The identification of demonstration organization as a key factor opens avenues for further research. Future work could build upon S2C to develop more autonomous systems for defining capability targets and adapting environment scheduling, potentially integrating with causal world modeling to enhance the robot's understanding of task outcomes and error recovery. This could move beyond purely predictive models to ones that explicitly reason about causal mechanisms, fostering more intelligent and adaptable robotic behaviors.
5. **Addressing Deformable Object Challenges:** While not fully resolved, the S2C approach for deformable objects (towel folding) demonstrates progress by introducing state normalization, a crucial step in managing the inherent variability. This provides a foundation for future exploration into more fine-grained intermediate stages and robust error recovery mechanisms for deformable object manipulation.

In summary, this research contributes to the understanding of data collection as a fundamental component of effective VLA learning, offering a structured methodology that can significantly improve the performance and stability of robotic manipulation policies.
