# Towards Robust Zero-Shot Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The Chinese University of Hong Kong and Tsinghua University developed BREEZE, a framework for robust zero-shot reinforcement learning that enhances Forward-Backward representations. It addresses issues of extrapolation error and limited model expressivity, achieving superior or near-best performance on the ExORL benchmark and D4RL Kitchen datasets with faster convergence and improved stability compared to prior methods.

## Problem

- Existing Forward-Backward (FB) representation-based methods in zero-shot RL often produce inconsistent and biased successor measures and Q-value distributions, including negative M-values and significant scale mismatches.
- Current FB models lack sufficient expressivity to accurately learn complex approximators and multimodal behaviors required for effective generalization to diverse tasks.
- Offline unsupervised pretraining in FB methods is susceptible to extrapolation errors from out-of-distribution (OOD) actions, leading to unstable value estimates and propagating inaccuracies.

## Method

- Introduces ==Behavior-Regularized== Optimization, which constrains action selection during value function learning to in-distribution samples using expectile regression to mitigate OOD extrapolation errors.
- Employs a Task-Conditioned Diffusion Model for policy extraction, enabling the generation of high-quality, multimodal action distributions weighted by advantage, thus improving expressivity for complex tasks.
- Utilizes Expressivity Enhancement for Representation Modeling, integrating attention-based network architectures (Transformers) for both Forward and Backward representations to better capture complex state-action-task relationships.

## Results

- BREEZE achieved superior or near-best Interquartile Mean (IQM) returns across the majority of domains and datasets in the ExORL benchmark (e.g., **693** for Walker RND, **84** for Jaco RND), outperforming prior FB-based methods.
- The framework demonstrated faster convergence and enhanced stability during training compared to baselines, evident through smoother learning curves and lower variance in locomotion and manipulation domains.
- BREEZE maintained a significant performance advantage and exhibited superior stability even on small-sample (100k transition) datasets, indicating robust performance under limited data coverage and quality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mitigating offline extrapolation errors through in-sample behavioral regularization is crucial for learning stable and accurate representations in zero-shot reinforcement learning.
> - Adopting generative models, specifically task-conditioned diffusion policies, enables the capture of multimodal action distributions, which is essential for tackling complex tasks requiring diverse behaviors in a zero-shot manner.
> - Enhancing the expressivity of the neural network architectures, particularly with attention mechanisms for modeling representations, significantly improves the quality and generalization capabilities of the learned forward and backward functions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities across various domains, including robotics, autonomous systems, industrial control, and large language models. However, its practical application is often constrained by two factors: the necessity of manually designed reward functions and its task-specific learning paradigm, which limits generalization to novel or multiple tasks.

Zero-shot RL emerges as an approach to address these limitations by enabling the development of versatile agents pretrained on reward-free transitions that can then adapt to arbitrary new reward functions during inference without further fine-tuning. This capacity for generalization is considered beneficial for deploying RL agents in open-world scenarios with diverse and evolving tasks.

Current zero-shot RL methods generally fall into two categories:
1. **Task/skill-conditioned RL:** These methods typically encode demonstrations or reward functions into embeddings that serve as conditioning signals for policy learning. While effective for task generalization, they often involve substantial manual design in the pretraining phase and may not offer optimality guarantees.
2. **Dynamic representation-based methods:** These approaches aim to decompose the RL problem into dynamic representations that can be recombined for novel tasks. Forward-Backward (FB) representations are a notable example within this category. The FB framework factorizes occupancy measures into a forward representation (capturing policy dynamics) and a backward representation (encoding global state information). Through offline, unsupervised pretraining, FB aims to learn linearized representations that approximate value functions for arbitrary tasks, thereby facilitating zero-shot generalization.

Despite the theoretical foundation of FB representations, empirical observations suggest that existing FB-based methods exhibit limitations. Specifically, these methods often produce inconsistent and biased successor measures and Q-value distributions. This inconsistency can manifest as scale mismatches and the presence of invalid negative values, ultimately affecting learning stability and overall performance. The paper identifies two primary causes for these shortcomings:
- **Limited Model Expressivity:** The models used in current FB methods are considered insufficient for learning complex approximators and multimodal behaviors.
- **Offline Extrapolation Errors:** During offline, unsupervised pretraining, out-of-distribution (OOD) actions can lead to extrapolation errors, similar to issues observed in offline RL, but with potentially increased complexity. Existing regularization techniques, such as CQL-style regularizers, have shown partial but incomplete mitigation of these errors.

This research aims to address these identified empirical and methodological issues within the FB framework, positioning itself as an advancement in developing more robust and expressive zero-shot RL agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enhance the robustness and generalization capabilities of zero-shot Reinforcement Learning, specifically within the Forward-Backward (FB) representation framework. This overarching goal is motivated by several identified empirical shortcomings of existing FB-based methods.

The primary motivations for this work stem from empirical observations that highlight fundamental issues with current FB approaches:

1. **Inconsistent and Biased Representations:** Empirical studies conducted by the authors revealed that successor measures ($M_{\pi_z}$) and Q-value ($Q_z$) distributions derived from existing FB-based methods (e.g., vanilla FB, MCFB, VCFB) are often inconsistent and biased.
 - **Scale Mismatch:** The absolute values of these measures were observed to be excessively large.
 - **Negative Values:** A significant proportion of empirically evaluated $M_{\pi_z}$ values, which theoretically represent positive future state occupancies, were found to be negative.
 - These inaccuracies lead to a consistent prediction bias, which is exacerbated by suboptimal action rollouts, ultimately compromising the stability and overall performance of the agent. Existing regularization techniques, such as CQL-style regularizers, were found to only partially mitigate these issues, indicating a need for more effective strategies.

2. **Lack of Model Expressivity:** The paper posits that existing FB methods utilize models (often MLP-based) that lack the expressivity required to learn complex approximators and effectively capture multimodal behaviors. Modeling successor measures and zero-shot policies across a potentially infinite range of task vectors ($z$) introduces substantial complexity, demanding higher capacity and more sophisticated model architectures. Simple scaling up of existing MLP networks was empirically shown not to yield significant performance gains, highlighting a need for architectural redesign rather than mere capacity increase.

3. **Extrapolation Errors from Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Actions:** The offline, unsupervised pretraining stage in FB methods, similar to offline RL settings, is susceptible to extrapolation errors. These errors arise when the policy generates actions that are out-of-distribution with respect to the training dataset. While such actions might appear optimal under learned (but potentially biased) representations, they lead to inaccurate value overestimation and propagation of errors. The authors argue that this problem is particularly complex in zero-shot RL settings, requiring a delicate regularization mechanism beyond direct value constraints.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the proposed BREEZE framework are to:

- **Enhance Offline Learning Stability:** Develop mechanisms to mitigate extrapolation errors caused by OOD actions during offline pretraining.
- **Improve Policy Extraction Capability:** Enable the generation of high-quality and multimodal action distributions in zero-shot RL settings, moving beyond simple unimodal policy representations.
- **Enhance Representation Learning Quality:** Improve the accuracy and consistency of the learned forward and backward representations to ensure that $M_{\pi_z}$ and $Q_z$ values align more closely with theoretical expectations (e.g., being positive and within a reasonable scale).
- **Increase Model Expressivity:** Incorporate more expressive network architectures to better capture complex relationships within environmental dynamics and task specifications.

By addressing these objectives, BREEZE aims to provide a more robust, stable, and high-performing zero-shot RL framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The BREEZE (Behavior-REgularizEd Zero-shot RL with Expressivity Enhancement) framework integrates three core technical advancements to address the identified issues of OOD extrapolation and limited expressivity in FB-based methods.

#### 3.1. Behavior-Regularized Optimization

BREEZE introduces behavioral regularization to stabilize both representation learning and policy extraction, moving towards an in-sample learning paradigm.

**Behavior-Regularized Representation Guidance:**
The primary source of OOD extrapolation error in existing FB losses (e.g., $L_{FB}$ and $L_F$) is attributed to the policy-generated actions $\hat{a}_{t+1} \sim \pi_z(s_{t+1})$ used in the temporal difference targets. When these actions are OOD, they can lead to overestimated values and biased representations. To counter this, BREEZE redefines the task-conditioned state-value function $V_{\pi_z}(s, z)$ by constraining action selection to only those present in the dataset, i.e., $V_{\pi_z}(s, z) := \max_{a \in A \text{ s.t. } \mu(a|s)>0} F(s, a, z)^T z$. This formulation seeks to learn an optimal task-conditioned V-function solely from in-distribution samples, avoiding explicit estimation of the behavior policy $\mu(a|s)$.
This optimization problem is solved using an expectile regression objective, similar to Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), where $L_{\tau^2}(u) = |\tau - I(u < 0)|u^2$ with $\tau > 0.5$ as the expectile parameter. This objective ensures that the learned V-function is well-regularized and nearly optimal.
The original $L_F$ loss (Eq. 5) is then modified to $L_{F\text{-reg}}$ (Eq. 9) by replacing the potentially unstable $F(s_{t+1}, \pi_z(s_{t+1}), z)^T z$ target with the more stable $V_{\pi_z}(s_{t+1}, z)$. This substitution implicitly regularizes the Q-function, preserving representation structure while allowing for tunable conservatism.

**Behavior-Regularized Policy Extraction:**
For policy learning, BREEZE replaces the original policy objective with a behavior-regularized optimization problem. The goal is to maximize the advantage function $F(s, a, z)^T z - V_{\pi_z}(s, z)$, subject to a KL-divergence constraint $D_{KL}(\pi_z \| \mu) \le \epsilon$. This constraint ensures that the learned policy $\pi_z$ remains close to the behavior policy $\mu$ from the dataset, thereby preventing distributional shift in the offline setting.
The closed-form solution to this constrained optimization problem is derived as $\pi_z^*(s) \propto \mu(a|s) \exp(\alpha \cdot (F(s, a, z)^T z - V_{\pi_z}(s, z)))$, where $\alpha$ acts as a temperature parameter controlling the balance between behavior policy regularization and value function optimization. This solution combines the behavior policy distribution with the optimal value functions, encouraging high-advantage actions within the support of the dataset.

#### 3.2. Policy Extraction via Task-Conditioned Diffusion Model

To effectively implement the weighted behavior cloning objective from the behavior-regularized policy extraction, BREEZE employs a task-conditioned diffusion model. Traditional Gaussian policies may struggle to capture the complex, multimodal action distributions often required for arbitrary task learning. Diffusion models, known for their ability to learn and generate complex distributions through iterative denoising, are utilized.
Instead of separate time-dependent guidance terms, BREEZE trains the diffusion model using a weighted regression objective:
$\min_{\theta} E_{t \sim U([0,T]), \epsilon \sim N(0,I), (s,a) \sim D} \left[ \exp\left( \alpha \cdot (F(s, a, z)^T z - V_{\pi_z}(s, z)) \right) \| \epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta,z}(a_t, s, z, t) \|_2^2 \right]$.
Here, $a_t = \alpha_t a + \sigma_t \epsilon$ follows the forward noise process. The weight term $\exp(\alpha \cdot \text{Advantage})$ directly biases the diffusion model towards high-advantage actions.
For action selection during evaluation, a rejection sampling mechanism is used to balance conservatism and optimism. This involves sampling $K_{eval}$ candidate actions from the diffusion policy $\pi_z(s)$ and then selecting the action $a^*$ with the highest Q-value, $a^* \triangleq \arg \max_{a \in \{a^{(1)}, \dots, a^{(K_{eval})} \}\sim \pi_z(s)} F(s, a, z)^T z$. A similar mechanism with $K_{train}$ is used during training to select next actions for the representation losses.

#### 3.3. Expressivity Enhancement for Representation Modeling

Recognizing the need for more expressive models to accurately capture complex task and dynamic relationships, BREEZE introduces enhanced network architectures for the forward (F) and backward (B) representations.

**Forward Network (F):**
The F network processes state-action ($s, a$) and state-task ($s, z$) pairs. Two separate linear encoders initially project these inputs into a 512-dimensional feature space. These features, representing behavioral and task contexts, are then concatenated into a length-2 embedding sequence. This sequence is passed through self-attention blocks, allowing bidirectional refinement of information between task conditions and agent behaviors. The output is finally projected to a d-dimensional space via linear layers.

**Backward Network (B):**
The B network, responsible for encoding state-level structural information as a global environment embedding, is designed as a stack of standard transformer-based networks with multi-head attention. Its output is projected to a d-dimensional space and then L2-normalized. This architecture aims to ensure that B captures complex relationships and maintains orthogonality properties essential for effective representation.

The overall training process involves normalizing the dataset, initializing networks, and iteratively sampling mini-batches to update $F_{\phi}$, $B_{\psi}$, $V_{\omega}$, and the diffusion actor $\pi_z$ (represented by $\epsilon_{\theta}$). The representation learning step combines $L_{FB}$, $L_{F\text{-reg}}$, and an orthogonality regularization loss $L_{ortho}$ on $B_{\psi}$. Value function learning updates $V_{\omega}$ with the expectile regression loss, and the diffusion policy $\epsilon_{\theta}$ is updated using its weighted regression objective. Clipped double value learning is employed for Q- and M-value computation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors evaluate BREEZE against prior offline zero-shot RL methods on the ExORL benchmark and the D4RL Kitchen dataset, focusing on zero-shot performance and robustness.

#### 4.1. Empirical Observations of Existing FB-Based Methods (Pre-BREEZE)

Before presenting BREEZE's performance, the paper details empirical findings regarding existing FB methods. Visualizations of $M_{\pi_z}$ and $Q_z$ distributions for vanilla FB and MCFB (an FB extension with CQL-style regularization) on ExORL Walker tasks showed significant issues:
- **Scale Mismatch:** The distributions exhibited absolute values considerably larger than theoretical expectations.
- **Negative Values:** A substantial number of $M_{\pi_z}$ values were negative, which contradicts its definition as a measure of future state occupancy.
These inaccuracies led to consistent prediction bias. While MCFB partially mitigated these issues by shifting distributions, significant estimation bias persisted. This motivated the development of BREEZE, which, in contrast, generated $M_{\pi_z}$ and $Q_z$ distributions that more closely aligned with theoretical expectations, exhibiting values primarily within a reasonable positive range. This improvement was attributed to architectural modifications and a refined OOD regularization strategy, rather than merely increased model capacity.

#### 4.2. Overall Zero-Shot Performance

BREEZE's zero-shot generalization capabilities were evaluated on the full ExORL benchmark (48 tasks across Walker, Jaco, Quadruped domains, collected by RND, APS, PROTO, DIAYN algorithms) and D4RL Kitchen (mixed and partial datasets).
- **ExORL Benchmark:** BREEZE achieved the best or near-best Interquartile Mean (IQM) returns across the majority of domains and datasets (Table 1). For example, on RND datasets, BREEZE scored 693 for Walker, 84 for Jaco, and 725 for Quadruped, outperforming baselines like SF-LAP, vanilla FB, VCFB, MCFB, and HILP in most cases.
- **D4RL Kitchen:** For long-horizon multi-stage tasks in the D4RL Kitchen environment, BREEZE demonstrated significantly higher performance compared to vanilla FB, as shown in Figure 4. This suggests BREEZE's improved ability to leverage datasets for complex sequential manipulation.

#### 4.3. Stability and Convergence Speed

Learning curves on RND datasets (Figure 5) illustrate the training dynamics.
- **Faster Convergence:** In locomotion domains (Quadruped and Walker), BREEZE converged more rapidly to higher performance levels.
- **Enhanced Stability:** The learning curves for BREEZE exhibited greater smoothness and lower variance (indicated by smaller shaded areas representing standard deviation), signifying enhanced stability during training.
- **Outperformance in Manipulation:** In the Jaco manipulation domain, BREEZE substantially outperformed all baselines with a higher learning speed.

#### 4.4. Robustness to Data Quality and Diversity (Small-Sample Datasets)

To assess robustness, experiments were conducted on uniformly subsampled 100,000-transition subsets of ExORL datasets (Table 2).
- **Pronounced Advantage:** BREEZE maintained a significant advantage over FB-based baselines (FB, VCFB, MCFB) in this limited and potentially lower-quality data regime.
- **Superior Stability:** Learning curves on 100k-subsample transitions (e.g., ExORL APS Walker domain, Figure 6) further demonstrated BREEZE's superior stability in scenarios with reduced data coverage.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies investigated the necessity and contribution of BREEZE's core components and hyperparameters.
- **Hyperparameter Choices:**
 - **Expectile (τ):** Performance generally improved monotonically with $\tau$, with peak performance observed at $\tau=0.99$, indicating the benefit of more aggressive, value-weighted optimization while maintaining in-sample conservatism.
 - **Temperature (α):** Performance peaked at $\alpha=0.05$, suggesting an optimal balance between the regularization strength of the behavioral policy and the optimization of value functions.
- **Necessity of Components:** Ablating the enhanced representation model (using MLP-based networks) and the diffusion policy (using a Gaussian policy variant) showed that each component individually contributed to performance gains. However, their combined synergy resulted in a "leap" in zero-shot capability (Table 3), indicating that the innovations function interdependently to address the system's issues.

In summary, BREEZE consistently demonstrated superior or competitive performance, faster convergence, and enhanced stability across diverse zero-shot RL benchmarks and data regimes. The empirical findings indicate that its proposed behavior regularization, task-conditioned diffusion policy, and expressive attention-based architectures effectively address the limitations of prior FB-based methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Towards Robust Zero-Shot Reinforcement Learning" by Zheng et al. introduces BREEZE, a framework that addresses identified limitations in existing Forward-Backward (FB) representation-based zero-shot Reinforcement Learning methods. This work holds significance for the advancement of generalist RL agents and has several potential impacts:

1. **Addressing Core Limitations of FB-based Zero-Shot RL:** The paper empirically identifies and systematically addresses two critical issues: the lack of expressivity in prior FB models and the occurrence of offline extrapolation errors caused by out-of-distribution (OOD) actions. By mitigating these issues, BREEZE contributes to a more reliable and theoretically grounded application of FB representations. The visualization of $M_{\pi_z}$ and $Q_z$ distributions demonstrates BREEZE's ability to produce representations that align more closely with theoretical expectations, a fundamental improvement over previous methods.

2. **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** BREEZE consistently achieved superior or near-best performance across various challenging domains and tasks in the ExORL benchmark and D4RL Kitchen datasets. Its demonstrated robustness in small-sample data regimes and during the learning process (faster convergence, lower variance) suggests that it is more resilient to variations in data quality and quantity, which is a practical consideration for real-world applications where high-quality, abundant data may not always be available.

3. **Methodological Contributions:**
 - **Behavior-Regularized Value Estimation:** The introduction of a behavior-regularized reformulation of FB, which leverages expectile regression and in-sample learning principles, provides a stable approach to learn value functions while preserving representation fidelity and mitigating OOD errors. This method implicitly regularizes the Q-function, promoting more reliable value estimates.
 - **Task-Conditioned Diffusion Policies:** Employing diffusion models for policy extraction enables the generation of high-quality, multimodal action distributions. This is crucial for complex tasks where a single Gaussian policy might be insufficient to capture the diversity of optimal behaviors. The weighted regression objective for the diffusion model offers an efficient way to integrate value information for policy guidance without additional time-dependent components.
 - **Expressive Attention-Based Architectures:** The design of attention-based networks for both forward and backward representations (F and B networks) allows for a more accurate modeling of complex relationships between environmental dynamics, states, actions, and tasks. This architectural enhancement is shown to be a key factor in improving the overall performance ceiling.

4. **Advancement Towards General-Purpose RL Agents:** By enabling learning of generalist policies that can adapt to arbitrary new tasks in a zero-shot manner, BREEZE moves closer to the vision of developing versatile RL agents capable of operating across diverse tasks in open-world scenarios. This reduces the need for extensive re-training or fine-tuning for every new task, potentially accelerating deployment in various applications.

5. **Implications for Real-World Applications:** The improved robustness and generalization capabilities of BREEZE could have practical implications for domains such as robotics, autonomous driving, and industrial automation, where agents need to adapt to unforeseen situations or new objectives quickly. For instance, a robot trained with BREEZE could potentially perform new manipulation tasks based on a simple reward function without needing new demonstrations or extensive task-specific training.

While BREEZE demonstrates improved robustness and performance, the paper acknowledges that its primary limitation is the increased computational cost associated with diffusion-based sampling. This is a common trade-off for utilizing high-performance generative policies, and future research could focus on optimizing the efficiency of such policies. Overall, BREEZE represents a notable step in enhancing the foundational robustness and expressivity of zero-shot RL within the FB framework.
