# AcL: Action Learner for Fault-Tolerant Quadruped Locomotion Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the National University of Singapore developed the Action Learner (AcL) framework, enabling quadrupedal robots to autonomously adapt locomotion under multiple simultaneous joint faults, including up to four faulty joints across two legs. This framework employs a novel teacher-student reinforcement learning approach with an encoder-decoder policy, allowing for smooth, bidirectional gait transitions and robust performance on a physical Unitree Go2 robot.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning methods for quadruped locomotion are vulnerable to internal joint failures, particularly in complex scenarios involving multiple simultaneous faults.
- Prior fault-tolerant approaches mainly address single-motor failures, often leading to abrupt gait transitions and neglecting the critical recovery phase to a normal gait.
- Training separate policies for each fault scenario is resource-intensive and lacks the ability to generalize or seamlessly switch between diverse fault conditions.

## Method

- **AcL** introduces a two-stage teacher-student ==reinforcement learning== framework, where multiple specialized teacher policies are trained for specific fault cases.
- A unified student policy, utilizing an encoder-decoder architecture, learns to autonomously identify fault types from proprioceptive observations and generate appropriate control actions.
- Student policy training is guided by 'style rewards' from teacher policies (promoting action amplitude and direction similarity) and 'regularization rewards' for overall robustness and smooth gait transitions across diverse terrains.

## Results

- The AcL framework enabled a Unitree Go2 robot to maintain stable locomotion with up to four simultaneous faulty joints across one or two legs in real-world experiments.
- The robot demonstrated autonomous, smooth, and bidirectional transitions between normal, various limping, and recovery gaits upon fault introduction or resolution.
- Ablation studies confirmed the essential role of style rewards for learning effective gaits, regularization rewards for robustness and adaptability, and the encoder for autonomous fault detection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling learning into fault-specific teacher policies and a general student policy, guided by 'style rewards,' allows for distilling diverse gaits into a single network capable of generalizing to complex and unseen fault conditions.
> - An encoder-decoder policy architecture enables autonomous fault detection and classification from proprioceptive data, facilitating adaptive gait selection without external state estimators.
> - Combining style rewards for specific behavior imitation with regularization rewards for stability and smooth transitions is crucial for consolidating multiple adaptive behaviors and achieving robust, bidirectional gait changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Quadrupedal robots are increasingly recognized as versatile platforms for applications in diverse and challenging environments, including search and rescue, cargo delivery, and planetary exploration. A critical requirement for their deployment in such scenarios is robust locomotion, particularly the ability to continue operating despite encountering physical damage or internal failures. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a prominent approach for developing adaptive and robust locomotion policies, enabling quadrupeds to navigate complex terrains and perform various gaits.

However, a notable limitation in existing RL-based locomotion control for quadrupeds is their vulnerability to internal joint faults, such as motor failures. While some research has addressed fault-tolerant locomotion, these methods primarily focus on single-motor failures. They often lack scalability to complex, multi-joint fault scenarios and typically exhibit abrupt gait transitions, which can compromise stability. Furthermore, many approaches do not adequately address the recovery phase, where the robot transitions back to a normal gait after a fault is resolved.

This research aims to advance fault-tolerant locomotion by addressing these gaps. It positions itself within the domain of teacher-student reinforcement learning frameworks, which are commonly used in quadruped control to facilitate sim-to-real transfer and robust behavior learning by leveraging privileged information or expert policies. Unlike prior teacher-student paradigms that often enforce strict imitation, this work introduces a mechanism where teacher policies guide student learning through "style rewards" without requiring precise replication of actions or network parameters. This allows for the distillation of multiple fault-specific gaits into a single, unified student policy, enabling autonomous adaptation to diverse and complex fault conditions not explicitly covered by individual teacher policies. The proposed framework directly confronts the challenge of sustaining stable, adaptive locomotion with multiple simultaneous joint faults, including those affecting multiple legs, and managing smooth transitions between various gaits, including recovery.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable quadrupedal robots to autonomously adapt their locomotion for stable walking under multiple joint faults, with a particular focus on scenarios where motor failures affect one or more legs. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Enhancing Robot Resilience and Autonomy**: Quadrupedal robots operating in real-world, often unpredictable, environments are susceptible to physical damage or internal malfunctions, such as joint motor failures. For these robots to be truly versatile and reliable, they must be capable of continuing their assigned tasks despite such impairments, much like biological counterparts (e.g., limping animals). Current robust locomotion policies often fail when internal faults disrupt performance, limiting the operational autonomy of quadrupeds in critical applications.
2. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Fault-Tolerant Methods**:
 - **Scalability to Multiple Faults**: Previous fault-tolerant locomotion methods predominantly address only single-motor failures. There is a need for approaches that can scale to more complex scenarios involving multiple simultaneous joint faults, potentially across different legs, which significantly alter the robot's kinematics and degrees of freedom. The paper highlights the challenge of training a single model for all fault cases due to varied reward scales and the risk of instability, especially with two-leg failures.
 - **Smooth Gait Transitions**: Existing methods often result in abrupt transitions between normal and fault-tolerant gaits, which can compromise the robot's stability. Furthermore, the critical reverse transition (recovering to a normal gait after a fault is resolved) is often neglected. The research seeks to ensure seamless, bidirectional gait transitions.
 - **Unified Policy for Diverse Gaits**: Training separate policies for each fault scenario is resource-intensive and complicates deployment, requiring high-level coordination for fault identification and policy switching. The motivation is to distill multiple, fault-specific gaits into a single policy network that can autonomously identify fault conditions and adapt accordingly.
3. **Leveraging and Advancing Teacher-Student RL Frameworks**: The concept of Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) techniques and teacher-student frameworks has shown promise in guiding RL for complex locomotion. This work is motivated to extend these concepts by proposing a novel teacher-student framework, AcL, that provides guidance through "style rewards" rather than strict imitation. This approach is intended to allow the student policy to learn diverse gaits and generalize to unseen fault conditions, while also incorporating regularization rewards for robustness beyond what individual teacher policies might provide.
4. **Developing an Adaptive Architecture**: The need for autonomous fault recognition and seamless gait switching motivates the development of a specific policy network architecture. An encoder-decoder structure is proposed to enable the policy to identify fault types from proprioceptive observations and generate appropriate actions, thereby facilitating dynamic adaptation without external state estimators or manual intervention.

In summary, the core motivation is to equip quadrupedal robots with enhanced adaptability and resilience, allowing them to maintain stable and versatile locomotion under various, complex joint fault conditions, with seamless gait transitions, using an integrated learning framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Action Learner (AcL) framework is a novel teacher-student reinforcement learning approach designed to enable fault-tolerant quadruped locomotion. It addresses the complexity of learning diverse, fault-specific gaits by decoupling the training process into stages and employing a specialized policy architecture.

**A. AcL Overview: A Novel Teacher-Student RL Training Framework**

AcL operates in a two-stage training framework to overcome the challenges of training a single policy for vastly different fault conditions, each requiring distinct reward scales and types.

1. **Teacher Policies Training**:
 - **Architecture**: Each teacher policy is implemented as a simple three-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
 - **Training**: Multiple teacher policies are trained separately using end-to-end Reinforcement Learning (RL). Each teacher policy is specialized for a distinct fault scenario, with tailored training methods, domain randomization, and specific reward functions. This allows for fine-tuning to produce effective gaits for each individual fault condition.
2. **Student Policy Training**:
 - **Architecture**: The student policy features a more complex encoder-decoder backbone.
 - **Guidance**: Instead of direct parameter copying or strict action imitation, teacher policies guide the student by generating "style rewards." This approach recognizes the structural differences between teacher and student networks and the student's need to generalize beyond individual teacher specializations.
 - **Robustness**: Regularization rewards are applied throughout student policy training to encourage stability and smooth gait transitions, addressing aspects not explicitly covered by individual teacher policies.

**B. Student Policy Backbone**

The student actor policy network is designed with an encoder-decoder architecture to facilitate fault identification and action generation without relying on an independent state estimator.

1. **Encoder**:
 - **Function**: Extracts information from historical proprioceptive observations to identify and classify fault cases. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are used for this temporal processing.
 - **Pre-training**: The encoder is pre-trained using supervised learning. A dataset, collected during the training of teacher policies, provides observations mapped to manually assigned fault case labels (a four-digit binary code for fault types). This pre-training helps the encoder learn fault recognition.
2. **Decoder**:
 - **Function**: Generates control actions based on the classified fault information and current observations.
 - **Pre-training**: The decoder is pre-trained independently using an RL method. During this phase, the quadruped is exposed to various fault scenarios with periodic switches between normal and faulty states. Ground truth fault case labels are concatenated with the original observation input to help the decoder learn fault-specific action generation.
3. **Joint Online Training**:
 - **Refinement**: The pre-trained encoder and decoder are further trained together online in simulation. This phase exposes the encoder to more dynamic conditions and additional fault cases, improving its accuracy.
 - **Process**: During joint training, the quadruped's state periodically switches between normal and faulty modes. The encoder is refined via supervised learning (using actual fault types as ground truth), while the decoder is optimized using RL.

**C. Input to the Policy Network**

The policy network relies exclusively on onboard proprioceptive sensors, eliminating the need for state estimations or external cameras during deployment.

- **Proprioceptive Observations**: `o_proprioceptive = [ω_base^⊤, g^⊤, p_DoF^⊤, ω_DoF^⊤]^⊤`, where `ω_base` is the base angular velocity, `g` is gravity, `p_DoF` is joint position, and `ω_DoF` is joint velocity.
- **Commands and Previous Actions**: The input also includes `c` (velocity command) and `a_previous` (action from the last step).
- **Combined Input**: `Input = [o_proprioceptive^⊤, c^⊤, a_previous^⊤]^⊤`.
- **Time-Sequence Data**: For the RNN-based encoder, a mini-batch of time-sequence data is used, resulting in an input shape of `(N, S, D)`, where `N` is batch size, `S` is input sequence length (set to 10), and `D` is feature dimension.

**D. Reward Design**

The reward system is crucial for guiding both teacher and student policy training.

1. **Teacher Policy Rewards**: Custom reward functions are designed for each fault case to promote smooth and feasible gaits. These include penalties for faulty leg ground contact, rewards for faulty leg lift-off, rewards for maintaining base link height, and rewards for healthy leg ground contact. The relative significance and numerical scales of these rewards are fine-tuned for each scenario (summarized in Figure 5 of the paper).
2. **Student Policy Rewards**: The student policy's total reward (`r`) is a weighted sum of style rewards and regularization rewards: `r = c1 * r_scale + c2 * r_direction + c3 * r_regularization`.
 - **Style Rewards**: These guide the student policy based on teacher policy actions.
 - `r_scale = e^(-||a - a*||)`: Encourages similarity in action amplitude (position/torque command) by minimizing Euclidean distance between student action (`a`) and teacher reference action (`a*`).
 - `r_direction = e^(a · a* / (||a|| ||a*||))`: Encourages alignment in action direction, accelerating initial learning for challenging gaits where `r_scale` might initially be low.
 - Reference actions (`a*`) are dynamically generated by the appropriate teacher policy when the fault case changes.
 - **Regularization Rewards**: These ensure robust performance across diverse terrains and smooth gait transitions, consolidating multiple gaits into a single policy. They address aspects not inherently covered by teacher policies, which are trained from static poses on flat terrains.

**E. Training Details and Environment**

- **Simulation Platform**: Isaac Gym is used for massively parallel RL training due to its GPU-based physics simulation capabilities.
- **RL Algorithm**: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is employed for both teacher and student policy training.
- **Hyperparameters**: Standard Actor-Critic PPO parameters are used (detailed in Appendix, Table I).
- **Domain Randomization**: Applied during training to enhance robustness. Parameters like terrain friction, base link mass, and motor output disturbances are randomized. Intermittent push forces are also applied.
- **Fault Switching**: During student policy training, fault cases are switched randomly every 300 simulation steps. The corresponding teacher policy is simultaneously switched to provide relevant style rewards, facilitating learning of balance under sudden faults and seamless transitions. The time step is not input to the policy, preventing interval-dependent learning.
- **Fault Simulation**: Torque-losing faults are simulated by setting the motor output torque of affected joints to zero in both simulation and real-world experiments.
- **Real-world Validation**: The learned policies are deployed and tested on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot. Teacher policies are also evaluated in Gazebo before deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides evidence for the effectiveness of the AcL framework through various experiments and ablation studies.

**A. Teacher Policy Performance**

- **Simulation (Gazebo)**: The individual teacher policies, each trained for a specific fault case, demonstrated natural and effective gaits in the Gazebo simulation environment (illustrated in Figure 3). This indicates that the case-specific reward functions and independent fine-tuning successfully produced viable locomotion patterns for various fault conditions.
- **Real-world Deployment**: The teacher policies were directly deployed on the physical Unitree Go2 quadruped robot without further adaptation, achieving reliable performance. This suggests good sim-to-real transfer capabilities for the individual fault-tolerant gaits.

**B. AcL (Student Policy) Performance**

The student policy, trained with the AcL framework, exhibited several capabilities on the real robot:

1. **Multi-Fault Tolerance**: The quadruped successfully maintained stable locomotion with up to four faulty joints across one or two legs simultaneously. This addresses a significant limitation of previous work, which primarily focused on single-joint faults.
2. **Autonomous Gait Transitions**:
 - **Detection and Adaptation**: When joint faults were manually introduced by setting motor torques to zero, the quadruped adapted rapidly and automatically transitioned to an appropriate limping gait without requiring external commands or high-level control signals.
 - **Bidirectional Transitions**: The robot could transition smoothly not only from a normal gait to a fault-tolerant gait but also back to a normal gait once the faults were resolved. Furthermore, it demonstrated the ability to transition adaptively among different limping gaits when the fault configuration changed.
 - **Style Reward Dynamics**: Figure 4 illustrates the dynamic changes in style rewards. When a fault occurred (e.g., at step 300), the style reward aligning with the normal gait teacher policy decreased sharply, while the style reward for the corresponding limping gait teacher policy increased rapidly. Conversely, when faults were removed (e.g., at step 600), the trend reversed. These dynamics quantitatively support the claim of prompt and autonomous gait transitions.
3. **Stability During Transitions**: Figure 6 depicts the quadruped's angular and linear velocities during a locomotion task with fault introduction and removal. The stable evolution of these metrics indicates that the robot maintained overall system stability throughout the bidirectional gait transitions, minimizing disruption.
4. **Robustness and Disturbance Rejection**:
 - **External Disturbances**: The robot demonstrated resistance to external kicks and pushes while performing limping gaits, recovering quickly from these disturbances (Figure 1(b)).
 - **Load Carrying**: The quadruped was able to walk and smoothly transition between gaits while carrying unsecured parcels on its back without dislodging them (Figure 1(c)), indicating a level of stability and control robustness.
5. **Uneven Terrain Navigation**: The robot was capable of completing locomotion tasks on uneven terrain, further highlighting the robustness achieved through domain randomization and regularization rewards.

**C. Ablation Studies**

Three ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of key components of the AcL framework:

1. **Training without Style Rewards**: When the student policy was trained without the style rewards (replacing them with the teacher policy reward functions), the resulting gaits were not similar to the teacher policies and were not feasible for transfer to real-world scenarios in simulation. This indicates that style rewards are essential for guiding the student to learn effective, teacher-like fault-tolerant gaits.
2. **Training without Regularization Rewards**: Without the regularization rewards, the quadruped was unable to learn locomotion tasks when fault joints were enabled in simulation. This suggests that regularization rewards are crucial for consolidating diverse gaits into a single policy, ensuring robustness, and enabling adaptive fault-tolerant locomotion, especially during transitions and across varying environments.
3. **Policy Network Backbone without Encoder**: When the encoder component of the policy network was removed, the quadruped failed to identify different fault cases. This led to a complete failure when faults were enabled, demonstrating that the encoder is critical for autonomous fault detection and classification, which is a prerequisite for selecting and executing appropriate fault-tolerant gaits.

In summary, the AcL framework demonstrated the ability to teach a quadruped to adapt to multiple joint faults, including up to four concurrent failures across two legs, with smooth, autonomous, and stable gait transitions on a real robot. The ablation studies confirmed the necessity of style rewards, regularization rewards, and the encoder-decoder architecture for achieving these capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AcL framework represents an advancement in the field of fault-tolerant locomotion for quadrupedal robots, with several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Fault Tolerance and Adaptability**: The work introduces a policy capable of sustaining locomotion with up to four faulty joints across one or two legs simultaneously, which is a substantial improvement over prior research primarily addressing single-joint faults. This significantly enhances the resilience and operational capabilities of quadruped robots in unpredictable environments where physical damage is a possibility.
2. **Seamless Gait Transitions and Recovery**: The ability to autonomously and smoothly transition between normal, different limping, and recovery gaits addresses a critical limitation of existing methods that often result in abrupt, destabilizing changes. This ensures continuous, stable operation, even during the onset or resolution of faults.
3. **Novel Teacher-Student Learning Framework**: The proposed teacher-student reinforcement learning framework, which leverages "style rewards" rather than strict imitation, offers a generalizable method for distilling multiple expert behaviors into a single policy. This approach allows the student policy to learn diverse gait patterns and to generalize to complex fault scenarios not explicitly covered by individual teacher policies, reducing the need for extensive, specialized training for every possible fault configuration. This framework has broader potential for developing other fast-adaptive locomotion policies across various tasks.
4. **Efficient Policy Architecture**: The introduction of an encoder-decoder architecture for the actor policy is significant. The encoder efficiently extracts fault-type information from proprioceptive data, facilitating autonomous fault detection, while the decoder generates appropriate actions. This architecture enables seamless gait transitions without external state estimators or manual command, contributing to a more autonomous and integrated control system.
5. **Modular and Extensible Design**: The modular nature of the AcL framework, particularly the independent training and fine-tuning of teacher policies, makes it highly flexible. It can be readily extended to incorporate new tasks or behaviors (e.g., stair-climbing, slope-climbing, or step-stone crossing) by simply integrating corresponding teacher policies. This suggests a pathway towards developing highly versatile robots that can adapt to both internal faults and environmental challenges.
6. **Agnostic to Teacher Policy Type**: The framework's ability to integrate various teacher policies, including model-based controllers, as long as they operate under position control, offers flexibility. This allows the student policy to potentially inherit desirable characteristics like symmetric and elegant motions from model-based approaches while retaining robust fault-tolerant capabilities.
7. **Real-World Validation**: Validation on a physical Unitree Go2 quadruped robot under various fault conditions and external disturbances demonstrates the practical applicability and robustness of the learned policy, bridging the gap between simulation and real-world deployment.

In summary, AcL contributes a robust and adaptive control solution for quadrupedal robots facing complex joint failures. Its novel learning framework and policy architecture offer a scalable, flexible, and generalizable approach for creating more resilient and autonomous robotic systems, with implications for a wide range of mobile robotics applications in challenging and unstructured environments.
