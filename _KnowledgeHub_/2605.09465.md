# High Precision Hydraulic Excavator Control for Heavy-Duty Grading

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich developed a control system for hydraulic excavators that enables sub-2 cm grading accuracy in heavy-duty in-soil conditions, utilizing the machine's full hydraulic potential. The system, featuring architecture-specific hydraulic compensation and a fast calibration procedure, generalizes across Load Sensing (LS) and Negative Flow Control (NFC) hydraulic machines.

## Problem

- Manual heavy-duty grading is labor-intensive, requires high operator skill, and is prone to inconsistencies, while autonomous systems need to address safety and efficiency.
- Existing commercial semi-automatic grading systems often lack precision in deep cuts, stall prematurely, and under-utilize the excavator's full hydraulic capabilities.
- Developing generalizable autonomous control for diverse hydraulic architectures (e.g., Load Sensing, Negative Flow Control) is challenging due to their distinct load-dependent responses and the need for extensive, machine-specific tuning.

## Method

- A hierarchical control system combines a hydraulic-aware low-level joint velocity controller with a higher-level ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== path-tracking layer.
- A novel multi-dimensional ==Lookup Table (LUT)== based hydraulic feedforward model explicitly accounts for load-dependent dynamics in both ==Load Sensing (LS)== and ==Negative Flow Control (NFC)== systems.
- A rapid, retrofittable, and non-invasive 20-minute calibration procedure for both hydraulic and MPC models eliminates the need for vendor-specific access or machine disassembly.

## Results

- Achieved an RMSE height error of **1.8 cm** on a 25 t NFC excavator and **1.4 cm** on an 11.5 t LS excavator, outperforming a commercial controller by **2.6 times** (**4.7 cm** RMSE).
- The proposed controller effectively utilized the machine's full hydraulic torque, stalling only at maximum function pressure, unlike commercial systems that stalled prematurely.
- Demonstrated superior surface quality with laser scans showing flatter, more consistent surfaces and earlier convergence to target height compared to commercial solutions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling and compensating for load-dependent hydraulic dynamics, particularly with a novel pressure-adaptive NFC model, is crucial for achieving high-precision control in heavy-duty grading.
> - A hierarchical control architecture allows for machine-agnostic path planning while integrating architecture-specific low-level hydraulic compensation, enhancing generalizability across different excavators.
> - A fast, non-invasive calibration procedure significantly improves the practicality and retrofittability of advanced control systems for diverse heavy machinery, enabling wider adoption.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The automation of heavy machinery in construction, particularly for earthmoving tasks like grading, is an ongoing area of research driven by the need for increased safety, consistency, efficiency, and to address persistent labor shortages. Traditionally, such tasks are performed manually by skilled operators, requiring substantial machine-specific experience due to the complex interaction between soil and machine, and the diverse responses of hydraulic architectures to operator inputs and external loads.

Prior work in this domain spans several areas:
- **General Earthmoving Automation:** Research prototypes and full-scale autonomous systems have demonstrated capabilities in free-form trenching, short-cycle loading, and higher-level task and motion planning for bulk earth removal. These systems often focus on productivity and cycle time, frequently relying on proprietary machine-control layers tailored to specific hydraulic architectures.
- **Hydraulic Motion Control:** Approaches range from physics-based modeling and constrained optimization to learning-based and reduced-order methods. While some methods learn inverse mappings for desired joint velocities or use data-driven model predictive control (MPC), they often assume a specific hydraulic topology, abstract hydraulics as simple joint actuation, or require extensive machine-specific data collection and tuning. Reinforcement learning (RL) has been applied to various excavation tasks, but has not consistently achieved centimeter-level accuracy in heavy-duty grading, especially when considering load-dependent hydraulics or transfer across different machines.
- **Grading and Surface Flattening:** Previous studies have explored Inverse Kinematics (IK) with PI hydraulic control, contour control based on geometric path-following, and advanced contouring-error formulations with disturbance observers. Some commercial systems, such as Trimble Earthworks and Cat Grade Control, offer GNSS- and total-station-based guidance with semi-automatic control. These commercial solutions generally perform well in light grading and finishing tasks, but often exhibit limitations in deeply loaded cuts, may stall prematurely before reaching maximum hydraulic pressure, and do not typically disclose details on their hydraulic control strategies or adaptation across different architectures.

The presented work addresses several identified gaps in this landscape:
- The absence of real in-soil centimeter-level grading solutions that explicitly combine soft hydraulic actuation, load-dependent compensation, short calibration procedures, and transferability across diverse hydraulic architectures (specifically Load Sensing (LS) and Negative Flow Control (NFC)).
- The need for a retrofittable, machine-agnostic controller that can adapt to different hydraulic systems without requiring vendor-specific access or extensive data collection, a common limitation of many model-based and data-driven approaches.
- The observed performance deficiencies of existing commercial systems in heavy-duty grading scenarios, particularly their tendency to stall prematurely and deviate from target surfaces under high load.

This research aims to bridge these gaps by developing a robust and adaptable control strategy that can perform high-precision, heavy-duty grading across a range of excavators and hydraulic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges associated with high-precision heavy-duty grading in earthworks. Manual operation of excavators for such tasks is labor-intensive, demands significant operator skill, and exposes personnel to hazardous environments. Furthermore, the construction industry faces persistent labor shortages, making automation a critical area for development.

The complex interplay between the excavator's hydraulic system, the soil, and varying operational loads presents significant hurdles for autonomous control. Different hydraulic architectures (e.g., Load Sensing (LS) and Negative Flow Control (NFC)) exhibit distinct responses to operator inputs and soil interaction forces, making generalized control solutions difficult to implement effectively. Current commercial systems often perform sub-optimally in deep cuts, exhibiting premature stalling and deviations from the target surface, thereby under-utilizing machine capabilities. State-of-the-art Inverse Kinematics (IK)-based methods also tend to lose accuracy due to unmodeled, load-dependent joint responses.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop an Autonomous Controller for High-Precision Grading:** Create a system capable of achieving centimeter-level surface accuracy in heavy-duty grading tasks (defined as cuts that fill the bucket in less than one stroke).
2. **Achieve Expert-Operator Speed:** Ensure that the autonomous system operates at speeds comparable to those of a skilled human operator.
3. **Ensure Generalizability Across Hydraulic Architectures:** Design a controller that is applicable to both Load Sensing (LS) and Negative Flow Control (NFC) excavators, addressing their distinct hydraulic behaviors.
4. **Implement Hydraulic-Aware Low-Level Control:** Integrate architecture-specific hydraulic compensation to accurately model and control soft, load-dependent actuation.
5. **Enable Rapid, Non-Invasive Calibration:** Develop a fast, one-time calibration procedure that does not require vendor-specific access or machine disassembly, making the controller retrofittable and practical for field deployment.
6. **Outperform Current Solutions:** Demonstrate superior performance in precision and machine utilization compared to commercial state-of-the-art semi-automatic grading systems.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to deliver an advanced, practical automation solution for earthmoving that enhances safety, consistency, efficiency, and resource utilization on construction sites.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed control system is structured into a hierarchical architecture comprising two main components: a hydraulic-aware low-level joint velocity controller and a higher-level path-tracking Model Predictive Control (MPC) layer. This separation allows for machine-agnostic path planning while accommodating the specific characteristics of different hydraulic systems.

**A. Controller Architecture**

1. **Joint Velocity Controller (Low-Level Loop):**
 - **Function:** This component tracks target joint rates by computing appropriate valve commands or pilot-stage currents. It translates target joint rates (rad/s) into cylinder speeds (m/s) using the closed-loop kinematics of each joint.
 - **Components:**
 - **Calibrated Hydraulic Model (Feed Forward - FF):** This is the core innovation, providing a hydraulic-architecture-specific FF element. It is implemented as a multi-dimensional Lookup Table (LUT) that maps desired cylinder velocities or function flows to valve commands, incorporating measurements such as cylinder back pressure and joint acceleration. Each joint is calibrated individually.
 - **Parallel PID Controller:** Compensates for unmodeled effects and disturbances, such as flow sharing, pump boost, or recuperation, acting in parallel with the FF element.
 - **Hydraulic Model for Load Sensing (LS):** For LS hydraulics, which exhibit load-aware and compensating behavior, the model is a straightforward 1:1 LUT mapping desired oil flow to valve command.
 - **Hydraulic Model for Negative Flow Control (NFC):** NFC hydraulics display load-dependent motion characteristics. The proposed model involves:
 - **Inertia Compensation:** To stabilize transients and prevent positive feedback loops, inertia forces are estimated at the cylinder level and compensated for, as cylinder forces derived from pressure readings include both external and internal system forces (gravity, friction, inertia).
 - **Model Derivation:** An "artificial cylinder back pressure" is computed from inertia-compensated cylinder forces. The pump's pressure loop setpoint is modeled as a LUT with respect to the Directional Control Valve (DCV) command. The relationship between valve command and the hydraulic resistance of the variable in-line orifice in the DCV (R3) is calibrated using a parameterized equation. These models are then inverted to form a 2D LUT that maps desired flow and current function pseudo back pressure to a valve command.

2. **Path Tracking Controller (MPC Layer):**
 - **Function:** This controller uses the machine kinematics and the transient behavior of the plant (which includes the joint velocity controller and the excavator itself) to execute a grading pass along a design surface.
 - **Prediction Model:** Each joint is approximated as a second-order system in velocity with a specified gain, damping ratio, natural frequency, and an added dead-time delay. Dead time is managed using a discrete input buffer.
 - **Task-Space Quantities:** The MPC cost function is defined in task space. Excavator forward kinematics are used to predict the end-effector (EE) position, linear velocity, angular velocity, and bucket pitch angle.
 - **Optimization Problem:** Formulated as a discrete-time nonlinear MPC in joint space with a task-space tracking objective. It minimizes deviations from desired forward velocity, near-zero vertical EE velocity, near-zero EE pitch rate, the target EE height, and the desired blade pitch. An increment penalty on control inputs promotes smooth joint velocity commands.
 - **Constraints:** Actuator and joint limits are enforced.
 - **Real-time Implementation:** The MPC is solved at 10 Hz with a horizon length of 20, with only the first control input executed in each tick.

**B. Calibration Procedure**

The calibration process is designed to be retrofittable, take approximately 20 minutes, and requires no disassembly or vendor-specific access. Each joint is calibrated sequentially.

1. **Joint Velocity Controller Calibration:**
 - **Load Sensing (LS):** For LS machines, a dataset is collected by spanning the operational range of valve commands with small increments. The steady-state cylinder velocity is measured for each command, and linear interpolation is used to generate the 1D LUT mapping valve command to cylinder velocity.
 - **Negative Flow Control (NFC):** This involves a novel procedure:
 - Inertia-compensated cylinder forces are calculated from pressure readings.
 - The pump's pressure loop setpoint (`P_p(x)`) is identified by stalling the function and measuring function pressure for different valve commands.
 - The hydraulic resistance of the variable in-line orifice (`R(x)`) is characterized. For stick and bucket, this involves commanding a constant valve opening and progressively stalling the bucket in the ground to generate data trajectories of increasing load and decreasing flow. For the boom, different joint loads (e.g., empty, curled, full bucket) are tested at the same command.
 - A parameterized orifice model is fitted to the experimentally determined resistance values using a logarithmic cost function.
 - The identified pump and orifice models are then inverted to create a 2D LUT that maps desired flow and current function pseudo back pressure to a valve command.
2. **MPC Model Calibration:**
 - The transient response of the entire plant (FF element, PID controller, and hydraulic system) is identified from step responses. This involves calibrating the dead-time, scalar gain, damping, and natural frequency for each joint model used in the MPC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The presented methodology was evaluated on two hydraulic excavators: an 11.5 t Load Sensing (LS) Menzi Muck M445 and a 25 t Negative Flow Control (NFC) CASE250. The performance was benchmarked against a commercial state-of-the-art semi-automatic grading controller.

**A. Hydraulic Feedforward (FF) Validation**

- **LS M445:** The calibrated LUT for the LS system demonstrated a joint-velocity tracking accuracy of approximately 2%, confirming the effectiveness of the model and its calibration for stiff hydraulic responses.
- **NFC CASE250:** Experiments showed that the proposed hydraulic model successfully compensated for load-dependent joint dynamics. For instance, a Boom lifting command of 0.05 rad s⁻¹ was accurately tracked across various payloads (empty, curled, full bucket), with the FF term adapting its output to changing gravity loading. This validation was conducted with the PID controller disabled, highlighting the inherent load-compensation capability of the FF model.

**B. Grading Performance**

The full control stack, encompassing both the joint velocity control and MPC path tracking, was tested in in-soil grading scenarios ranging from light surface grading to deep cuts (2 cm to 40 cm depth) that filled the bucket in less than one stroke. A blade velocity of approximately 0.5 m/s was used for all tests.

1. **Path Tracking:**
 - **Commercial Controller (CASE250):** Over 10 grading passes in compacted soil, the commercial controller achieved an RMSE height error of 4.7 cm (within 9m of the machine), with a maximum path deviation of 17 cm. It frequently stalled prematurely, around 5m into the pass, with stick pressures significantly below the maximum function pressure (350 bar). In hard soil, it often failed to reach the target depth, and in light soil, it showed a noticeable initial overshoot. The shovel was observed to be pushed upwards near the end of travel in some hard soil conditions.
 - **Our Controller (CASE250):** On the same machine and under identical conditions, the proposed controller achieved an RMSE height error of 1.8 cm, with a maximum path deviation of 5 cm. This represents a 2.6-fold improvement in precision over the commercial solution. The controller consistently maintained high accuracy regardless of soil conditions, reaching the target height reliably after approximately one meter of approach. It exhibited no initial overshoot in light grading passes and only stalled when the stick pressure reached the machine's maximum function pressure, indicating efficient utilization of the machinery's full hydraulic torque.
 - **Our Controller (M445):** On the LS Menzi Muck M445, the controller achieved an RMSE height error of 1.4 cm and a maximum path deviation of 4 cm. It demonstrated consistent precision across varying cutting depths and showed no significant initial overshoot, stalling only at maximum function pressure.

2. **Surface Quality (CASE250):**
 - A separate test evaluated the final surface finish on compacted sand with a cutting depth of approximately 25 cm, covering an area four shovel widths wide with 20% overlap across five passes. Surface quality was assessed using a high-precision laser scanner.
 - **Commercial Controller:** The laser scan revealed that the commercial controller did not consistently reach the target height until the midpoint of the pass, exhibiting large deviations and surface oscillations exceeding 10 cm. This necessitated additional passes to achieve a comparable surface quality.
 - **Our Controller:** The proposed controller reached the target surface earlier and created a flatter, more consistent surface at the target height, with minimal oscillations.

**C. Discussion**

The experimental results indicate that the calibrated hydraulic FF terms effectively enable accurate joint velocity tracking across various payloads for both LS and NFC machines. The comprehensive grading experiments demonstrated that the proposed controller consistently reached the target surface within approximately one meter and maintained low deviation in compacted material, even with cutting depths up to 40 cm. The system significantly outperformed the commercial baseline controller in terms of precision (RMSE 1.8 cm vs. 4.7 cm) and optimized machine usage by leveraging the maximum function pressure, preventing premature stalling. Laser scans confirmed the superior surface quality, characterized by flatter surfaces, reduced oscillations, and earlier convergence to the target height. The retrofittable, machine-agnostic calibration process, requiring only about 20 minutes for identification and tuning, further highlights the practicality and versatility of the developed control framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of autonomous heavy-duty grading for hydraulic excavators, offering several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Precision and Efficiency:** The controller achieves sub-2 cm grading accuracy in challenging, heavy-duty in-soil conditions, demonstrably outperforming commercial solutions by a factor of 2.6 in precision. This level of accuracy, coupled with the ability to utilize the machine's full hydraulic torque envelope (stalling only at maximum function pressure), leads to more efficient material removal and higher quality finished surfaces. This can reduce the number of passes required, thereby lowering operational costs and increasing overall project efficiency.
- **Broad Applicability and Generalizability:** A key contribution is the development of a hydraulics-aware control stack that is retrofittable and machine-agnostic, capable of adapting to both Load Sensing (LS) and Negative Flow Control (NFC) hydraulic architectures. This addresses a significant limitation of many previous approaches that often assumed specific hydraulic topologies or required extensive, machine-specific tuning.
- **Practical Field Deployment:** The novel pressure-adaptive NFC model and the fast, non-invasive calibration routine (approximately 20 minutes) eliminate the need for vendor-specific access or machine disassembly. This ease of deployment makes the technology highly practical for integration into existing fleets of excavators, accelerating the adoption of advanced automation in construction.
- **Improved Safety and Reduced Labor Dependence:** By automating a demanding and potentially hazardous task, the system can reduce operator exposure to risks and alleviate the physical and cognitive loads associated with manual operation. In the context of persistent labor shortages in the construction industry, intelligent assistive systems and full automation offer a viable solution for maintaining productivity and operational continuity.
- **Foundation for Future Automation:** The control framework, with its explicit transient and dead-time modeling, provides a robust basis for further automation developments. This includes potential extensions to track arbitrary shapes, implement impedance-style control for proprioceptive compaction, or facilitate the redistribution of material piles, thereby expanding the range of tasks autonomous excavators can perform with high precision.

**Limitations:**
The study notes that the applicability of the method to Positive Flow Control (PFC) machines has not been evaluated due to machine availability. Additionally, while the current approach effectively compensates for cross-coupling effects at the targeted speeds and precision, the hydraulic model could be further extended to explicitly capture these effects for even faster motions or higher precision requirements.

In conclusion, this work represents a substantial step towards practical, high-precision automation in earthmoving. The demonstrated centimeter-level accuracy, cross-architecture generalizability, and efficient calibration process position this technology to significantly impact construction practices by reducing costs, improving quality, and enhancing safety.
