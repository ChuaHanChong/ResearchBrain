# SPARR: Simulation-based Policies with Asymmetric Real-world Residuals for Assembly

> [!summary] Summary
> The SPARR framework bridges the sim-to-real gap in robotic assembly by combining a simulation-trained base policy with a vision-conditioned real-world residual policy, achieving 95-100% success rates on diverse tasks without requiring human supervision during real-world adaptation. This hybrid approach significantly improves real-world performance and robustness against pose estimation errors and object displacements.

## Problem

- Simulation-trained robotic assembly policies often suffer significant performance degradation (sim-to-real gap) when deployed in physical environments, leading to moderate success rates below industrial requirements.
- Current real-world reinforcement learning (RL) methods for contact-rich assembly tasks are heavily reliant on human expertise for demonstrations, supervision, and interventions, limiting their scalability and autonomy.
- Robotic assembly solutions need robustness to real-world variations such as part pose uncertainties, manufacturing tolerances, and sensor noise, which existing approaches often struggle to handle.

## Method

- Pre-trains a state-based base policy in simulation using PPO and dense imitation rewards, employing domain randomization to establish a strong initial behavioral prior.
- Deploys the base policy in the real world to autonomously generate demonstrations for a residual policy by injecting Gaussian noise, eliminating the need for human expert demonstrations.
- Learns a vision-conditioned residual policy directly in the real world using the RLPD algorithm, combining sparse rewards with a dynamically updated demonstration buffer to correct base policy actions and adapt to real-world discrepancies and visual cues.

## Results

- SPARR achieved 95-100% success rates on 10 diverse assembly tasks from the AutoMate dataset, demonstrating a 38.4% relative improvement over AutoMate and performance comparable to human-supervised HIL-SERL, but without any human intervention.
- The framework showed enhanced robustness to state noise and socket displacements, outperforming the base policy by 38.6% and state-based residual policies by 20.8% on average across varying physical socket positions.
- SPARR generalized to three unseen NIST assembly tasks, achieving up to 100% success for rectangular peg insertion by correcting rotational mismatches and showing a 74.5% relative improvement in success rate and 36.5% reduction in cycle time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hybrid approach, combining a strong simulation-trained base policy with a real-world vision-conditioned residual policy, can effectively bridge the sim-to-real gap and achieve industrial-grade assembly success rates without human supervision.
> - Visual observations in the real-world residual policy are crucial for enhancing robustness to real-world variations like pose estimation errors and physical displacements, providing vital information not captured by state-based simulation.
> - Autonomous demonstration generation by the base policy and the use of a dynamic demonstration buffer significantly improve data efficiency and learning performance for sparse reward real-world adaptation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic assembly represents a persistent challenge within robotics due to its inherent requirements for precision and contact-rich manipulation. Existing research in this area generally explores two primary paradigms: simulation-based learning with sim-to-real transfer and direct real-world reinforcement learning (RL).

Simulation-based learning methods have facilitated the development of robust assembly policies, achieving success rates up to 80% on complex benchmarks. However, a significant limitation is the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation exhibit performance degradation when deployed in physical environments. This gap arises from discrepancies in physical parameters, state estimation noise, camera calibration errors, and visual domain shifts. Domain randomization and adaptation techniques have been developed to mitigate this, but often require extensive computational resources, high-fidelity rendering, or significant real-world fine-tuning. Some approaches incorporate residual policy learning, where a base controller is augmented with an RL-learned residual, but these often rely on pre-trained simulation policies or human demonstrations.

Conversely, real-world RL approaches bypass the sim-to-real gap by optimizing policies directly in the physical environment. These methods have shown promise in contact-rich assembly, particularly with visual observations and sparse rewards. However, a notable drawback of current real-world RL techniques is their heavy reliance on human expertise for demonstration collection, active supervision, and intervention during training. This dependence limits scalability and autonomy in industrial deployment settings, where success rates of 95% or higher are typically required without substantial human involvement.

This work addresses the limitations of both paradigms by proposing a hybrid approach. It leverages the efficiency and structured priors offered by simulation-based training while incorporating a real-world adaptation mechanism that operates without human supervision. By combining a simulation-trained base policy with a real-world learned residual policy, this research aims to bridge the sim-to-real gap more effectively and achieve higher success rates and autonomy than existing methods in robotic assembly tasks. This positions the work as an advancement in developing more practical and scalable solutions for industrial robotic assembly.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a hybrid robotic assembly framework, termed SPARR (Simulation-based Policies with Asymmetric Real-world Residuals), that can effectively and efficiently adapt simulation-trained policies to real-world environments to achieve high success rates without human supervision.

This objective is motivated by several identified challenges in current robotic assembly practices:

- **Insufficient Real-world Performance of Simulation-trained Policies:** While simulation-based policies have shown strong performance (up to 80% success) in simulated environments and moderate zero-shot transfer, their performance frequently degrades in the real world due to the sim-to-real gap. Factors contributing to this degradation include mismatches in physical parameters, state estimation inaccuracies, and visual domain shifts. The goal is to elevate these moderate success rates to industrial-grade levels (e.g., 95% or higher).
- **High Human Dependency in Real-world Reinforcement Learning:** Current real-world RL methods for contact-rich tasks, despite addressing the sim-to-real gap, often necessitate substantial human involvement. This includes providing expert demonstrations for bootstrapping, active supervision, and frequent interventions during the learning process. Such reliance on human expertise significantly limits the scalability and autonomy of these methods for broad industrial deployment.
- **Need for Robustness to Real-world Variations:** Robotic assembly in manufacturing environments requires policies that are robust to variations in part poses, manufacturing tolerances, and sensor noise (e.g., pose estimation errors). Existing methods may struggle with these unmodeled variations, leading to brittleness.
- **Efficient Adaptation:** The financial and operational costs associated with real-world robot training emphasize the need for data-efficient learning strategies. Developing a system that can adapt quickly and autonomously to real-world conditions with limited interaction time is a key motivation.

To address these motivations, SPARR aims to:
- Pre-train a base policy in simulation using low-level state observations and dense rewards, leveraging the efficiency of simulation for initial behavior acquisition and structured priors.
- Develop a residual policy learned directly in the real world, utilizing visual observations and sparse rewards, to specifically compensate for dynamic discrepancies, sensor noise, and visual or environmental differences that are not captured in simulation.
- Enable autonomous adaptation to real-world environments by using successful trajectories from the deployed base policy as demonstrations for the residual policy, thereby eliminating the need for human expertise or interventions.
- Demonstrate robustness to typical real-world challenges such as pose estimation errors and socket displacements.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to advance robotic assembly capabilities towards more practical, autonomous, and high-performance deployment in industrial settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SPARR framework is a hybrid approach combining a simulation-trained base policy with a real-world learned residual policy for robotic assembly tasks. The overall process is structured into three main phases: pre-training the base policy in simulation, collecting demonstrations with the base policy in the real world, and learning the residual policy in the real world.

**A. Problem Description**
The task is formulated as a standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) where a Franka robot inserts a plug into a designated socket. The objective is to maximize accumulated discounted rewards. State observations can be low-dimensional (proprioceptive signals, part poses) or visual. Actions are defined as incremental end-effector pose targets. The approach focuses on boosting success rates of simulation-trained policies to near-perfect levels on specific plug-socket pairs in the real world.

**B. Pre-training in Simulation (Base Policy)**
1. **Policy Type:** A base policy ($\pi_b$) is pre-trained in simulation. This policy is state-based, using low-dimensional observations.
2. **Observations:** The state observation ($s_b_t$) includes robot joint angles, current end-effector pose, goal end-effector pose, and their differences. This approach avoids visual inputs at this stage to enhance computational efficiency and robustness to visual variations upon transfer.
3. **Actions:** Actions ($a_t$) are incremental pose targets, executed by a Cartesian impedance controller.
4. **Training Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is employed.
5. **Rewards:** Imitation rewards derived from disassembly trajectories are used, providing dense feedback.
6. **Robustness Enhancement:** To improve robustness, robot joint configurations, socket poses, and plug poses are randomized at the start of each episode during simulation training.
7. **Performance:** This phase typically yields a high success rate (e.g., >99%) in simulation.

**C. Policy Formulation in the Real World**
1. **Deployment of Base Policy:**
 - The pre-trained base policy ($\pi_b$) is deployed in the real world.
 - **Goal Pose Estimation:** Socket poses are obtained using a perception pipeline involving Grounding DINO, SAM2, and FoundationPose. The end-effector goal pose is set as the estimated socket pose with a z-axis offset, assuming the plug is consistently gripped.
 - **State-estimation Noise Simulation:** To model real-world inaccuracies, a uniform noise of up to 1mm in x and y axes is added to ground-truth goal poses at the start of each episode.
 - **Initial Position:** The end-effector holding the plug is initialized 2cm above the noisy goal pose.
 - **Sim-to-Real Gap Impact:** Due to dynamics mismatches and state-input inaccuracies, the zero-shot success rate of $\pi_b$ in the real world is moderate.
2. **Design of Residual Policy ($\pi_r$):**
 - **Input:** The residual policy takes real-world observations ($s_r_t$), which are designed to be robust to state-estimation errors. These include low-dimensional proprioceptive states (end-effector pose, linear/angular velocities, force, torques) and visual observations (two RGB images from wrist-mounted cameras). Object pose is excluded from these inputs.
 - **Visual Information Utility:** Visual inputs provide complementary information not present in simulated CAD models, such as object asymmetries, textures, manufacturing tolerances, and fine-grained contact features, which are crucial for precise assembly (e.g., one-way insertion for USB sockets).
 - **Output:** $\pi_r$ outputs residual actions ($a_r_t$) as incremental pose targets.
 - **Combined Action:** The residual action $a_r_t$ is added to the base action $a_b_t$ from $\pi_b$, resulting in the combined action $a_t = a_b_t + a_r_t$.
 - **Rewards:** A sparse success reward is used: $r_t = 1$ if the end-effector is within 3mm translation and 5$^\circ$ rotation of the goal; otherwise, $r_t = 0$. Dense rewards are avoided due to their potential to be misleading in contact-rich real-world scenarios.

**D. Demonstration Collection in the Real World**
1. **Autonomous Demonstration Generation:** Instead of human experts, the deployed simulation policy ($\pi_b$) is used to collect demonstrations for the residual policy.
2. **Pseudo-label Generation:** Residual actions ($a_r_t$) are generated as Gaussian noise sampled from $N(0, \sigma_t)$, where $\sigma_t$ is the standard deviation of actions output by $\pi_b$. The base action $a_b_t$ is the mean $\mu_t$ from $\pi_b$'s output distribution $N(\mu_t, \sigma_t)$.
3. **Trajectory Recording:** Trajectories are recorded as sequences of transitions $\{(s_r_0, a_b_0, a_r_0, r_0), \dots \}$.
4. **Demonstration Buffer Initialization:** Successful trajectories obtained from zero-shot deployment of $\pi_b$ with injected residual actions are stored in a demonstration buffer to bootstrap residual policy training.

**E. Residual Policy Learning in the Real World**
1. **Policy Learning:** The residual policy $\pi_r$ learns to predict residual actions $a_r_t \sim \pi_r(\cdot|s_r_t, a_b_t)$. It is conditioned on both real-world observations ($s_r_t$) and the base action ($a_b_t$), which provides contextual information for correction.
2. **RL Algorithm:** The RLPD algorithm is adopted for its sample efficiency and ability to leverage prior demonstration data, which is crucial given sparse rewards and limited real-world interaction data.
3. **Buffer Management:**
 - Transitions for policy updates are sampled equally from a demonstration buffer and an online RL buffer.
 - The demonstration buffer is initialized with offline successful trajectories.
 - **Dynamic Demonstration Buffer Update:** During residual policy learning, high-quality trajectories that achieve task success faster than the median of existing demonstrations are added to the demonstration buffer. This mechanism allows the policy to exploit valuable experiences gathered during training, aiming to reduce cycle time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate SPARR's effectiveness in real-world adaptation, robustness to state noise, and generalization to unseen tasks, all while requiring no human supervision during real-world learning.

**A. Effective Real-world Adaptation**
- **Tasks:** Evaluation was performed on 10 diverse two-part assembly tasks from the AutoMate dataset, selected for their high simulation success (>99%).
- **Baselines:** SPARR was compared against:
 - **SERL:** A real-world RL approach, bootstrapped with 20 successful trajectories from the simulation policy.
 - **AutoMate:** A state-of-the-art zero-shot sim-to-real transfer method.
 - **HIL-SERL:** An oracle baseline representing the SOTA real-world RL with human demonstrations, frequent interventions, and supervision.
- **Training Budget:** All methods were constrained to 0.5 hours of real-world training per task, emphasizing data efficiency.
- **Results:**
 - **SERL** showed poor performance due to sparse rewards and lack of human intervention, struggling to learn effective behaviors.
 - **AutoMate** achieved moderate success rates, confirming the sim-to-real gap.
 - **SPARR (Ours)** achieved success rates between 95% and 100%, demonstrating a relative improvement of 38.4% in success rate and a 29.7% reduction in cycle time compared to AutoMate.
 - SPARR's performance was comparable to HIL-SERL, despite requiring no human supervision or intervention.
 - **HIL-SERL** exhibited the lowest cycle times, attributed to the quality of human demonstrations and the use of action smoothing and policy-level action integrators in sim-to-real methods which can impact execution speed.

**B. Robustness to State Noise**
- **Setup:** Experiments were conducted on AutoMate task 00731. Socket poses were physically displaced by 2cm from the training position (up, down, left, right), and a uniform 1mm noise was added to the ground-truth goal pose to simulate pose-estimation error.
- **Comparison:** SPARR (image-based residual policy) was compared with the base policy alone and a variant using a state-based residual policy.
- **Results:**
 - The **base policy** showed sensitivity to pose variations, with success rates ranging from 0.5 to 0.8 depending on the displacement.
 - The **state-based residual policy** also exhibited sensitivity to errors in estimated goal pose and out-of-distribution socket positions.
 - **SPARR (Ours) with the image-based residual policy** demonstrated greater robustness to socket pose changes, achieving success rates between 0.8 and 1.0. Its performance degradation was minimal even under extreme pose shifts.
 - Overall, SPARR achieved a 38.6% improvement over the base policy and outperformed the state-based residual policy by 20.8% averaged across varying physical socket positions.

**C. Generalization to Unseen Tasks**
- **Tasks:** SPARR's generalizability was evaluated on three NIST assembly tasks (4mm round peg, 12mm rectangular peg, small gear insertion) not included in the AutoMate pre-training dataset. Base policies were selected from relevant AutoMate tasks based on object size and behavior patterns.
- **Results:**
 - The **base policy** alone achieved reasonable zero-shot success rates (0.4–0.7), despite differences in dynamics and visual observations, demonstrating its functional prior.
 - For **round peg insertion**, SPARR achieved an 80% success rate, improving performance despite challenges with detecting the tiny hole on a reflective surface.
 - For **rectangular peg insertion**, where an initial yaw noise of up to 3 degrees was added, the base policy struggled due to rotational mismatch. SPARR's residual policy learned the necessary rotation, achieving 100% success. An action visualization demonstrated the residual policy correcting the yaw rotation.
 - For **small gear insertion**, with much tighter clearances (0.005–0.014mm) than its AutoMate counterpart, SPARR's residual policy learned to correct insertion angle and applied actions, leading to successful insertions.
 - Across these NIST tasks, SPARR demonstrated a relative improvement of 74.5% in success rate and 36.5% in cycle time.

**D. Ablation Study**
- **Effect of Demo Buffer Update:** Updating the demonstration buffer with high-quality trajectories (faster than median) during RLPD training was shown to improve success rates and reduce cycle times. For instance, on Task 01041, SPARR without demo update achieved 0.95 success rate and 8.1s cycle time, while SPARR (with update) achieved 1.0 success rate and 6.81s cycle time.
- **Effect of Base Action as Input to Residual Policy:** Including the base action as input to the residual policy significantly enhanced performance. Without this input, the residual policy still improved zero-shot deployment but to a lesser extent. For example, on Task 00015, SPARR without base action input achieved 0.8 success rate and 6.12s cycle time, while SPARR (with input) achieved 1.0 success rate and 3.51s cycle time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPARR framework demonstrates a method for robust and autonomous robotic assembly in real-world environments, addressing key limitations of existing approaches. Its significance and potential impact stem from several aspects:

- **Autonomous Adaptation without Human Supervision:** A primary contribution is the ability to achieve high success rates (95–100%) on real-world assembly tasks with no human involvement in the learning process. This distinguishes SPARR from state-of-the-art real-world RL methods that heavily rely on expert demonstrations and interventions, thereby significantly improving scalability and reducing deployment costs in industrial settings.
- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer:** SPARR effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap by leveraging a strong simulation-trained base policy for initial behavior and a real-world vision-conditioned residual policy for fine-grained adaptation. This hybrid design allows for efficient training in simulation while providing the necessary real-world correction for dynamics mismatches, sensor noise, and visual discrepancies.
- **Robustness to Real-world Variations:** The vision-based residual policy enhances robustness to practical challenges such as pose estimation errors and physical displacements of the socket. This capability is critical for manufacturing environments lacking precise fixturing, allowing for more flexible automation.
- **Generalization to Unseen Tasks:** The framework exhibits the ability to adapt base policies trained on known tasks to conceptually similar, but previously unseen, real-world assembly tasks. This generalization potential, even under varying dynamics and visual observations, reduces the need for entirely new policy training for every new assembly.
- **Data Efficiency:** By leveraging existing simulation policies for demonstration collection and employing an algorithm like RLPD with a dynamically updated demonstration buffer, SPARR achieves effective learning within limited real-robot interaction time (e.g., 0.5 hours per task). This minimizes the expensive and time-consuming aspects of real-world robot training.

**Potential Impact:**
SPARR's capabilities could lead to more widespread adoption of robots in complex, high-precision assembly lines, particularly in high-mix, low-volume manufacturing where manual methods are currently prevalent or current robotic solutions are too brittle. The reduction in reliance on human expertise for robot learning can streamline deployment processes, lower operational costs, and accelerate the development of autonomous manufacturing systems.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several assumptions and areas for future research:
- **Pre-grasped Plugs:** SPARR currently assumes the plug is rigidly pre-grasped. Future work aims to investigate the full grasp-to-insertion pipeline and improve robustness to grasp perturbations.
- **Dependence on Base Policy Performance:** The framework relies on the base policy achieving at least a moderate zero-shot success rate in the real world. If the base policy fails catastrophically, the residual policy alone may be insufficient.
- **Automated Reward Detection:** SPARR assumes access to automated reward or success detection during real-world deployment. Future research could explore more reliable success classifiers, potentially integrating multimodal data (visual, force, audio).
- **Expressiveness and Task Diversity:** Future work could explore more expressive residual policies and extend the framework to a broader range of complex manipulation tasks beyond insertion-style assemblies.
