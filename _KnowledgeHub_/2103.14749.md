# Pervasive Label Errors in Test Sets Destabilize Machine Learning Benchmarks

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper reveals pervasive label errors in the test sets of 10 widely-used machine learning benchmarks, demonstrating that while overall model rankings might appear stable, evaluating on corrected data or in slightly noisier conditions can significantly alter model performance comparisons. The work identifies that high-capacity models often "overfit" to these test set label errors, leading to misleading benchmark results and providing open-source tools to address these data quality issues.

## Problem

- Machine learning relies heavily on large labeled datasets, but inaccuracies in ground-truth labels (noisy labels) are a known challenge.
- Prior research has primarily focused on label errors within training sets, often assuming test sets are perfectly clean 'gold standards' for model evaluation.
- This assumption about test set purity could undermine the reliability of performance metrics, model selection, and the accurate measurement of scientific progress in machine learning.

## Method

- Systematically identified and quantified ==naturally occurring label errors== in the test sets of 10 highly-cited machine learning benchmarks across image, text, and audio modalities.
- Employed the **Confident Learning (CL)** framework for ==algorithmic error identification==, followed by human validation using ==crowdsourcing (Mechanical Turk)== and expert review.
- Analyzed the impact of identified errors on ==benchmark stability== by comparing model performance on original versus corrected labels and by simulating increased test set noise prevalence.

## Results

- Confirmed pervasive label errors across all 10 benchmarks, with ImageNet's validation set having at least **6% errors** (**2,916 out of 50,000**) and QuickDraw over **10%**.
- On ImageNet's correctable set, NASNet-large dropped from **1st to 29th** rank when evaluated on corrected labels, while ResNet-18 improved from **34th to 1st**, demonstrating overfitting to errors by high-capacity models.
- A mere **6% increase** in the prevalence of mislabeled test examples caused ResNet-18 to outperform ResNet-50 in corrected accuracy on ImageNet, indicating benchmark instability.
- Open-sourced the Confident Learning framework (Cleanlab) and provided corrected test sets, enabling the community to audit and clean their own datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Label errors are pervasive, with an average of at least 3.3% across surveyed datasets, and specific instances like ImageNet validation set showing at least 6% confirmed errors, potentially closer to 20% by expert estimates.
> - Higher-capacity models can 'overfit' to systematic label errors in test sets, performing better on original (erroneous) benchmarks but worse when evaluated against truly corrected labels.
> - Even a slight increase in test set noise prevalence can drastically destabilize model rankings, causing lower-capacity models to outperform higher-capacity models in terms of true, corrected accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of machine learning heavily relies on large, labeled datasets for supervised learning. Consequently, the issue of "noisy labels" – inaccuracies in the ground-truth annotations – has received considerable attention. Prior research on learning with noisy labels has primarily focused on errors within **training sets**, with many studies exploring techniques to make models robust to such noise or even concluding that deep learning models are inherently robust. The approaches often involve adding synthetic noise to clean data to evaluate robustness, which may not fully capture real-world noise characteristics.

This paper critically challenges a widespread, yet often unstated, assumption in the machine learning community: that **test sets** are largely free from label errors and can serve as reliable "gold standards" for benchmarking model performance. While some individual studies have pointed out label issues in specific datasets like ImageNet, a systematic, large-scale characterization of errors across a diverse range of popular benchmark test sets has been largely absent. The authors explicitly call this assumption a "fallacy," arguing that test sets do contain errors, and these errors can have profound consequences different from those of training set noise.

By focusing on *naturally-occurring* label errors in *test sets* of 10 commonly used benchmarks (spanning computer vision, natural language processing, and audio), this work fills a crucial gap. It moves beyond synthetic noise experiments to analyze real-world data quality issues that directly impact how researchers measure progress and how practitioners select models for deployment. The paper's contribution is not in proposing a novel method for handling label noise during training, but rather in rigorously quantifying the prevalence of test set errors and elucidating their practical implications for benchmark stability and model selection, particularly the potentially misleading conclusions about model capacity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to comprehensively investigate the prevalence and implications of label errors within the test sets of widely-used machine learning benchmark datasets.

The **key objectives** can be broken down as follows:
1. **Discovery and Characterization:** To systematically identify and quantify naturally-occurring label errors in the test sets of 10 standard machine learning benchmarks across different modalities (image, text, audio). This includes estimating the error prevalence and categorizing the types of errors found.
2. **Impact on Benchmarks:** To analyze how these pervasive label errors affect benchmark results, particularly concerning model selection and the stability of model rankings.
3. **Provision of Resources:** To provide open-source tools and corrected datasets to enable other researchers and practitioners to identify, correct, and mitigate the effects of label errors in their own work.

The **motivation** for this study stems from several critical observations and unaddressed concerns within the machine learning community:
- **Reliance on Labeled Data:** The success of supervised machine learning across various domains is heavily dependent on large, high-quality labeled datasets.
- **Error-Prone Data Collection:** The processes used to construct these datasets, often involving automatic labeling or crowdsourcing, are inherently susceptible to errors, even with correction controls.
- **Blind Spot in Test Sets:** While training set label noise is acknowledged, test set labels are frequently (and incorrectly) assumed to be perfectly correct, leading to a false sense of reliability in performance metrics.
- **Undermining Progress Measurement:** If test sets contain significant errors, they can undermine the very framework by which state-of-the-art models are evaluated and scientific progress is measured, leading to potentially flawed conclusions about which models are truly superior.
- **Real-World Applicability:** Practitioners working with real-world datasets, which are often noisier than carefully curated benchmarks, risk making incorrect model deployment decisions if standard test accuracies are misleading. The paper aims to highlight the potential for lower-capacity models to be more practically useful in such noisy environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multi-stage approach combining algorithmic identification with human validation and subsequent analysis to achieve their objectives.

#### 3.1. Dataset Selection

The study examines 10 highly-cited, open-source datasets created within the last 20 years, chosen to represent diverse modalities:
- **Image:** MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Caltech-256, ImageNet, QuickDraw.
- **Text:** 20news, IMDB, Amazon Reviews.
- **Audio:** AudioSet.
For datasets without an explicit test set (e.g., QuickDraw, Amazon Reviews, Caltech-256), the entire dataset is analyzed to ensure comprehensive coverage.

#### 3.2. Algorithmic Label Error Identification (Confident Learning - CL)

The core of error identification relies on the **Confident Learning (CL) framework**, which is model-agnostic and robust to class imbalance.
- **Noise Characterization:** CL estimates the joint distribution $Q_{\tilde{y},y^*}$, representing the probability of observing a noisy label $\tilde{y}$ given a true label $y^*$. This framework assumes class-conditional noise (e.g., a "tiger" image is more likely to be mislabeled "cheetah" than "car").
- **Predicting Probabilities:** To estimate $Q_{\tilde{y},y^*}$, the method requires out-of-sample predicted probabilities for each example. These are obtained by either pre-training models on the training set and fine-tuning on the test set using cross-validation, or by performing cross-validation on the entire dataset if no explicit train/test split exists. The model yielding the highest cross-validation accuracy for each dataset is selected.
- **Error Flagging:** The "fraction of label errors" ($\rho$) is calculated from $Q_{\tilde{y},y^*}$. The top $\rho \cdot n$ examples (where $n$ is dataset size) with the smallest normalized margin (difference between predicted probability for the given label and the highest predicted probability for an alternative label) are flagged as putative label errors.

#### 3.3. Human Validation (Mechanical Turk and Expert Review)

Algorithmic flags are then human-validated to confirm actual errors and categorize them.
- **Crowdsourcing (Mechanical Turk):** For most datasets, all identified errors are checked; for very large datasets, a random sample is validated. Workers are shown a flagged example, its given label, and the top CL-predicted alternative label, alongside high-confidence examples of both classes. They choose whether the example matches the given label, the predicted label, both, or neither.
- **Agreement Threshold:** An example is considered an "error" if fewer than three out of five workers agree with the original label.
- **Error Categorization:** Errors are categorized as "correctable" (majority agrees on CL-predicted label), "multi-label" (majority agrees on both), or "neither" (majority agrees on neither).
- **Expert Review (ImageNet subset):** To assess the reliability of CL and MTurk, the authors conduct an expert review (by the authors and an experienced labeler) on both CL-flagged and non-CL-flagged images from ImageNet. Experts spend significantly more time per label and reach a consensus.

#### 3.4. Analysis of Implications for Benchmarks

The study introduces several key definitions to analyze the impact of identified errors:
- **Original Accuracy ($\tilde{A}$):** Standard accuracy calculated against original, potentially erroneous, labels.
- **Corrected Accuracy ($A^*$):** Accuracy calculated against human-corrected labels (where possible).
- **Correctable Set (C):** Examples flagged by CL and human-validated with a different true label.
- **Benign Set (B):** Examples not flagged by CL or where human reviewers agreed with the original label.
- **Unknown-label Set (U):** Examples where human labelers couldn't agree on a single correct label.
- **Pruned Set (P):** The total set of data after removing the unknown-label set ($P = B \cup C$).
- **Noise Prevalence ($N$):** The proportion of the pruned set that is correctable ($|C|/|P|$).

The analysis proceeds in two main parts using ImageNet and CIFAR-10 with pre-trained models:
1. **Benchmarking on the Correctable Set:** Models' performance on the specific subset of originally mislabeled data (C) is compared using original vs. corrected labels.
2. **Benchmark Instability with Increasing Noise:** The study simulates increasing noise prevalence by incrementally removing correctly-labeled examples from the benign set, effectively increasing the proportion of the correctable set in the test data. This allows observation of how model rankings change under varying noise levels in the test set.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings that underscore the pervasive nature of test set label errors and their profound implications for machine learning benchmarks:

- **Pervasive Label Errors:** The study confirms that label errors are widespread across all 10 common benchmark datasets.
 - An average of **at least 3.3% errors** is estimated across the datasets.
 - The **ImageNet validation set contains at least 6% errors** (2,916 out of 50,000 examples), and **QuickDraw is estimated to have over 10% errors** (over 5 million examples).
 - Error rates vary, from a low of 0.15% (MNIST) to a high of 10.12% (QuickDraw), generally correlating with dataset simplicity and curation effort.
 - On average, **51% of algorithmically-flagged candidates were indeed human-validated as erroneous**.
- **Overall Benchmark Stability (with removal):** When considering the entire test set, removing or correcting label errors **does not significantly alter the overall relative rankings** of models in benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet validation set accuracy). This suggests that for general performance measurement, the current benchmarks might be somewhat robust to the *presence* of a small percentage of errors spread across the entire dataset.
- **Benchmark Instability on the Correctable Set:** A crucial finding emerges when analyzing model performance specifically on the *correctable set* (C) – the subset of data originally mislabeled but now corrected.
 - Models that perform best on the *original (incorrect) labels* of the correctable set often perform **worst** on the *corrected labels* of the same set.
 - For example, on ImageNet, **NASNet-large**, a high-capacity model, ranks 1st based on original labels but drops to 29th (out of 34) when evaluated on corrected labels within the correctable set. Conversely, **ResNet-18**, a lower-capacity model, improves from 34th to 1st. A similar trend is observed with VGG-11 outperforming VGG-19 on CIFAR-10.
 - This phenomenon suggests that higher-capacity models tend to "overfit" not in the classical sense of failing to generalize to new data, but by learning and reflecting the *systematic patterns of label errors* present in the original test data. This capability allows them to score higher on the *erroneous* benchmark but perform worse on the *true* labels, which is what matters in practice.
- **Impact of Increased Noise Prevalence (Benchmark Destabilization):** The study demonstrates that even slight increases in the prevalence of mislabeled test examples can drastically change model rankings and lead to incorrect model selection.
 - For instance, on ImageNet, **ResNet-18 outperforms ResNet-50** in terms of corrected accuracy if the prevalence of originally mislabeled test examples increases by just 6% relative to the original pruned test data. Similar results are found for CIFAR-10.
 - This implies that in real-world scenarios where datasets often have higher inherent noise than highly curated benchmarks, practitioners using standard test accuracy could be led to deploy **higher-capacity models that are actually inferior** to their lower-capacity counterparts when evaluated on truly correct labels.
- **Validation of Confident Learning and MTurk:**
 - **Expert review** confirms that CL is highly effective in prioritizing errors: CL-flagged examples were **2.6 times more likely to be erroneously labeled** than randomly selected non-CL-flagged examples. This validates CL as an efficient filtering mechanism.
 - The expert review generally **agreed with the MTurk workers' assessments**, suggesting that well-designed crowdsourcing interfaces can yield high-quality label verification even from non-expert annotators.
 - The expert review also estimated that the **ImageNet validation set might contain closer to 20% label errors** (compared to the initial 6% estimate), indicating the problem's actual severity is much greater than initially reported, as some errors in non-CL-flagged data were missed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes a profound contribution to the field of machine learning by systematically exposing and quantifying the pervasive issue of label errors in widely-used benchmark test sets. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Rethinking Benchmarking Practices:** The most immediate impact is a call to critically re-evaluate how machine learning models are benchmarked. The paper demonstrates that simply relying on "original accuracy" on existing test sets can be misleading, especially for noisy real-world applications. It proposes that "corrected accuracy" on carefully curated test sets is a more reliable metric for assessing real-world performance.
- **Challenging "State-of-the-Art" Claims:** The finding that higher-capacity models can "overfit" to systematic label errors in benchmarks (leading to inflated performance on noisy data but worse performance on truly correct data) casts doubt on some reported "state-of-the-art" improvements. It suggests that architectural advancements might sometimes inadvertently optimize for dataset quirks rather than genuine generalization to the true underlying task.
- **Informing Model Selection in Practice:** For ML practitioners, the discovery that lower-capacity models might be more robust and perform better in environments with high label noise is highly significant. This advises caution against automatically deploying the largest, most complex model based solely on benchmark performance, especially when dealing with proprietary datasets whose quality might be lower than public benchmarks. It encourages consideration of simpler models, potentially leading to more efficient and reliable deployments.
- **Emphasizing Data Quality:** The work strongly reinforces the importance of data quality, particularly for test sets. It advocates for allocating resources to diligently clean and correct test labels, even if budget constraints necessitate lower quality for training data. This shift in focus, from solely model development to rigorous data curation, is crucial for building trustworthy AI systems.
- **Actionable Tools and Resources:** By open-sourcing the Confident Learning framework (Cleanlab) and providing corrected test sets, the authors provide concrete, actionable tools for the community to address the identified problems. This empowers researchers and practitioners to audit their own datasets, identify potential errors, and incorporate data cleaning into their ML workflows.
- **Guiding Future Research:** The paper opens several avenues for future research, including a rigorous analysis of the specific causes of high-capacity models' susceptibility to test set noise (e.g., training set noise, hyperparameter tuning, train/test distribution shift), and how to optimally allocate human annotation budgets between training and test data for maximum impact.
- **Raising Awareness of "Data Cascades":** This research aligns with the growing awareness of "data cascades", where issues originating in data collection and annotation propagate and amplify through the entire ML pipeline, leading to significant real-world consequences. This paper provides concrete evidence of such cascades stemming from test set errors.

In conclusion, "Pervasive Label Errors in Test Sets Destabilize Machine Learning Benchmarks" is a timely and impactful paper that delivers a stark warning to the machine learning community. It highlights a critical blind spot in current practices and provides both empirical evidence and practical tools to foster more robust, reliable, and trustworthy machine learning development.
