# TONAV: Task-Oriented Navigation and Action-Velocity Chunk Learning for Articulated Object Quadrupedal Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> TONAV is a quadrupedal mobile-manipulation framework for articulated objects that closes the navigation-manipulation gap. It pairs a perception-planning chain-of-thought navigator plus an adaptive last-mile approach module with action-velocity chunk learning, where policies jointly predict joint positions and velocities from velocity-coupled teleoperation demos. Real-world tests reach 60% navigation and 80% full-task success.

## Problem

- Object-navigation systems stop within 1-2 meters of a target, a granularity too coarse to guarantee a base pose actually compatible with the intended manipulation.
- Last-mile base-pose methods rely on task-specific annotations, large supervised datasets, or fixed geometric heuristics, limiting flexibility on unseen tasks and cluttered household layouts.
- Action-chunking policies encode only joint positions, so they never model how motion evolves, causing lag, jitter, and contact loss during sustained articulated-object interaction.

## Method

- **Action-Velocity Teleoperation Framework** injects clipped finite-difference joint-velocity references into an MIT-style ==impedance controller== at 220 Hz, cutting master-follower lag and yielding temporally consistent demonstrations.
- **Perception-Planning Chain-of-Thought** splits ==MLLM== inference into task-conditioned scene perception then landmark-based route planning; an **Adaptive Approach Module** refines the base using ==SAM2== masks and an ego-motion-aware ==Kalman estimator==.
- ==Action-velocity chunk learning== adds a velocity head to an ==ACT-style CVAE==, trained with position L1, velocity supervision, a ==motion-consistency loss== tying position differences to predicted velocities, and ==KL regularization==.

## Results

- Navigation success reached **60.00%** overall versus **26.67%** for InternNav and **13.33%** for StreamVLN, gains of **33.33** and **46.67** percentage points across three articulated-object tasks.
- From shared manipulation-ready poses, the full system hit **80.00%** overall success, beating ACT at **46.67%** and Diffusion Policy at **26.67%**; removing velocity-coupled teleoperation dropped it to **53.33%**.
- Ablating the chain-of-thought decomposition cut Qwen-3.7-Max navigation from **60.00%** to **26.67%** and Doubao-Seed-2.1-Pro from **46.67%** to **13.33%**; joint-angle plots showed less oscillation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Navigation success should be scored against downstream manipulation feasibility, not target proximity; a reachable pose is not necessarily a manipulable one.
> - Supervising velocity alongside position, and enforcing consistency between them, converts action chunks from where-to-move into how-to-move, stabilizing contact across chunk boundaries.
> - Demonstration quality is a policy bottleneck: teleoperation tracking lag propagates into the learned policy, so fixing the data-collection controller can outweigh architecture changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses challenges within the field of mobile manipulation, particularly for quadrupedal robots interacting with articulated objects in household environments. Mobile manipulation typically involves two coupled capabilities: navigation to a target and subsequent manipulation.

Existing research in navigation often focuses on guiding robots to the vicinity of a target, often within 1–2 meters. While effective for general object navigation, this level of precision is frequently insufficient for direct manipulation tasks. This creates a "navigation-manipulation gap," where the robot's final pose after navigation may be poorly aligned, out of reach, or obstructed for subsequent interaction. Current approaches to address this last-mile navigation problem include manually specifying poses, learning object affordances, estimating reachability, or optimizing geometric and kinematic feasibility. However, these methods often rely on task-specific annotations, extensive training data, or predefined criteria, which can limit their generalizability and flexibility. Recent advancements in vision foundation models and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have opened avenues for open-vocabulary perception and task-level visual reasoning, but directly predicting a final base pose from limited cues may still lack the fine-grained spatial reasoning needed for complex household environments.

Concurrently, a second challenge in mobile manipulation pertains to maintaining continuous and stable interaction, particularly with articulated objects (e.g., drawers, cabinet doors, toilet lids). Such tasks require not only reaching an initial contact point but also continuously following constrained object trajectories. Learning-based manipulation policies, such as action-chunking methods, have shown promise in learning complex behaviors from demonstrations. However, most existing formulations represent actions primarily as joint positions. This position-only representation can result in delayed responses, discontinuous motion, and unstable contact, especially during sustained interaction or transitions between action chunks, because it does not explicitly model the temporal evolution of motion (i.e., velocity).

TONAV positions itself as a unified framework to bridge these two challenges. It aims to integrate a more sophisticated, "task-oriented" navigation strategy that ensures a manipulation-ready base pose with a novel manipulation learning approach that explicitly incorporates motion velocity for smoother and more stable contact-rich interactions. By addressing both the navigation-manipulation gap and the stability issues in sustained-contact manipulation, TONAV contributes to enhancing the overall autonomy and reliability of quadrupedal mobile manipulators in complex, real-world scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable quadrupedal mobile robots to perform stable and continuous-contact manipulation of articulated objects by first navigating to a manipulation-ready base pose. This overarching goal is motivated by two core challenges identified in existing mobile manipulation systems:

1. **Addressing the Navigation-Manipulation Gap (Task-Oriented Navigation)**:
 - **Motivation**: Current object navigation systems are often designed to bring a robot into the general vicinity (e.g., 1–2 meters) of a target. This level of precision is insufficient for manipulation, which requires the robot to be positioned at a "manipulation-ready" base pose—a configuration that is not only near the target but also specifically aligned to enable the intended interaction without kinematic limits, obstructions, or poor reachability. The mismatch between navigation termination and manipulation readiness can lead to task failure or require substantial manual intervention.
 - **Objective**: To develop a task-oriented navigation system that can interpret high-level language instructions, decompose them into executable subgoals, and progressively refine the robot's base pose towards a configuration that is spatially and kinematically compatible with the subsequent manipulation task. This involves moving beyond mere proximity to target-specific alignment.

2. **Ensuring Smooth and Stable Sustained-Contact Manipulation (Action-Velocity Chunk Learning)**:
 - **Motivation**: Many articulated-object manipulation tasks (e.g., opening/closing drawers) require establishing and maintaining continuous contact while following a constrained trajectory. Existing learning-based manipulation policies, particularly those using action chunking, primarily represent actions through joint positions. This approach often neglects the explicit modeling of motion velocities. Without velocity information, control systems can exhibit delayed responses, discontinuous movements, and instability during sustained interaction, especially with low-cost manipulators or across chunk transitions. This can lead to loss of contact or task failure.
 - **Objective**: To develop a manipulation learning method that jointly models both joint positions and their temporal transitions (velocities). This aims to enable smoother, more responsive, and more stable sustained-contact manipulation by capturing how the robot's motion should evolve, not just where it should move. A complementary objective is to develop a teleoperation framework that can collect demonstrations that inherently capture this position-velocity coupling, providing higher-fidelity expert data.

In summary, TONAV's key objectives are to provide a unified solution that first intelligently positions the robot for manipulation through vision-language reasoning and adaptive refinement, and then executes the manipulation task with enhanced stability and smoothness by explicitly integrating velocity dynamics into the learning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TONAV is a unified framework designed for articulated-object quadrupedal mobile manipulation, integrating task-oriented navigation with action-velocity chunk learning. The framework consists of three main components: an Action-Velocity Teleoperation Framework (AVTF), a Perception-Planning Chain-of-Thought (PP-CoT) for navigation, an Adaptive Approach Module (AAM) for last-mile navigation, and an Action-Velocity Chunk Learning policy for manipulation.

**A. Action-Velocity Teleoperation Framework (AVTF)**
The AVTF aims to collect high-quality, temporally consistent demonstrations for manipulation by explicitly coupling joint positions with their corresponding velocities during teleoperation.
- **Mechanism**: Unlike conventional teleoperation that transmits only joint-position references, AVTF incorporates joint-velocity references. The master arm's joint configuration `q_m_t` is measured, and its joint velocity `ˆ˙q_m_t` is estimated using a backward finite difference. These estimated velocities are clipped to physical limits (`˙q_m,ref_t`).
- **Command Formulation**: The teleoperation command `a_tele_t` is formed by concatenating `q_m_t` and `˙q_m,ref_t`.
- **Follower Control**: A follower arm tracks this coupled reference using an MIT-style impedance controller. The control torque `τ_t` includes a proportional term based on position error (`q_m_t - q_f_t`) and a derivative term based on velocity error (`˙q_m,ref_t - ˙q_f_t`), along with a feedforward torque `τ_ff`. This velocity term is intended to improve follower responsiveness and reduce temporal and trajectory mismatch.
- **Data Collection**: The control loop operates at a high frequency (220 Hz), decoupled from a lower-frequency (30 Hz) demonstration recording loop. The recorded data `D_k` includes visual observations, follower-arm proprioceptive feedback (`q_f_k`), expert actions (`q_m_k`), and estimated corresponding action velocities (`˙q_m_k`).

**B. Perception-Planning Chain-of-Thought (PP-CoT)**
PP-CoT addresses the decomposition of high-level language instructions into executable navigation subgoals. It uses a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) in a two-stage, zero-shot inference process.
- **Task-Conditioned Scene Perception**: Given a global observation `I` and task instruction `T`, the MLLM (Qwen3.7-Max) is prompted to identify the task target, candidate landmarks, and traversable regions, filtering out irrelevant objects. This produces a task-relevant scene representation `P`.
- **Perception-Guided Route Planning**: Using `P`, `I`, and `T`, the MLLM then reasons about robot-centric spatial relationships (e.g., robot position, forward direction). It plans a visible, obstacle-avoiding route towards the target and associates directional changes with landmarks. This stage generates executable navigation sub-instructions (e.g., move forward, turn, approach, stop).

**C. Adaptive Approach Module (AAM)**
AAM refines the robot's base pose in the near-field after initial coarse navigation, aiming to achieve a manipulation-ready configuration.
- **Target Grounding and 3D Observation**: An open-vocabulary detector grounds the target, and SAM2 propagates its mask across frames. The median (u,v) coordinates of the propagated mask and median valid depth `Z` are used to back-project to a 3D target observation `z_t_k` relative to the onboard camera.
- **Ego-motion-aware Kalman Estimator**: To compensate for latency and spatiotemporal mismatch from asynchronous visual observations, AAM uses a Kalman filter. This estimator maintains a target position-velocity state `x_t = [p_T_t, v_T_t]^T`. At the control rate, the state is propagated using constant-velocity dynamics and robot odometry. When a visual observation `z_t_k` becomes available (captured at `t_k`), it is transformed to the current camera frame `C_t` using odometry and used to update the Kalman filter. This provides a continuously updated, control-rate target state.
- **Hybrid Controller**: The module calculates a normalized horizontal alignment error `e_u_t` (centering the target in the image) and a stand-off distance error `e_d_t` (distance to a desired manipulation-optimal `d*`). A hybrid controller then alternates between regulating angular motion to center the target and regulating forward motion to achieve the desired stand-off distance. A safety stop is implemented for unreliable perception or safety constraint violations.

**D. Action-Velocity Chunk Learning for Continuous Contact**
This policy aims to enable smooth and stable sustained-contact manipulation by jointly predicting future joint positions and velocities.
- **Policy Architecture**: After task-oriented navigation, the policy takes an RGB observation `o_t` and proprioceptive state `q_t` as input. It utilizes an ACT-style CVAE. An encoder infers a latent variable `z` from `q_t` and the expert action chunk. A decoder employs two parallel heads to predict a future joint-position chunk `ˆq_t:t+H` and a corresponding joint-velocity chunk `ˆ˙q_t:t+H`.
- **Loss Functions**: The policy is trained with a combined loss:
 - `L_pos`: L1 loss between predicted joint positions `ˆq_t:t+H` and the physically executed follower joint positions `q_t:t+H` from demonstrations.
 - `L_vel`: L1 loss between predicted joint velocities `ˆ˙q_t:t+H` and velocities `˙q_t:t+H` estimated from consecutive executed positions in demonstrations.
 - `L_con`: A motion-consistency constraint that enforces alignment between the temporal difference of predicted positions `(ˆq_t+h+1 - ˆq_t+h)/∆t` and the predicted velocities `ˆ˙q_t+h` within each chunk. This is designed to regularize intra-chunk motion evolution.
 - `L_KL`: KL regularization for the CVAE's latent space.
 - Weights `λ_v`, `λ_c`, and `β` balance these terms.

The integration of these components forms TONAV, where PP-CoT and AAM collaboratively guide the robot to a manipulation-ready pose, and the action-velocity chunk learning policy, trained with AVTF demonstrations, executes the fine-grained, sustained-contact manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated TONAV's performance in both task-oriented navigation and complete mobile manipulation across three articulated-object tasks: "Close Drawer," "Turn On Lamp," and "Lower Toilet Lid."

**Navigation Performance:**
- **Quantitative Results**: TONAV achieved an overall navigation success rate (SR) of 60.00%, with individual task success rates of 80.00% for "Close Drawer," 60.00% for "Turn On Lamp," and 40.00% for "Lower Toilet Lid." This performance exceeded baselines like StreamVLN (13.33% overall SR) and InternNav (26.67% overall SR) by significant margins (46.67 and 33.33 percentage points, respectively).
- **Ablation Study on PP-CoT**: An ablation study confirmed the contribution of the Perception-Planning Chain-of-Thought (PP-CoT) module. Without PP-CoT, the overall navigation SR for MLLMs (Doubao-Seed-2.1-Pro and Qwen-3.7-Max) dropped to 13.33% and 26.67%, respectively. With PP-CoT, their performance improved to 46.67% and 60.00%. Qwen-3.7-Max consistently outperformed Doubao-Seed-2.1-Pro, indicating its superior capability in instruction decomposition.
- **Progressive Navigation Design**: The progressive approach, combining PP-CoT for coarse target-directed navigation and the Adaptive Approach Module (AAM) for fine-grained, last-mile adjustment, was observed to effectively reduce the gap between navigation termination and manipulation readiness. AAM's visual feedback, delayed-observation compensation, and alignment-distance regulation enabled the robot to refine its base orientation and distance for optimal manipulation.
- **Qualitative Navigation**: Baselines like StreamVLN and InternNav, while reaching the target vicinity, often resulted in terminal base poses unsuitable for manipulation, potentially forcing the manipulator to its kinematic limits. In contrast, TONAV's refined poses provided a more appropriate starting configuration for interaction.

**Manipulation Performance:**
- **Quantitative Results**: For manipulation evaluation, all methods started from identical manipulation-ready configurations to ensure a fair comparison. TONAV achieved an overall manipulation success rate of 80.00%, with 80.00% success across all three tasks (4/5 attempts each). This significantly outperformed ACT (46.67% overall SR) and Diffusion Policy (DP) (26.67% overall SR) when using demonstrations collected with the Action-Velocity Teleoperation Framework (AVTF).
- **Impact of Position-Velocity-Coupled Teleoperation (AVTF)**: The AVTF's contribution was evidenced by comparing TONAV with and without position-velocity (P-V) control during demonstration collection. Without P-V control, TONAV achieved an overall SR of 53.33%, which improved by 26.67 percentage points to 80.00% when P-V coupled demonstrations were used. This indicates that AVTF provides higher-quality, more temporally consistent demonstrations, reducing motion mismatch between master and follower arms. Similarly, ACT and DP also showed lower success rates (13.33%) when trained without P-V control demonstrations.
- **Effect of Learning Objective**: The improvement in manipulation performance was attributed to two main aspects: (1) the higher quality of demonstrations provided by AVTF, and (2) TONAV's learning objective that explicitly utilized velocity information through `L_vel` (velocity supervision) and `L_con` (motion consistency constraint), which couples predicted positions and velocities.
- **Qualitative Manipulation and Trajectory Analysis**: Qualitatively, TONAV exhibited more stable and complete interactions, such as lamp switching and toilet-lid lowering, compared to ACT and DP. It maintained more reliable end-effector contact and showed fewer contact losses. Analysis of joint trajectories (e.g., Piper Joints 1–3 for "Close Drawer") revealed that TONAV produced smoother trajectories with reduced oscillation compared to ACT and DP. This smoothness was linked to the velocity supervision and motion-consistency constraint, which preserved motion dynamics and reduced discontinuities across action chunks, thus enhancing sustained-contact stability.

In summary, the results indicate that TONAV effectively integrates task-oriented navigation with precise, stable manipulation. The hierarchical navigation strategy (PP-CoT + AAM) successfully addresses the manipulation-readiness challenge, while the action-velocity learning, supported by position-velocity-coupled teleoperation and explicit velocity modeling, significantly improves the smoothness and stability of contact-rich tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TONAV framework contributes to the field of mobile manipulation by addressing two distinct but interconnected challenges. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Bridging the Navigation-Manipulation Gap**: TONAV's task-oriented navigation, through the combination of Perception-Planning Chain-of-Thought (PP-CoT) and the Adaptive Approach Module (AAM), provides a structured approach to transition from coarse object-vicinity navigation to fine-grained, manipulation-ready base pose adjustment. This capability is significant for enabling robots to autonomously perform complex tasks in unstructured environments, as it reduces the need for manual intervention or extensive, task-specific predefined poses. This approach enhances the practical applicability of mobile manipulators by ensuring they are appropriately positioned for interaction.

2. **Enhancing Sustained-Contact Manipulation Stability and Smoothness**: The explicit modeling of joint velocities in both the Action-Velocity Teleoperation Framework (AVTF) and the Action-Velocity Chunk Learning policy represents an advancement in learning contact-rich behaviors. By incorporating "how to move" alongside "where to move," TONAV mitigates issues such as tracking lag, motion jitter, and contact instability common in position-only control. This is particularly relevant for articulated objects in household settings, where continuous and stable interaction is critical for task success. The demonstrated improvement in trajectory smoothness and contact reliability suggests a pathway towards more robust and reliable manipulation policies.

3. **Improving Expert Demonstration Quality**: The AVTF offers a method for collecting higher-fidelity expert demonstrations. By capturing both position and velocity information and reducing master-follower motion mismatch, AVTF provides a richer data source for imitation learning. This is impactful because the quality of expert demonstrations directly influences the performance of learned policies, especially for complex, contact-rich tasks.

4. **Leveraging Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) for Task Reasoning**: The use of PP-CoT with MLLMs for decomposing high-level language instructions into executable navigation subgoals demonstrates a practical application of these models for robust task planning in robotics. This enables a more intuitive and flexible human-robot interaction paradigm, where robots can interpret abstract commands and translate them into a sequence of actionable steps tailored to the environment and task.

5. **Relevance to Quadrupedal Mobile Manipulation**: The focus on quadrupedal robots highlights the potential for deploying mobile manipulators in more diverse and challenging terrains beyond flat laboratory floors. Combining robust navigation with precise manipulation on a mobile platform enhances the versatility of these robots for various service and industrial applications.

In conclusion, TONAV provides a coherent framework that addresses critical bottlenecks in current mobile manipulation systems. By enabling more intelligent navigation to manipulation-ready states and more stable, smooth execution of contact-rich tasks, it advances the capabilities of autonomous robots. Future work building on TONAV could explore integrating richer sensory feedback like tactile sensing to further enhance robustness against contact disturbances and generalize across even wider ranges of environmental layouts and initial configurations, pushing towards broader real-world deployment.
