# Reinforce to Learn, Elect to Reason: A Dual Paradigm for Video Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The RLER framework from the National University of Defense Technology and Sun Yat-sen University introduces a dual paradigm to enhance video reasoning in large multimodal models by explicitly linking evidence production during training with evidence-aligned verification during inference. It achieved an average accuracy improvement of 6.3% over its base model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) across eight diverse video benchmarks, including outperforming GPT-4o on VideoMME.

## Problem

- Existing large multimodal models (LMMs) for video reasoning often perform inference in a single, unverified pass, leading to outputs not explicitly grounded in visual evidence.
- This single-pass approach makes LMMs susceptible to minor perturbations and prone to generating unreliable or uninterpretable conclusions, particularly in complex video scenarios.
- Current methods to improve LMM reasoning lack systematic mechanisms to enforce evidence consistency across multiple reasoning traces or to verify chains of thought against key visual evidence.

## Method

- **RLER**-Training utilizes ==Group Relative Policy== Optimization (GRPO) with five task-driven rewards to shape the model's outputs for structured, verifiable, and evidence-centric generation, strengthening reasoning capacity.
- RLER-Inference, a train-free orchestrator, generates diverse candidate reasoning traces and parses them for canonical answers, cited key-frame indices, reasoning text, and confidence proxies.
- It employs an evidence-aligned scoring mechanism, robust aggregation (including outlier trimming and evidence weighting), and a referee-style self-check to elect the most reliable and evidence-supported answer.

## Results

- RLER delivered an average accuracy improvement of **6.3%** over its base model (Qwen2.5-VL-**7B**) across eight diverse video benchmarks, including video reasoning, general video understanding, and long video understanding tasks.
- It achieved state-of-the-art results among open-source LMMs and notably exceeded the proprietary GPT-4o entry on the VideoMME benchmark.
- Ablation studies confirmed that all custom reward components during training and components of the inference pipeline (diversity, evidence weighting, referee self-check) are crucial for achieving robust accuracy and grounding.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The framework successfully shifts video reasoning from an 'answer-driven' to an 'evidence-driven' paradigm by explicitly integrating evidence production during training and evidence-based verification during inference.
> - RLER-Training effectively teaches LMMs to generate structured, machine-parsable evidence, including specific key frame citations and concise reasoning, which directly informs and strengthens the inference process.
> - An 'aha moment' of self-correction emerges during training, where models identify and re-evaluate flaws in their reasoning, a capability mirrored and leveraged by the referee-style self-check in the inference stage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly in the domain of multimodal understanding, has seen significant advancements with the development of large multimodal models (LMMs). These models extend the capabilities of large language models to process and interpret visual information, including images and videos. Early applications demonstrated proficiency in tasks such as captioning and visual question answering, with a growing focus on more complex video understanding tasks involving cross-frame and open-domain inference.

Despite these advancements, a recognized limitation of current LMMs in video reasoning is their tendency to perform inference in a single, unverified pass. This approach can lead to outputs where the underlying reasoning is not explicitly grounded in evidence, making the models susceptible to minor perturbations and prone to generating unreliable or uninterpretable conclusions, particularly in complex video scenarios characterized by long temporal horizons and multi-view noise.

Previous research efforts to enhance reasoning in LMMs have generally followed two complementary paths:
1. **Reinforcement Learning (RL)-style Training:** This involves training models with rule-based rewards, format constraints, and spatio-temporal guidance to strengthen fine-grained perception and reasoning. While showing promise, especially in adapting to visual settings, these methods often focus on improving the model's *ability* to reason without a systematic mechanism to verify *how* that reasoning is aligned with evidence.
2. **Test-time Scaling Methods:** These techniques, such as diversified sampling, guided beam search, or Monte Carlo tree search, aim to improve reasoning at inference time without additional training. However, their application to LMMs frequently mirrors text-only heuristics and often lacks mechanisms to enforce evidence consistency across multiple generated reasoning traces or to systematically verify chains of thought against key visual evidence.

A critical gap exists where multiple reasoning traces are rarely checked for evidence consistency, and there is no principled method for arbitration across different potential reasoning paths. This work addresses this gap by introducing a dual paradigm that systematically integrates evidence production during training with evidence-aligned verification and election during inference, aiming to move video reasoning from an answer-driven approach to an evidence-driven one.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the reliability and interpretability of video reasoning performed by large multimodal models. The authors identify a significant limitation in existing LMMs: their single-pass inference process often delivers an answer without an explicit mechanism to verify whether the reasoning supporting that answer is aligned with visual evidence or follows a logical, verifiable path. This fragility is particularly pronounced in video contexts due to inherent complexities like long temporal horizons and diverse visual information.

The core motivation is to transition video reasoning from an "answer-driven" paradigm, where the model directly outputs a conclusion, to an "evidence-driven" paradigm, where the conclusion is supported by verifiable evidence. This shift entails two main goals:

1. **Shaping Evidence-Centric Outputs during Training:** The first objective is to train the model to explicitly generate structured, machine-readable evidence alongside its reasoning and answer. This involves teaching the model *where to look* (identifying key frames), *how much to say* (information density), and *how to express it* (readability and transparency) in a format amenable to systematic verification. This training phase is designed to potentiate the model's reasoning capabilities by instilling a discipline of evidence citation.
2. **Electing Reliable Answers based on Evidence during Inference:** The second objective is to develop a robust, train-free inference mechanism that consumes these structured, evidence-rich outputs. This mechanism aims to compare multiple candidate reasoning traces, systematically verify their consistency with cited evidence, and arbitrate among them to arrive at a reliable and interpretable answer. This inference process separates the model's *ability to think* from its *thinking correctly* by closing the loop between producing and utilizing evidence.

By decoupling the learning of evidence production from the final answer selection, the authors aim to create a framework that enhances the trustworthiness of video reasoning without requiring modifications to the core model architecture or significantly increasing computational burden during inference.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, **Reinforce to Learn, Elect to Reason (RLER)**, is a dual paradigm designed to improve video reasoning by explicitly linking evidence production during training with evidence-based selection during inference. It comprises two main stages: RLER-Training and RLER-Inference.

#### 3.1. RLER-Training

RLER-Training aims to shape the model's outputs to be structured, verifiable, and evidence-centric, while also strengthening its reasoning capacity. The training process utilizes **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, an on-policy reinforcement learning method, which optimizes the policy without a learned critic by using z-score normalized advantages within groups of sampled candidates. The objective combines a clipped surrogate loss with a KL regularizer to prevent unstable updates and limit policy drift.

To guide this optimization, RLER-Training incorporates five reward components, including three novel task-driven rewards:

1. **Frame-sensitive Reward (R_fs):** This reward encourages the model to identify and explicitly cite key frames within its reasoning trace that support its conclusion. It is defined as a bounded score that increases with the number of valid keyframe citations and decreases with invalid ones, gated by the accuracy of the answer and adherence to the output schema. This promotes spatiotemporal grounding.
2. **Think-transparency Reward (R_tt):** This reward encourages the generation of reasoning traces that are structured and of moderate length, discouraging both excessively terse and unnecessarily verbose narratives. It employs a unimodal bonus based on a robustly normalized length of the reasoning segment, gated by answer accuracy.
3. **Anti-repetition Reward (R_ar):** This reward penalizes the repetition of surface forms within the reasoning text, using fixed n-grams to calculate a repetition ratio. The goal is to increase information density and provide cleaner signals for subsequent comparison by suppressing low-information content.
4. **Formatted Reward (R_fmt):** This binary reward ensures that the model's output adheres to a predefined schema, which includes tags for `<think>`, `<answer>`, and `<keyframes>`. Correctly formatted outputs are crucial for downstream parsing and evidence extraction during inference.
5. **Accuracy Reward (R_acc):** This is the principal reward signal, indicating whether the model's final answer is correct.

These five rewards are combined with non-negative weights that sum to one, forming a total reward function that is optimized by GRPO. This process teaches the policy to emit outputs containing structured, machine-parsable evidence signals.

#### 3.2. RLER-Inference

RLER-Inference is a train-free orchestrator that consumes the evidence-shaped traces produced during training. Its goal is to derive a robust and reliable answer by electing among multiple candidate reasoning paths. The process involves several steps:

1. **Diversified Candidate Generation:** For a given video-question pair, the system generates a small set of diverse candidates by introducing light perturbations to the decoding process (e.g., varying temperature, top-p sampling, distinct seeds) and the visual input (e.g., cropping, blurring, brightness adjustments). One conservative run is always included.
2. **Structured Parsing:** Each generated candidate output is parsed into its canonical answer string, a set of cited key-frame indices, the reasoning text, and a confidence proxy derived from token probabilities. This parsing relies on the schema enforced during RLER-Training.
3. **Evidence-aligned Scoring:** Each candidate is assigned a composite evidence score, which mirrors the training rewards. This score is an average of four components:
 - **Frame-sensitive score:** Quantifies the validity and relevance of cited keyframes.
 - **Transparency score:** Measures the readability and moderate length of the reasoning.
 - **Non-redundancy score:** Reflects the information density by penalizing repetition.
 - **Confidence proxy:** An internal confidence measure from the model's token probabilities.
 This composite score favors candidates that are well-grounded, structured, concise, and internally consistent.
4. **Robust Aggregation:** Answers are aggregated through an evidence-weighted election process. Unique canonical answers are identified, and for each unique answer, a soft consensus of supporting key frames is formed. An evidence-weighted answer score is then calculated by summing the composite evidence scores of supporting candidates, weighted by the Jaccard overlap of their cited frames with the consensus frame set. To prevent outlier influence, the highest and lowest evidence scores within each answer group are trimmed before aggregation. Early stopping is implemented, allowing the process to conclude when a sufficient margin between the top answer and runner-up is achieved, along with a high mean confidence for the leading cluster, adaptively expanding the sample budget if needed.
5. **Referee-style Self-check:** Before finalizing the answer, RLER-Inference performs a single, one-shot self-check. Conditioned on the video, question, and the elected answer and its consensus keyframes, the model generates a short critique. This critique inspects the sufficiency of support and potential omissions in the cited frames. If the critique detects significant support gaps, it induces a nonpositive adjustment to the leading answer's score. If a stronger counter-evidence leads the runner-up to win, an additional sample is drawn, and aggregation is recomputed; otherwise, the initial winner is confirmed. This step mirrors the "aha moment" observed during training and provides a final layer of verification.

This dual paradigm ensures that the properties learned during reinforcement training, such as explicit evidence citation and structured reasoning, are directly leveraged at inference to make reliable and interpretable decisions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The RLER framework was comprehensively evaluated against various open-source and RL-based LMMs on eight representative video benchmarks, categorized into video reasoning, general video understanding, and long video understanding. The primary metric was accuracy (%). Auxiliary metrics included Evidence Grounding Score (EGS), Transparency Index (TI), and Redundancy Ratio (RR) to assess evidence quality.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

RLER consistently demonstrated improvements across all evaluated benchmarks, achieving state-of-the-art results among open-source LMMs.

- **Average Improvement:** RLER delivered an average improvement of 6.3% over its base model, Qwen2.5-VL-7B (e.g., from 37.4% to 43.3% on VSIBench).
- **Video Reasoning Benchmarks:** Achieved 43.3% on VSIBench and 54.2% on VideoMMMU, surpassing recent RL-based models and the base model.
- **General Video Understanding Benchmarks:** Achieved 68.5% on VideoMME (without subtitles), 76.2% on TempCompass, 72.9% on MVBench, and 57.5% on WildVideo. Notably, on VideoMME, RLER even exceeded the proprietary GPT-4o entry.
- **Long Video Understanding Benchmarks:** Attained 50.7% on LVBench and 63.0% on LongVideoBench, outperforming all reported open-source and RL-based baselines.

These results indicate that the integration of structured evidence during training and evidence-aligned election during inference leads to enhanced accuracy across diverse video understanding tasks involving complex reasoning, mixed perception-reasoning skills, and long temporal contexts.

#### 4.2. "Aha Moment" Emergence in RLER-Training

During the RLER-Training process, the researchers observed the emergence of what they termed an "aha moment." This refers to instances where the model explicitly identifies a flaw in its current hypothesis or reasoning path and initiates a corrective thought process, such as interjecting with phrases like, "Wait. Let me re-evaluate." This self-correction behavior provides actionable cues for RLER-Inference, where the referee-style self-check mechanism can detect similar patterns to challenge provisional answers and potentially trigger reweighting or one-shot resampling. This finding highlights a direct link between the model's learned self-reflection capabilities and the inference-time verification process.

#### 4.3. Case Study

A specific case study illustrated RLER's workflow. In an instance where a single-pass model incorrectly counted items due to visual conflation, RLER generated multiple candidates. It then parsed these candidates, extracted keyframes and reasoning, and scored them based on evidence consistency, transparency, and non-redundancy. Robust aggregation down-weighted outlier candidates and selected the most evidence-aligned hypothesis. A subsequent referee self-check triggered an "aha moment," prompting the candidate to re-examine its cited evidence and correct the miscount, leading to the accurate final answer. This example showcased RLER's ability to turn ambiguous visual scenes into reliable and interpretable decisions through principled arbitration and targeted self-revision.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Training Ablations:**
 - Removing the **Frame-sensitive reward** resulted in the largest performance drop (e.g., 43.3% to 41.0% on VSIBench), underscoring the importance of explicitly linking reasoning to visual evidence.
 - Removing **Think-transparency** or **Anti-repetition rewards** also led to performance declines, indicating the value of readable, dense reasoning traces.
 - Evaluating without **RLER-Inference** showed that reward shaping alone improves reasoning (e.g., 41.7% on VSIBench) but that the full dual paradigm is more effective.
 - Replacing GRPO with **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** resulted in significantly lower accuracy, even when the required output format was enforced, demonstrating GRPO's effectiveness in activating stronger reasoning capabilities through reward signals.

- **Inference Ablations:**
 - Disabling **diversity input** (K=1) caused the largest performance drop (e.g., 72.9% to 69.1% on MVBench), emphasizing the benefit of exploring alternative reasoning paths.
 - Replacing **evidence weighting** with equal-weighted majority voting reduced accuracy, confirming that grounded evidence should carry more influence.
 - Removing **outlier trimming**, **transparency/redundancy scoring**, or the **referee self-check** consistently led to small but measurable reductions in accuracy, affirming the contribution of each component to overall robustness.
 - Applying the RLER-Inference pipeline to a model *without RLER-Training* yielded lower performance, indicating that reward-shaped outputs from training provide stronger and more consistent evidence for the inference process.

#### 4.5. Compute–Quality Tradeoff

RLER demonstrated a favorable balance between computational cost and quality. With an average of 3.1 candidates evaluated per question due to early stopping (K_max=8), RLER-Inference significantly improved accuracy compared to a single-sample inference (e.g., 41.0% to 43.3% on VSIBench). Increasing to a fixed budget of K=8 samples provided only marginal additional gains (0.4-0.6%) at a substantially higher computational cost (approximately 2.6x the sampling cost), suggesting that the evidence-aligned election mechanism quickly reaches a robust consensus.

#### 4.6. Evidence–Performance Mirroring

The auxiliary metrics for evidence quality directly correlated with accuracy changes.
- Full RLER achieved high EGS (0.72), TI (0.64), and low RR (0.11), along with high accuracy (43.3%).
- Removing the **frame-sensitive reward** significantly reduced EGS (0.58) and accuracy.
- Removing the **think-transparency reward** lowered TI (0.48) and accuracy.
- Removing the **anti-repetition reward** increased RR (0.18) and reduced accuracy.
This mirroring effect indicates that the training rewards effectively shape verifiable evidence, and the inference process successfully leverages these evidence characteristics to select more accurate candidates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents RLER as a dual paradigm that systematically addresses the unreliability inherent in single-pass video reasoning by large multimodal models. Its significance lies in several key contributions:

1. **Closing the Evidence Loop:** RLER introduces a novel framework that integrates reward-shaped training with evidence-aligned election at inference. This approach moves beyond simply producing an answer to ensuring that the answer is supported by verifiable and interpretable evidence, thereby enhancing the trustworthiness of LMM outputs in complex video reasoning tasks.
2. **Structured Evidence Generation:** The design of three novel task-driven rewards (Frame-sensitive, Think-transparency, and Anti-repetition) for RLER-Training is crucial. These rewards explicitly guide the model to emit structured, machine-checkable reasoning traces that cite key visual evidence, maintain information density, and improve transparency. This learned behavior naturally interfaces with the inference-time orchestration.
3. **Robust, Train-Free Inference Mechanism:** RLER-Inference provides a principled, parameter-light orchestrator that processes multiple diverse candidate reasoning paths. By extracting, scoring, and aggregating evidence using an evidence-weighted election, along with outlier removal, adaptive budgeting, and a referee-style self-check, the system transforms multi-sample generation into a stable, evidence-based decision-making process. This train-free nature makes it a practical addition to existing LMMs.
4. **Improved Reliability and Interpretability:** The comprehensive experimental results demonstrate consistent accuracy gains across a wide range of video reasoning benchmarks, often achieving state-of-the-art performance among open-source models. Beyond raw performance, the framework inherently improves the interpretability of model decisions by making the underlying evidence and reasoning process explicit and verifiable. The observed "aha moments" further suggest an emergent self-correction capability in the trained models.
5. **Compute-Efficient Design:** RLER-Inference achieves significant performance improvements with a favorable compute-quality tradeoff, utilizing an average of only 3.1 candidates per question. This efficiency makes the paradigm broadly applicable without imposing excessive computational demands.

The potential impact of RLER extends to various applications requiring high-stakes video analysis, such as surveillance, autonomous driving, medical diagnostics, or educational content analysis, where not only the correctness of an answer but also the verifiability and interpretability of its derivation are critical. By establishing a robust path to trustworthy video reasoning, this research contributes to the development of more accountable and reliable AI systems.
