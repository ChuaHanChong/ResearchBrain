# Cross-Embodiment Offline Reinforcement Learning for Heterogeneous Robot Datasets

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Tokyo and RIKEN developed a framework for cross-embodiment offline reinforcement learning that effectively utilizes heterogeneous robot datasets, including suboptimal data. Their Embodiment Grouping strategy, which clusters robots by morphological similarity for policy updates, mitigated inter-robot gradient conflicts, leading to a 33.99% average performance improvement on suboptimal datasets compared to standard cross-embodiment offline RL.

## Problem

- The high cost and difficulty of collecting large volumes of high-quality expert demonstration data for each individual robot platform or task.
- Robot learning systems, including emerging robot foundation models, are limited by data scarcity and reliance on expert demonstrations.
- The systematic integration of offline reinforcement learning (offline RL) with cross-embodiment learning to exploit heterogeneous robot data, including suboptimal trajectories, was largely unexplored.

## Method

- Conducted a systematic empirical study combining offline reinforcement learning (==IQL==) with cross-embodiment learning using an extended Universal Robot Morphology-Agnostic (URMA) architecture.
- Developed a new locomotion dataset in MuJoCo featuring 16 distinct robot platforms and varying data qualities (expert, expert replay, 70% suboptimal replay).
- Proposed Embodiment Grouping (EG), a novel strategy that clusters robots based on Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) distance and updates actor policies group-wise to resolve inter-robot gradient conflicts.

## Results

- Implicit Q-learning (IQL) consistently outperformed Behavior Cloning (BC) on datasets containing suboptimal trajectories, confirming its ability to leverage lower-quality data in a cross-embodiment context.
- Cross-embodiment pre-training with offline RL significantly accelerated the fine-tuning convergence for individual robots compared to training from scratch.
- The proposed Embodiment Grouping (EG) strategy achieved the best average performance (**33.99%** improvement for IQL+EG over standard cross-embodiment IQL on **70%** suboptimal data), with a strong correlation (r=**0.815** for IQL) between transfer gain and gradient cosine similarity confirming gradient conflicts as the cause of negative transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Offline reinforcement learning (IQL) consistently outperforms Behavior Cloning (BC) when leveraging suboptimal robot interaction data in cross-embodiment settings.
> - Cross-embodiment pre-training significantly accelerates the fine-tuning process for individual robots, indicating positive transfer of general control capabilities.
> - Negative transfer observed in cross-embodiment learning, especially with high proportions of suboptimal data and diverse robots, stems from conflicting policy gradients among different robot morphologies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in machine learning, particularly in large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), have been driven by the scaling of model architectures and training datasets. This trend has extended to robotics, leading to the development of "robot foundation models" that leverage transformer-based architectures and large, heterogeneous robot datasets for multi-task capabilities. However, a significant limitation for these robot foundation models is the scarcity and high cost of collecting sufficient high-quality demonstration data for each specific robot platform or task. Unlike the vast text and image corpora available for other ML domains, robot data collection is time-consuming, expensive, and often requires specialized hardware and manual teleoperation.

To address this data bottleneck, the field has explored cross-embodiment learning, where a single model is pre-trained on data from multiple robot platforms to learn more generalized control primitives. This approach aims to improve adaptability, allowing a pre-trained policy to be fine-tuned for new robots with less additional data. Current robot foundation models predominantly rely on imitation learning, which necessitates expert-level demonstration data.

The present work introduces offline reinforcement learning (offline RL) as a complementary approach to cross-embodiment learning. Offline RL can leverage not only expert demonstrations but also abundant suboptimal interaction data, offering the potential to learn policies that can surpass pure imitation learning, especially when high-quality data is difficult to acquire. Previous research has shown that offline RL can outperform behavior cloning (BC) when applied to large robotic datasets containing suboptimal trajectories. However, the systematic combination of cross-embodiment learning with offline RL to exploit heterogeneous robot data, including suboptimal trajectories, remains largely unexplored. This paper aims to fill this gap by providing an empirical study and systematic analysis of this combined paradigm, investigating its challenges and potential benefits.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to overcome the high cost and difficulty associated with collecting large volumes of high-quality, expert-level demonstration data for each individual robot platform or task. This data bottleneck limits the scalability and generalization capabilities of robot learning systems, including emerging robot foundation models.

The key objectives of this study are:

- **Systematic Analysis of Cross-Embodiment Offline RL:** To conduct a principled and systematic investigation into the challenges and potential of combining offline reinforcement learning with cross-embodiment learning. This involves understanding how these two paradigms interact and identifying their strengths and limitations when applied concurrently.
- **Development of a New Benchmark:** To construct a novel benchmark for evaluating cross-embodiment offline RL. This benchmark is designed to incorporate data from a diverse set of robot platforms (up to 16 distinct types) and varying qualities of interaction data, including expert, replay, and significantly suboptimal trajectories.
- **Comparison with Existing Methods:** To rigorously compare the performance of offline RL methods (specifically Implicit Q-learning, IQL) against Behavior Cloning (BC), a common approach in cross-embodiment robot learning, particularly in scenarios where suboptimal data predominates.
- **Evaluation of Pre-training Benefits:** To assess how cross-embodiment pre-training with offline RL impacts the efficiency and speed of fine-tuning policies for individual, potentially unseen, robot embodiments.
- **Identification and Analysis of Transfer Effects:** To analyze the occurrence of both positive and negative transfer in cross-embodiment offline RL. A particular focus is placed on understanding how the proportion of suboptimal data and the diversity of robot embodiments influence these transfer phenomena.
- **Investigation of Learning Impairments:** To identify the underlying causes of performance degradation observed in cross-embodiment learning when dealing with a high proportion of suboptimal data and a large number of diverse robot types. The hypothesis is that conflicting gradients across different robot morphologies impede effective learning.
- **Development of a Mitigation Strategy:** To propose and evaluate a novel strategy, Embodiment Grouping (EG), designed to mitigate identified learning impairments, particularly inter-robot gradient conflicts, by clustering robots based on morphological similarity and performing group-wise policy updates.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic empirical approach to investigate cross-embodiment offline RL, involving dataset construction, architectural modifications, and comparative evaluations.

#### 3.1. Problem Setting

The study addresses a multi-embodiment offline RL setting where a single policy must control multiple robot morphologies (embodiments) using a common state-action interface. Each embodiment $\tau$ is associated with an MDP $M_\tau$, which shares observation and action spaces but features embodiment-specific initial state distributions, dynamics, and reward functions. The policy conditions on a morphology descriptor $f_{morph}(\tau)$ rather than a one-hot index, aiming for generalization across embodiments. The goal is to learn a single policy $\pi_\theta(a | s, f_{morph}(\tau))$ that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted return using only a pooled offline dataset $D$ comprising trajectories from all embodiments.

#### 3.2. Environments and Dataset Construction

A new locomotion dataset was constructed within the MuJoCo simulation environment, based on walking tasks. The dataset includes 16 distinct robot platforms: nine quadrupeds, six bipeds, and one hexapod. For each robot, 1 million-step datasets were curated in six variants based on data quality and commanded walking direction (Forward at 1 m/s, Backward at -1 m/s):
- **Expert data:** Collected from fully converged PPO policies.
- **Expert Replay data:** Uniformly subsampled 1 million steps from the entire PPO training process (up to expert performance), capturing a mix of suboptimal and expert behavior.
- **70% Suboptimal Replay data:** Composed of 700,000 steps from the early, suboptimal phase of PPO training and 300,000 steps from the later, expert-like phase.

#### 3.3. Network Architecture

The approach extends the Universal Robot Morphology-Agnostic (URMA) architecture to facilitate cross-embodiment learning within an offline RL framework.
- **URMA Encoder:** Factorizes observations into a general part and robot-specific parts (joint and foot observations). Descriptor-conditioned attention aggregates variable-length joint and foot observation sets into fixed-size latent representations, which are then concatenated with the general observation to form a morphology-agnostic core representation.
- **Actor Network:** A core MLP maps the URMA encoder output to an action latent, from which per-joint Gaussian policy parameters are decoded, conditioned on encoded joint descriptors, joint latents, and the action latent.
- **State Value Network:** An MLP predicts the state value directly from the URMA latent.
- **State-Action Value Network (Q-function):** An action encoder processes (zero-padded) actions to produce a latent action vector, which is then concatenated with the URMA latent to feed into two separate Q-networks.

#### 3.4. Analysis of Standard Offline RL Algorithms

- **Comparison of BC and Offline RL:** Behavior Cloning (BC) was compared against Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) on the constructed datasets. IQL was selected as a representative offline RL method capable of leveraging suboptimal data.
- **Cross-Embodiment Pre-training Evaluation:** A "leave-one-out" experiment was performed. A model was pre-trained via offline RL on data excluding one specific robot, and then fine-tuned on that robot. Its learning speed was compared to a model trained from scratch without cross-embodiment pre-training.
- **Positive and Negative Transfer Analysis:** The final performance of cross-embodiment IQL was compared to single-robot IQL training on Expert and 70% Suboptimal datasets to identify instances of positive and negative transfer.

#### 3.5. Gradient Conflicts and Resolution via Embodiment-Based Grouping

- **Gradient Conflict Analysis:** The study hypothesized that negative transfer arises from conflicting policy gradients among robots, especially with suboptimal data. Per-embodiment actor gradients $g_\tau = \nabla_\theta L_{\pi,\tau}(\theta)$ were computed, where $L_{\pi,\tau}(\theta)$ is the advantage-weighted actor objective for embodiment $\tau$. Pairwise cosine similarity $C[\tau_i, \tau_j]$ was used to quantify gradient alignment. The proportion of negative cosine similarities was analyzed across datasets with varying suboptimal data ratios and embodiment diversity. The correlation between a robot's transfer gain and its average gradient cosine similarity was also computed. This analysis was also performed using TD3+BC to confirm generality.
- **Embodiment-Based Grouping (EG) Strategy:**
 - **Robot Graph Representation:** Each robot embodiment was represented as a graph. Nodes corresponded to the torso, joints, and feet. Edges connected kinematically adjacent parts. Node features were derived from the local descriptors used by the URMA encoder (joint descriptors $d_j$ for joint nodes, foot descriptors $d_f$ for foot nodes, and a zero vector for the torso node).
 - **Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) Distance:** Pairwise distances between these robot graphs were computed using the FGW distance, which jointly considers graph structure (shortest path distances) and node features (Euclidean distance on standardized descriptors). These distances were converted to similarities for analysis.
 - **Hierarchical Clustering:** Hierarchical agglomerative clustering was applied to the FGW distance matrix to partition the robots into $M$ fixed groups.
 - **Embodiment-Grouped Offline RL Update (Algorithm 1):** Training involved a single global critic update per outer iteration using a global minibatch. This was followed by sequential actor updates, where each group's policy parameters were updated using only samples belonging to that specific group.

#### 3.6. Experimental Evaluation

- **Comparative Performance:** Eight methods were compared across six datasets: Behavior Cloning (BC), TD3+BC, Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), IQL with gradient projection (IQL+PCGrad), IQL with selective grouping (IQL+SEL), and the Embodiment Grouping variants for BC, TD3+BC, and IQL (BC+EG, TD3+BC+EG, IQL+EG).
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Grouping Strategy:** EG was compared against random partitioning and a heuristic grouping (bipeds, quadrupeds, hexapods, torso-less bipeds).
 - **Number of Groups ($M$):** The sensitivity of performance and training time to the number of clusters $M$ was evaluated.
 - **Compute Normalization:** A compute-normalized comparison was performed where the IQL baseline's optimizer steps were scaled by $M$ and batch size reduced by $1/M$ to match the total number of processed samples and optimizer updates of EG.
- **Critic Grouping:** An additional ablation explored applying EG to the critic updates in addition to the actor updates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the combination of offline RL and cross-embodiment learning:

- **Offline RL Outperforms Behavior Cloning with Suboptimal Data:** In datasets with relatively uniform high-quality behavior (e.g., Expert Forward and Backward), Behavior Cloning (BC) and Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) demonstrated comparable performance. However, on datasets predominantly composed of suboptimal trajectories (e.g., Expert Replay and 70% Suboptimal Replay), IQL consistently surpassed BC. This finding is consistent with results on benchmarks like D4RL and suggests that offline RL effectively leverages suboptimal data in a cross-embodiment context.

- **Accelerated Fine-tuning via Cross-Embodiment Pre-training:** Cross-embodiment pre-training with offline RL significantly accelerated the learning process during fine-tuning for individual robots. For quadrupedal (Badger) and bipedal (Unitree G1, Cassie) robots, policies pre-trained on diverse cross-embodiment data converged more rapidly to effective performance compared to models trained from scratch. This indicates that cross-embodiment learning serves as an effective pre-training strategy for offline RL, enabling positive transfer of general control priors.

- **Positive and Negative Transfer Phenomena:**
 - On the Expert Forward dataset, cross-embodiment IQL achieved performance comparable to single-robot training, suggesting effective positive transfer or neutral impact.
 - On the 70% Suboptimal Replay Forward dataset, the average performance of cross-embodiment IQL was lower than isolated single-robot training. However, specific quadrupedal robots (Unitree A1, Unitree Go1, Unitree Go2, Badger) showed substantial performance gains, indicating positive transfer among morphologically similar and well-represented robots.
 - Conversely, bipedal robots (Unitree H1, Unitree G1), with less similar-embodiment data, experienced significant performance degradation under cross-embodiment learning with suboptimal data. This negative transfer was observed to emerge primarily when large amounts of suboptimal data were combined with cross-embodiment training.

- **Gradient Conflicts as a Cause of Negative Transfer:**
 - Analysis of actor gradients revealed that increasing the proportion of suboptimal data led to a higher fraction of negative pairwise cosine similarities between robot gradients, indicating more frequent and severe gradient conflicts.
 - Similarly, increasing the number and morphological diversity of robots also amplified inter-robot gradient conflicts.
 - A strong positive correlation (r = 0.815 for IQL, r = 0.766 for TD3+BC) was observed between a robot's transfer gain and its average gradient cosine similarity, empirically confirming that gradient conflicts underlie negative transfer.
 - The correlation between morphological similarity (measured by Fused Gromov-Wasserstein distance between robot graphs) and gradient alignment was found to be statistically significant and positive (r = 0.63 for IQL, r = 0.711 for TD3+BC). This indicates that morphologically similar robots tend to have more aligned policy gradients, while dissimilar robots exhibit greater conflicts.

- **Effectiveness of Embodiment-Grouped Offline RL Update (EG):**
 - The proposed Embodiment Grouping (EG) strategy, which clusters robots by morphology-aware distances and performs group-wise actor updates, achieved the best average performance across all datasets, particularly exhibiting substantial gains on datasets rich in suboptimal trajectories. For instance, on the 70% Suboptimal datasets, IQL+EG showed an average improvement of 33.99% compared to the standard IQL cross-embodiment baseline.
 - EG also consistently improved the performance of other offline learning baselines, with TD3+BC+EG showing a +19.5% average improvement and BC+EG showing a +26.3% improvement on the 70% Suboptimal datasets.
 - Ablation studies demonstrated that the EG grouping strategy (based on FGW distances) yielded a significantly greater improvement (+38.34%) on the 70% Suboptimal Forward dataset compared to random grouping (+3.08%) or a heuristic grouping (-8.31%). This supports that groups formed by morphological similarity better align gradients.
 - Performance generally peaked at small to moderate numbers of groups (e.g., M=7 for 70% Suboptimal Forward), with larger numbers of groups leading to diminishing returns and increased computational time.
 - A compute-normalized comparison confirmed that the benefits of EG stem from its grouping strategy and mitigation of gradient interference, rather than simply an increased number of policy updates.
 - Applying EG to the critic updates in addition to the actor updates did not yield meaningful additional performance improvements and increased computational cost, suggesting that policy gradient conflicts are the primary target for EG.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study makes several contributions to the field of robot learning, with potential implications for developing more scalable and robust robot policies.

**Addressing Data Scarcity and Cost:** The research directly addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: the high cost and difficulty of acquiring large, high-quality demonstration datasets for every new robot or task. By systematically investigating the combination of offline RL and cross-embodiment learning, this work provides a method to leverage more readily available, diverse, and suboptimal robot interaction data. This could significantly reduce the data collection burden and accelerate the deployment of robotic systems.

**Unlocking Suboptimal Data Potential:** The findings demonstrate that offline RL, when integrated with cross-embodiment learning, can effectively utilize suboptimal data to learn policies that outperform traditional imitation learning methods like Behavior Cloning. This capability is crucial as it expands the usable data pool beyond expert demonstrations, which are inherently limited.

**Systematic Understanding of Transfer Learning:** The study provides a systematic analysis of positive and negative transfer effects in cross-embodiment offline RL. By empirically identifying when and why negative transfer occurs (specifically, due to inter-robot gradient conflicts in the presence of high suboptimal data and diverse embodiments), it contributes to a deeper understanding of the complexities of learning across heterogeneous robot platforms.

**Novel Conflict Mitigation Strategy:** The introduction of Embodiment Grouping (EG) offers a principled and effective method to mitigate gradient conflicts. By clustering robots based on morphological similarity and performing group-wise policy updates, EG facilitates more aligned gradient updates, leading to substantial performance gains, especially in challenging, data-rich but suboptimal scenarios. This strategy represents a significant step towards enabling more stable and efficient learning in heterogeneous robot environments.

**Foundational Work for Generalizable Robot Policies:** This research lays groundwork for the development of more generalizable robot "foundation models." By demonstrating how to effectively pre-train policies on diverse robot morphologies and data qualities, it moves towards a paradigm where a single policy can adapt to a wide range of robots and tasks, reducing the need for per-robot, from-scratch training.

**Future Directions and Limitations:** While significant, the study has limitations, including its restriction to MuJoCo locomotion simulations, which leaves open questions about sim-to-real transfer and applicability to other domains like manipulation. The proposed Embodiment Grouping is static, based on pre-computed morphological distances, and may not adapt to dynamic changes in learning progress or data characteristics in more complex offline-to-online RL settings. Future work could focus on validation on real robots and manipulation tasks, and explore dynamic grouping strategies that integrate learning progress and data characteristics. Additionally, developing group-specific embodiment or task representations within these clusters could further enhance within-group knowledge sharing.
