# Learn More with Less: Uncertainty Consistency Guided Query Selection for RLVR

> [!summary] Summary
> An active learning framework is introduced for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) in mathematical reasoning tasks, utilizing an "uncertainty consistency" metric. This approach enables RLVR to achieve performance comparable to full-dataset training, such as 91.7% on GSM8K and 72.9% on MATH with Qwen2.5-7B, while using only 30% of the training data.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) methods for LLMs in mathematical reasoning require substantial query budgets (tens of thousands of examples), incurring high annotation costs.
- The role of query selection strategies in RLVR for reasoning tasks has been largely overlooked, leading to potential issues like model bias, entropy collapse, and gradient instability.
- Traditional active learning (AL) strategies, including uncertainty-based and feature-space coverage methods, fail to significantly improve performance over random selection in the RLVR setting.

## Method

- Proposed an "==uncertainty consistency==" metric to quantify the alignment between subjective model uncertainty (e.g., perplexity) and objective ground-truth correctness (rule-based reward).
- Introduced an offline Point-Biserial Correlation Coefficient (rpbr_{pb}rpb) for static dataset selection and an online ronlinepbr_{online_pb}ronlinepb metric based on advantage estimates for dynamic, real-time query selection during RL training.
- Developed a selection strategy where, at each online training step, a top percentage (e.g., 30%) of queries exhibiting the largest ronlinepbr_{online_pb}ronlinepb values are selected for computing RL gradients, facilitating stable and efficient learning.

## Results

- The online uncertainty consistency (ronlinepbr_{online_pb}ronlinepb) method achieved performance comparable to or slightly exceeding full-dataset training (e.g., **91.7%** on GSM8K vs. **91.5%** for full data with Qwen2.5-**7B**) while utilizing only **30%** of the data.
- All tested classic active learning baselines consistently performed worse than full-dataset training and showed only marginal improvements over random sampling, underscoring the limitations of prior methods in this domain.
- Consistency sampling generalized effectively across various RLVR algorithms (GRPO, RLOO, DAPO, REINFORCE++) and different LLM architectures, maintaining higher policy entropy during early training and ensuring stable learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Classic active learning methods, which primarily rely on subjective uncertainty, are insufficient for data-efficient training in RLVR contexts for mathematical reasoning.
> - "Inconsistent samples"—where high subjective uncertainty does not align with objective incorrectness—generate extreme policy gradients, leading to high variance and instability during RL training.
> - Prioritizing "uncertainty consistent" samples, where subjective and objective uncertainties are aligned, promotes better exploration, maintains higher policy entropy, and stabilizes RL training, leading to improved sample efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated advancements in complex mathematical reasoning. A significant contributing factor to this progress is Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR). RLVR methods, such as GRPO, DAPO, REINFORCE++, and RLOO, leverage explicit, rule-based rewards in mathematical tasks (correct or incorrect answers), eliminating the need for learned reward models and critics often found in actor-critic reinforcement learning. This simplifies the training process for tasks with clear factual correctness.

However, a recurring challenge in the application of these RLVR algorithms is their demand for extensive query budgets, often tens of thousands of examples, to achieve optimal performance. The annotation of ground-truth answers for mathematical reasoning problems is a resource-intensive and costly process. Furthermore, prior research in RLVR has generally not thoroughly investigated the impact of query selection strategies on model reasoning ability. Suboptimal query selection can lead to undesirable training outcomes, including model bias, entropy collapse, and gradient instability, which can impede convergence and limit performance gains.

This paper positions itself within the intersection of LLM reasoning, RLVR, and Active Learning (AL). Active learning is a machine learning paradigm designed to improve model generalization with minimal labeled data by selectively querying the most informative unlabeled examples for annotation. While active learning has seen applications in various LLM contexts, such as in-context learning, preference alignment, supervised fine-tuning, and knowledge distillation, its efficacy and specific challenges within the RLVR framework for reasoning tasks remain underexplored. The work addresses this gap by investigating whether AL strategies can reduce the data requirements of RLVR while maintaining or improving performance, particularly in mathematical reasoning. It identifies a specific limitation of classic AL in this domain and proposes a novel approach to overcome it, thereby contributing to more data-efficient and stable RL training for LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether the performance of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) for mathematical reasoning tasks can be maintained or improved using a smaller, more strategically selected subset of queries, thereby reducing the substantial annotation costs typically associated with these methods.

This objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **High Annotation Costs in RLVR**: Existing RLVR algorithms, while effective for improving LLM mathematical reasoning, typically require tens of thousands of queries to achieve optimal performance. Annotating correct answers for such a large volume of mathematical reasoning problems is costly and labor-intensive, posing a barrier to the wider application and scalability of these methods.
- **Neglected Role of Query Selection in RL for Reasoning**: Prior research in RL, especially concerning LLMs for reasoning, has largely overlooked the specific influence of query selection strategies on training dynamics and the resulting reasoning ability of the model. This lack of attention suggests an untapped potential for efficiency gains through intelligent data sampling.
- **Adverse Effects of Poor Query Selection**: Suboptimal query selection is identified as a factor that can lead to significant issues during RL training. These issues include the introduction of model bias, premature entropy collapse (limiting exploration), and gradient instability, all of which can hinder model convergence and ultimate performance. The paper highlights that certain types of samples can produce extreme policy gradients, leading to high variance and unstable training.
- **Failure of Classic Active Learning (AL) Strategies**: A crucial preliminary experiment conducted by the authors revealed that traditional active learning strategies—including uncertainty-based methods (e.g., perplexity, entropy), feature-space coverage methods (e.g., K-center, K-means), and LLM-based prompting—failed to significantly outperform random query selection in the RLVR setting for mathematical reasoning. This empirical observation indicated that these established AL approaches were not directly applicable or effective in this specific domain, necessitating a novel approach.
- **Identification of "Inconsistent Samples"**: Through further analysis, the authors identified a specific problem with traditional AL. Classic AL often prioritizes samples with high subjective uncertainty (e.g., high perplexity). However, in RLVR, some samples may exhibit high subjective uncertainty yet still correspond to correct answers (high subjective uncertainty, high objective reward). These "inconsistent samples" were found to generate extreme policy gradients, leading to high variance and instability in RL training. This finding motivated a shift from simply seeking "uncertain" samples to seeking "consistent" samples where subjective and objective uncertainties are aligned.
- **Need for a Tailored Consistency Metric**: The recognition of the "inconsistency" problem motivated the development of a new metric that quantifies the alignment between subjective uncertainty (how uncertain the model is) and objective uncertainty (whether the answer is actually correct or incorrect). Such a metric would enable the selection of "consistency samples" that are hypothesized to contribute more effectively to stable and robust RL training. This metric needed to be applicable in both static (offline) data selection and dynamic (online) training scenarios.

In summary, the research aims to address the high cost and instability of RLVR training for mathematical reasoning by developing and validating a novel active learning strategy centered on an "uncertainty consistency" metric, designed to select more informative and stable training samples.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes an active learning framework for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) that prioritizes "uncertainty consistency" in query selection to improve data efficiency and training stability. The methodology addresses both offline and online settings for query selection.

#### 3.1. Problem Identification and Core Hypothesis

The work begins by empirically demonstrating that classic active learning (AL) strategies, such as those based on perplexity (PPL), entropy, feature-space coverage (K-center, K-means), or LLM-based prompting (AskLLM), fail to significantly improve performance over random selection in the RLVR setting for mathematical reasoning. This failure is attributed to these methods' sole reliance on subjective uncertainty (model's confidence) while neglecting objective uncertainty (ground-truth correctness).

The core hypothesis is that "consistency samples"—where subjective uncertainty (e.g., perplexity) aligns with objective uncertainty (correctness, reflected by the rule-based reward)—are more valuable for stable and effective RL training. Specifically, samples where the model is confident and correct, or uncertain and incorrect, are considered more consistent. Inconsistent samples (e.g., high subjective uncertainty but correct answer) are shown to induce larger variance in gradient norms, leading to unstable training.

#### 3.2. Uncertainty Estimation

Subjective uncertainty of the LLM for a given query $x^{(i)}$ and its $k$-th response $y^{(i)}_k$ is primarily estimated using **Perplexity (PPL)**, calculated as $e^{-\frac{1}{|y^{(i)}_k|}\sum_{t=0}^{|y^{(i)}_k|} \log \pi_{ref}(y^{(i)}_{k,t}|x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{k,<t})}$. A higher PPL indicates lower probability and thus higher subjective uncertainty. Alternative metrics like Margin Score (MS) and Entropy (ENT) are also mentioned. Objective uncertainty is inherently captured by the binary rule-based reward function $R(c, a|x) = 1$ if the predicted answer $a$ matches the reference answer $a^*$, and $0$ otherwise. A lower accuracy rate over multiple samples for a query indicates higher objective uncertainty for that query.

#### 3.3. Offline Uncertainty Consistency Metric and Selection

In the offline setting, where a fixed dataset is pre-selected for RL training, the research introduces the **Point-Biserial Correlation Coefficient (r_pb)** to quantify uncertainty consistency for a given query $x^{(i)}$. This coefficient measures the correlation between the continuous subjective uncertainty scores (U) and the binary objective rewards (R) obtained from $K$ generated responses $\{y^{(i)}_j\}_{j=1}^K$ for that query.

The formula for $r_{pb}$ is:
$r_{pb}(x^{(i)}; \{y^{(i)}_j\}_{j=1}^K) = \frac{\bar{U}_1 - \bar{U}_0}{s_K} \sqrt{\frac{K_0 K_1}{K^2}}$
where $\bar{U}_1$ is the mean subjective uncertainty for correct responses, $\bar{U}_0$ for incorrect responses, $s_K$ is the standard deviation of all subjective uncertainties, and $K_1$, $K_0$ are the counts of correct and incorrect responses, respectively.
A negative $r_{pb}$ indicates that higher subjective uncertainty is associated with incorrect responses and lower subjective uncertainty with correct responses, signifying consistency. Therefore, for offline query selection, the strategy is to pre-select the top $p\%$ of queries from the entire training set that exhibit the *smallest* (most negative) $r_{pb}$ values.

#### 3.4. Online Uncertainty Consistency Metric and Selection

The offline $r_{pb}$ metric is deemed impractical for online RL settings due to two main challenges:
1. **Estimation Accuracy**: $r_{pb}$ requires a large number of samples ($K$) for accurate estimation, which is not feasible in dynamic online settings where sampling is limited.
2. **Model Update Dynamics**: The model's output distribution (and thus subjective uncertainty estimates) continuously changes during online training, making a static $r_{pb}$ irrelevant.

To address these, an **online uncertainty consistency metric, $r_{online\_pb}$**, is proposed:
$r_{online\_pb}(x^{(i)}; \{y^{(i)}_j\}_{j=1}^K) = \frac{1}{K} \left( \sum_{\hat{A}_j > 0} \hat{A}_j U^\theta_j + \gamma \sum_{\hat{A}_j < 0} \hat{A}_j U^\theta_j \right)$
Here, $\hat{A}_j$ is the advantage estimate (e.g., from GRPO) for the response $(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}_j)$, $U^\theta_j$ is the current model $\pi_\theta$'s estimation of subjective uncertainty, and $\gamma > 0$ is a hyperparameter balancing contributions from positive and negative advantages.

**Theoretical Support for $r_{online\_pb}$**:
- **Theorem 1 (Negative Correlation between $r_{pb}$ and $r_{online\_pb}$)**: The paper theoretically proves that the covariance between $r_{pb}$ and $r_{online\_pb}$ is strictly negative (i.e., $Cov(r_{pb}, r_{online\_pb}) < 0$). This establishes a formal link, indicating that selecting for high $r_{online\_pb}$ in online settings implicitly aligns with selecting for low $r_{pb}$ (consistency) in offline settings.
- **Theorem 2 (Equivalence to Maximizing Decrease in Sample Uncertainty)**: Under assumptions of sample gradient orthogonality and bounded gradient norms, it is proven that maximizing $r_{online\_pb}$ in one optimization step of an on-policy RL algorithm is equivalent to maximizing the decrease in the overall subjective uncertainty for that sample. This provides a mechanistic explanation for its effectiveness.

For online query selection, at each training step, a mini-batch of queries is sampled. For each query in the mini-batch, responses are generated, and $r_{online\_pb}$ is computed. The top $p\%$ of queries with the *largest* $r_{online\_pb}$ values are then selected for computing the RL gradients and updating the model.

#### 3.5. RL Algorithm and Experimental Setup

- **RL Algorithm**: The primary RLVR algorithm used is GRPO, with additional validations on RLOO, DAPO, and REINFORCE++. The RLVR loss function unifies into $L_{RLVR}(\theta|x^{(i)}) = - \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{1}{|y^{(i)}_k|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y^{(i)}_k|} \hat{A}^{(i)}_{k,t} \log \pi_\theta (y^{(i)}_{k,t}|x^{(i)})$.
- **Models**: Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-3B, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.
- **Datasets**: MATH and GSM8K, both mathematical reasoning tasks.
- **Baselines**: Full dataset, Random sampling, PPL, Entropy, K-center, AskLLM, Active Prompt, ZS-CAL.
- **Metric**: Greedy decoding Pass@1 score on the test set.
- **Hyperparameters**: Standard RL training hyperparameters are used, with $K=8$ for online response generation, and $\gamma$ for $r_{online\_pb}$ tuned from $\{0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0\}$. The sampling ratio $p$ is primarily set to 30%.

The methodology establishes a theoretical and empirical framework for identifying and leveraging uncertainty consistency to make RLVR training for mathematical reasoning more data-efficient and robust.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted using various LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-3B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) on two mathematical reasoning datasets (MATH, GSM8K), with a consistent sampling ratio of 30% unless otherwise specified.

#### 4.1. Ineffectiveness of Classic Active Learning Strategies

- **Offline Setting**: In the offline setting (pre-selecting a fixed dataset), classic active learning strategies (PPL, Entropy, K-center, AskLLM, Active Prompt, ZS-CAL) demonstrated performance comparable to or only marginally better than random query selection. These methods consistently yielded results noticeably worse than training on the full dataset (Table 2). This finding validates the initial premise that traditional AL approaches are insufficient for the RLVR reasoning context.
- **Online Setting**: Similar trends were observed in the online setting for PPL and Entropy-based methods, which showed some improvement over random selection but did not reach the performance of training with the full dataset (Table 2).

#### 4.2. Efficacy of Uncertainty Consistency Metrics

- **Offline Uncertainty Consistency ($r_{pb}$)**:
 - Selecting the 30% of samples with the lowest $r_{pb}$ (indicating high consistency between subjective and objective uncertainty) in the offline setting notably outperformed random selection and all classic AL baselines. For instance, on Qwen2.5-7B, $r_{pb}$ improved performance by 1.5% on GSM8K and 1.3% on MATH compared to random selection (Table 2).
 - While effective, the offline $r_{pb}$ selection still showed a performance gap compared to using the full dataset, which was attributed to estimation bias from changes in model output distribution during subsequent RL parameter updates.
 - An ablation study (Figure 3 (1-a) & (1-b)) confirmed that selecting samples with low $r_{pb}$ (consistency samples) consistently outperformed selecting samples with high $r_{pb}$ (inconsistency samples), with the latter even performing worse than random selection. This indicates that inconsistency samples can be detrimental to RL training.

- **Online Uncertainty Consistency ($r_{online\_pb}$)**:
 - The dynamic online selection of the top 30% of samples with the highest $r_{online\_pb}$ in each mini-batch consistently and significantly outperformed both random selection and classic AL strategies.
 - Crucially, this online method achieved performance comparable to or, in some instances, slightly *surpassed* the accuracy of training with the full dataset, despite using only 30% of the data. For example, on Qwen2.5-7B, $r_{online\_pb}$ yielded 91.7% on GSM8K (+0.2% over Full) and 72.9% on MATH (-0.3% compared to Full). Similar trends were observed across Qwen2.5-3B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct models (Table 2).
 - This result indicates that uncertainty consistency query selection enables RLVR to achieve full-dataset performance with a significantly reduced data budget.
 - The empirical negative correlation between $r_{online\_pb}$ and $r_{pb}$ (Figure 1b), across various models and datasets, supports the theoretical proof that maximizing $r_{online\_pb}$ effectively promotes consistency.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies and Further Analyses

- **Sensitivity of $\gamma$**: Experiments showed that smaller values of $\gamma$ (e.g., 0.1) in the $r_{online\_pb}$ metric yielded better performance, suggesting a greater emphasis on positive advantages in the consistency calculation (Figure 3 (1-c) & (1-d)).
- **Different Sample Ratios**: While 30% proved effective, a 10% sampling ratio led to a noticeable performance drop compared to the full dataset, indicating a minimum data threshold. Increasing the ratio to 50% did not provide substantial further improvement, suggesting 30% is a reasonable balance (Figure 3 (2-c) & (2-d)).
- **Generalizability Across RLVR Algorithms**: The benefits of consistency sampling were observed across different RLVR algorithms, including GRPO, RLOO, DAPO, and REINFORCE++. For all tested algorithms, using 30% of consistency-sampled data achieved performance comparable to the full dataset (Table 3).
- **Hard Training Data**: The method's effectiveness was maintained even on more challenging datasets like DAPO-MATH-17K, showing similar improvements (Table 4).
- **Comparison with Highest Objective Uncertainty**: Selecting samples solely based on the highest objective uncertainty (i.e., hardest samples) performed worse than consistency sampling (Table 5). This emphasizes that the *consistency* of uncertainty, rather than just raw difficulty, is critical.

#### 4.4. Impact on Training Dynamics

- **Response Length**: Consistency sampling maintained the model's response length during training at levels comparable to full-dataset RL, implying the selection of appropriately difficult and informative samples.
- **Policy Entropy**: Consistency sampling helped maintain higher policy entropy in the early stages of training compared to full-data RL, which showed a rapid drop. This suggests that consistency sampling promotes better exploration, contributing to improved sample efficiency and avoiding premature convergence (Figure 3 (2-a) & (2-b)).

In summary, the research demonstrates that traditional active learning strategies are inadequate for RLVR reasoning tasks, but a novel approach based on "uncertainty consistency" significantly improves data efficiency, achieving full-dataset performance with only 30% of the data. This is supported by empirical results across multiple models and datasets, along with theoretical justifications and insights into training dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in this research have several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of large language model training and active learning:

- **Cost Reduction in RLVR Training**: The most direct and impactful consequence is the substantial reduction in the data labeling budget required for RLVR-based mathematical reasoning. By demonstrating that comparable or superior performance can be achieved with only 30% of the training data, the method offers a practical solution to mitigate the high costs associated with annotating correct answers for complex reasoning tasks. This can make advanced LLM reasoning capabilities more accessible and deployable.
- **Enhanced Training Stability and Efficiency**: The identification and selective use of "uncertainty consistent" samples address a critical issue of gradient instability and high variance observed with "inconsistent" samples. By focusing on data that provides more stable gradient signals, the proposed method contributes to more robust and efficient RL training. The maintenance of higher policy entropy in early training stages also suggests improved exploration, which is crucial for learning complex reasoning behaviors.
- **Novel Active Learning Paradigm for RL**: This work introduces a specialized active learning strategy tailored for the unique challenges of RLVR. Unlike classic AL methods that proved ineffective, the uncertainty consistency metric provides a theoretically grounded and empirically validated approach for data selection in dynamic RL environments. This could inspire further development of AL techniques specifically designed for various reinforcement learning paradigms.
- **Broader Applicability Across RLVR Algorithms and LLMs**: The consistent performance gains observed across different LLM architectures (Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-3B, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and multiple RLVR algorithms (GRPO, RLOO, DAPO, REINFORCE++) suggest the generalizability of the uncertainty consistency principle within the RLVR framework. This indicates that the proposed method is not limited to a specific model or RL algorithm configuration.
- **Improved Understanding of Informative Data for RL**: The research offers insights into what constitutes "valuable" data for RL-based reasoning tasks. It moves beyond simple notions of model uncertainty or sample difficulty, highlighting the crucial role of alignment between subjective model confidence and objective correctness. This deeper understanding can guide future data curation efforts and dataset design for reinforcement learning.
- **Potential for Extension to Other Domains**: While demonstrated in mathematical reasoning, the underlying principle of uncertainty consistency could potentially be extended to other domains where explicit, verifiable rewards are available (e.g., code generation, fact verification, logical puzzles). The online metric's formulation, using advantage estimates, suggests its adaptability to various RL settings.
- **Advancement of Sample-Efficient RL**: The work contributes to the broader goal of sample-efficient reinforcement learning, a critical area given the ever-increasing data demands of large models. By making RL training more efficient, it accelerates research and application development in complex AI tasks.
