# On Feasible Rewards in Multi-Agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Zurich provide a theoretical analysis of multi-agent inverse reinforcement learning, addressing challenges in defining feasible reward functions and achieving reward identifiability. They characterize feasible reward sets in entropy-regularized Markov games and establish conditions for identifying average rewards or specific components of linearly separable reward functions, including associated error propagation and sample complexity bounds.

## Problem

- Designing effective reward functions for multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is challenging due to interacting agents and potentially conflicting objectives.
- Multi-agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning (MAIRL) struggles with the ill-posed nature of inferring reward functions from expert demonstrations, particularly due to the multiplicity of Nash equilibria in Markov games.

## Method

- The study proposes focusing on entropy-regularized Markov games, which yield unique ==Quantal Response Equilibria== (QRE), to address the equilibrium ambiguity and selection problem in MAIRL.
- It provides an explicit characterization of feasible reward functions for entropy-regularized settings, expressing an agent's reward based on its observed QRE policy, the opponent's QRE policy, value function, and transition dynamics.
- To achieve reward identifiability, the paper demonstrates that average rewards can be identified under specific rank conditions, and full reward functions can be identified (up to constants) in linearly separable reward structures by observing expert behavior across multiple environments or opponent policies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simply extending single-agent IRL to MAIRL with a single Nash equilibrium observation is insufficient, as it results in an under-constrained feasible reward set that can introduce significant exploitability in the recovered game.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has demonstrated capabilities in modeling scenarios involving interacting agents across various domains, including autonomous driving, multi-robot control, and multi-player games. A fundamental challenge in these applications is the design of effective reward functions, a task that becomes particularly complex in multi-agent environments due to the presence of multiple agents with potentially conflicting objectives.

Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) addresses scenarios where expert demonstrations are available, but the underlying reward function driving these actions is unknown. The objective of IRL is to infer plausible reward functions that rationalize the observed behavior as optimal. Early IRL research identified a core challenge: the problem is inherently ill-posed, as multiple reward functions can explain the same observed behavior. Subsequent work in single-agent IRL has focused on reformulating the problem to enhance its tractability and applicability, including characterizing feasible reward sets and analyzing error propagation and sample complexity.

Extending IRL to the multi-agent setting (MAIRL) introduces additional complexities. A key aspect is the definition of optimality, which typically relies on game-theoretic solution concepts such as the Nash equilibrium (NE). Unlike single-agent systems, Markov games often feature multiple Nash equilibria, and each agent’s optimal strategy is interdependent with the strategies of other agents. The theoretical foundations of MAIRL, particularly regarding the characterization of feasible reward functions and reward identifiability, remain less explored compared to single-agent IRL. This paper aims to bridge this theoretical gap by providing foundational analyses for MAIRL, specifically addressing the challenges posed by equilibrium multiplicity and policy estimation errors.

The work also relates to Inverse Algorithmic Game Theory, which focuses on rationalizing observed behavior in specific game structures. Previous studies in this area have noted the computational hardness of inversely solving for Nash equilibria without additional assumptions, highlighting the need for structured approaches in MAIRL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

This paper addresses two primary research questions to advance the theoretical understanding of Multi-agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning:

**(Q1) What constitutes a rigorous definition of Multi-agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning?**

The motivation behind this question stems from the fundamental differences between single-agent and multi-agent optimality. In single-agent IRL, the concept of an optimal policy is singular. However, in multi-agent systems, optimality is defined by equilibrium concepts, such as the Nash equilibrium (NE). A single Markov game can possess multiple NE, and observing only one such equilibrium can be ambiguous. The authors initially define the feasible reward set as all reward functions under which a single observed NE policy is indeed an NE. However, they observe that this direct extension from single-agent IRL is insufficient. Distinct observed equilibria can induce different feasible reward sets, and a reward function recovered from a single NE observation can allow for the emergence of new, potentially undesirable, equilibria in the true game that were not part of the original Nash equilibrium set. This phenomenon, which they illustrate with an example (Figure 1 and Proposition 3.4), can lead to a "Nash Gap" of order $(1-\gamma)^{-1}$, indicating significant exploitability when transferring the learned reward. This ambiguity—the "equilibrium selection problem"—motivates the need for a more robust definition of MAIRL that accounts for the multiplicity of equilibria. To mitigate this, the authors propose exploring entropy-regularized Markov games, which often yield a unique equilibrium, thereby simplifying the reward inference problem.

**(Q2) Is reward identifiability achievable in Multi-agent settings?**

This question is motivated by previous work in single-agent entropy-regularized IRL, which has shown that reward functions can be identifiable up to additive constants under certain conditions (e.g., observing experts in multiple environments with different transition dynamics). Extending this to the multi-agent context is complex because each agent's reward can depend on the joint actions of all agents, and the optimal strategy for one agent is contingent on the strategies of others. The general reward function in MAIRL has a dimensionality of $|S||A||B|$ (for two players), while the conditions derived from optimal policies typically only provide constraints on $|S||A|$ and $|S||B|$ dimensions, making the system of equations under-determined. The motivation is to determine if multi-agent systems, even with entropy regularization, can achieve a similar level of reward identifiability as single-agent systems, or if additional structural assumptions are necessary. This investigation aims to clarify the limits of reward inference in multi-agent environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology is structured around addressing the two core research questions through theoretical characterization, error analysis, and sample complexity bounds, supported by illustrative empirical examples.

**Rethinking the Feasible Reward Set for Multi-agent Systems (Section 3):**

1. **Initial Definition and Limitations:** The paper first extends the single-agent definition of a feasible reward set to MAIRL by defining it as the set of all reward functions under which a single observed Nash equilibrium ($\pi_{\text{Nash}}$) remains an equilibrium. It then introduces the "Nash Imitation Gap" as an optimality criterion to measure the exploitability of a recovered policy. Through theoretical analysis (Proposition 3.4) and empirical illustration (Figure 2), the authors demonstrate that relying on a single Nash equilibrium observation leads to an insufficiently constrained feasible reward set. This set can include reward functions that fundamentally alter the game's nature, allowing for new equilibria not present in the original game, leading to high exploitability. This problem is termed "equilibrium ambiguity."

2. **Addressing Ambiguity with Entropy Regularization:** To resolve the equilibrium ambiguity and selection problem, the authors propose focusing on entropy-regularized Markov games. These games are known to yield unique Quantal Response Equilibria (QRE), reflecting agents with bounded rationality. Empirical validation (Figure 3) suggests that QRE observations reduce the emergence of new, exploitable pure equilibria and lead to recovered rewards with higher correlation to the true rewards compared to standard Nash equilibrium observations.

3. **Characterization of Feasible Rewards in Entropy-Regularized Games:** For the entropy-regularized setting, the authors provide an explicit characterization of feasible reward functions (Lemma 3.6). This formulation expresses the reward function for an agent in terms of its optimal policy (QRE), the opponent's QRE policy, the state value function, and the transition dynamics. This characterization highlights the interdependencies in multi-agent reward inference.

4. **Error Propagation and Sample Complexity:**
- **Error Propagation:** The paper analyzes how errors in estimating the transition probabilities and the expert QRE policies (both the agent's own and the opponent's) propagate to the recovered feasible reward function (Theorem 3.7). This analysis reveals that estimation errors are amplified by the inverse of the opponent's policy probability and directly influenced by the total variation distance between true and estimated opponent policies.
- **Sample Complexity:** Assuming access to a generative model and a minimum probability bound ($\Delta_{\min}$) for agent actions, the authors derive a sample complexity bound for uniformly sampling (Theorem 3.9). The total number of samples required to satisfy a defined optimality criterion (Regularized Optimality Criterion, Definition E.2) is of order $\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\gamma^2 R_{\max}^2|S||A||B|}{(1-\gamma)^4 \varepsilon^2 \Delta_{\min}^4}\right)$. This bound demonstrates a dependency on the product of action spaces, which would be exponential in the number of players for general *n*-player games.

**Identifiability in Multi-agent Games (Section 4):**

1. **Average Reward Identification:** The authors demonstrate that in general-sum Markov games without additional structural assumptions, exact reward identifiability (up to constants) is generally not possible for the full reward function $R(s, a, b)$. However, they show that average reward identification is achievable. By fixing the opponent's policy, an *n*-player Markov game reduces to an induced single-agent MDP for each player, where the reward is the average reward received by that player. Leveraging results from single-agent IRL, they establish conditions (specifically, a rank condition on a matrix involving induced transition dynamics across multiple environments or opponent policies) under which this average reward can be identified up to a constant (Theorem 4.1). They also provide a sample complexity for estimating these induced transitions (Theorem 4.2).

2. **Reward Identification in Linearly Separable Markov Games:** To achieve identifiability of the specific reward function $R(s, a, b)$ beyond just the average, the paper considers a structural assumption: linearly separable rewards, where an agent's reward can be decomposed into terms dependent solely on its own action and its opponent's action (e.g., $R_1(s, a, b) = R_A(s, a) + R_B(s, b)$). Under this assumption, and given observations from multiple environments with different opponent policies, a system of equations for the reward components can be formed. Proposition 4.3 outlines rank conditions on an extended matrix that, if met, allow for the identification of the separable reward components up to an additive constant, provided one component is normalized (e.g., $R_B(s, b_0) = 0$).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings concerning the theoretical underpinnings of Multi-agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning:

1. **Inadequacy of Single Nash Equilibrium Observations:** A primary finding is that simply extending single-agent IRL's feasible reward set definition to MAIRL using a single observed Nash equilibrium is problematic. Such a set can contain reward functions that fundamentally alter the game's strategic structure, allowing for the existence of new equilibria in the recovered game that lead to high exploitability in the true game, quantifiable as a Nash Gap of order $(1-\gamma)^{-1}$. This highlights a key difference and challenge in MAIRL compared to its single-agent counterpart.

2. **Mitigation of Ambiguity with Entropy Regularization:** The adoption of entropy-regularized Markov games, which yield unique Quantal Response Equilibria (QRE), is demonstrated to be a viable approach to address the equilibrium selection problem. Empirical results suggest that QRE observations provide more informative data for reward recovery, leading to fewer undesirable emergent equilibria and higher correlations between recovered and true reward functions.

3. **Explicit Characterization of Feasible Rewards:** For entropy-regularized Markov games, the paper provides an explicit characterization of the feasible reward set. This formulation expresses an agent's reward function based on its observed QRE policy, the opponent's QRE policy, the estimated value function, and the transition dynamics. This provides a direct framework for understanding the structure of rewards that rationalize observed behavior in this setting.

4. **Error Propagation and Sample Complexity in Regularized MAIRL:** The analysis details how estimation errors in both the transition model and the expert policies (for both agents) propagate to errors in the recovered reward function. The derived sample complexity for uniformly sampling from a generative model is $\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\gamma^2 R_{\max}^2|S||A||B|}{(1-\gamma)^4 \varepsilon^2 \Delta_{\min}^4}\right)$. This bound indicates a dependency that scales polynomially with state space size and product of action spaces (exponential in the number of players for general n-player games), and inversely with the fourth power of the minimum action probability ($\Delta_{\min}$) and the square of the target error ($\varepsilon$).

5. **Conditions for Reward Identifiability:**
- **Average Reward Identifiability:** In general-sum Markov games, exact reward identifiability (up to an additive constant) is not universally possible for the full reward function. However, the average reward received by an agent, given a fixed opponent policy, can be identified under specific rank conditions on the system of equations derived from observing expert behavior in multiple induced environments (e.g., with different opponent policies or transition dynamics).
- **Identifiability in Linearly Separable Games:** When the reward function is assumed to be linearly separable (i.e., decomposable into terms dependent on individual agent actions), identification of the individual reward components (up to a constant) becomes possible. This requires additional conditions, such as observing behavior against different opponent policies and satisfying specific rank conditions on an augmented system of equations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work contributes to the theoretical foundation of Multi-agent Inverse Reinforcement Learning in several ways:

1. **Formalizing MAIRL Challenges:** The paper rigorously identifies and formalizes the unique challenges of MAIRL that distinguish it from single-agent IRL, particularly the "equilibrium ambiguity" arising from the multiplicity of Nash equilibria. This formalization is crucial for developing robust MAIRL algorithms.

2. **Guidance for Algorithm Design:** By demonstrating the limitations of single Nash equilibrium observations, the research suggests that MAIRL algorithms should either account for the full set of equilibria or adopt equilibrium concepts that guarantee uniqueness, such as Quantal Response Equilibria. The empirical results supporting entropy regularization provide a basis for future algorithmic development.

3. **Theoretical Guarantees for Reward Recovery:** The provision of explicit feasible reward characterizations, error propagation analysis, and sample complexity bounds offers initial theoretical guarantees for learning transferable reward functions in multi-agent systems. These bounds provide insights into the resources required for reliable reward inference and highlight the factors that influence learning efficiency.

4. **Understanding Identifiability Limits:** The investigation into reward identifiability clarifies the conditions under which meaningful reward functions can be uniquely inferred in multi-agent settings. The finding that full reward identifiability is challenging without structural assumptions guides future research toward either imposing such assumptions (e.g., linear separability) or focusing on identifying more restricted forms of rewards (e.g., average rewards).

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper outlines several potential directions for future work, including removing the assumption of a generative model, designing algorithms that enforce unique equilibria, and exploring the benefits of observing multiple equilibria. This indicates that the work serves as a foundational step, opening avenues for both theoretical advancements and practical algorithm development in MAIRL. The insights gained are relevant for fields like multi-robot control, autonomous driving, and game AI, where understanding underlying motivations from observed behavior is critical.
