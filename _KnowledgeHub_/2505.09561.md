# Learning Long-Context Diffusion Policies via Past-Token Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> This Stanford University research introduces Past-Token Prediction (PTP) to enable diffusion policies to effectively learn from long observational contexts by explicitly regularizing temporal action dependencies. The approach yields a 50% average performance improvement on complex simulation tasks and over 4x higher success rates on real-world tasks compared to baselines, while also speeding up training by more than 5x through a multi-stage process.

## Problem

- Many complex robotic tasks are non-Markovian, necessitating policies to reason over long histories of observations and actions.
- Existing approaches to handling long contexts in robot learning often discard crucial historical information, leading to performance drops.
- Diffusion policies surprisingly under-utilize past action information, resulting in weaker temporal dependencies compared to expert demonstrations.

## Method

- Introduces Past-Token Prediction (**PTP**), an auxiliary task that explicitly regularizes the policy to jointly predict past and future action tokens given an observation history.
- Develops a memory-efficient, multi-stage training strategy involving encoder pre-training, visual feature caching, and policy head training on cached long-context embeddings.
- Leverages **PTP** during inference for ==test-time== self-verification, selecting the sampled action sequence whose reconstructed past actions best match the ground-truth executed actions.

## Results

- Past-Token Prediction (PTP) yielded a **50%** average performance improvement for diffusion policies on long-context simulation tasks and achieved nearly **20%** better performance than regression-based counterparts.
- On real-world history-critical tasks, PTP-trained policies achieved an average **70%** success rate, outperforming no-history baselines by over **4x**.
- The multi-stage training strategy with embedding caching provided over a **5x** speedup in training time for complex long-context policies, making training computationally feasible.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modern diffusion-based policies, despite conditioning on long observation histories, frequently fail to capture essential dependencies between past and future actions, leading to weaker temporal dependencies than expert demonstrations.
> - The benefits of Past-Token Prediction (PTP) primarily arise from enhancing the policy head's (action decoder's) temporal modeling, rather than the visual encoder, which enables a memory-efficient multi-stage training strategy.
> - Explicitly forcing the policy to reconstruct its own past actions through PTP effectively regularizes temporal consistency, allowing diffusion policies to genuinely leverage long historical contexts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of **imitation learning (IL)**, **long-context modeling**, and **diffusion models** in robotics.

**Imitation Learning (IL)** has long been a powerful paradigm for teaching robots complex behaviors from human demonstrations. Early IL methods often framed the problem as a direct mapping from observation to action. More recently, the field has seen a shift towards modeling the *distribution* of expert demonstrations, leading to methods like behavior cloning with generative models, including diffusion models. This paper operates firmly within this modern context, aiming to enhance the efficacy of IL for increasingly complex tasks.

A critical and persistent challenge in robot learning is **handling long sequences of observations and actions**, often referred to as "long-context policies." Many realistic robotic tasks are inherently non-Markovian, meaning that optimal actions depend not just on the current state but on a history of past observations (e.g., visual occlusions, multi-stage tasks) and past actions (e.g., stylistic consistency, previously committed strategies). Existing approaches to address this include truncating context, engineering compact representations (keyframes, summaries), or using autoregressive action tokenizers. However, these often risk discarding crucial historical information or struggle to capture long-range dependencies effectively.

The paper focuses on **diffusion-based policies**, a class of generative models that have recently achieved remarkable success in visuomotor control due to their ability to model complex, multi-modal action distributions. Notable examples include the foundational Diffusion Policy itself, and its integration into larger generalist models like RT-1 and RT-2. While powerful, the authors identify a previously underexplored limitation of these policies when scaling to long contexts: a surprising *under-reliance* on past action information, resulting in weaker temporal dependencies compared to expert demonstrations. This contrasts sharply with the classic "copycat problem" in IL, where policies *over-rely* on past actions. This insight is a significant contribution to the understanding of failure modes in modern IL systems.

The work also connects to the broader trend of **test-time scaling** and self-correction mechanisms, drawing parallels with advancements in large language models (LLMs) and image generation. By leveraging the internal structure of the policy for self-verification during inference, the paper contributes to the growing body of literature on improving model performance through additional compute at deployment, rather than solely during training.

In summary, this paper pushes the boundaries of robot imitation learning by specifically addressing the unique challenges of long-context modeling within the highly effective framework of diffusion policies. It offers a novel regularization technique and a practical training strategy that not only enhances performance on complex, history-critical tasks but also significantly improves training efficiency, thus making advanced robot capabilities more accessible.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enable the learning of effective long-context policies from demonstrations for complex robotic tasks**, specifically addressing the issues of performance degradation and prohibitive training costs that arise when scaling context length.

The motivation stems from several critical observations and challenges:

1. **Non-Markovian Task Requirements**: Many real-world robotic tasks necessitate reasoning over extended sequences of observations and actions. Examples include manipulation under occlusions, multi-stage tasks where early steps dictate later strategies, or tasks requiring consistent execution styles over time. Current methods often struggle to capture these long-range temporal dependencies.
2. **Limitations of Existing Long-Context Solutions**: Prior approaches to handle long contexts typically involve limiting the input history (truncation, keyframe selection, summarization) to manage computational burden. However, these strategies risk discarding information that might be crucial for subsequent decisions, leading to performance drops.
3. **Identified Failure Mode in Modern Diffusion Policies**: The authors pinpoint a novel and counter-intuitive problem. Unlike classical regression-based imitation learning methods which often suffer from "copycat behavior" (over-reliance on past actions), they discover that *modern diffusion-based policies, despite conditioning on long observation histories, frequently fail to capture essential dependencies between past and future actions*. This leads to policies exhibiting *weaker* temporal dependencies than the expert demonstrations (action predictability ratio significantly below 1). Bridging this gap is a core motivation.
4. **Prohibitive Training Costs**: Training end-to-end visuomotor policies with high-dimensional image sequences and long contexts incurs rapidly escalating memory and computational demands. This makes large-scale training excessively expensive and impractical.
5. **Robustness of Sampled Predictions**: Even when policies learn effectively, the stochastic nature of generative models like diffusion policies can lead to sub-optimal or inconsistent sampled actions at test time. A mechanism to improve the robustness of these predictions is desirable.

In essence, the paper aims to develop a method that allows diffusion policies to truly leverage long historical contexts without incurring excessive computational overhead or degrading performance due to a failure to capture critical temporal action dependencies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Past-Token Prediction (PTP)**, along with a multi-stage training recipe and a test-time verification mechanism, offers a comprehensive solution for learning long-context diffusion policies.

#### 3.1. Past-Token Prediction (PTP)

The core innovation is **Past-Token Prediction (PTP)**, an auxiliary task designed to explicitly regularize the policy's temporal modeling. While standard approaches predict only future actions (or a chunk of them), PTP extends this prediction window to include past actions.

- **Objective**: Given a sequence of observations $o_{t-k:t}$ (current and $k$ past observations), the policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained to jointly predict action tokens from a past time step ($t-k$) up to an upcoming future time step ($t+h$), i.e., $\hat{a}_{t-k:t+h} = \pi_\theta(o_{t-k:t})$.
- **Mechanism**: By forcing the policy to reconstruct its own past actions from the given observation history, PTP encourages the model to actively preserve and attend to information about past actions. This directly counters the observed "under-reliance" on historical action information in diffusion policies.
- **Key Insight**: Through ablation studies, the authors discover that the benefits of PTP primarily manifest in the *policy head* (action decoder) responsible for sequence modeling, rather than the visual encoder. This insight is crucial for the memory-efficient training strategy.

#### 3.2. Memory-Efficient Training with PTP (Multi-stage Strategy)

To overcome the computational burden of end-to-end training with long contexts, a three-stage training recipe is introduced:

1. **Encoder Training**: A visual encoder is initially trained with a *short* observation context but a *long* prediction horizon. This pre-training step ensures the encoder learns to extract robust visual representations that are informative enough for subsequent action prediction.
2. **Feature Caching**: After encoder training, it is frozen. Embeddings for all frames in the entire training dataset are precomputed and cached. This eliminates the need to repeatedly process high-dimensional visual inputs through the encoder during policy training.
3. **Policy Training**: Finally, the policy head (action decoder) is trained. This stage conditions on the *cached long-context visual embeddings*. Here, the PTP objective is applied, enabling the policy to learn long-range temporal dependencies from the precomputed, compact visual features.

This multi-stage approach significantly reduces memory and computational overhead, making it feasible to train policies with very long observation contexts.

#### 3.3. Test-Time Verification with PTP

PTP is further leveraged during inference as a self-verification mechanism to enhance the robustness of sampled action sequences.

- **Candidate Sampling**: At each inference step, the policy generates multiple candidate action sequences ($B$ samples). Each candidate sequence, $\hat{a}^{(i)}$, includes both reconstructed past actions and predicted future actions.
- **Self-Verification**: Since the actual past actions (up to $t-1$) are known (having already been executed), the policy can assess the consistency of each sampled candidate. The candidate whose *reconstructed past actions* best match the *ground-truth executed past actions* is selected as the final output. The selection criteria is minimizing the L2 norm between the predicted past actions and the true past actions.
- **Benefits**: This process allows the policy to "self-correct" by choosing the most temporally consistent action sequence, mitigating potential errors from suboptimal single samples or unexpected environmental changes. It is also parallelizable on GPUs, adding minimal computational overhead during deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Past-Token Prediction (PTP) is rigorously evaluated across a diverse set of tasks in both simulation and the real world, demonstrating significant improvements in policy performance, temporal modeling, and training efficiency.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Experiments were conducted on six simulation tasks: Push-T, Square, Tool Hang, and Transport (from RoboMimic), and two newly introduced history-critical tasks, Long-Horizon Square and Long-Horizon ALOHA.

- **PTP Mitigates Deficiencies in Temporal Action Dependencies**: By varying the number of past tokens predicted (c=1 for no-PTP, c=8 for half-PTP, c=16 for full-PTP), the authors show that PTP consistently increases the action predictability ratio. The non-PTP baseline exhibits 10-100x weaker action predictability compared to expert demonstrations, whereas full-PTP policies achieve temporal dependencies comparable to experts (Fig. 6). This directly validates PTP's ability to correct the "under-reliance on history" problem.
- **PTP Significantly Improves Policy Performance**: Averaged across all six tasks, PTP yields a **50% improvement** for diffusion-based policies when conditioned on long contexts, and nearly **20% better performance** than regression-based counterparts. The gains are particularly striking on history-critical long-horizon tasks (Long-Horizon Square, Long-Horizon ALOHA), where PTP-trained policies achieve near-perfect success rates, while baselines (no-history or no-PTP) struggle with success rates below 30% (Fig. 9).
- **PTP-trained Policies Benefit from Longer Contexts**: Contrary to common observations where performance degrades with longer contexts, PTP-trained policies show progressive performance enhancement with increasing observation history length (from 2 to 16 time steps). This benefit is most pronounced in complex tasks like Transport and the new long-horizon tasks, demonstrating that PTP truly enables effective utilization of extended histories (Fig. 7).
- **Embedding Caching Accelerates Training**: The proposed multi-stage training strategy with embedding caching dramatically improves efficiency. It achieves comparable performance in just **20% of the training time** and surpasses non-cached training within 40% of the compute budget (Fig. 8). This represents a **>5x speedup** in training for complex long-context policies.
- **PTP Verification Boosts Test-Time Performance**: Leveraging PTP for self-verification during inference (by sampling multiple candidates and selecting based on consistency with executed past actions) provides notable performance gains. Increasing the sampling budget from 1 to 5 leads to an approximate **5% improvement in success rate** on challenging tasks like Tool Hang, Transport, and Long Square (Fig. 10).
- **Ablation Studies (Appendix A)**:
 - **PTP's Benefit is in the Decoder**: Ablations showed that applying PTP only to the action decoder recovers full PTP policy performance, while applying it only to the visual encoder does not. This confirms that PTP primarily enhances temporal modeling in the policy head, directly supporting the multi-stage training strategy (Fig. 12).
 - **Reduced Reliance on Action Chunking**: PTP-trained policies with long contexts and single-step actions (chunk size 1) significantly outperform short-context policies and approach the performance of policies using larger action chunks. This suggests PTP allows for more reactive, closed-loop control without relying on open-loop chunking (Fig. 13).
 - **PTP Critical Even with Past Actions as Input**: Even when past actions are explicitly provided as input to the policy, PTP remains crucial for strong performance, highlighting its fundamental role in internal temporal structure modeling (Fig. 14).

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

The method was tested on four history-critical real-world tasks using Franka Panda and ALOHA bimanual robot platforms (block switch, two scoops, mug replacement, tape replacement).

- **PTP Outperforms Baselines by >4x**: The no-history baseline is limited to an average 15% success due to lack of context. The no-PTP baseline, conditioning on history without PTP, yields near-zero success on three of four tasks. In stark contrast, PTP-trained policies achieve an average **70% success rate** (Fig. 11). On the challenging Tape Replacement task, PTP achieves **80% success**, while baselines completely fail.
- **Qualitative Improvements**: PTP-trained policies exhibit improvements in both **high-level planning** (e.g., replacing the correct object, accurate counting) and **low-level motor control** (successful grasps, accurate placements), leading to more coherent and reliable robot behavior, as shown in supplementary videos. Baselines suffered from distinct high-level (no-history) or low-level (no-PTP) failure modes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work "Learning Long-Context Diffusion Policies via Past-Token Prediction" presents a significant advance in robot imitation learning, offering a practical and effective solution to a long-standing challenge. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Addresses a Core Limitation in Modern IL**: The paper uniquely identifies and remedies a crucial weakness in contemporary diffusion policies: their tendency to under-utilize historical action information. By introducing Past-Token Prediction (PTP), it provides a targeted regularization mechanism that strengthens temporal dependencies, making these powerful generative models truly capable of long-context reasoning. This is a fundamental contribution to the understanding and improvement of diffusion-based robot policies.
- **Enables Complex, Non-Markovian Robotic Tasks**: The dramatic improvements in success rates on history-critical tasks, both simulated (e.g., Long-Horizon Square/ALOHA) and real-world (e.g., Tape Replacement), demonstrate that PTP allows robots to perform tasks requiring complex memory, multi-stage planning, and consistent execution styles that were previously out of reach for current state-of-the-art methods. This expands the practical applicability of imitation learning to a broader spectrum of real-world problems.
- **Significant Training Efficiency Gains**: The multi-stage training strategy with embedding caching represents a practical breakthrough. By accelerating training by over 10 times and reducing memory overhead, it makes learning long-context policies computationally feasible and scalable. This is crucial for iterating on models and for future development of even more complex, high-dimensional robotic skills.
- **Enhanced Policy Robustness and Reliability**: The integration of PTP into a test-time self-verification mechanism provides an additional layer of robustness. By allowing the policy to choose the most consistent action sequence from multiple samples, it mitigates the effects of noisy predictions and helps maintain coherence in closed-loop settings, which is vital for real-world deployment.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: While focused on diffusion policies, the core insight of explicitly regularizing temporal consistency through "past-token prediction" could be generalized to other generative or sequential policy architectures. The paper also opens up avenues for future research in designing action tokenizers that intrinsically preserve temporal structure and in further accelerating inference for large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which are a major trend in robot learning.

In conclusion, this research not only offers a highly effective method for improving long-context robot imitation learning but also deepens our understanding of the specific failure modes of modern diffusion policies. Its practical contributions in performance, efficiency, and robustness pave the way for more intelligent and capable autonomous robots that can reliably execute complex tasks requiring extensive temporal reasoning.
