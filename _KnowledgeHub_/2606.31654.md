# DynFly: Dynamic-Aware Continuous Trajectory Generation for UAV Vision-Language Navigation in Urban Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> DynFly introduces a framework that bridges high-level vision-language navigation reasoning with continuous UAV trajectory generation in urban environments. The system uses B-spline representations and dynamic-aware supervision to produce stable, executable flight paths, achieving improved navigation performance and trajectory quality on the OpenUAV benchmark.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) methods for UAVs primarily output discrete actions, local targets, or sparse waypoints.
- This creates a 'motion-interface gap' because UAVs require continuous, dynamically feasible trajectories that respect kinematic constraints for real-world flight.
- Current approaches struggle to directly translate high-level navigation intent into smooth and executable continuous motion.

## Method

- **DynFly** proposes a modular motion layer that separates high-level visual-language reasoning from low-level continuous motion generation.
- It reformulates trajectory generation as ==conditional flow-field modeling== in ==B-spline control-point space==, representing trajectories as compact, smooth B-spline curves.
- A ==Spline-DiT== generator, trained with ==flow matching==, learns to produce these control points conditioned on visual-language context and local navigation targets, guided by ==dynamic-aware loss functions== for position, velocity, acceleration, heading consistency, and target alignment.

## Results

- DynFly improved the Success Rate (SR) by **2.08%** (from **38.54%** to **40.62%**) and reduced Navigation Error (NE) by **4.39m** on the OpenUAV Test Seen dataset (full-data setting) compared to previous state-of-the-art.
- On the OpenUAV Test Unseen dataset, the framework achieved the best performance across all **six** navigation metrics, including a **4.69** improvement in Normalized Dynamic Time Warping (NDTW) and a **4.51m** reduction in NE on the full split.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the B-spline representation, the Spline-DiT generator, and the dynamic-aware loss components each contribute positively to improved navigation metrics and enhanced trajectory quality, yielding smoother and more stable flight paths.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling continuous, dynamically-aware trajectories through B-spline representation significantly enhances both navigation performance and physical executability for UAVs.
> - Dynamic-aware supervision, integrating motion priors (velocity, acceleration, heading consistency) into the learning objective, is crucial for generating stable and physically plausible UAV flight paths.
> - A generative flow-matching approach in B-spline control-point space effectively learns complex trajectory distributions, enabling robust and flexible continuous motion generation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) aims to enable embodied agents to navigate environments based on natural language instructions and visual observations. Recent advancements in multimodal large models have substantially enhanced high-level perception and reasoning in VLN, including for Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) in what is termed UAV-VLN. These models have improved visual perception, language understanding, and the capacity for high-level navigation planning in complex 3D environments.

Existing UAV-VLN methods often focus on improving semantic understanding and spatial reasoning through stronger visual encoders, geometry-enhanced scene representations, historical memory, or large vision-language models. Approaches like TravelUAV, AutoFly, and LongFly have demonstrated advancements in high-level reasoning. However, a common characteristic of these methods is their output format: discrete actions, local targets, or sparse waypoint sequences. While these outputs address "where to fly" by defining navigation goals, they provide an incomplete representation for UAVs, which operate in continuous three-dimensional space.

UAV flight requires continuous motion that respects kinematic constraints, including coherent changes in displacement, velocity, acceleration, and heading. Discretely defined waypoints do not explicitly model these continuous transitions, which can lead to trajectories that are not smooth, dynamically feasible, or easily executable by a downstream flight controller. This disparity constitutes a "motion-interface gap" between high-level navigation intent and the requirements of continuous UAV flight execution.

Prior research on continuous UAV trajectory modeling primarily involves traditional methods like B-splines or minimum-snap optimization for generating dynamically feasible trajectories, often relying on geometric maps or local sensing rather than visual-language intent. More recent generative action models, such as Diffusion Policy, have shown potential for generating continuous action sequences but generally operate in action spaces rather than explicitly exploiting the continuous curve structure of UAV trajectories.

DynFly addresses this gap by introducing an explicit motion interface. It aims to bridge high-level visual-language reasoning with continuous UAV motion generation, providing a representation that is both contextually guided and dynamically aware, thus contributing to the development of more robust and physically executable UAV-VLN systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from an identified "motion-interface gap" in current UAV-VLN systems. While multimodal large models have significantly advanced UAV-VLN by enhancing high-level perception and reasoning capabilities, most existing methods primarily focus on generating discrete navigation outputs. These outputs, such as discrete actions, local targets, or sparse waypoints, effectively define *where* a UAV should fly, but they do not adequately model the continuous transition required for actual UAV motion.

UAVs operate in a continuous 3D environment, and their successful navigation depends on generating executable flight trajectories that exhibit motion continuity and adhere to kinematic constraints. Directly translating sparse waypoints into flight commands can lead to discontinuous motion, abrupt changes in velocity or acceleration, and unstable local transitions, rendering the trajectories difficult for a physical UAV to execute. This problem highlights a deficiency in current approaches: the high-level navigation intent, derived from visual and language inputs, is not directly translated into a dynamically feasible and continuous UAV flight path.

To address this limitation, the key objectives of the DynFly framework are:

1. **To bridge the high-level navigation intent and executable continuous UAV motion:** This involves developing a framework that explicitly models the conversion from abstract navigation reasoning to a concrete, continuous flight trajectory.
2. **To generate continuous, stable, and executable UAV trajectories:** The framework aims to produce trajectories that are smooth, maintain consistent heading, and are dynamically feasible for UAVs.
3. **To represent expert trajectories in B-spline control-point space:** This objective aims to leverage the mathematical properties of B-splines, such as local continuity and smoothness, to represent trajectories in a compact and differentiable manner, moving beyond discrete waypoints.
4. **To learn conditional trajectory generation via flow matching:** Employing a lightweight Spline-DiT generator to learn the probabilistic distribution of B-spline control points conditioned on visual-language context and local targets, thereby enabling robust and flexible trajectory generation.
5. **To incorporate UAV-oriented dynamic-aware supervision:** This involves integrating motion priors during the training process, specifically over position, finite-difference velocity, finite-difference acceleration, heading consistency, and local target alignment. These priors are designed to guide the generated trajectories to inherently satisfy the physical motion characteristics and constraints of UAVs.
6. **To maintain compatibility with existing UAV-VLN visual-language reasoning pipelines:** The framework is designed as an independent motion layer, allowing it to be integrated with existing high-level reasoning modules without altering their core design, thereby enhancing their practical applicability.

By achieving these objectives, DynFly seeks to enhance both the navigation performance and the physical executability of UAV trajectories in complex urban environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DynFly is proposed as a dynamic-aware continuous trajectory generation framework that separates high-level visual-language reasoning from low-level continuous motion generation, connecting them via a motion interface.

**System Overview:**
The framework comprises two main components:
1. **Visual-Language Front-end:** This module is responsible for high-level semantic and spatial reasoning. It takes multi-view RGB observations ($R_t$), a natural language instruction ($L$), and the current UAV pose ($S_t$) as input. Using a pre-trained model (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B with an EVA ViT-G vision tower), it produces a visual-language context representation ($h_t$) and a local 3D navigation target ($g_t$). This front-end determines "where the UAV should fly next."
2. **Motion Layer (DynFly's Contribution):** This layer consumes ($h_t$, $g_t$) and transforms this navigation intent into a continuous short-horizon 3D UAV trajectory, addressing "how to fly."

**Pseudo Control-Point Label Generation:**
Instead of directly predicting discrete waypoints, DynFly reformulates trajectory generation as conditional flow-field modeling in B-spline control-point space. The initial step involves converting discrete expert waypoint trajectories ($Y^*$) into continuous-curve B-spline control points ($C^*$), which serve as pseudo control-point labels for training.
- An expert trajectory $Y^* = [y^*_0..., y^*_{T-1}]$ is given.
- An open-uniform clamped B-spline basis of degree $K$ (typically 3 for cubic splines) with $N_{cp}$ control points (typically 8) is established.
- A basis matrix $\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times N_{cp}}$ is constructed, where $\Phi_{t,j}$ represents the B-spline basis function $N_{j,K}(u_t)$ evaluated at normalized trajectory time $u_t = t/(T-1)$.
- The pseudo control-point label $C^* \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{cp} \times 3}$ is obtained by solving a least-squares problem: $C^* = \arg \min_C \|\Phi C - Y^*\|_F^2$. This is resolved using the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse: $C^* = \Phi^\dagger Y^*$. This process ensures that the decoded curve $\Phi C^*$ approximates the expert trajectory $Y^*$, providing a compact and smooth representation.

**DiT-Based Control Point Flow Generation (Spline-DiT):**
The core of the motion layer is a Spline-DiT generator that learns to produce control points $\hat{C}$ conditioned on $h_t$ and $g_t$. This is achieved through flow matching, which trains a conditional vector field $v_\theta(X_\tau, h_\tau)$ in the control-point space, transforming a noisy initial state ($X_0$) into the target control points ($C^*$).
- **Condition Encoder:** The visual-language context ($h_t$), local target ($g_t$), and flow-matching generation time ($\tau \in$) are projected into a shared hidden space. These embeddings ($c^{vis}_t, c^{tar}_t, c_\tau$) are then fused into a global condition vector $h_\tau$.
- **Spline-DiT Architecture:** The intermediate control-point state $X_\tau$ is embedded as tokens, augmented with learnable positional encodings. These tokens are processed by a Transformer encoder (similar to a Diffusion Transformer, DiT). The global condition $h_\tau$ is normalized and broadcast to all control-point tokens. A final LayerNorm and linear layer predict the control-point vector field $v_\theta(X_\tau, h_\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{cp} \times 3}$.
- **Training with Flow Matching:** During training, Gaussian noise $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and a random flow time $\tau \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$ are sampled. The intermediate state $X_\tau$ is generated by linear interpolation: $X_\tau = (1-\tau)Z + \tau C^*$. The model is trained to predict the target vector field $u_\tau = C^* - Z$. The flow-matching objective is $L_{FM} = \mathbb{E}_{h} [\|v_\theta(X_\tau, h_\tau) - (C^* - Z)\|_2^2]$.
- **Inference:** During inference, the model initializes with Gaussian noise $C^{(0)}$, performs Euler integration over $S$ steps (e.g., 10 steps) to iteratively refine the control points using the learned vector field. The final control points $C^{(S)}$ are then decoded using the B-spline basis matrix $\Phi$ to obtain the predicted 3D trajectory $\hat{Y} = \Phi C^{(S)}$.

**Loss Design (Dynamic-Aware Supervision):**
To ensure both control-point generation accuracy and trajectory-level dynamic consistency, DynFly employs a joint objective function.
- **Flow Matching Loss ($L_{FM}$):** Supervises the control-point generation in the latent space.
- **Position Loss ($L_{pos}$):** Measures the SmoothL1 difference between the decoded trajectory $\hat{Y}$ and the expert trajectory $Y^*$.
- **Velocity Loss ($L_{vel}$):** Approximates velocity using first differences ($\hat{v}_t = \hat{y}_{t+1} - \hat{y}_t$) and applies SmoothL1 loss to match expert velocities.
- **Acceleration Loss ($L_{acc}$):** Approximates acceleration using second differences ($\hat{a}_t = \hat{y}_{t+2} - 2\hat{y}_{t+1} + \hat{y}_t$) and applies SmoothL1 loss. An additional $\ell_2$ penalty on $\|\hat{a}_t\|_2^2$ regularizes excessive acceleration, with a weight $\alpha = 0.1$.
- **Heading Consistency Loss ($L_{head}$):** Encourages consistent local flight directions by minimizing the angular difference between normalized velocity directions of $\hat{Y}$ and $Y^*$.
- **Target Alignment Loss ($L_{tar}$):** Anchors an intermediate point of the predicted trajectory (e.g., $y_r$ at $r = \min(4, T-1)$) to the local target $g$ provided by the front-end, using SmoothL1 loss. This ensures responsiveness to the high-level target while allowing for dynamic smoothing in later points.

The final training objective is a weighted sum of these losses: $L = \lambda_{FM}L_{FM} + \lambda_{pos}L_{pos} + \lambda_{vel}L_{vel} + \lambda_{acc}L_{acc} + \lambda_{head}L_{head} + \lambda_{tar}L_{tar}$, with empirically determined weights (e.g., $\lambda_{FM}=1.0, \lambda_{pos}=1.0, \lambda_{vel}=0.2, \lambda_{acc}=0.05, \lambda_{head}=0.05, \lambda_{tar}=0.1$). This comprehensive loss design guides the model to generate trajectories that are both contextually appropriate and dynamically consistent with UAV motion characteristics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of DynFly was evaluated through extensive experiments on the OpenUAV UAV-VLN benchmark, comparing it against several state-of-the-art methods and conducting detailed ablation studies.

**Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods:**
DynFly was compared against baselines such as Random Action, Fixed Action, CMA, TravelUAV, NavFoM, LongFly, and SpatialFly. For fair comparison, LongFly, SpatialFly, and DynFly utilized the same Qwen2.5-3B visual-language front-end.

- **Quantitative Evaluation on OpenUAV Test Seen Dataset (Table I):**
 - **25% Low-Data Setting:** DynFly consistently achieved superior performance across all metrics (NE, SR, OSR, SPL) on Full, Easy, and Hard splits. For instance, on the Full split, DynFly reduced Navigation Error (NE) from 113.31 m (SpatialFly) to 63.96 m and improved Success Rate (SR) from 16.84% to 32.93%. This suggests DynFly's ability to learn stable trajectory generation even with limited data.
 - **100% Full-Data Setting:** DynFly maintained its lead, showing consistent improvements. On the Full split, it improved SR from 38.54% (SpatialFly) to 40.62% and reduced NE from 58.05 m to 53.66 m. On the Hard split, NE was reduced from 84.76 m to 76.85 m. These results indicate that the dynamic-aware motion interface enhances navigation performance on top of strong visual-language frameworks.

- **Quantitative Evaluation on OpenUAV Test Unseen Dataset (Table II):**
 - **Full Split:** DynFly achieved the best performance across all six metrics (NDTW, SDTW, NE, SR, OSR, SPL). It improved Normalized Dynamic Time Warping (NDTW) from 11.20 (SpatialFly) to 15.89 and reduced NE from 87.82 m to 83.31 m.
 - **Hard Split:** Improvements were more pronounced, with NE reduced from 108.56 m to 99.73 m. This suggests that in challenging unseen environments, modeling continuous motion is particularly beneficial.
 - **Easy Split:** DynFly achieved the best NDTW, SDTW, SR, OSR, and SPL, while NE was marginally higher than SpatialFly, indicating strong performance in simpler scenarios as well.

- **Performance on Unseen Map and Unseen Object (Figure 6):**
 - DynFly showed the best overall performance across Overall, Unseen Object, and Unseen Map protocols.
 - It achieved larger improvements on the Unseen Object split, suggesting better generalization when encountering novel object categories. While the performance gap was smaller on the Unseen Map split (due to completely unseen scene layouts), DynFly still exhibited the best overall performance. This indicates improved generalization to both object-level and environment-level changes.

**Ablation Studies (Table III):**
The contribution of each component was systematically evaluated:
- **B-spline Representation:** Replacing a conventional MLP trajectory head with a B-spline head consistently improved navigation metrics (e.g., NDTW from 12.30 to 13.72 on Full split), demonstrating its effectiveness for continuous trajectory representation.
- **Spline-DiT Generator:** Introducing Spline-DiT further enhanced performance (e.g., SR increased to 27.28% on Full split), indicating the benefits of a generative model for learning trajectory distributions.
- **Dynamic-Aware Loss:** Adding the dynamic-aware loss resulted in the best overall performance (e.g., NDTW to 15.89, NE to 83.31 m, OSR to 49.28% on Full split). This validated the importance of motion priors in constraining trajectory properties.

**Sensitivity Analysis on Control-Point (Figure 7):**
The study on the number of B-spline control points revealed that eight control points provided the best balance between trajectory representation capacity and generalization, achieving optimal performance. Too few (e.g., two) or too many (e.g., twelve) control points led to reduced navigation performance.

**Sensitivity Analysis on Dynamic-Aware Loss (Table IV & V):**
- **Navigation Performance (Table IV):** The default ("Base Dyn") dynamic-aware loss weights yielded the best navigation performance. Weaker weights did not improve results, while excessively strong weights led to a decrease in performance, suggesting a trade-off between motion guidance and navigation objective optimization.
- **Trajectory Quality (Table V):** "Base Dyn" consistently produced smoother (lower SmoothMean, SmoothVar) and more stable (lower TurnMean) trajectories compared to models without dynamic-aware loss. Overly strong regularization (Strong Dyn) maintained turning stability but significantly degraded overall smoothness, demonstrating that moderate dynamic constraints achieve the best balance.

**Integration with Existing UAV-VLN Frameworks (Table VI):**
When integrated into SpatialFly, DynFly consistently improved all navigation metrics, including NDTW (+2.56), SDTW (+1.21), NE (-5.28 m), SR (+2.14%), OSR (+4.36%), and SPL (+1.89%). This demonstrates its compatibility and modularity, confirming that the improvements originate from the trajectory generation framework rather than the visual-language backbone.

**Generated Trajectory Analysis (Figures 8 & 9):**
- **Qualitative Navigation (Figure 8):** DynFly generated longer, continuous trajectories that successfully reached targets, whereas baselines sometimes terminated prematurely. This indicated more stable navigation behavior.
- **Multi-view Trajectory Visualization (Figure 9):** Visualizations showed that DynFly produced trajectories closer to the ground truth, exhibiting greater smoothness and coherence with consistent flight directions, compared to baselines that showed larger local oscillations and deviations. This supports the effectiveness of the dynamic-aware trajectory layer in generating physically plausible and accurate flight paths.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DynFly contributes to the field of UAV Vision-Language Navigation by addressing a critical "motion-interface gap" that exists between high-level navigation reasoning and the requirements for executable UAV motion. Instead of solely enhancing the visual-language front-end, this work focuses on explicitly modeling the transition from abstract navigation intent to continuous, dynamically feasible UAV trajectories.

The significance of DynFly is underscored by several key aspects:

1. **Improved Navigation Performance:** The framework consistently demonstrates quantitative improvements across various standard navigation metrics (NDTW, SDTW, NE, SR, OSR, SPL) on both seen and unseen environments. This suggests that explicitly modeling continuous motion not only enhances trajectory quality but also contributes to better navigation success rates, accuracy, and path efficiency.
2. **Enhanced Trajectory Quality:** By utilizing a B-spline-based representation and incorporating dynamic-aware supervision over position, velocity, acceleration, and heading, DynFly generates smoother, more stable, and physically executable UAV trajectories. This is crucial for real-world UAV applications, where abrupt movements or unstable paths can lead to inefficiency or safety issues.
3. **Better Generalization:** The experiments indicate that DynFly exhibits improved generalization, particularly in unseen object scenarios. This suggests that dynamic-aware trajectory generation aids in producing robust flight paths even when object categories change, thereby enhancing the adaptability of UAV-VLN systems to novel environments.
4. **Modular and Compatible Design:** The framework is designed as a modular component that can be integrated with existing UAV-VLN visual-language front-ends without requiring modifications to their core reasoning pipelines. This compatibility facilitates broader adoption and allows for enhancements to current systems without a complete overhaul.
5. **Bridging High-Level Reasoning and Low-Level Execution:** DynFly provides an effective bridge that transforms high-level semantic and spatial understanding into continuous, low-level flight commands, thereby addressing a fundamental challenge in embodied AI for UAVs. This can lead to more autonomous and reliable UAV operation.

**Potential Impact:**

The development of DynFly has the potential to impact the practical deployment of UAV-VLN systems by enabling the generation of more robust and directly executable flight plans. This could facilitate applications in areas such as:

- **Autonomous Inspection and Monitoring:** UAVs could follow complex natural language instructions to inspect infrastructure or monitor environments with greater precision and stability.
- **Search and Rescue:** Improved trajectory generation could enhance UAVs' ability to navigate challenging terrains and dynamic environments based on real-time instructions.
- **Delivery Services:** More reliable and smooth trajectories could lead to safer and more efficient drone delivery operations in urban settings.
- **Further Research in Embodied AI:** DynFly's approach of integrating dynamic-aware motion modeling could inspire similar developments in other embodied AI domains where continuous, physically constrained actions are critical.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Simulation-Only Evaluation:** The framework has only been evaluated in simulation environments. Its performance on real UAV platforms requires verification.
- **Short-Horizon Focus:** The current framework focuses on short-horizon trajectory generation. Its integration with low-level flight control systems and its effectiveness in long-horizon navigation tasks are not yet fully explored.
- **Practical Application Gaps:** Further advancements are needed in terms of model lightweighting, real-time inference capabilities, and sim-to-real transfer methods to enable practical applications.

Future work includes evaluating DynFly on real UAV platforms, improving its computational efficiency for real-time operation, and exploring its application to a wider range of UAV vision-language navigation and vision-language-action systems.
