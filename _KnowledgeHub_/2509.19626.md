# EgoBridge: Domain Adaptation for Generalizable Imitation from Egocentric Human Data

> [!summary] Summary
> EgoBridge co-trains robot manipulation policies with egocentric human data by explicitly aligning human-robot policy latent spaces via Optimal Transport, using Dynamic Time Warping over action trajectories to guide the transport cost so alignment preserves action-relevant structure, yielding up to 44% absolute success-rate gains and generalization to human-only scenes, objects, and behaviors.

## Problem

- Prior human-robot co-training methods like EgoMimic rely on visual masking, normalization, and retargeting but leave visual, sensor, and kinematic domain gaps largely unresolved.
- Naive co-training assumes a domain-invariant latent-to-action mapping emerges automatically, but induced latent marginals actually diverge into disjoint clusters across domains.
- Standard domain adaptation methods (adversarial training, MMD) align only global marginal distributions and discard fine-grained, temporally correlated action information needed for control.

## Method

- ==Joint Optimal Transport loss== that aligns the empirical joint distribution of encoder latent features and actions between human and robot batches, not just marginal observation distributions.
- ==Dynamic Time Warping== over action sequences builds a pseudo-pairing cost matrix, discounting the OT ground cost for the most behaviorally similar human-robot trajectory pairs via a scalar λ.
- Shared ==DETR-style transformer== encoder trunk with modality-specific input stems and a shared decoder head, trained end-to-end with combined behavior-cloning and weighted ==Sinkhorn OT-joint== losses.

## Results

- Across three real-world tasks (Scoop Coffee, Drawer, Laundry) EgoBridge improves absolute success rate by **7-44%** over co-training, EgoMimic, MimicPlay, and ATM baselines.
- On drawer locations seen only in human data, EgoBridge reaches **33%** success while most baselines fail entirely (**0%**); scoop-to-new-scene generalization holds at **27%** versus near-zero for others.
- Ablations show swapping DTW pairing for MSE drops drawer success rate from **47%** to **14%**, and replacing joint-OT with standard marginal OT or plain co-training also causes large drops, especially in generalization (down to **0%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aligning marginal observation distributions alone is insufficient for cross-embodiment transfer; jointly aligning latent features with their associated actions is what preserves control-relevant structure.
> - Motion-similarity-based soft pseudo-pairing (here via DTW) is a more effective supervision signal for cross-domain alignment than naive distance metrics like MSE.
> - A well-aligned shared latent space lets a policy interpolate entirely new behaviors demonstrated only in cheap human data, suggesting scaling robot competence primarily through human-data collection is viable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning, particularly through imitation learning, has made significant strides, with behavior cloning emerging as a promising paradigm for scaling robot capabilities. However, a major bottleneck remains the acquisition of large-scale, diverse, and high-quality teleoperated robot demonstration data. Unlike large language models (LLMs) and vision models that benefit from vast internet-scale datasets, collecting comparable data for physical robots is economically and practically intractable due to the complexities of real-world deployment, physical interaction, and safety constraints.

This paper addresses this fundamental challenge by proposing to leverage **egocentric human experience data**, collected through increasingly ubiquitous wearable devices like smart glasses (e.g., Meta Project Aria). This type of data is cheap, scalable, and intrinsically embodies human interactions with the world, offering both observations (first-person RGB images) and actions (hand motions). The ability to learn from such data promises to unlock unprecedented scalability for robot policies.

However, integrating human and robot data is fraught with difficulties due to significant **domain gaps**:
- **Visual Appearance:** Humans and robots look different.
- **Sensor Modalities:** Robots often have additional sensors (e.g., wrist cameras) not present in human setups.
- **Kinematics and Dynamics:** Even with shared action spaces (e.g., end-effector poses), kinematic differences and behavioral distribution shifts exist (e.g., humans move faster).

Existing approaches have attempted to bridge these gaps. Early works in learning from human data utilized unlabeled web videos, often requiring complex pseudo-labeling or inverse dynamics models. More directly, methods like EgoMimic and others have explored co-training robot and human demonstrations, often employing techniques like visual masking, data normalization, or motion retargeting. However, these methods often fall short when it comes to truly *generalizing* to novel behaviors or scenarios observed *only* in the human dataset. Simply co-training data from different domains does not automatically guarantee effective knowledge transfer, as noted by recent studies.

EgoBridge positions itself as a crucial advancement within this landscape by reframing the human-robot cross-embodiment learning problem as a **domain adaptation (DA) problem**. Traditional DA methods, such as adversarial training or Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) minimization, primarily focus on global distribution alignment. While effective for tasks like image classification, they often fail to preserve the fine-grained, action-relevant information critical for sequential decision-making in robotics. EgoBridge's novel contribution lies in its **action-aware domain adaptation** approach, specifically using Optimal Transport (OT) to align *joint policy latent features and actions*, rather than just observations. This allows for a more semantically meaningful alignment that preserves the behavioral nuances necessary for successful imitation learning and generalization.

The work also contributes to the broader trend of developing generalist robot policies, as seen in projects like RT-1/RT-2, Pi0.5, OpenVLA, and GR00T N1, which increasingly leverage diverse data sources. EgoBridge provides a method to robustly incorporate human egocentric data into this ecosystem, enhancing the diversity and scalability of available training data for such models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of EgoBridge is to enable robots to effectively learn from a combination of limited teleoperated robot data and abundant, diverse egocentric human experience data, specifically overcoming the significant domain gaps that traditionally impede knowledge transfer. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and leads to distinct objectives:

1. **Scaling Robot Data Collection:** The prohibitive cost and labor intensity of collecting large-scale, diverse robot demonstrations motivate the exploration of alternative data sources. Egocentric human data, readily available from wearable devices, presents a compelling, scalable, and natural source of embodied experience. The goal is to leverage this vast resource to unlock the full potential of end-to-end imitation learning for robotic manipulation.

2. **Bridging Human-Robot Domain Gaps:** The inherent differences between human and robot embodiments—in visual appearance, sensor modalities (e.g., robot wrist cameras vs. human head-mounted cameras), and kinematics—create significant "covariate shifts" that prevent direct, effective knowledge transfer. Standard co-training approaches often lead to disjoint latent representations, undermining the assumption that a shared policy can map observations to actions across domains.

3. **Action-Aware Latent Space Alignment:** The limitations of general domain adaptation techniques, which might align marginal observation distributions but lose critical action-relevant information, highlight the need for a more specialized approach. EgoBridge aims to develop a novel domain adaptation method that explicitly aligns the *joint distributions of latent policy features and corresponding actions* between human and robot domains. This is crucial for ensuring that the learned shared latent space is not only domain-invariant but also preserves the information necessary for precise policy learning.

4. **Enabling Generalization to Novel Scenarios:** Beyond merely improving performance on tasks seen by both human and robot, a core motivation is to achieve two crucial levels of generalization:
 - **Observation Generalization:** Bridging visual and sensor gaps to allow the robot to perform tasks in novel scenes or with novel objects *seen only in human data*. This includes adapting to appearance changes that don't alter the core behavior.
 - **Behavior Generalization:** The most ambitious objective is to enable the robot to learn and execute *entirely new behaviors or task variations* that are demonstrated *only* in the human dataset, without any corresponding robot demonstrations. This would effectively allow the robot's capabilities to scale primarily with human experience.

In essence, EgoBridge seeks to establish a robust, principled framework for cross-embodiment imitation learning that can harness the richness of human demonstrations to produce robot policies capable of executing complex manipulation tasks with high success rates and strong generalization capabilities in the real world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EgoBridge is presented as a unified co-training framework that integrates policy learning with an explicit domain adaptation mechanism to align human and robot policy latent spaces. Its core novelty lies in using Optimal Transport (OT) to align the *joint distributions of latent policy features and actions*, guided by action-aware pseudo-pairing.

#### 3.1. Joint Domain Adaptation via Optimal Transport (OT)

- **Problem Formulation:** The work formalizes cross-embodiment learning as a domain adaptation problem. Instead of merely aligning the marginal distributions of learned latent features `P(f_phi(O))`, EgoBridge aims to align the *joint distributions* `P(f_phi(O), A)` of latent features `z = f_phi(O)` and their corresponding action trajectories `A`.
- **OT Loss (`L_OT-joint`):** Given mini-batches of human `(o_H, a_H)` and robot `(o_R, a_R)` data, a differentiable Sinkhorn OT formulation is used to compute a loss based on the alignment of empirical joint distributions `(f_phi(o_H), a_H)` and `(f_phi(o_R), a_R)`. This loss minimizes the expected transport cost between these joint distributions.
- **Encoder Optimization:** Minimizing `L_OT-joint` directly influences the feature encoder `f_phi`. The gradients encourage `f_phi` to produce latent features that minimize the transport cost, particularly when their associated actions are behaviorally similar. This iteratively shapes the latent space to be domain-invariant with respect to the joint observation-action manifold.

#### 3.2. Designing the Action-Aware OT Cost Function

The success of the joint OT hinges on a well-designed cost function `C((z_H, a_H), (z_R, a_R))` that measures dissimilarity between human and robot latent feature-action pairs, robust to domain differences like temporal misalignments and kinematic variations.

- **Dynamic Time Warping (DTW):** To identify behaviorally similar action sequences despite temporal and kinematic differences, EgoBridge leverages DTW. DTW measures the similarity between two temporal sequences that may vary in speed or duration by finding an optimal alignment path. For two action sequences `a_H` and `a_R`, `DTW(a_H, a_R)` gives a cumulative distance.
- **Soft Supervision for Pseudo-Pairing:** Instead of directly using the DTW cost, it's used as "soft supervision." For each robot action trajectory `a_R_j` in a mini-batch, EgoBridge identifies its most behaviorally similar human action trajectory `a_H_i*` by finding the minimum DTW cost across all human trajectories in the batch (`i*(j) = arg min_i DTW(a_H_i, a_R_j)`).
- **Shaped Cost Matrix (`~C`):** The final cost matrix `~C` for `L_OT-joint` is then constructed:
 - For `(i, j)` pairs where `i = i*(j)` (i.e., behaviorally matched by DTW), the cost is `D_ij * lambda`, where `D_ij = ||f_phi(o_H_i) - f_phi(o_R_j)||^2` is the Euclidean distance between latent features, and `0 < lambda << 1` is a small scalar. This strongly incentivizes OT to match behaviorally similar pairs.
 - For all other `(i, j)` pairs, the cost is simply `D_ij`. This "soft supervision" guides latent space alignment towards behaviorally relevant correspondences.

#### 3.3. EgoBridge System Details

- **Policy Co-Training with Joint Adaptation:** The overall objective function is `L_Total = L_BC-cotrain(phi, theta) + alpha * L_OT-joint(phi)`, where `L_BC-cotrain` is the standard Behavior Cloning loss applied across both human and robot data, and `alpha` is a tunable weight. `L_OT-joint` specifically optimizes the encoder `f_phi`, while `L_BC-cotrain` optimizes the entire network (both `f_phi` and `pi_theta`).
- **Data Sources:**
 - **Egocentric Human Data (`D_H`):** Collected using Meta Project Aria smart glasses, providing egocentric RGB images (`I_H_ego`) and human hand actions (`a_H`) as Cartesian poses in the egocentric camera frame. Actions are converted into fixed-length trajectory chunks with respect to a stable reference frame.
 - **Teleoperated Robot Data (`D_R`):** Collected using a custom robot platform inspired by the Eve robot. It uses Aria glasses for egocentric vision (`I_R_ego`), mounted to emulate human hand-eye configuration, and additional wrist cameras (`I_R_wrist`). Robot actions (`a_R`) are also sequences of future end-effector poses in the egocentric frame.
- **Shared Policy Architecture:**
 - **Encoder (`f_phi`):** A transformer-based architecture. It uses modality-specific "stems" (shallow networks) to tokenize raw observations: a shared vision stem for `I_ego` (human and robot), and separate stems for robot `I_wrist` and proprioceptive data. These tokens are concatenated with `M` learnable context tokens. The encoder trunk (multi-layer transformer) processes this sequence, and the OT loss is applied to the first `M` output tokens, which represent the learned latent features `z`.
 - **Policy Decoder (`pi_theta`):** A shared multi-block transformer decoder (DETR-style). It generates action trajectories by attending to the encoded context `z` using `T` learnable action tokens through alternating self and cross-attention blocks.
- **Action Space:** Actions are represented as trajectory chunks of Cartesian poses (SE(3) + gripper state). For human data, the loss is masked to only include 3D position, while for robot data, it includes 3D position, orientation (MSE), and gripper value. Normalization is applied individually to proprioception and actions for both embodiments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

EgoBridge was evaluated against several baselines in both a reproducible simulation environment (planar pushing) and three challenging real-world single-arm and bimanual manipulation tasks (Drawer, Scoop Coffee, Laundry). The experiments were designed to validate three core hypotheses:

**H1: EgoBridge improves co-training performance for scenarios present in both human and robot data.**
- **Real-World Results:** EgoBridge consistently achieved significant improvements in absolute task success rates, ranging from 7% to 44% over human-augmented imitation learning baselines (Co-train, EgoMimic, MimicPlay, ATM) and robot-only BC policies (Table 1). For instance, in "Scoop Coffee," EgoBridge achieved a 67% success rate compared to 60% for EgoMimic and 53% for Co-train. This indicates that the explicit latent alignment effectively facilitates knowledge transfer, leading to more robust policies even for in-distribution tasks.

**H2: EgoBridge enables generalization to scenarios only seen in human data.**
- **Observation Generalization (Scoop Coffee):** In the Scoop Coffee task, human data introduced a new coffee grinder and a novel scene (table, lighting, height variations) completely unseen by the robot during its training. EgoBridge significantly outperformed all baselines, showing a 7-33% improvement when tested on the new grinder. Crucially, when evaluated on the new grinder *in the new scene*, most baselines failed entirely (0% success), while EgoBridge maintained a performance of 27% (Table 1).
- **Behavior Generalization (Drawer & Sim PushT):** The Drawer task was designed to test learning entirely new motions from human data. Robot data covered 3 out of 4 drawer quadrants, while human data covered all 4. EgoBridge was able to generalize to the novel, robot-unseen drawer locations with a 33% success rate, whereas most other methods failed completely (Table 1).
- **Simulation Results (Push-T):** Similar trends were observed in the simulated Push-T task (Figure 3, Table 7). EgoBridge showed superior generalization to a new background color and a mirrored T-shape configuration (requiring a novel pushing motion), experiencing the lowest performance drop (14%) compared to baselines. This reinforces EgoBridge's ability to transfer both visual and behavioral knowledge from human-only data.

**H3: EgoBridge learns a shared latent space where human and robot data are aligned in task-relevant manners.**
- **Latent Space Visualization (TSNE):** TSNE visualizations of the learned action tokens revealed that EgoBridge produces a shared latent space with significantly higher overlap between human and robot data, as quantified by a lower Wasserstein-2 distance (Figure 5).
- **Semantic Interoperability:** Upon inspecting K-nearest neighbor pairs in this latent space, EgoBridge exhibited the most semantically similar pairings, where human and robot demonstrations corresponded to the same phase or intent of a given task. In contrast, baselines with poorer alignment (e.g., MimicPlay, which aligns marginals using KL-divergence) showed a lack of semantic similarity in their nearest neighbors. This finding strongly correlates the quality of latent space alignment with overall task success and generalization ability.

**Ablation Studies:**
- Ablation results (Table 2) highlighted the critical importance of the **DTW-based pseudo-pairing** in the cost function. Replacing it with simple MSE led to the largest performance drop (from 47% to 17% SR in Drawer task), underscoring that behaviorally similar pairings are essential.
- Removing the **Joint-OT objective** (i.e., using standard marginal alignment or no alignment, as in "Standard-OT" and "Co-train") also resulted in significant performance degradation, particularly for generalization tasks. This confirms the necessity of explicitly aligning joint feature-action distributions for effective knowledge transfer from human data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

EgoBridge represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the critical challenge of data scalability and generalization for robotic manipulation. Its key significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Unlocking Scalable Data Sources:** By effectively bridging the embodiment gaps between humans and robots, EgoBridge enables the robust utilization of vast and inexpensive egocentric human experience data. This moves beyond the current reliance on labor-intensive and costly robot teleoperation, promising to accelerate the development of more capable and versatile robot policies. The ability to learn from data collected by ubiquitous wearable devices opens up a new paradigm for data-driven robot learning.

2. **Enabling Generalizable Robot Policies:** A core strength of EgoBridge is its demonstrated ability to generalize to novel objects, scenes, and, most importantly, *new behaviors* observed only in human demonstrations. This is a crucial step towards creating truly generalist robots that can adapt to unseen variations and perform tasks without requiring explicit robot-specific training for every new scenario. This level of generalization is vital for real-world deployment where perfect data coverage is impossible.

3. **Principled Domain Adaptation for Robotics:** EgoBridge introduces a novel and principled approach to domain adaptation in robotics by aligning *joint policy latent feature-action distributions* using Optimal Transport guided by Dynamic Time Warping. This action-aware alignment distinguishes it from generic DA methods that might discard behaviorally relevant information. This methodology offers a blueprint for how to perform effective cross-embodiment transfer, ensuring that the learned shared representations are not only domain-invariant but also semantically meaningful for control.

4. **Improved Robustness and Performance:** The consistent improvements in success rates across diverse real-world manipulation tasks, both for in-domain scenarios and generalization challenges, highlight the practical utility and robustness of EgoBridge. This suggests that the framework yields more reliable robot behaviors in complex, dynamic environments.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

Despite its significant contributions, the paper acknowledges certain limitations and outlines compelling avenues for future research:

- **DTW Scalability:** The current DTW-based action alignment cost, while effective for single-task transfer, might become less informative or computationally expensive in multi-task or highly complex, long-horizon scenarios.
- **Generalizable Alignment Costs:** Future work aims to explore more generalizable alignment costs, such as those derived from natural language embeddings (e.g., from Vision-Language Models) or visual features extracted from powerful foundation models. This could enable more abstract and robust behavioral alignments, potentially even from unlabeled human internet data.
- **Multi-Embodiment Adaptation:** Extending the joint distribution adaptation framework to accommodate multiple robot embodiments or diverse human data sources is another promising direction.
- **Leveraging Unlabeled Data:** Incorporating Internet-sourced human data without explicit action labels, perhaps through self-supervised learning or inverse dynamics, could further amplify the scalability benefits.

In conclusion, EgoBridge offers a foundational step towards closing the data gap in robot learning, paving the way for robots that can learn and generalize from the richness of human experience, thereby accelerating the development of truly intelligent and adaptable robotic systems.
