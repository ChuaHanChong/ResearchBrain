# Beyond Heuristics: A Standardized Real2Sim Pipeline for Physical Human Robot Interaction in Human-in-the-Loop Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A Real2Sim pipeline identifies the coupled pelvis-strap dynamics of a mobile gait-balance assistant instead of hand-tuning them. The interface is a 6-DoF viscoelastic joint whose 12 stiffness/damping terms are fit per subject by CMA-ES at a normalized "Safe & Comfortable" tightness; ICC analysis splits them into 7 shared priors and 5 subject-specific ones, letting an unseen user be configured by tuning only 5 parameters.

## Problem

- Human-in-the-loop simulations of coupled assistive robots set spring-damper impedance constants by empirical trial or visual inspection, never grounded in real coupled motion data.
- Harness tightness is chosen empirically to trade safety against comfort, so no single nominal coupling model is defined or comparable across differing anthropometrics.
- Inter-subject variability of interface impedance is unassessed, and no prior work checks whether such models induce biomechanically correct digital-twin responses.

## Method

- ==Tightness quantification== normalizes ordinal strap levels about each subject's self-selected "Safe & Comfortable" point, verified by a crouch test, giving a reproducible operating point.
- ==Two-stage calibration==: max-range pelvic motions fix the 6-DoF joint limits, then ==CMA-ES== in log-space fits stiffness and damping by penalizing position and velocity residuals at both strap attachment points.
- ==Shareability characterization== applies a one-way random-effects ==ICC== over pooled log-space fits, mapping each parameter to a fixed point prior, a prediction-interval search range, or per-subject calibration.

## Results

- ICC classified **7** of **12** parameters as shareable; leave-one-subject-out deployment of the shared prior cost only **+6.5%** position RMSE and **+7.4%** cost over per-subject best fits.
- The calibrated prior matched the real interaction envelope, recovering spread (**3.04**, **8.44**, **2.21** mm vs real **3.82**, **9.81**, **4.19**) and anisotropy **0.360** vs real **0.389**, while soft and stiff baselines failed.
- The digital twin reproduced robot-induced range-of-motion loss: hip **-5.5** degrees vs real **-6.4**, knee **-5.4** vs real **-5.4**, with hip waveform correlation r = **0.99**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directional anisotropy in a compliant interface must be identified from data; isotropic constants either over-travel or collapse natural sway, and neither extreme is distinguishable by manual tuning.
> - A low intraclass correlation is a practical shareability test: when optimizer noise masks physiological spread, one population prior can replace per-subject calibration.
> - Anchoring data collection to a psychophysical operating point turns a subjective comfort judgment into a reproducible, cross-subject-comparable experimental condition.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The global demographic trend of an aging population is increasing the demand for assistive and rehabilitation robots to address mobility and balance issues stemming from neurological and musculoskeletal disorders. While these robots offer clinical benefits, their development is resource-intensive, requiring extensive and costly physical testing. A significant challenge in this process involves ensuring safety for users, particularly those with impairments, and accommodating the wide heterogeneity in user physiology and adaptive responses.

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) simulation has emerged as a strategy to mitigate the risks and costs associated with physical testing by allowing Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) to be investigated in a virtual environment. HITL simulations typically involve co-simulating human and robot dynamics. HRI can be broadly categorized into discrete interactions with intermittent contact and coupled interactions characterized by continuous physical linkage, such as gait assistants or exoskeletons. This paper focuses on the latter, where compliant soft-to-soft interfaces (e.g., straps) introduce complex, highly nonlinear constraints.

Existing HITL simulations for coupled physical HRI (pHRI) often employ simplified interaction models, typically 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (DoF) viscoelastic (spring-damper) mechanisms. While the general representation of such models is established, a critical limitation in the state-of-the-art is the heuristic determination of their impedance parameters (stiffness and damping). These parameters are frequently assigned through empirical testing or visual inspection, without systematic identification from real coupled motion data. Consequently, such models often lack grounding in physical reality, do not account for inter-subject variability, and their ability to induce biomechanically accurate responses in the human digital twin (HDT) remains largely unverified. Furthermore, subjective factors like harness tightness, which significantly influence coupling dynamics, are also typically set empirically, hindering model standardization.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing a standardized, data-driven pipeline for identifying pHRI dynamics in coupled systems. It aims to replace heuristic tuning with a systematic identification process, thereby improving the fidelity and predictive capability of HITL simulations for assistive robots, particularly for gait assistance involving pelvis-strap interfaces.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the increasing global demand for assistive and rehabilitation robots due to an aging population and the associated strain on healthcare systems. While these robots offer potential for enhancing independence and quality of life, their development and clinical implementation face substantial obstacles:
- **Safety and Ethical Constraints:** Direct physical testing of early-stage robotic controllers on mobility-impaired individuals carries inherent safety risks, including skin shear, joint hyper-extension, or loss of balance.
- **Development Costs and Time:** The iterative design, testing, and refinement process for assistive robots is slow and expensive.
- **User Heterogeneity and Personalization:** Individuals exhibit diverse anthropometrics and impairments, requiring extensive per-user personalization of robot assistance. Current development pipelines struggle to efficiently address this variability.

Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) simulation offers a pathway to mitigate these challenges by providing a safer, more efficient virtual environment for testing. However, the efficacy of HITL simulation is directly dependent on the accuracy with which physical human-robot interactions are modeled. The authors identify a significant fidelity gap in current HITL simulations for coupled pHRI, specifically concerning the heuristic tuning of impedance parameters in interaction models. This lack of data-driven parameter identification contributes to several unresolved issues:
- **Lack of Quantitative Parameterization:** Impedance parameters are often set based on expert judgment or trial-and-error, lacking grounding in real physical interaction data.
- **Inter-Subject Variability:** There is no systematic approach to determine whether pHRI parameters are generalizable across different users or require subject-specific calibration.
- **Harness Tightness Ambiguity:** Subjective concepts like "harness tightness," which critically influence coupling dynamics, are not quantitatively standardized, making it difficult to establish a reproducible operating point.
- **Unverified Biomechanical Responses:** Existing models have not been rigorously verified to ensure they induce biomechanically accurate adaptive responses in the human digital twin (HDT), which is crucial for predictive simulation.

To address these limitations, the paper proposes a standardized Real2Sim pipeline with the following key objectives, articulated as research questions (RQs):
- **RQ1: Quantitative Definition of Harness Tightness:** To quantitatively define qualitative harness tightness feedback ("Safe" vs. "Comfortable") and understand its impact on coupling dynamics, thereby establishing a reproducible operating point for data collection.
- **RQ2: Shareability of pHRI Parameters:** To determine if identified pHRI parameters exhibit common cross-subject distributions, enabling the development of generalized priors that can reduce the need for extensive per-subject calibration for unseen users.
- **RQ3: Biomechanical Accuracy of Digital Twin Responses:** To verify that the calibrated pHRI model induces biomechanically accurate adaptive responses in the Human Digital Twin during simulated robot-assisted gait, confirming its predictive utility.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to enhance the fidelity of HITL simulations, support the development of personalized robot controllers, and ultimately accelerate the safe and efficient design of assistive robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research presents a standardized Real2Sim pipeline designed to identify the coupled pHRI dynamics of a pelvis–strap interface for gait-assistive HITL simulation. The methodology is structured into four main stages: (1) Tightness Quantification, (2) Two-Stage Calibration, (3) Shareability Characterization, and (4) Model Validation.

The overall framework builds upon an established HITL co-simulation environment in MuJoCo, which integrates a 27-DoF Human Digital Twin (HDT) with a Deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy for naturalistic gait, and a robot digital twin replicating the Dynamic Robotic Balance Assistant (DRBA). The pelvis–interface coupling is modeled as a 6-DoF viscoelastic mechanism (three prismatic, three revolute) connecting the pelvis to the interface frame. This model employs a spatial spring–damper law (F_int = Kq + Dq̇) with 12 direction-specific linear and rotational stiffness (K) and damping (D) parameters, operating within calibrated joint limits (q_min, q_max).

**A. Tightness Quantification and Normalization:**
To address the ambiguity of harness tightness, a normalization procedure was introduced. Strap tightness (T) was normalized relative to a subject-specific psychophysical operating point (T*), which was the self-selected "Safe & Comfortable" level. T* was identified through a "crouch" test, ensuring vertical descent without slippage while maintaining comfort. This normalization aimed to make comparisons across subjects meaningful despite anthropometric variations.

**B. Two-Stage Calibration:**
The identification of the pHRI model parameters (K, D, q_min, q_max) proceeded sequentially:
- **Stage 1: Joint Limit Calibration (q_min, q_max):** Participants performed maximum-range pelvic motions along each of the six DoF. The minimum and maximum recorded values for each coordinate defined its respective q_min and q_max. These bounds delineate the natural "slack" range of the strap before physical engagement and rigid constraint forces.
- **Stage 2: Viscoelastic Parameter Identification (K, D):** With joint limits fixed, the 12 stiffness and damping parameters were identified by minimizing the discrepancy between measured and simulated interface motion. The optimization objective (J_d) combined squared residuals of both position (e_c_p) and velocity (e_c_v) of two strap–pelvis attachment points, non-dimensionalized by characteristic scales. The non-linear, non-convex optimization problem was solved using Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) in log-space for robustness and scale-invariance. Search bounds for parameters were derived from strap mechanics.

**C. Statistical Characterization of Parameter Shareability:**
To assess parameter generalizability across users (N=5 subjects), a statistical analysis was performed:
- **Data Collection:** For each subject, 10 parameter fits (θ_Opt) were generated (five CMA-ES restarts on each of two dynamic-motion trials).
- **Statistical Model:** The N × R (5 × 10 = 50) fits per parameter were analyzed in log-space using a one-way random-effects decomposition: ln(θ_s,r) = μ + b_s + ε_s,r, separating population mean (μ), between-subject deviation (b_s), and within-subject (algorithmic) noise (ε_s,r).
- **Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC):** The single-measurement ICC(1,1) (ρ = σ²_B / (σ²_B + σ²_W)) was used to quantify subject dependency, with a low ICC indicating parameter shareability. 95% confidence intervals were computed via hierarchical bootstrap due to the small sample size.
- **Parameter Classification:** Parameters were classified as:
 - **Common (C_com):** ρ_U < 0.50, deployed as a fixed point prior (geometric mean, μ).
 - **Distinct (C_dis):** ρ_L ≥ 0.75, requiring per-subject calibration (not found in this study).
 - **Uncertain (C_unc):** Otherwise, providing a bounded search range (95% spread) for unseen users.
- **Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) Validation:** The transferability of the derived priors was tested by evaluating the cost overhead of using a prior computed from N-1 subjects on the held-out subject's model.

**D. Model Validation Strategy:**
The calibrated model was validated on an unseen subject across two axes:
- **Interaction Envelope Fidelity:** The interface midpoint position relative to the pelvis center (r = p_mid - p_pelvis) was analyzed to characterize postural sway. Metrics included per-axis marginal spreads (σ_x, σ_y, σ_z) and transverse-plane anisotropy ratio (γ = σ_x/σ_y) to assess the model's ability to replicate the spatial scale and directional bias of real interaction.
- **Biomechanical Adaptation:** Simulated sagittal-plane hip/knee/ankle trajectories during robot-assisted walking were compared against real-world data. Metrics included Range of Motion (RoM), peak joint angles, and waveform similarity (Pearson r). The validation aimed to verify if the HDT autonomously reproduced robot-induced gait adaptations, specifically RoM reduction, in a manner consistent with human responses. The calibrated model's performance was compared against overly compliant ("soft") and overly stiff ("stiff") isotropic baseline settings.

**Experimental Setup:**
A pilot study involved six healthy subjects (five for calibration, one for validation). Data collection utilized a hybrid motion capture system: markerless reconstruction for full-body kinematics (Rajagopal OpenSim model) and marker-based tracking for interface joint centers and pelvis landmarks. Participants performed standardized tasks for calibration (maximum pelvic motions, continuous dynamic motion) and validation (Normal Walking, robot-assisted walking). Kinematic replay was used in simulation to drive the HDT during parameter identification, treating human kinematics as a fixed input.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Analyzing the Safe-Comfortable Tightness Threshold**
Analysis of relative motion ranges (∆x, ∆y, ∆ϕ) across varying strap tightness levels demonstrated that pelvic rotation (∆ϕ) exhibited a clear "knee point" for all subjects. This point, characterized by a rapid drop in allowable rotation followed by a plateau, coincided with each subject's self-selected "Safe & Comfortable" tightness. Planar translations (∆x, ∆y) did not show a consistent cross-subject transition or breakpoint. The knee point in ∆ϕ was thus identified as a reproducible, quantitative operating point for defining the "Safe & Comfortable" coupling condition (RQ1).

**B. Optimization Efficacy and Parameter Classification**
1. **Identification Efficacy and Cross-Trial Generalization:**
 The CMA-ES optimization pipeline showed robust convergence for all subjects, with five restarts consistently achieving a plateau in the dimensionless cost function (J), indicating insensitivity to initial conditions. Cross-validation between two dynamic-motion trials per subject revealed a modest average overhead of +14.5% in cost J when parameters identified from one trial were applied to the other. This consistency between within- and cross-trial fits suggests that the calibrated viscoelastic parameters capture a stable, trial-invariant compliance of the pelvis–strap interface, rather than overfitting to specific trial noise.

2. **Statistical Characterization of Shareability (ICC Analysis):**
 The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) analysis classified the 12 impedance parameters, addressing RQ2:
 - **Shareable Parameters (7 out of 12):** Seven parameters (k_tx, d_ty, k_tz, d_tz, k_rx, d_ry, k_rz) were classified as "SHAREABLE" (upper 95% CI of ICC < 0.50). These exhibited negligible subject dependency, clustering tightly around the population mean, suggesting they are dominated by harness material properties. These parameters were deployed as fixed point priors for unseen users. Notably, the torsional stiffness k_rz showed a low value, sitting at its lower admissible bound, indicating boundary saturation rather than a consensus on a specific physiological value.
 - **Uncertain Parameters (5 out of 12):** The remaining five parameters (d_tx, k_ty, d_rx, k_ry, d_rz) were classified as "UNCERTAIN," exhibiting higher between-subject variance where their 95% ICC confidence intervals spanned the classification thresholds. For these, a single fixed value was deemed insufficient. Instead, their 95% prediction interval (PI) was provided as a bounded search range, indicating that an unseen user would only require refinement of these five parameters, reducing per-user tuning from 12 to 5 dimensions. The current cohort size (N=5) did not allow for regression of these parameters against anthropometrics.

3. **Generalization to Unseen Users (LOSO):**
 Under a Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) protocol, deploying the shared prior (geometric mean of N-1 subjects) for a held-out subject resulted in a small average overhead compared to that subject's best per-subject fit: +6.5% in position RMSE and +7.4% in cost J. This minimal loss of fidelity provides empirical evidence that the shareability classification and the resulting priors generalize to unseen users with reasonable accuracy (RQ2).

**C. pHRI Validation**
Validation on an unseen subject during dynamic overground walking was performed, comparing the calibrated population prior (θ_prior) against overly compliant ("soft") and overly stiff ("stiff") isotropic baselines.

1. **Interaction Envelope:**
 The calibrated θ_prior model accurately replicated the spatial structure of the pelvis–interface displacement, as visualized by X–Y trajectory clouds and 95% confidence ellipses. In contrast, the "soft" baseline exhibited significant over-travel, under-constraining the pelvis, while the "stiff" baseline over-constrained the user, collapsing natural pelvic movement. Quantitatively, θ_prior recovered both the absolute spatial scale (e.g., σ=[3.04, 8.44, 2.21] mm compared to real [3.82, 9.81, 4.19] mm) and the laterally-dominant anisotropy (γ=0.360 vs. real 0.389) characteristic of human pelvic sway. This indicated that capturing directional anisotropy, rather than assuming isotropic constants, is important for interaction fidelity.

2. **Biomechanical Adaptation:**
 The unguided Human Digital Twin (HDT), configured with the θ_prior, autonomously reproduced key kinematic adaptations observed in real robot-assisted gait, addressing RQ3. In the physical experiment, robot assistance caused a reduction in lower-limb sagittal Range of Motion (RoM): hip RoM dropped by 6.4° (33.4° → 27.0°) and knee RoM by 5.4° (57.5° → 52.1°).
 The HDT with θ_prior reproduced this reduction: hip RoM decreased by 5.5° (vs. real 6.4°), with a high waveform similarity (Pearson r = 0.99). Knee RoM reduction was 5.4°, matching the real reduction. This indicated that the calibrated model accurately represents the "hard stop" mechanics of the strap, forcing the HDT to adapt correctly. While the unassisted ankle NW baseline in simulation was underestimated compared to reality, the characteristic decrease in ankle dorsiflexion before toe-off under robot assistance was still clearly observed in both simulation and reality. The "soft" and "stiff" baselines failed to reproduce these specific biomechanical adaptations, further confirming the accuracy of the calibrated model at the identified operating point.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a Real2Sim pipeline that addresses critical limitations in the fidelity of Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) simulations for coupled Physical Human-Robot Interaction (pHRI). By moving beyond heuristic parameter tuning to a data-driven identification approach, the work makes several contributions:

- **Enhanced Simulation Fidelity:** The pipeline significantly improves the realism and accuracy of pHRI models, particularly for complex, compliant interfaces like pelvis-strap systems in gait assistance. This allows HITL simulations to more closely reflect real-world physical interactions.
- **Standardized Calibration Procedure:** The method provides a standardized, reproducible operating point for harness tightness based on objective physiological responses, resolving ambiguity inherent in subjective feedback. This enhances the consistency and comparability of pHRI studies.
- **Reduced Calibration Effort for Personalization:** Through statistical characterization of parameter shareability using ICC analysis, the pipeline distinguishes between common (shareable) and subject-specific (uncertain) parameters. This allows for configuring an unseen user by refining only a subset of parameters (5 out of 12 in this study), substantially reducing the time and data required for individual personalization. This is particularly impactful for applications requiring frequent or extensive user adaptation.
- **Predictive Human Digital Twins:** By demonstrating that the calibrated model induces biomechanically accurate gait adaptations in the Human Digital Twin (HDT), the research establishes the HDT as a more reliable predictive tool. This capability is crucial for pre-clinical verification of personalized robot controllers, enabling safer and more efficient design iterations before physical testing on human subjects.
- **Accelerated Robot Development:** The improved fidelity and reduced calibration effort contribute to a more efficient and cost-effective development cycle for assistive and rehabilitation robots. This can accelerate the translation of robotic innovations into clinical practice to meet the demands of an aging population.
- **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated on a pelvis-strap interface for gait assistance, the principles of data-driven pHRI identification, parameter shareability analysis, and biomechanical validation are potentially applicable to other forms of coupled pHRI, such as exoskeletons or other assistive devices with direct physical contact.

The findings indicate a foundation for future work, including the implementation of interval-based search for uncertain parameters, development of closed-loop robot-aware HDTs, and validation at the force level. Expanding the research to include broader populations, especially those with impairments, would further solidify the pipeline's clinical relevance and generalizability. Overall, this work represents a step towards establishing robust, data-grounded methodologies for creating realistic virtual testbeds for assistive robotics.
