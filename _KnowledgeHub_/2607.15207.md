# BadWAM: When World-Action Models Dream Right but Act Wrong

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the National University of Singapore and The Hong Kong Polytechnic University introduced BadWAM, a framework for "World-Action Drift Attacks" that desynchronize a robot's executed actions from its imagined future in World-Action Models (WAMs). This work demonstrates that WAMs can be manipulated to cause task failures even when their predicted future world states appear plausible, challenging the assumption that future prediction inherently guarantees safe behavior.

## Problem

- WAMs couple action generation with future world prediction, assuming this provides inherent robustness and a safety signal by allowing anticipation of consequences.
- Existing adversarial attacks primarily target either reactive policies or corrupt imagined futures, not the desynchronization between a WAM's plausible imagination and its faulty action.
- A lack of specific understanding regarding WAM vulnerabilities where a visually plausible imagined future co-exists with an adversarially manipulated, task-failing action.

## Method

- Introduced ==World-Action Drift Attacks== to specifically target the desynchronization between a WAM's imagined future and its executed action using imperceptible ==visual perturbations==.
- Developed **BadWAM**, a unified ==black-box== framework that uses ==query-based online optimization== to generate adversarial perturbations without needing model gradients.
- Designed two attack instantiations: an ==action-only attack== focused on maximizing execution disruption, and an ==imagination-preserving attack== for stealthier failures that maintains future plausibility.

## Results

- BadWAM significantly reduced closed-loop task success rates, dropping by over **50%** for action-only WAMs and comparably for imagination-preserving attacks, confirming the vulnerability.
- Attacks induced structured and gradual action shifts that compounded over time, leading to progressive task failures, particularly in tasks requiring precise spatial control.
- Perturbations exhibited transferability across different WAM variants, suggesting widespread vulnerabilities, and non-adaptive preprocessing defenses proved insufficient for robust detection or mitigation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The inherent coupling of action and world prediction in WAMs does not automatically guarantee robustness or safety against targeted desynchronization attacks.
> - WAMs exhibit a specific vulnerability where their 'imagination' can appear correct while their 'actions' are adversarially steered towards task failure.
> - Relying solely on the visual plausibility of an imagined future for WAM safety checks is insufficient; explicit verification of action-imagination consistency is critical.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI) is progressing rapidly, with a particular focus on robotic foundation models. World-Action Models (WAMs) represent an evolution in this area, moving beyond simple action prediction to learn representations that inherently couple action generation with predictions about future world states. This design is often considered advantageous for embodied AI, as future prediction can offer richer physical representations, facilitate planning or verification, and provide an intuitive safety signal. The underlying assumption is that if a robot can anticipate the consequences of its actions, unsafe or inconsistent behaviors might be detected before execution. Various WAM architectures exist, some explicitly generating future observations, others using video modeling as an auxiliary training objective, and newer variants extending to object-addressable manipulation, mobile manipulation, and visual-tactile control. The central tenet across these designs is that coupling action with world prediction should lead to more physically grounded and potentially safer robot behavior.

The broader landscape of adversarial robustness in AI has extensively studied attacks on image classifiers, visual world models, and reactive robot policies, where small input perturbations can significantly alter model predictions or behavior. Recent work has begun to explore security risks for world models and WAMs, including attacks that degrade future rollouts (e.g., BadWorld) or induce semantic distortions by perturbing condition channels. Oracle-level integrity attacks (e.g., Chen et al., 2026) have also explored compromising systems that rely on imagined futures for safety checks or planning.

This paper positions itself by identifying a distinct and WAM-specific vulnerability: the potential for *desynchronization* between a WAM's imagined future and its executed action. While previous work has focused on corrupting the imagined future itself or directly hijacking actions in reactive policies, this research investigates situations where the imagined future remains plausible, yet the associated action output is adversarially manipulated to cause task failure. This constitutes a qualitative difference from prior attacks, specifically targeting the alignment between the WAM's two coupled capabilities: imagining and acting. The work introduces "World-Action Drift Attacks" to formalize and evaluate this gap, suggesting that future prediction alone might not be a reliable safety signal if the action pathway can be desynchronized.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the robustness of World-Action Models (WAMs) against adversarially induced execution failures and, more specifically, to determine if the inherent coupling between action generation and future world prediction in WAMs is resilient to targeted attacks.

The core motivation stems from a security question regarding the fragility of the assumption that WAMs' coupled action and world prediction capabilities inherently provide robustness, interpretability, and safety. While the ability to imagine future consequences is often seen as a mechanism for detecting unsafe or inconsistent behavior, the authors hypothesize that this assumption may be vulnerable. They identify a novel class of WAM-specific adversarial attacks, termed "World-Action Drift Attacks," where small visual perturbations can break the alignment between a WAM's imagined future and its executed actions. This desynchronization can lead to task failure even when the model's predicted future appears plausible.

The key objectives are detailed as follows:
- **Identify a WAM-Specific Vulnerability:** To formally define and demonstrate "World-Action Drift Attack" as a unique threat to WAMs, where the action outputs can be adversarially hijacked while the model's predicted futures remain visually plausible. This challenges the notion that observing a reasonable imagined future guarantees safe or aligned action execution.
- **Develop a Unified Attack Framework (BadWAM):** To design a framework for modeling and evaluating these world-action drift attacks. BadWAM aims to characterize the attack surface along two natural criteria: attack strength (maximization of execution disruption) and stealthiness (preservation of the imagined future).
- **Instantiate Diverse Attack Scenarios:**
 - **Action-Only Adversarial Attack:** To model a scenario where the adversary prioritizes maximum disruption of task execution, directly driving the model toward task-failing actions without explicit constraints on the imagined future. This represents the overt end of the threat spectrum.
 - **Imagination-Preserving Adversarial Attack:** To model a stealthier scenario where the adversary aims to induce harmful action shifts while keeping the WAM's predicted future close to its clean, unperturbed imagination. This captures cases where a safety monitor relying solely on future prediction might fail to detect the attack.
- **Empirically Validate the Vulnerability:** To evaluate BadWAM across different WAM variants in closed-loop robot manipulation tasks. The goal is to quantify the reduction in task success rates and provide detailed analyses of attack dynamics, including which action channels and parts of the action horizon are affected, and how imagination preservation impacts attack performance.

The motivation is supported by empirical observations (visualized in Figure 1) suggesting that task failures often correlate with larger action shifts, but the corresponding imagination shifts can overlap significantly between successful and failed executions. This indicates that action-imagination alignment, rather than imagination plausibility alone, is a critical security property in WAMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces BadWAM, a unified black-box framework designed to model and evaluate World-Action Drift Attacks against deployed WAM-based robot policies. The methodology is grounded in a specific threat model and employs query-based online optimization to generate adversarial perturbations.

#### 3.1. Threat Model

The adversary operates at inference time, applying small visual perturbations to the robot's observation ($o_t$) before it is processed by the WAM. This perturbed observation, $\tilde{o}_t = \text{clip}(o_t + \delta_t)$, is subject to an $\ell_\infty$ norm bound ($||\delta_t||_\infty \le \epsilon$), ensuring visual imperceptibility. The adversary does not manipulate language instructions, robot state, environment dynamics, model parameters, or training data.

Two levels of WAM access are considered:
- **Action-Only Setting:** The adversary can query the WAM and observe only its output action chunk ($a_{t:t+H-1}$). This reflects black-box access to a policy API.
- **Imagination-Visible Setting:** The adversary can additionally observe the model's imagined future ($z_{t+1:t+K}$ or $v_{t+1:t+K}$), relevant for systems exposing future predictions for monitoring or planning.

The adversary's primary goal is un-targeted task failure, meaning the robot fails to complete the instructed task during closed-loop execution. For the imagination-preserving attack, an additional objective is stealthiness, degrading execution while keeping the imagined future close to the clean prediction.

#### 3.2. BadWAM Framework Design

BadWAM is motivated by the observation that while actions and imaginations are coupled in WAMs, they can be desynchronized. The framework is built around two distance metrics:
- $D_{act}$: Measures the deviation between clean and attacked action chunks.
- $D_{img}$: Measures the drift between clean and attacked imagination.

#### 3.2.1. Action-Only Adversarial Attack

This instantiation prioritizes execution disruption. The objective is to find a bounded visual perturbation $\delta_t$ that maximizes the deviation between the action chunk generated by the perturbed observation ($a^\delta_{t:t+H-1}$) and the action chunk from the clean observation ($a_{t:t+H-1}$).
$\delta^\star_t = \arg \max_{||\delta_t||_\infty \le \epsilon} D_{act}(a^\delta_{t:t+H-1}, a_{t:t+H-1})$
This attack requires only observable action outputs.

#### 3.2.2. Imagination-Preserving Adversarial Attack

This attack additionally aims for stealth by constraining the drift in the imagined future. It optimizes a Lagrangian relaxation of the objective:
$\delta^\star_t = \arg \max_{||\delta_t||_\infty \le \epsilon} D_{act}(a^\delta_{t:t+H-1}, a_{t:t+H-1}) - \lambda D_{img}(z^\delta_{t+1:t+K}, z_{t+1:t+K})$
The coefficient $\lambda$ controls the trade-off between attack strength and stealthiness. $D_{img}$ can be an average frame-level distance for decoded future videos or a latent-space distance for latent futures. This objective explicitly targets the WAM-specific failure where the robot appears to imagine a plausible future but executes a desynchronized action.

#### 3.3. Query-Based Online Optimization

BadWAM operates without requiring gradients or white-box access to WAM parameters. At each replanning step, it treats the WAM as a black-box system and uses zeroth-order finite-difference queries to optimize the attack objective.
- For a current perturbation $\delta_t$, random directions $u_i$ are sampled.
- The scalar objective function $J$ (either action-only or imagination-preserving) is evaluated for positively and negatively perturbed observations ($\delta_t + cu_i$ and $\delta_t - cu_i$).
- A gradient estimator $\hat{\nabla}J(\delta_t)$ is computed using the difference in objective values.
- The perturbation is updated using a projected gradient ascent step: $\delta_t \leftarrow \Pi_{[-\epsilon,\epsilon]}(\delta_t + \eta \hat{\nabla}J(\delta_t))$, where $\Pi$ projects back into the $\ell_\infty$ ball.
- This process is repeated for a fixed query budget at each replanning step, ensuring the attack adapts to the current observation and robot state.

#### 3.4. Closed-Loop Execution and Measurements

Evaluation is performed under closed-loop execution, where the attack is recomputed at each replanning step, and the robot executes the produced action chunk. This simulates real-world interaction more faithfully than single-step comparisons.
Measurements include:
- **Task-level effectiveness:** Closed-loop success rate and induced failures.
- **Action disruption:** Action distance, action-channel statistics (e.g., XYZ, rotation, gripper), and horizon-level action shifts.
- **Stealthiness (for imagination-preserving attacks):** Predicted-future distance and a normalized decoupling score (ratio of action to future distance).

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Benchmarks:** LIBERO (four suites of tabletop manipulation tasks) and RoboTwin (dual-arm manipulation).
- **WAM Policies:** Evaluated three WAM-style controllers: an action-only WAM (direct observation-to-action), a joint WAM (jointly predicts future visual states and actions), and an IDM WAM (constructs a future-imagination representation then decodes actions).
- **Hyperparameters:** Default perturbation bound $\epsilon = 0.06$, search budget of 8 optimization iterations (17 WAM queries) per replan. Default $\lambda = 0.015$ for imagination-preserving attack.
- **Baselines:** Compared against random uniform perturbations and a white-box gradient-access reference (used as an upper bound, not part of BadWAM's black-box threat model).
- **Defenses:** Explored simple non-adaptive preprocessing methods (Gaussian blur, JPEG-noise ensemble, resize-crop ensemble) and an augmentation-consistency detector.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of BadWAM across various WAMs and benchmarks yielded several key findings regarding the robustness and security properties of these models.

#### 4.1. BadWAM Reliably Induces Task Failures (RQ1)

- **Substantial Success Rate Reduction:** BadWAM attacks significantly reduced closed-loop task success rates. For example, on the action-only WAM in LIBERO, the task success rate dropped from 96.5% to 43.1% (a 53.4% reduction). Similar substantial drops were observed for joint WAMs (e.g., 98.1% to 61.5%) and IDM WAMs (e.g., 98.4% to 66.1%).
- **Stealthy Attacks Maintain Potency:** The imagination-preserving attack achieved comparable task success reductions (e.g., joint WAM 63.0%, IDM WAM 67.0%) to the action-only attack, while explicitly regularizing against future prediction drift. This demonstrates that WAMs can be pushed towards failure even when their imagined futures appear plausible.
- **Targeted vs. Random Perturbations:** BadWAM's targeted attacks were considerably more effective than random uniform perturbations under the same $\ell_\infty$ budget, indicating that the failures are due to specific vulnerabilities rather than generic visual fragility.
- **White-Box Upper Bound:** A white-box attack (with gradient access) achieved even lower success rates, suggesting that BadWAM's black-box approach, while effective, does not saturate the full extent of the vulnerability.

#### 4.2. Failures Are Systematic Across Suites and Trials (RQ2)

- **Suite-Specific Vulnerability:** The attack impact varied across LIBERO suites. Tasks requiring precise spatial control (e.g., "Spatial" suite) and long-horizon execution (e.g., "Long-horizon" suite) experienced the largest drops in success (e.g., Spatial success for action-only WAM fell from 96.5% to 16.0%). Object-centric tasks demonstrated comparative robustness. This suggests that the attack is most effective when small action deviations can compound through geometric or sequential dependencies.
- **Persistence Across Trials:** The observed reduction in success rates was consistent across repeated trials (pass@k analysis), indicating that the attacks cause systematic reliability loss rather than exploiting isolated unlucky instances.

#### 4.3. How BadWAM Failures Emerge (RQ3)

- **Gradual Drift to Failure:** Qualitative analysis showed that attacked WAMs do not immediately collapse into random behavior. Instead, actions gradually drift from task intent, leading to progressive task failure over time, often through compounding errors like knocking over objects or failed grasps (Figure 9).
- **Accumulation of Action Shifts:** Failed episodes consistently showed larger cumulative action shifts over replanning steps compared to successful ones, highlighting that failures often emerge from a sustained adversarial influence (Figure 10).
- **Structured Action Shifts:** The attacks induced structured deviations, primarily affecting continuous action channels (translation, rotation, gripper commands) and specific portions of the action horizon, rather than uniform noise (Figure 2).

#### 4.4. Stealth Under Matched Strength (RQ4)

- The imagination-preserving objective successfully maintained smaller average predicted-future distances compared to the action-only attack, under similar attack strength (task success reduction) and perturbation budgets. This confirms the ability of BadWAM to induce stealthier failures where the imagined future remains closer to the clean prediction, potentially deceiving monitors that solely check future plausibility.

#### 4.5. Ablations: Future Preservation, Perturbation Budget, and Runtime (RQ5)

- **Future-Preserving Weight ($\lambda$):** The coefficient $\lambda$ effectively controlled the trade-off. Increasing $\lambda$ generally reduced future drift ($D_{img}$) but could also slightly reduce attack strength (task success), demonstrating a tunable balance between stealth and disruption.
- **Perturbation Size ($\epsilon$):** Attack strength scaled directly with the allowed $\ell_\infty$ perturbation budget. Larger $\epsilon$ values consistently led to greater reductions in task success.
- **Query Budget:** Increasing the query budget improved attack effectiveness up to a certain point (e.g., 16 iterations), but beyond that, the benefits plateaued while per-replan optimization time continued to increase. This indicates a practical efficiency-effectiveness frontier.

#### 4.6. Transferability Across WAM Variants (RQ6 - Transferability)

- BadWAM perturbations exhibited transferability across different WAM variants (e.g., attacks optimized on a Joint WAM remained effective against an IDM WAM, and vice-versa). This suggests that the identified vulnerabilities are not model-specific artifacts but rather stem from shared observation-space directions affecting action generation in WAM architectures.

#### 4.7. Non-Adaptive Defenses and Detection (RQ6 - Defenses)

- **Preprocessing Defenses:** Simple non-adaptive preprocessing techniques, such as Gaussian blur and JPEG-noise ensembles, showed some promise in recovering attacked success rates while preserving clean performance. However, these are non-adaptive and would likely be overcome by an adversary aware of the defense. Other methods like resize-crop ensembles severely degraded clean performance, rendering them impractical.
- **Consistency Detection:** A simple augmentation-consistency detector (using JPEG augmentation) showed moderate AUROC but achieved low True Positive Rates (TPR) at practical False Positive Rates (FPR) (e.g., 13.4% TPR at 5% FPR), indicating it is insufficient for reliable real-time safety monitoring.

#### 4.8. Implications for WAM Safety

The findings collectively indicate that relying solely on the visual plausibility of an imagined future for WAM safety checks is insufficient. The critical security property lies in the *synchronization* between the imagined future and the action executed. BadWAM demonstrates that this synchronization can be adversarially broken, leading to situations where the robot's "dream" (imagined future) appears right, but its "action" is wrong. This highlights a need for more robust safety mechanisms that explicitly verify action-imagination consistency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant implications for the understanding, development, and deployment of World-Action Models (WAMs) in embodied AI, particularly in robotics.

#### 5.1. Identification of a Novel WAM-Specific Vulnerability

The primary significance of this work is the identification and formalization of "World-Action Drift Attack." This attack class is distinct from prior adversarial attacks that target reactive policies or solely corrupt visual world models. It highlights a unique vulnerability arising from the *coupling* of action generation with future prediction in WAMs. The ability to desynchronize a WAM's action from its seemingly plausible imagined future challenges a foundational assumption about WAMs: that their predictive capabilities inherently lead to more robust or safer behavior.

#### 5.2. Challenge to Existing Safety Paradigms for WAMs

WAMs are often proposed with intrinsic safety benefits, where the robot's ability to "imagine" consequences could be used for self-correction or external monitoring. This paper demonstrates that a monitor solely relying on the visual plausibility of the imagined future might be overconfident and vulnerable to stealthy attacks. The work underscores that future plausibility alone is an incomplete safety criterion; instead, the mutual consistency and synchronization between the imagined future and the executed action are the critical security properties.

#### 5.3. Introduction of a Practical Black-Box Attack Framework

BadWAM provides a concrete and practical framework for evaluating this WAM-specific vulnerability under black-box access. Its query-based, zeroth-order optimization approach means it does not require access to model gradients or internal parameters, making it applicable to deployed WAM-based robot policies. This framework offers a valuable tool for researchers and developers to assess the robustness of their WAM implementations.

#### 5.4. Diagnostic Insights into WAM Failure Modes

The detailed analysis conducted with BadWAM provides important diagnostic insights. It reveals that WAM failures under these attacks are not merely random or immediate, but often emerge from structured and progressive deviations that compound over closed-loop execution. The analysis of action channel shifts, horizon-level effects, and the trade-offs between attack strength and stealthiness (via imagination preservation) contributes to a deeper understanding of how WAMs behave under stress and where their vulnerabilities lie. The transferability results further suggest that these vulnerabilities are not isolated implementation artifacts but potentially systemic across different WAM architectures.

#### 5.5. Implications for Future WAM Development and Defense

The findings necessitate a re-evaluation of WAM design principles and safety mechanisms. Future WAM architectures may need to incorporate more robust mechanisms to ensure strong action-imagination synchronization. The paper implicitly calls for the development of adaptive and principled defenses that can detect or mitigate such desynchronization, moving beyond simple non-adaptive preprocessing. This could involve developing runtime monitors that not only check the plausibility of futures but also actively verify the alignment between imagined outcomes and planned actions. Ultimately, BadWAM serves as both a concrete attack methodology and a diagnostic protocol, aiming to guide the development of more secure and trustworthy world-action models for embodied AI.
