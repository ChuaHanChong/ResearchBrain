# Adaptive Companionship for Group-Following Robots: Handling Dynamically Changing Group Formations

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces an adaptive method for robots to accompany human groups, integrating Vision-Language Models for reasoning about dynamically changing group formations and membership. The approach demonstrated an average 15% improvement in success rate and a 25% reduction in collision rate compared to baseline methods in simulated dynamic scenarios, also receiving higher user study scores for comfort, sociability, and intelligence.

## Problem

- Existing robot companionship strategies primarily focus on single-person accompaniment or assume fixed group formations, struggling with the dynamic nature of human groups.
- Human groups exhibit unpredictable changes in formation, available walking space, and membership (individuals joining or leaving), which current methods often fail to adapt to.
- Current group-following approaches rely on pre-defined patterns or static group assumptions, limiting their applicability to real-world, fluid social environments.

## Method

- A ==hierarchical control framework== that integrates ==Vision-Language Models (VLMs)== for high-level decision-making with a ==Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)== controller enhanced by a ==Control Barrier Function (CBF)== for low-level control.
- A VLM-based ==group detection module== that uses 2D visual representations of proxemic distances and trajectory similarity to identify group members and dynamically recognize changes in membership.
- A ==grid-based group interaction space== that provides structured input for a second VLM, enabling it to infer socially appropriate accompaniment positions through ==Chain-of-Thought prompting== while considering obstacles and social norms.

## Results

- VLM-based decision-making achieved high success rates (up to **98%** with Gemini 2.5 Flash) in generating socially appropriate and navigable positions across **30** distinct dynamic scenarios.
- The proposed method improved the average success rate by **15%** and reduced the average collision rate by **25%** compared to baseline approaches in comparative evaluations across **five** dynamic scenarios.
- User studies indicated that the robot's behaviors were perceived as more comfortable, sociable, and intelligent, with the proposed method consistently receiving the highest average scores across these metrics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating VLM-based reasoning allows robots to robustly adapt to complex, dynamic human group behaviors, including changes in formation and membership, without relying on pre-defined patterns.
> - Providing VLMs with a structured, grid-based interaction space for reasoning significantly improves their ability to generate accurate and socially appropriate robot positions within a dynamic group.
> - The combination of VLM-driven high-level social intelligence and a robust low-level controller (MPPI+CBF) is effective for ensuring both adaptive companionship and safe, collision-free navigation in human environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of social robots capable of seamless human interaction in everyday environments has received considerable attention. A key aspect of human-like social cognition for robots involves accompanying individuals or groups of pedestrians.

Previous research in companionship strategies has primarily focused on single-person accompaniment, exploring various methods such as following from behind, walking side-by-side, and leading. These studies have incorporated socially aware navigation frameworks to respect interpersonal distances and learning-based methods to adapt to individual walking patterns, with recent extensions to quadruped robots.

However, robots accompanying pedestrian groups face additional complexities due to the dynamic and unpredictable nature of human groups. Human groups often do not maintain fixed formations (e.g., side-by-side or V-shaped) and exhibit complex social dynamics, including variations in available walking space and changes in group membership as individuals join or leave.

While some studies have addressed group detection for navigation and collision avoidance, and others have explored synchronizing group movements, research specifically on enabling robots to *accompany* pedestrian groups is less extensive. Existing group-following approaches, such as those by Repiso et al., have utilized social force models and movement patterns like V-shaped or side-by-side formations. However, these methods often assume known and fixed group members, typically consider only groups of two, and rely on pre-defined formations, which limits their applicability to real-world dynamic scenarios where formations are not consistently maintained or group membership changes. Leader-selection strategies have also been explored, but they generally do not account for disruptions in group formation due to members joining or leaving.

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have demonstrated capabilities in contextual understanding, commonsense reasoning, and multi-step inference, finding applications in various robotics domains, including social navigation and group detection. Despite these developments, the application of language models in companion robotics, particularly for adaptive group-following tasks that account for dynamic changes, has received limited attention. This paper seeks to address this gap by integrating VLM-based reasoning into group-following robots to enable adaptive companionship in dynamically changing group formations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to propose an adaptive group-accompaniment method for social robots that can effectively manage dynamically changing group formations and membership without relying on pre-defined patterns. The motivation stems from the limitations of existing methods that struggle with the inherent fluidity and unpredictability of human groups in real-world scenarios.

Specifically, the paper outlines three main contributions and objectives:

1. **Integration of VLM-based reasoning for adaptive group following:** The authors aim to leverage the semantic reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models to enable robots to infer appropriate companion positions within a group and to recognize or anticipate changes in group membership (e.g., individuals joining or leaving). This integration is intended to enhance the robustness of group-following behaviors by endowing the robot with human-like inference capabilities.
2. **Development of a VLM-based group detection module and interaction space:** A specific objective is to design a module that uses VLMs to detect changes in group membership based on proxemic distances and path similarity of individuals. Furthermore, the paper proposes the introduction of a grid-based group interaction space. This space is intended to provide a structured representation for the VLM, allowing it to reason about and make accompaniment decisions within a defined context rather than directly processing raw visual information or performing complex mathematical computations, thereby guiding the robot's actions more appropriately.
3. **Experimental validation and comparative analysis:** The research aims to validate the proposed approach through a series of experimental evaluations. This includes testing the method across multiple scenarios, comparing its performance against established baseline approaches, conducting a user study to assess the naturalness and social appropriateness of the robot's behaviors, and performing an ablation study to quantify the contribution of individual components of the proposed framework. The objective is to demonstrate improvements in key performance metrics such as success rate, collision rate, and user experience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method for adaptive group accompaniment integrates Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with a Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) controller, augmented by a Control Barrier Function (CBF). The system architecture consists of a human detection module, two VLM modules for group identification and companionship decision-making, and a human-companion controller.

**A. Companion Group Identification:**
The initial step involves identifying the individuals comprising the companion group. Unlike prior studies that often assume static group membership, this method addresses dynamic changes such as members joining or leaving.
- **Human Detection:** Individuals are detected using 3D LiDAR point cloud data, processed by the PointPillars algorithm. Each detected object is assigned a unique ID.
- **2D Visual Representation:** The detected individuals, their trajectories, and the robot's position and trajectory are projected into a 2D grid-based visual representation. This representation encodes proxemic distances and trajectory similarity.
- **VLM for Group Detection (VLM_group):** This VLM module receives the 2D visual representation (I_group) and an instruction prompt (P_group, as illustrated in Fig. 3). The prompt guides the VLM to identify companion group members based on spatial closeness and trajectory similarity, enabling it to continuously update the group by adding newcomers or removing those who move away. The output is a list of IDs for the current group members.

**B. VLM-Based Group Companionship:**
To enable the VLM to infer socially appropriate companion positions, a grid-based group interaction space (Q) is introduced. This framework limits the direct computational demands on the VLM by allowing it to reason over predefined candidate positions rather than raw visual data.
- **Group Interaction Space:** The space Q is defined as a grid of potential accompaniment positions (q_d_n,m), with N columns and M rows. The grid coordinates are calculated based on the group's spatial extent (x_min, x_max, y_min, y_max), grid spacing (∆_grid), and offsets (x_offset, y_offset).
- **Visual Input to VLM:** The group interaction space Q is presented as a visual input to the VLM. This input includes information about group members (positions, IDs, trajectories), the robot's position and trajectory, and 2D environmental obstacles (O) derived from LiDAR data. The grid cells are indexed to facilitate systematic selection.
- **VLM Decision-Making:** The VLM (VLM(I, O, N, M, P)) processes the visual input (I) and uses Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting with a one-shot example (Fig. 4). This CoT approach guides the VLM through step-by-step reasoning processes. The reasoning considers factors such as avoiding collisions with group members and obstacles, maintaining appropriate proxemic distances (intimate and personal zones), respecting social norms, and adapting to changes in human formation. The VLM outputs the selected (n, m) column and row indices within the interaction space, which are then converted into spatial coordinates (q_d) to serve as the robot's desired accompaniment position.

**C. Human-Companion Controller:**
An MPPI controller, enhanced with a CBF, is employed to guide the robot to the desired accompaniment position (q_d) while ensuring stability and safety.
- **MPPI Controller:** The controller minimizes a cost function that includes terms for tracking and obstacle avoidance.
 - **Tracking Cost (C_tracking):** Penalizes deviations between the robot's current position (q_r) and the desired position (q_d) along the x and y axes.
 - **Obstacle Avoidance Cost (C_obs):** Applies a penalty if the robot's position is within a collision threshold (τ) based on a local costmap generated from LiDAR data.
- **Control Barrier Function (CBF):** To explicitly guarantee the preservation of users' intimate zones and the safety of group members, a CBF is implemented. For each group member, a barrier function h(q_r)_i is defined to ensure that the robot maintains a minimum allowable distance (r) from individuals, thereby preventing intrusion into their personal space.
- **Implementation:** The MPPI controller is implemented in Python using PyTorch for GPU-accelerated parallel processing. Key parameters include x_offset = 1.5, y_offset = 1.5, ∆_grid = 0.5, weighting factors for tracking (w_x = 50, w_y = 10), obstacle avoidance (w_o = 10000), a collision threshold (τ = 50), and a minimum intimate zone radius (r = 1.0).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated the proposed method through several evaluations, including VLM performance assessment, comparative analysis with baselines, a user study, an ablation study, and an evaluation of scalability with varying group sizes.

**A. Evaluation of VLM-Based Group Accompaniment Strategy:**
This evaluation assessed the social appropriateness and navigability of VLM-generated positions across 30 distinct scenarios involving dynamic group membership (5 to 10 individuals), repeated 10 times for each VLM.
- **VLM Performance:** Larger models, Gemini 2.5 Flash and GPT-4.1, achieved success rates of 98±1.2% and 96±1.5%, respectively. Lighter variants, Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite and GPT-4.1 Mini, showed success rates of 89±2.1% and 87±2.7%.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Qwen2.5-VL-7B provided the fastest inference at 0.7s, but with a reduced accuracy of 72±10.5%. Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite had an average response time of 1.12s, balancing accuracy and latency. Larger models like GPT-4.1 had higher latencies (3.89s).
- **Selection:** Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite was selected for subsequent evaluations due to its balance of performance and response time without requiring fine-tuning.

**B. Comparative Evaluation:**
The proposed method was compared against MPPI, ESFM, and PP across five scenarios using an omnidirectional mobile robot. Evaluation metrics included Success Rate (SR), Collision Rate (CR), and Comfortable Distance (CD), averaged over 20 runs.
- **Overall Performance:** The proposed method demonstrated an average 15% improvement in success rate and a 25% reduction in collision rate compared to baseline approaches. The comfortable distance maintained between the robot and group members was consistently within 0.87–1.1 m.
- **Scenario 1 (Baseline):** All methods achieved 100% SR and 0% CR, with CD varying (e.g., Proposed: 1.1m, MPPI: 0.75m).
- **Scenario 2 (Obstacles):** Proposed and PP methods performed best (Proposed: 80% SR, 10% CR; PP: 75% SR, 10% CR). MPPI and ESFM showed significantly higher collision rates (80% and 60%, respectively) due to misidentification or constrained movement.
- **Scenario 3 (Member Leaves):** The proposed method achieved 90% SR and 0% CR by successfully detecting a member leaving and adapting its position. MPPI and PP often followed the departing individual, while ESFM lost the group.
- **Scenario 4 (New Member Joins):** The proposed method (85% SR, 0% CR) correctly recognized the new member and adjusted its position. Baseline methods often treated new members as obstacles, leading to collisions (e.g., MPPI: 70% CR, ESFM: 85% CR).
- **Scenario 5 (Formation Changes):** The proposed method maintained an appropriate accompanying position (95% SR, 5% CR). MPPI and ESFM exhibited collisions due to intrusive or excessive positional shifts.

**C. User Study:**
A user study with 20 participants evaluated the robot's behavior in four dynamic scenarios (excluding Scenario 1) using a five-point Likert scale across Comfort, Sociability, and Intelligence metrics.
- **Results:** The proposed method consistently received the highest average scores across all metrics. Intelligence scores were particularly high.
- **Statistical Analysis:** A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA confirmed a significant effect of the method choice on all metrics (p < 0.05), with post-hoc analysis indicating significantly higher scores for the proposed method. An interaction effect between Method and Scenario (p < 0.05) suggested that the performance advantage of the proposed method increased with scenario complexity.

**D. Ablation Study:**
An ablation study assessed the contribution of individual components across the five scenarios.
- **Without VLM group detection:** Performance was primarily affected in Scenario 3 (member leaves), where the robot continued to track the departed individual, reducing success.
- **Without VLM for companion:** The robot relied on a static accompanying position and only the tracking controller. This led to collisions in all but the baseline Scenario 1, indicating the VLM's crucial role in decision-making for adaptive companionship.
- **Without interactive space:** The robot failed in all scenarios, including Scenario 1, because the VLM generated inaccurate positions without the structured cues provided by the interaction space. This highlights the critical importance of the grid-based interaction space for the VLM's spatial reasoning.

**E. Evaluation of Different Group Sizes:**
The method's scalability was tested with group sizes ranging from 3 to 12 members.
- **Success Rate:** High success rates were observed for smaller groups (95% for 3 members, 90% for 5 members). Performance gradually degraded with increasing group size, dropping to 40% for 12 members.
- **Latency:** Average reasoning latency increased with group size, from approximately 1.07s for 3 members to 2.77s for 12 members.
- **Conclusion:** The framework demonstrated effectiveness for groups up to approximately 7 members, with larger groups presenting challenges in terms of both success rate and latency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a novel and adaptive approach for robots accompanying human groups, addressing significant limitations of previous methods. By integrating Vision-Language Models (VLMs), the proposed system enables robots to adapt to dynamic changes in group formation and membership, a capability that has been largely unaddressed in prior work.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy in Social Settings:** The ability of robots to infer companion positions and recognize changes in group composition using VLM-based reasoning advances their capacity for human-like social cognition. This allows for more robust and flexible behavior in real-world human environments where groups are rarely static.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** The quantitative results demonstrating improved success rates and reduced collision rates, coupled with the positive outcomes from the user study (higher scores for comfort, sociability, and intelligence), indicate that the robot's generated companionship behaviors are perceived as more natural and socially appropriate. This fosters a more positive and safer interaction experience for humans.
- **Robustness to Dynamic Environments:** The method's demonstrated ability to handle scenarios involving obstacles, members leaving, members joining, and changes in group formation without relying on pre-defined patterns represents a notable improvement over existing approaches. The grid-based interaction space and Chain-of-Thought prompting specifically aid the VLM in making contextually relevant and safe decisions.
- **Foundation for Future Social Robotics:** This work provides a framework for developing more sophisticated social robots capable of operating effectively in complex, dynamic human environments. The ablation study confirms the criticality of each component—VLM for group detection, VLM for companionship decision-making, and the interactive space—underscoring their collective contribution to the system's performance.

The potential impact extends to various practical applications, including assistive robotics for daily tasks, logistical support in public spaces, and general human-robot collaboration in dynamic settings. While scalability challenges with very large groups and crowded environments were identified, the research provides a robust foundation, indicating that future work could focus on optimizing VLM reasoning speed (e.g., with local VLMs) and refining handling of higher-density scenarios to further expand the applicability of such companionship robots.
