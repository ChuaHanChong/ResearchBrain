# HiFAR: Multi-Stage Curriculum Learning for High-Dynamics Humanoid Fall Recovery

> [!summary] Summary
> The HiFAR framework, developed by Tsinghua University and Booster Robotics, enables humanoid robots to autonomously recover from diverse fall scenarios using a multi-stage curriculum learning approach. This method achieved a 100% real-world success rate for supine and prone falls on the Booster T1 robot, with average recovery times of 2.711 seconds and 2.875 seconds respectively, demonstrating robustness to external disturbances, varying loads, and complex environments.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots lack robust, autonomous fall recovery capabilities, leading to potential damage, operational downtime, and limited practical deployment in dynamic environments.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches for fall recovery often struggle with high-dimensional dynamics, sparse rewards, and the sim-to-real gap, particularly in contact-rich and diverse fall scenarios.
- Traditional methods like Finite State Machines and optimization-based controllers offer limited adaptability and struggle with the computational complexity of multi-contact situations inherent in falls.

## Method

- HiFAR employs a multi-stage curriculum learning framework, progressively training the robot from low-dimensional (2D) to high-dimensional (3D) fall recovery tasks.
- Key State Initialization (KSI) uses predefined critical states to stabilize the learning process and accelerate policy convergence, complemented by a comprehensive, weighted reward function.
- The framework integrates extensive domain randomization in simulation, a mirror loss term for symmetric learning, and network expansion for transferring knowledge between stages to ensure robust sim-to-real transfer to the Booster T1 humanoid robot.

## Results

- Achieved a 100% success rate for supine and prone falls on the Booster T1 humanoid robot, with average recovery times of 2.711 ± 0.319 seconds and 2.875 ± 0.474 seconds respectively.
- Demonstrated high robustness in simulation, recovering from 150-200N push forces, an 80% increase in torso mass, and friction coefficients down to 0.4, with successful recovery on the physical robot even with a 5kg load or strong disturbances.
- Successfully generalized to diverse real-world fall scenarios (e.g., lateral, crossed-leg, seated) and complex environments (e.g., slopes, obstacles, outdoor settings) on the Booster T1 robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Progressively increasing task dimensionality and complexity through a multi-stage curriculum learning effectively addresses the challenges of sparse rewards and high-dimensional dynamics in complex robotic tasks like fall recovery.
> - Key State Initialization significantly stabilizes the RL training process and improves policy convergence by providing consistent, critical starting points for contact-rich recovery behaviors.
> - A combination of comprehensive reward shaping, extensive domain randomization, and targeted network architecture choices enables successful sim-to-real transfer of complex, high-dynamic behaviors to physical humanoid robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The capability for autonomous fall recovery is a critical and long-standing challenge in humanoid robotics, central to enabling robots to operate safely and effectively in human-centric environments. This work by Chen et al. directly addresses this challenge, building upon and advancing existing methodologies.

Historically, fall recovery solutions for humanoids have evolved through several stages. Early approaches relied on **finite state machines (FSMs)** and preprogrammed actions (e.g., Honda's ASIMO), which were computationally lightweight but lacked adaptability in unstructured environments. The field then progressed to **optimization-based methods**, such as those leveraging Capture Point theory, Model Predictive Control (MPC), and Whole-Body Control (WBC). These methods improved real-time adaptation and balance recovery by formulating stability as an optimization problem. However, they struggle with high-dimensional dynamics, contact-rich scenarios inherent in falls, reliance on precise system modeling, and computational latency, especially in multi-contact situations.

The emergence of **Reinforcement Learning (RL)** has offered a promising alternative, sidestepping the need for explicit physics modeling by allowing robots to learn recovery policies through environmental interaction. RL has shown success in humanoid locomotion, long-horizon behaviors, and contact-rich tasks. Despite these successes, applying RL to fall recovery presents its own set of challenges: sparse rewards, complex collision scenarios, and the notorious "sim-to-real gap"—the discrepancy between simulated and real-world performance.

Prior RL-based fall recovery approaches have explored various strategies, including predefined trajectories, symmetric behaviors, key state initialization (KSI), and task stage division. However, each of these methods has limitations. For instance, predefined trajectories can restrict the robot's adaptability (Haarnoja et al.), symmetric behaviors might oversimplify the problem (FRASA), and explicit task segmentation may limit generalization across diverse fall scenarios (HoST). Other works like HumanUp use multi-stage learning but limit recovery speed and adaptability through slow trajectory tracking. Chuanyu Yang et al. demonstrated KSI for fall recovery but primarily validated it in simulation for humanoids, with real-world validation only on quadruped robots.

"HiFAR" positions itself within this landscape by directly tackling these persistent limitations. It leverages the strengths of RL while mitigating its weaknesses through a **multi-stage curriculum learning framework**. This approach is designed to systematically overcome the complexity, sparse rewards, and sim-to-real challenges that have hindered previous methods. By progressively increasing task dimensionality and complexity, integrating tailored optimization techniques, and employing robust sim-to-real transfer strategies, HiFAR aims to develop more efficient, stable, robust, and generalizable fall recovery policies for real-world humanoid robots, thereby pushing the boundaries of autonomous resilience for humanoids.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent vulnerability of humanoid robots to falls, especially in the dynamic and unpredictable real-world environments where they are increasingly expected to operate. Falls are an inevitable occurrence, and without robust autonomous recovery capabilities, humanoid robots face significant risks of damage, operational downtime, and reduced safety, which fundamentally limits their practical deployment. The paper explicitly highlights the importance of quick and efficient recovery in competitive settings like RoboCup, underscoring the demand for high-performance solutions.

Given these challenges, the overarching objective of this work is to **develop a robust policy that enables a fallen humanoid robot to autonomously recover and stand up across a diverse range of fall scenarios, including supine, prone, and lateral falls.**

To achieve this, the authors identify several specific goals and criteria for evaluating their proposed fall recovery policy:

- **Success Rate:** The policy must consistently enable the robot to recover from falls.
- **Recovery Time:** The robot should recover quickly to minimize downtime.
- **Robustness:** The policy must be resilient to external disturbances, uncertainties (e.g., varying loads, terrain friction), and actuator limitations.
- **Generalization:** The robot should be able to recover from falls under different initial states and environmental conditions, not just predefined or limited scenarios.

The motivation behind the chosen methodology, a multi-stage curriculum learning framework named HiFAR, is to systematically address the inherent complexities of humanoid fall recovery that render single-stage RL problematic. These complexities include the high-dimensional dynamics, intricate collision and contact scenarios, sparse reward structures, and the significant sim-to-real gap exacerbated by hardware constraints and environmental variability. By progressively structuring the learning task, HiFAR aims to make the learning process more tractable, efficient, and ultimately lead to a deployable, robust, and generalized fall recovery policy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the HiFAR framework is a **multi-stage curriculum learning approach** designed to progressively introduce complexity and high-dimensional tasks to the robot. This systematic training facilitates the acquisition of robust and stable fall recovery strategies.

**A. Framework and Curriculum Setup:**
HiFAR is divided into two distinct stages:

1. **Stage 1: Basic Fall Recovery Policy (Low-Dimensional Task Environment):**
 - **Focus:** Developing fundamental recovery behaviors.
 - **Task Constraint:** Limited to two-dimensional fall scenarios (e.g., supine and prone falls).
 - **Control Space:** Constrained to joints operating predominantly within the (x, z) plane (e.g., main leg and arm joints for forward/backward movement, highlighted in yellow in Fig. 2A). This reduces exploration space and self-collision risk.
 - **Learning Facilitation:** Utilizes Key State Initialization (KSI) and reward shaping.

2. **Stage 2: Deployable Fall Recovery Policy (High-Dimensional Task Setting):**
 - **Focus:** Training for real-world deployable policies.
 - **Task Expansion:** Encompasses three-dimensional fall scenarios (e.g., cross-leg falls, lateral falls).
 - **Control Space Expansion:** Includes lateral hip roll joints (highlighted in orange in Fig. 2A) to handle diverse real-world falls.
 - **Constraints:** Incorporates realistic joint position, torque, and velocity limits.
 - **Variability:** Introduces further randomizations in terrain and robot state to enhance robustness.

The curriculum across these stages progressively increases **Task Dimension** (2D to 3D), tightens **Task Constraints** (lenient to stringent, with complex perturbations), and increases **Task Complexity** (straightforward to varied randomized fall scenarios).

**B. Training Setup:**
- **Simulator:** Booster Gym, an open-source framework incorporating sim-to-real transfer techniques.
- **Target State:** A stable upright posture, defined by specific Center of Mass (CoM) height, Degrees of Freedom (DoF) positions, and torso orientation.
- **Observations:** Proprioceptive data including robot orientation, angular velocity, joint positions, velocities, and previous actions.
- **Action Space:** Target joint positions for the controlled joints.
- **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) due to its stability and sample efficiency.
- **Network Architecture:** RMA (Rapid Motor Adaptation), known for its adaptation capabilities.
- **Cross-Stage Network Expansion:** Knowledge from Stage 1 is transferred to Stage 2 by integrating zero-initialized fully-connected layers, allowing for expanded action and perception dimensions while preserving prior learning.
- **Mirror Loss Term:** Included to encourage symmetric recovery strategies while allowing for necessary asymmetry, enhancing realism and generalizability.

**C. Key State Initialization (KSI):**
Six critical states (three prone, three supine) derived from established handcrafted fall recovery motions are used. At the beginning of each training episode, the agent's state is initialized to one of these key states. This technique stabilizes the learning process, improves policy convergence, and provides consistent starting points, especially crucial for sparse reward environments.

**D. Reward Design:**
A comprehensive reward function (Table I in the paper) is employed, consisting of multiple weighted components (ω_i).
- **Positive Rewards:** Encourage survival, desired base height, standing posture, correct orientation, and adherence to DoF reference positions.
- **Negative Rewards (Penalties):** Discourage high torques, excessive DoF velocities/accelerations, root acceleration, high action rates, DoF limit violations, torque fatigue, power consumption, and excessive hip roll. Weights are adjusted across stages to align with task objectives.

**E. Domain Randomization:**
To enhance robustness and generalizability and bridge the sim-to-real gap, extensive domain randomization is applied in the simulation environment. This includes randomizing:
- DoF positions, base XY position, and linear velocity.
- Joint stiffness and damping.
- Terrain friction, compliance, and restitution.
- Torso and other link CoM positions and masses.
Additionally, control delays are modeled within Booster Gym to increase simulation realism.

**F. Hardware Platform:**
The learned policy is validated on the **Booster T1**, a 118cm tall humanoid robot with 23 DoFs, equipped with IMUs, joint encoders, and an Nvidia AGX Orin GPU for on-board computation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling results from both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating the effectiveness of the HiFAR framework.

**A. Training Analysis:**
The training analysis, specifically the standing reward curve shown in Fig. 3, illustrates four distinct step-like increases. This pattern confirms that the Key State Initialization (KSI) strategy effectively guides the learning process, allowing the agent to progressively master recovery from different key states and stabilize policy convergence.

**B. Simulation Experiments (Webots Simulator):**
Experiments were conducted using Webots to evaluate success rate, recovery time, and robustness under various conditions.

- **Standard Supine and Prone Recovery:** The policy achieved a **100% success rate** for both prone and supine falls. Snapshots and torso pitch curves (Fig. 4) demonstrate rapid, highly dynamic recovery movements, closely matching expected performance.
- **Disturbance Experiments (Planar and Lateral Forces):**
 - **Planar Force:** For prone and supine falls, the robot maintained a 100% success rate against push forces up to 150-200N. Recovery gracefully degraded with stronger forces (e.g., 0% at 300N for prone, 60% at 300N for supine).
 - **Lateral Force:** Similarly, the robot showed high robustness, achieving 100% success up to 100-150N lateral forces, demonstrating resilience against pushes from the side.
- **Load Experiments:** The policy exhibited strong robustness to mass inaccuracies. The robot recovered with a **100% success rate** from prone and supine falls even with an **80% increase in torso mass** (e.g., +9.4kg), highlighting its ability to handle additional payloads or variations in robot configuration.
- **Friction Experiments:** The policy proved robust across varying friction coefficients. It achieved 100% success rates down to a friction coefficient of 0.4, and 90% success for supine recovery at a very low 0.1 friction coefficient, indicating applicability to diverse real-world terrains.
- **Torque Limits Experiments:** Demonstrating resilience to actuator degradation, the robot achieved a 100% success rate at an 85% torque limit and 60% for supine recovery at a 75% limit, suggesting effective operation even with reduced motor capabilities.

**C. Real-World Experiments (Booster T1 Humanoid Robot):**
Validation on the physical Booster T1 robot provided critical evidence for the sim-to-real transferability and practical utility of HiFAR.

- **Supine and Prone Recovery:** The robot achieved a **100% success rate** for both supine and prone falls, with average recovery times of **2.711 ± 0.319 seconds** for supine and **2.875 ± 0.474 seconds** for prone (Table VIII). The observed high-dynamic recovery behaviors closely matched simulation results, confirming successful sim-to-real transfer.
- **Random Initial State and Environmental Complexity Experiments:**
 - **Generalization:** The robot successfully recovered from a wide array of initial fall states, including lateral, apart-leg, seated, and crossed-leg postures (Fig. 6A), showcasing the adaptability of the learned policy.
 - **Environmental Robustness:** The robot consistently performed versatile recovery behaviors in complex environments, such as standing up from slopes, recovering with obstacles between its legs, and operating in outdoor settings (Fig. 6B).
- **Load and Disturbance Experiments:**
 - **Load Handling:** The robot successfully stood up while carrying a **5 kg load** (Fig. 7A), demonstrating its capacity to manage additional payloads.
 - **Strong Disturbances:** When subjected to strong push forces or impacts during recovery (Fig. 7B, C), the robot autonomously adopted a safe sitting posture to mitigate the impact before executing a high-dynamic recovery to a standing stance. This highlights the policy's exceptional robustness to unexpected external forces.
- **Various Behaviors:** The policy also demonstrated integrated capabilities, such as using ankle and hip strategies for minor perturbations and recovery steps for stronger pushes. Importantly, after successful recovery, the robot stabilized into a standard stance, allowing for a seamless transition into locomotion, further underscoring the practicality of the approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HiFAR framework represents a significant advancement in the field of humanoid robotics, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenge of autonomous fall recovery. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Real-World Deployment:** The most profound impact of this work is its direct contribution to making humanoid robots more resilient and, consequently, more viable for real-world deployment in dynamic and unstructured environments. By ensuring quick and reliable recovery from falls, HiFAR minimizes downtime, reduces the risk of costly damage, and enhances the overall safety and operational efficiency of humanoids. This is critical for applications ranging from competitive robotics (e.g., RoboCup) to future service and industrial roles.

- **Overcoming Key RL Limitations:** The multi-stage curriculum learning approach successfully tackles several persistent issues in applying reinforcement learning to complex robotic tasks. It effectively mitigates problems like sparse rewards, the intricacies of contact-rich dynamics, and the notorious sim-to-real gap. By progressively increasing task complexity and dimension, HiFAR makes the learning process more tractable and efficient, enabling the acquisition of high-dynamics skills that are difficult to learn otherwise.

- **Demonstrated Robustness and Generalization:** The extensive validation, both in simulation and on a physical robot, rigorously proves the policy's exceptional robustness and generalization capabilities. Recovery from diverse fall scenarios (supine, prone, lateral, crossed-leg), under significant external disturbances (pushes, impacts), varying loads, different friction terrains, and even motor torque limitations, showcases a level of resilience rarely achieved in previous works. The robot's ability to adapt its recovery strategy (e.g., adopting a safe sitting posture under impact) further underscores this robustness.

- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The empirical validation on the Booster T1 humanoid robot is a crucial achievement. Many RL solutions remain in simulation, but HiFAR's seamless transfer to physical hardware validates the effectiveness of the integrated domain randomization and training strategies, demonstrating practical applicability. This successful transfer builds confidence in using RL for complex, safety-critical tasks on real robots.

- **Methodological Contribution:** The HiFAR framework introduces a novel stage division strategy and curriculum setting, combined with Key State Initialization (KSI) and tailored reward shaping. The dimensionality expansion via supplementary actuated joints during the curriculum is a clever approach to gradually enhance generalization. These methodological contributions offer valuable insights and blueprints for future research in training complex, dynamic behaviors for other robotic platforms.

- **Foundation for Adaptive Robotics:** While focused on fall recovery, the principles demonstrated by HiFAR—especially multi-stage curriculum learning, robust sim-to-real transfer, and dynamic adaptability—can be extended to a wider range of adaptive robotics applications. This could include learning complex locomotion patterns, manipulation skills in cluttered environments, or human-robot interaction tasks where robustness to unexpected events is paramount.

In conclusion, HiFAR provides a robust and practically validated solution for a critical problem in humanoid robotics. Its contributions significantly advance the state of the art in autonomous fall recovery, laying important groundwork for the future deployment of highly resilient and adaptable humanoid robots. Future work aiming to enhance adaptability under space or speed constraints will further refine these capabilities, opening new avenues for humanoid robot interaction with the physical world.
