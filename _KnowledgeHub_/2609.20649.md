# DexTouch-WM: Learning Action-Conditioned Tactile World Models from Human Touch for Dexterous Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> DexTouch-WM is an action-conditioned visuo-tactile world model that jointly predicts future RGB and bilateral full-hand tactile maps for dexterous hands. It couples a pretrained Wan2.2 video expert with a lightweight tactile expert via Mixture-of-Transformers, trained on 100 hours of glove-based human touch sharing the robot's sensor layout and retargeted actions. Holding 5 hours of robot data fixed, adding human data raises held-out robot Contact-F1 from 0.55 to 0.71.

## Problem

- Tactile world-model datasets come from robot teleoperation, tying each dataset to one embodiment and sensor, so they stay small and mostly limited to parallel-jaw grippers or isolated fingertip sensors.
- Video-only action-conditioned world models cannot represent contact pressure, slip, or jamming that determine dexterous outcomes, especially when the hand occludes the object.
- Human visuo-tactile data mainly serves imitation, representation learning, or policy transfer, and lacks a controlled test of whether it scales robot-domain world models at fixed robot data.

## Method

- ==Mixture-of-Transformers architecture==: a ==Wan2.2-TI2V-5B== video expert and a lightweight ==tactile DiT== exchange context through ==masked joint self-attention== while keeping modality-specific ==AdaLN== and feed-forward pathways, trained with ==conditional flow matching==.
- **Split-Hands** anatomy-aware tactile tokenizer: shared fingertip and palm encoders with ==pad identity embeddings==, pretrained with ==contact-weighted reconstruction==; the tactile expert predicts ==residual latents== anchored to the initial tactile observation.
- **HumanTouch** pipeline plus ==dual-rate action conditioning==: shared 320-taxel glove layout on human and Wuji robot hands, ==IK retargeting== to a common 67-D pose space, injected via AdaLN per four-frame video chunk and per tactile frame.

## Results

- With robot data fixed at 5 h, adding 100 h of task-disjoint human data raises held-out robot visual PSNR **23.47** to **27.10**, Trajectory Accuracy **0.89** to **0.96**, Contact-IoU **0.42** to **0.59**.
- AdaLN action injection beats cross-attention in the joint model (PSNR **22.1** to **23.5**, Trajectory Accuracy **0.85** to **0.89**, Contact-F1 **0.52** to **0.55**); Split-Hands beats a grid tokenizer on reconstruction and Contact-IoU convergence.
- Replacing half the robot adaptation data with human demos raises evaluator mean Pearson with real scores **0.646** to **0.844** (MMRV **0.025** to **0.017**); yet half-imagined training data drops π0.5 from **0.625** to **0.494**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Matching the sensor layout and action space across embodiments lets cheap human interaction supervise robot dynamics directly, making human data a separate scaling axis without a learned domain mapping.
> - Contact dynamics scale later and longer than vision: visual gains saturate by 50 h while contact keeps improving, so small human-data pilots can underestimate tactile transfer.
> - A world model that ranks policies faithfully is not automatically a good data source; evaluator fidelity and training utility diverge and depend on policy and task.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

DexTouch-WM is situated at the intersection of tactile sensing, dexterous manipulation, multimodal world models, human-to-robot transfer, and synthetic data generation. World models are predictive models that estimate how an environment will evolve under a sequence of actions. In robotics, they can support policy evaluation, planning, learned simulation, and generation of additional training data. However, models based only on RGB observations have difficulty representing latent physical states such as contact pressure, friction, slip, jamming, and the onset of object motion, particularly when the robot hand occludes the manipulated object.

Recent visuo-tactile world models address this limitation by predicting both visual and tactile observations. Earlier work has generally collected tactile trajectories through robot teleoperation or robot-specific sensing systems. This makes the resulting data expensive to acquire and closely tied to a particular robot embodiment, hand design, and tactile sensor arrangement. Other research has used human-collected visual-tactile demonstrations for imitation learning, representation learning, or tactile policy transfer. DexTouch-WM differs by treating human touch as supervision for an action-conditioned predictive model intended to operate in the robot domain.

The paper’s central premise is that human and robot manipulation can share useful contact dynamics if two conditions are met. First, the tactile observations must be represented in a common sensing space. Second, human movements must be retargeted into the robot’s action space. The work therefore extends human-data transfer from behavioral imitation or tactile representation alignment to the learning of future multimodal interaction dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Reducing the Cost of Tactile Data Collection

Dexterous manipulation involves many simultaneous contacts across fingertips, fingers, and the palm. Learning predictive models for these interactions requires dense tactile trajectories, but collecting such data on a real robot is slow, costly, and constrained by hardware availability. The authors seek to establish whether human interaction can provide a scalable source of contact-rich experience while keeping the amount of robot data fixed.

The main scaling question is whether adding human interaction improves predictions on held-out robot trajectories, even when the human and robot task sets are disjoint. This design tests transfer of general contact and motion structure rather than simple task-specific data augmentation.

#### 2.2. Modeling Tactile Dynamics Alongside Video

The model is designed to predict future RGB frames and bilateral full-hand tactile maps given initial observations, an action sequence, and a language instruction. The motivation is that visual and tactile signals provide complementary information. RGB observations describe object identity, scene context, and gross motion, whereas tactile maps reveal contact location, pressure distribution, and changes associated with grasping, sliding, impact, or insertion.

#### 2.3. Handling the Structure of Full-Hand Tactile Data

The glove and robot hand contain spatially disconnected fingertip and palm sensing regions. Treating these regions as one regular image can create artificial spatial relationships—for example, implying that a palm taxel is physically adjacent to a fingertip taxel. The authors therefore aim to develop a tactile representation that preserves anatomical structure and focuses modeling capacity on sparse, task-relevant contact changes.

#### 2.4. Supporting Downstream Robot Learning

The paper evaluates whether the learned models are useful beyond reconstruction accuracy. Two downstream uses are examined: using a world model as a surrogate environment for comparing robot policies, and generating synthetic visuo-tactile trajectories for training policies on a real robot. These experiments test whether predictive accuracy translates into practical utility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Shared Interface

The model learns the conditional distribution

\[
p_\theta(v_{1:T}, x_{1:T} \mid v_0, x_0, a_{1:T}, \ell),
\]

where \(v_0\) and \(x_0\) are the initial RGB and tactile observations, \(a_{1:T}\) is a future pose sequence, and \(\ell\) is a language instruction.

Human demonstrations include wrist poses, 25-keypoint hand skeletons, finger configurations, and head-camera motion. Human motion is transformed into the robot’s reference frame, normalized for differences in hand morphology, and retargeted to a WujiHand2 dexterous hand through inverse kinematics. Both human and robot actions are represented by the same 67-dimensional vector containing bilateral wrist motion, bilateral 20-degree-of-freedom hand configurations, and head-camera motion. Relative wrist and camera motion are used, while hand joint angles are represented absolutely.

#### 3.2. Data Collection

The HumanTouch dataset contains approximately 100 hours of egocentric human interaction across 50 everyday bimanual tasks. The tasks include transport, grasping and regrasping, impact, insertion, manipulation of articulated and deformable objects, and sustained contact across multiple hand regions.

Participants wear bilateral piezoresistive tactile gloves under Manus MetaGloves used for finger tracking. HTC Vive trackers provide six-degree-of-freedom wrist poses. Two wrist-mounted cameras and a head-mounted camera record local hand-object interactions and broader workspace context. Calibration estimates camera intrinsics, tracker-to-camera extrinsics, and temporal alignment across devices. The gloves record 360 taxels per hand at 60 Hz, of which 320 are retained for modeling: five 4×4 fingertip pads and one 15×16 palm pad.

Robot data are collected with a Tianji robotic arm and a 20-degree-of-freedom Wuji dexterous hand fitted with the same full-hand piezoresistive sensing layout. Six robot tasks provide five hours of world-model pretraining data. Four additional robot tasks are reserved for downstream adaptation and evaluation. The human and robot pretraining task sets are disjoint.

#### 3.3. Model Architecture

DexTouch-WM combines a pretrained video expert based on Wan2.2-TI2V-5B and its frozen video VAE with a lightweight tactile expert called TactileDiT. The visual and tactile streams exchange information through bidirectional or joint masked self-attention, following a mixture-of-transformers design. Each modality retains its own normalization and feed-forward pathways, allowing shared context without fully merging the visual and tactile computations.

The initial RGB frame is encoded as a clean latent and retained at temporal index zero. Future video latents are generated from noised representations. Tactile observations are encoded using frozen tactile encoders and decoded by frozen split-hand decoders.

#### 3.4. Anatomy-Aware Tactile Tokenization

The tactile tokenizer uses separate shared encoders for the ten fingertip pads and the two palm pads. Pad-identity embeddings preserve which anatomical region produced each token, while shared encoders allow local pressure patterns to be represented consistently across corresponding regions.

The tokenizer is pretrained using contact-weighted reconstruction. This weighting limits the dominance of inactive taxels, which are numerous relative to active contact regions. The authors compare this Split-Hands design with a Grid baseline that places all tactile regions on a single spatial canvas. The Split-Hands representation converges faster on Contact-IoU and produces lower reconstruction error.

#### 3.5. Residual Tactile Prediction and Action Conditioning

Instead of predicting the entire future tactile state independently, the model encodes the initial tactile observation as \(z_0\) and predicts residuals:

\[
r_t=z_t-z_0.
\]

The predicted tactile observation is reconstructed from the initial latent plus the predicted residual. This approach preserves the static pressure background while concentrating prediction on action-induced changes.

Video and tactile streams operate at different temporal resolutions. The video VAE compresses future frames in four-frame chunks, whereas tactile observations retain their native rate. Accordingly, four-frame pose chunks condition video latents, while frame-level pose signals condition tactile predictions. AdaLN-based pose conditioning injects bilateral hand, wrist, and camera motion into each expert. Training uses conditional flow matching with separate visual and tactile losses. The tactile loss emphasizes contact or temporally changing tokens.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Architecture Ablations

The Split-Hands tactile representation outperforms the Grid representation in tactile reconstruction and contact prediction convergence. This supports the claim that anatomical structure is an important inductive bias for distributed full-hand sensing.

The authors also compare action-conditioning mechanisms. A visual-only model, a joint visual-tactile model using cross-attention for action injection, and the final Robot-only architecture using AdaLN are evaluated on the five-hour robot dataset. Adding tactile prediction through cross-attention leaves low-level visual metrics largely unchanged but reduces semantic and trajectory consistency. Trajectory Accuracy falls from approximately 0.884 for the visual-only model to 0.850.

Replacing this action injection with AdaLN improves both modalities. The Robot-only model achieves visual PSNR of 23.473, compared with 22.099 for the visual-only baseline and 22.130 for cross-attention. Its Trajectory Accuracy reaches 0.891. In the tactile domain, Contact-IoU increases to 0.415 and Contact-F1 to 0.551, compared with 0.388 and 0.523 for the cross-attention model.

#### 4.2. Human-to-Robot Scaling

With robot supervision fixed at five hours, adding 10, 50, and 100 hours of human data produces a general improvement in held-out robot-domain prediction. At 100 hours, visual PSNR rises from 23.473 to 27.096, SSIM from 0.824 to 0.890, and LPIPS falls from 0.098 to 0.048. Trajectory Accuracy increases from 0.891 to 0.962, while Geometry Error decreases from 0.131 to 0.100.

Tactile improvements are more pronounced. Tactile PSNR increases from 24.813 to 29.179, SSIM from 0.810 to 0.896, and heatmap LPIPS decreases from 0.008 to 0.002. Contact-MAE declines from 0.806 to 0.516. Contact-IoU rises from 0.415 to 0.588, and Contact-F1 from 0.551 to 0.706. Ten hours of human data produces relatively small tactile gains, whereas improvements become clearer at 50 and 100 hours. Visual metrics largely plateau between 50 and 100 hours, while contact prediction continues to improve.

#### 4.3. Policy Evaluation

The model is adapted for four downstream tasks and evaluated as a surrogate environment for FTP-1, π0.5, and X-VLA. WM-Robot uses 200 robot adaptation trajectories, while WM-Mix uses 100 robot and 100 task-specific human demonstrations.

WM-Mix has a higher mean Pearson correlation with real-world policy scores than WM-Robot, 0.844 versus 0.646, and a lower mean MMRV, 0.017 versus 0.025. It corrects a policy-ranking inversion on the Place Shoes task. However, both models still reverse the ranking of two policies on Stand Bottle. Thus, human data improves average agreement with real-world evaluation but does not eliminate task-specific ranking errors.

#### 4.4. Synthetic Data Generation

The authors generate synthetic trajectories by retaining initial observations and actions from held-out real episodes while predicting future RGB and tactile observations. These trajectories are combined with 100 real trajectories to train robot policies.

The results are mixed. For FTP-1, the human-adapted WM-Mix model produces a mean task score of 0.694, compared with 0.731 using all-real training data. For π0.5, the mean declines from 0.625 to 0.494, and for X-VLA from 0.500 to 0.400. The findings indicate that visual and tactile rollouts can resemble real trajectories and still provide training signals that are less useful than real data. Evaluator fidelity and policy-training utility are therefore not equivalent.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DexTouch-WM provides evidence that human tactile interaction can serve as a complementary data source for dexterous robot world models. Its contribution is not simply the collection of human tactile demonstrations; it combines a shared tactile hardware interface, action retargeting, anatomical tokenization, residual contact modeling, and multimodal predictive generation into one transfer framework.

If replicated across additional robot hands and sensor designs, the approach could reduce dependence on prolonged robot teleoperation for learning contact dynamics. Human data may be particularly useful for broadening the range of contact patterns, object interactions, and manipulation strategies represented during pretraining. The policy-evaluation results also suggest that world models trained with mixed human and robot data may support preliminary screening of policies before physical execution.

The results also identify important constraints. Transfer depends on sufficiently compatible tactile layouts and accurate human-to-robot action retargeting. The experiments use one principal robot embodiment and a specific sensor arrangement, so the extent of cross-embodiment generalization remains unresolved. Moreover, synthetic data does not consistently improve downstream policy learning, indicating that prediction quality, physical consistency, and usefulness for policy optimization require separate evaluation. Overall, the paper supports human touch as a scalable source of supervision for predictive robot learning while showing that additional real-world validation is needed before synthetic world-model rollouts can replace substantial robot data.
