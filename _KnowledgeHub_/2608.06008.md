# Adaptive-WAM: Quality-Guided Early-Exit Planning from Intermediate Video-Diffusion Features

> [!summary] Summary
> Adaptive-WAM presents a quality-guided early-exit planning framework for autonomous driving, utilizing intermediate features from video diffusion models. This method dynamically adjusts the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) depth, achieving state-of-the-art planning performance on NAVSIM benchmarks while reducing inference latency by up to 47%.

## Problem

- Autonomous driving planning using large video diffusion models incurs high computational costs due to the need for full future video generation.
- Existing World-Action Models (WAMs) often employ rigid computational paths, executing entire backbones even when only low-dimensional ego trajectories are required.
- Prior adaptive inference methods do not directly address the complexities of diffusion-based trajectory generation or the high correlation of planning rewards for quality verification.

## Method

- **Adaptive-WAM** incorporates a ==multi-exit architecture== into a video ==Diffusion Transformer (DiT)== backbone, enabling ego trajectory decoding from selected intermediate layers.
- A lightweight, ==quality-aware scorer== predicts the planning quality of trajectories generated at each exit without requiring future video generation or VAE decoding.
- An ==adaptive routing algorithm== dynamically terminates inference early if a predefined quality threshold is met, reusing cached hidden states to avoid redundant computation from deeper layers.

## Results

- Achieved a Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS) of **90.8** on NAVSIM v1 and **89.9** EPDMS on NAVSIM v2, surpassing other front-view video world-model planners.
- Reduced average end-to-end planning latency to **170 ms** (on an A100 GPU) with adaptive routing, which is **10%** faster than a fixed block-15 planner and **47%** faster than a full-depth planner.
- Demonstrated zero-shot transferability to nuScenes with an average L2 error of **0.88 meters** and a collision rate of **0.08%**, while maintaining a substantially lower inference cost compared to similar methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-quality autonomous driving decisions can be effectively made using intermediate features of a video diffusion model, significantly before completing the computationally intensive full future video synthesis.
> - Planning performance demonstrates substantial depth-dependent variation within the DiT backbone, with certain intermediate layers yielding better or complementary results compared to the full depth.
> - Dynamically allocating computational resources based on predicted trajectory quality allows for an improved performance-computation trade-off, avoiding the fixed overhead of full model execution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous driving systems require robust planning capabilities that can anticipate the evolution of dynamic scenes and the behavior of other road users. Large video models have emerged as a valuable resource for providing rich spatiotemporal priors, enabling models to learn complex temporal dynamics and motion patterns relevant to driving scenarios. This has led to the development of driving-specific world-action models (WAMs) that adapt these priors for future prediction, simulation, and ultimately, planning.

Traditional approaches in this area often involve either decoding actions directly from video-model features or jointly generating visual futures and trajectories. While these methods strengthen the link between prediction and action, their deployment often entails significant computational overhead. This is primarily due to the iterative nature of future-video generation or the rigid adherence to a predetermined computation path through a large video backbone, even when the ultimate requirement is merely a low-dimensional ego trajectory.

The paper situates itself within the context of efficient video representations and adaptive inference. Prior work has demonstrated that predictive video representations can support action generation without necessitating the full rendering of future observations. For instance, models like DiT4DiT and DriveLaW use early-denoising video representations for robotic control and trajectory planning, respectively, bypassing explicit image imagination during deployment. However, these systems often still rely on a fixed representation interface or a predetermined computational trajectory through the backbone. Separately, early-exit networks (e.g., BranchyNet, MSDNet) exist for tasks like image classification, allowing inference termination based on confidence, but they do not directly address the complexities of diffusion-based trajectory generation, highly correlated planning rewards, or trajectory-quality verification.

Adaptive-WAM integrates these research directions by proposing a method that leverages the powerful spatiotemporal priors from large video diffusion models while addressing the computational inefficiencies inherent in their full execution for planning tasks. It seeks to dynamically adjust the computational effort based on the evolving quality of the generated plan, moving beyond fixed-path or full-synthesis deployment strategies for autonomous driving. This work contributes to the development of more resource-efficient and adaptable driving policies derived from advanced generative models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from an identified inefficiency in existing world-action models (WAMs) for autonomous driving. While large video diffusion models provide valuable spatiotemporal priors for anticipating future scenarios, their deployment often involves generating complete future videos. This process is computationally intensive, requiring iterative denoising loops and VAE decoding, despite the fact that the ultimate output needed for driving is a relatively low-dimensional ego trajectory. The authors observe that current WAM designs are "unnecessarily rigid" in their computational commitment.

This observation led the authors to pose a fundamental question: "how much of a video diffusion model must be executed to make a reliable driving decision?" To investigate this, they conducted a controlled study on the NAVSIM dataset, examining two key factors:
1. **Video denoising timesteps**: They evaluated planning performance across five different video noise levels (sampling indices {1, 9, 17, 25, 32}) for fixed DiT layers (blocks 15 and 18). They found that planning performance showed minimal sensitivity to these tested video-noise levels, with scores varying by at most 0.15 points. This indicated that the video-noise level was not the primary bottleneck for planning quality.
2. **Diffusion Transformer (DiT) depth**: They analyzed how planning performance varied with the depth of the DiT backbone, using fixed intermediate layers (blocks 5, 9, 15, 18, 22, 30) as readout points. This revealed substantial depth-dependent variation in action quality, with certain intermediate layers (e.g., block 15) outperforming the full-depth exit (block 30) after both imitation learning and planner reinforcement learning.
3. **Redundancy and Complementarity of Layers**: Further analysis using Jaccard overlap of "high-quality" scene sets showed that while different DiT depths produced largely overlapping sets of successfully solved scenes (Jaccard indices ranging from 0.69 to 0.82), the overlap was incomplete. This indicated that no single fixed exit universally dominated all scenes; different depths offered complementary advantages for specific scenarios. Pairwise directional advantage counts further corroborated this, showing that even a globally strong layer like block 15 still had scenes where deeper or shallower layers performed better.

These findings motivated the primary objective: to develop a quality-aware, layer-adaptive world-action model that dynamically allocates computational resources (DiT depth) based on the predicted quality of the ego trajectory. The goal is to avoid the full cost of future-video synthesis and iterative denoising while still leveraging the rich features learned by the video diffusion model, thereby achieving an improved performance-computation trade-off for real-time autonomous driving planning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Adaptive-WAM, is a quality-aware, layer-adaptive world-action model designed for efficient planning by dynamically determining the required depth of a video Diffusion Transformer (DiT) backbone.

**Problem Formulation and Backbone Initialization:**
The planner takes as input an observation `o` comprising the current front-camera image (`I`), current and historical ego states (`S_ego`), and a navigation command (`L_nav`). It outputs a four-second ego trajectory `τ` consisting of eight poses at 0.5-second intervals.
The core visual dynamics backbone is initialized from **Wan2.2-TI2V-5B**, a latent video DiT. During inference, a conditional Wan branch processes the current image and a text description `d(o)` (derived from deployment-available structured attributes) at a fixed video-noise index `s⋆ = 17`. This operation `hℓ = F1:ℓ(I, d(o); s⋆)` represents a single forward pass up to DiT block `ℓ`, crucially avoiding ground-truth future video input or VAE decoding during deployment.

**Multi-Exit Trajectory Decoding:**
Adaptive-WAM incorporates multiple "exits" at selected intermediate DiT blocks: `E = {5, 9, 15, 18, 22, 30}`. At each exit `ℓ`:
- A projection layer `Pℓ` converts the hidden representation `hℓ` into trajectory-conditioning tokens.
- An independent diffusion head `Gℓ` generates a single ego trajectory `τℓ`. These heads are ReCogDrive-style five-step trajectory DiTs, all sharing the same architecture, optimization budget, and number of denoising steps. This design ensures that observed performance differences across exits are attributable to the backbone depth rather than variations in head capacity.

**Quality-Guided Adaptive Inference:**
The core of Adaptive-WAM's efficiency lies in its adaptive routing mechanism:
- **Trajectory Quality Scorer**: A lightweight scorer `Sϕ` is employed to predict the quality of decoded trajectories. This scorer fine-tunes a DINOv2-Small image encoder for visual features and uses an MLP to embed the flattened eight-pose trajectory. Image and trajectory features are concatenated and fed into six independent two-layer MLP heads, which output logits for individual planning sub-scores (No-at-fault Collision (NC), Drivable-Area Compliance (DAC), Driving-Direction Compliance (DDC), Time-to-Collision (TTC), Ego Progress (EP), Comfort (Comf)). These predictions (`ˆrℓ`) are then combined into a 100-point normalized PDMS score, `Q(ˆr)`. The scorer does not receive ego state or navigation commands.
- **Adaptive Routing Algorithm**:
 1. The system starts by evaluating the shallowest exit (e.g., Block 5).
 2. For each attempted exit `ℓ`, one trajectory `τℓ` is decoded, and its predicted quality `Q(ˆrℓ)` is cached.
 3. A pool `Aj` accumulates the trajectories decoded up to the current attempted exit `j`. The best trajectory `ˆτj` and its score `ˆqj` are tracked within this pool.
 4. If the current best accumulated score `ˆqj` meets a predefined quality threshold `η`, or if the deepest exit (Block 30) has been reached, the controller terminates inference and returns `ˆτj`.
 5. If the threshold is not met, computation continues from the cached hidden state to the next deeper exit. This avoids re-running earlier backbone blocks.
This process bypasses the iterative classifier-free denoising loop and VAE decoding required for future-video synthesis, dynamically allocating backbone depth based on the trajectory quality.

**Training:**
- **Video-Domain Adaptation**: During imitation learning, the Wan backbone is adapted using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), while the trajectory projection layers and heads are fully optimized. The actor objective `L_actor` combines the native Wan video-diffusion objective (`L_vid`) with the trajectory losses (`Lℓtraj`) from all six heads. Video prediction here serves as representation supervision during training and is not a required output during deployment.
- **Trajectory-Quality Scorer Training**: The scorer is fine-tuned on generated trajectories using evaluator-provided component targets. The loss (`L_score`) uses equal-weight soft-label BCE-with-logits, treating each oracle component as a target in. Crucially, generated trajectories are treated as stop-gradient inputs (`∇θWan,θG Lscore = 0`) to prevent the scorer from altering the actor's trajectory distribution. Actor and scorer updates alternate on the same training stream.
- **Planner-Only Reinforcement Learning**: After imitation learning, the Wan backbone and scorer are frozen. Each trajectory head is then refined using DiffGRPO, where a complete five-step action-denoising chain is considered a single action, and its NAVSIM evaluator score is used as a reward.

This methodology combines the representational power of a large video diffusion model with an adaptive, quality-guided inference strategy, aiming for both high performance and computational efficiency in autonomous driving planning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on NAVSIM v1, NAVSIM v2, and nuScenes support the paper's claims regarding performance, efficiency, and transferability.

**Planning Performance on NAVSIM:**
- **NAVSIM v1**: The adaptive single-trajectory planner achieved a Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS) of **90.8**. An auxiliary fixed-exit variant (using block 22 with 64 proposals without routing) reached **92.6 PDMS**, exceeding DriveVA's mixed-data headline result by 1.7 points and its NAVSIM-only result by 2.1 points. These results position Adaptive-WAM as a leading performer among compared front-view video world-model planners.
- **NAVSIM v2**: Adaptive-WAM obtained an Extended Predictive Driver Model Score (EPDMS) of **89.9**, outperforming the listed front-view world-model planners in this more comprehensive benchmark.

**Zero-Shot Transfer to nuScenes:**
- Without any fine-tuning on the nuScenes dataset, Adaptive-WAM achieved an average L2 error of **0.88 meters** and a collision rate of **0.08%**. While DriveVA reported slightly better metrics (0.84 m L2 error, 0.06% collision rate), Adaptive-WAM's inference cost is substantially lower. DriveVA executes the full Wan backbone and generates future images to support trajectory prediction, whereas Adaptive-WAM performs a single conditional early-exit planning pass, avoiding full video generation.

**Efficiency and Adaptive Trade-off:**
- The adaptive policy demonstrates an improved performance–computation frontier. With a quality threshold `η = 90`, Adaptive-WAM achieved **90.79 PDMS** with an average end-to-end planning latency of **170 ms** on an A100 GPU (batch size one).
- This latency is approximately **10% lower** than a fixed block-15 planner (190 ms, 90.62 PDMS) and **47% lower** than a fixed full-depth planner (320 ms, 85.82 PDMS). The full-depth planner additionally performs one VAE decode.
- The adaptive routing proved effective, with more than **94% of scenes terminating within the first three exits** at `η = 90`. This contrasts sharply with the computational cost of full 40-step classifier-free future-video generation, which takes 13.22 seconds.
- A sweep of thresholds showed the importance of the quality threshold: a permissive `η = 70` was faster (112 ms) but significantly reduced PDMS to 88.49, demonstrating that indiscriminate early exiting sacrifices performance.

**Scorer Reliability and Ablations:**
- **Scorer Reliability**: On an offline diagnostic pool of 12,146 scenes, the quality scorer achieved exact top-score selection in 91.2% of scenes. Its tie-aware soft accuracy (selecting a trajectory within 5% of the true top score) reached 94.4%. Consequential failures, where a trajectory at least 50% worse than an available near-perfect one was selected, occurred in only 0.42% of scenes.
- **Wan Adaptation**: Freezing the Wan backbone was insufficient (84.20 PDMS). Joint LoRA training of the Wan backbone provided a significant gain, improving the single-trajectory model to 90.62 PDMS, a 5.67-point increase over using separately cached features (84.95 PDMS). Full fine-tuning offered no meaningful additional performance (90.64 PDMS) over LoRA.
- **Visual Representation**: The Wan intermediate features were critical for planning quality, outperforming other visual backbones like ViT-Small (83.91 PDMS), ViT-Base (85.62 PDMS), and ViT-Large (88.88 PDMS) in the single-trajectory setting.
- **Scorer Backbone**: DINOv2-Small was chosen as the default scorer due to its efficiency and comparable performance to more complex options. It obtained a diagnostic score of 92.59, which was only 0.03 points below the best Wan-based scorer while incurring substantially lower inference cost.

Overall, the results validate that intermediate video-DiT features provide a robust planning substrate, and adaptive, quality-guided routing significantly enhances the performance-computation trade-off for autonomous driving.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Adaptive-WAM represents a significant advancement in the development of efficient and effective autonomous driving policies derived from large generative world models. Its core contribution lies in demonstrating that high-quality driving decisions can be made using intermediate features of a video diffusion model, well before the computationally intensive process of full video image synthesis is complete. This finding fundamentally challenges the assumption that full future video generation is a prerequisite for robust planning in such models.

The introduction of Adaptive-WAM, a multi-exit architecture with a quality-guided controller, provides a novel approach to resource allocation in deep learning models for control tasks. By dynamically selecting the required Diffusion Transformer (DiT) depth based on the predicted quality of the ego trajectory, the system optimizes computational cost without compromising performance. This adaptive strategy yields a notable improvement in the performance–computation frontier, as evidenced by achieving state-of-the-art planning performance on NAVSIM benchmarks while simultaneously reducing average inference latency compared to fixed-depth alternatives.

The practical implications of this work are substantial:
- **Efficiency for Real-Time Systems**: The substantial reduction in planning latency (e.g., 170 ms for adaptive vs. 320 ms for full-depth) makes it more feasible to deploy large generative world models in real-time autonomous driving systems, where low-latency decision-making is critical for safety and responsiveness.
- **Leveraging Powerful Backbones**: Adaptive-WAM provides a blueprint for how the rich spatiotemporal priors learned by very large generative models, such as the Wan family of video DiTs, can be efficiently harnessed for practical control applications, without incurring their full computational cost at inference.
- **Generalizability**: The demonstrated zero-shot transferability to the nuScenes dataset underscores the robustness and generalizability of the learned representations. This suggests that models trained on one complex driving simulator can be effectively applied to real-world datasets, reducing the need for extensive domain-specific fine-tuning.
- **New Design Paradigms**: This research opens avenues for designing future world models and control policies that are intrinsically aware of the trade-offs between computation and decision quality. It encourages exploration into other forms of adaptive computation and early-exit strategies within complex generative architectures, especially where the final output is a low-dimensional control signal rather than a high-fidelity visual prediction.

In conclusion, Adaptive-WAM offers a pragmatic and high-performing solution for integrating the capabilities of large generative video models into efficient autonomous driving policies, pointing towards a future where intelligent agents can leverage extensive world knowledge with optimized computational footprints.
