# HapticWAM: Distilling Imagined Touch into a World-Action Model without Inference-Time Tactile Sensing

> [!summary] Summary
> HapticWAM is a world-action model for contact-rich manipulation that encodes optical-tactile gel images plus mechanical fields into a frozen video diffusion backbone, jointly generating action chunks and a structured contact package of events, forces and geometry. Anticipatory Contact Coupling conditions attention on the previously imagined package, and Haptic-Imagination Distillation transfers contact futures to a tactile-free student, which reaches 77% per-task mean success across three real pick-and-place tasks.

## Problem

- Scene images cannot distinguish a stable grasp from light contact, especially when the gripper occludes the interaction, leaving forces, slip and contact geometry ambiguous.
- Prior world models predict visual or tactile pixel futures, spending capacity on appearance while leaving grip force, contact geometry and slip implicitly represented rather than directly supervised.
- Privileged-modality distillation transfers a teacher representation, action or single sensory token, so a student cannot keep a contact-conditioned observation interface once fingertip measurements are removed.

## Method

- **Heterogeneous Haptic Tokenization** encodes gel appearance through a ==frozen VAE== and eight mechanical channels through a convolutional encoder with ==FiLM modulation== from an 11-dimensional contact state.
- Structured contact prediction generates, for three future latent steps, typed events plus displacement and normal-force changes, masks, centre of pressure, slip and wrenches under a ==heteroscedastic uncertainty loss==.
- **Anticipatory Contact Coupling** fuses wrench, visual summary, prior action chunk and previously imagined contact package into a gate biasing attention on haptic keys; **Haptic-Imagination Distillation** matches teacher contact futures, event distributions and action velocities, weighted by event saliency and teacher uncertainty.

## Results

- The tactile-free student places **41 of 50** objects (**95%** waffles, **85%** cartons, **50%** eggs), beating the teacher's **30**, and beating both baselines at **10** and **9** placements.
- Clamping generated contact frames to zero drops placement from **21/30** to **4/30**, a **56.7-point** reduction; offline endpoint error rises from **22.31 mm** to **28.31 mm**, matching a stationary policy's **28.2 mm**.
- The student records zero reference exceedances above the **24.8 N** envelope and no waffle over- or under-grasps, with lower mean pinch force than the teacher on waffles (**14.3 N** versus **16.7 N**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting structured contact quantities, events, force changes and geometry, gives a policy usable supervision that pixel-level visual or tactile future prediction leaves implicit.
> - A distilled student can exceed its privileged teacher when distillation supplies expert action targets on the student's own rollout states and filters teacher targets by predicted uncertainty.
> - Offline endpoint error on demonstration states can reverse against closed-loop success, so covariate shift makes demonstration-state metrics an unreliable proxy for deployed performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Fragile-object pick-and-place asks a robot to judge what a camera may not reveal. The robot must know whether a fragile object is held securely, is slipping, or is being squeezed too hard. HapticWAM teaches a model to predict contact mechanics alongside its actions. It then gives a student that imagined-contact output even after the student's fingertip-observation inputs are removed. "Without fingertip observations" describes the student policy's inputs. It does not describe a robot without tactile sensors: the sensors remained mounted, and the shared execution controller retained tactile safeguards.

One of three recorded student trials, one per task, played at real-time speed. In this Waffles trial (seed 101), the student picks waffles and places them into a box, with an operator verdict of successful placement.

### 2. From measured touch to imagined contact

Each optical-tactile fingertip supplies gel appearance and mechanical readings, and the model encodes them as distinct inputs. The model predicts a structured contact package at three future steps, beyond future gel pixels. The package includes one of five event types: none, onset, hold, slip, or release. It also includes changes in displacement and normal-force fields, masks, centers of pressure, slip scores, fingertip wrenches, and an arm-side wrench. The model generates this package jointly with actions. Targets are derived from recorded fields without manual event annotation.

During deployment, Anticipatory Contact Coupling (ACC) uses current signals and the package imagined at the previous replan to bias attention on haptic keys. The tactile teacher blends this anticipatory gate with a reactive gate based on observed tactile change, and the student uses the anticipatory branch alone. ACC is internal attention modulation. It is separate from the scripted contact-probability veto that controls gripper closure.

Haptic-Imagination Distillation is the training bridge. The student inherits teacher weights and removes gel and mechanics observation frames, but retains its contact-generation head. It matches teacher-generated contact futures, event distributions, and action predictions, while retaining reduced-weight ground-truth supervision. This matching is offline, using recorded rollouts.

### 3. What the robot comparisons show

The robot is a UR3 with a Robotiq gripper and two DM-Tac W2L optical-tactile fingertips. It performs waffle-to-box, carton-to-box, and egg-to-tray pick-and-place, with 20 waffle, 20 carton, and 10 egg starts per model. Success is an operator-reviewed placement at the target, and force is measured separately. Starting positions are shared, but training data and execution details are not fully matched. The operator was not blinded to the running policy.

The student's 77% is the equal-weight mean of its three per-task placement rates, and its pooled rate is 41/50 (82%). The teacher scores a 57% per-task mean and 30/50 (60%) pooled. On waffles, the student placed 19 of 20 objects, versus 14 of 20 for its tactile-input teacher. The student had the highest observed placement rate on each task. However, its gap to the teacher was not statistically separable at this sample size. Egg handling remained difficult at 5/10, with a descriptive 95% Wilson interval of 23.7–76.3%. These results compare complete configurations and do not isolate the effect of contact distillation from training-data and execution differences.

The strongest mechanism check clamps the student's generated contact frames to zero while keeping the checkpoint and observation channels unchanged. Placements fell from 21/30 with the intact student to 4/30, a 56.7-percentage-point difference. This supports dependence on the contact-generation pathway in the evaluated system, without identifying that pathway as the only cause. The clamp changes a jointly generated representation, and contact-dependent selection and closing rules can mediate execution. The intact trials also preceded the intervention trials.

Across 30 starts, the intact student places 21 objects (70%), versus 4 (13.3%) with zeroed contact frames. Placement falls on all three task rows, with the largest drop on waffles. Intact outcomes are the first ten starts per task from the main comparison; the intervention used 30 additional trials with the same starts.

### 4. Contact remains a prediction

HapticWAM carries a predicted contact representation into action after fingertip readings are removed from the policy's input. In this setup, the robot intervention supports dependence on that generated-contact pathway. The main comparison shows the highest observed placement rates on the three tested tasks. The paper leaves one question open: whether contact-future supervision improves on action-only distillation remains to be isolated.
