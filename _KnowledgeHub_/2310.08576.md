# Learning to Act from Actionless Videos through Dense Correspondences

> [!summary] Summary
> The Actions from Video Dense Correspondences (AVDC) framework enables robots to learn and execute tasks solely from actionless video demonstrations by synthesizing future video plans using a conditional diffusion model and inferring 3D robot actions from the dense optical flow between synthesized frames and initial depth information. This approach achieves 43.1% average success across 11 Meta-World manipulation tasks and demonstrates successful zero-shot transfer from human videos to robot control, all while significantly reducing computational training costs.

## Problem

- Robot learning methods traditionally rely on expensive, robot-specific action labels, even for video-based approaches, hindering scalability and data efficiency.
- Current robot policies struggle with generalizability across diverse tasks, robot embodiments, and environments due to varying state and action representations.
- State-of-the-art video generation models for robot learning demand immense computational resources, creating a high barrier to entry for research and adoption.

## Method

- A three-stage pipeline (video generation, flow prediction, action regression) is introduced to infer robot actions directly from synthesized future video plans without explicit action labels.
- An efficient ==conditional diffusion model==, utilizing ==factorized spatial-temporal convolutions==, generates high-fidelity future video frames from initial RGBD observations and text prompts on commodity hardware.
- 3D rigid body transformations for objects or the robot are regressed by lifting ==2D optical flow== from synthesized videos and initial depth, incorporating a replanning strategy for closed-loop control.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **43.1%** across 11 Meta-World manipulation tasks, outperforming behavioral cloning baselines (**15.4-16.2%**) and prior methods like UniPi (**6.1%**).
- Demonstrated successful zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer, with a model trained on human videos achieving **90%** success controlling a simulated robot in a pushing task.
- Successfully deployed on a real-world Franka Emika Panda robot for tabletop manipulation, generalizing from a large dataset to a new setup with minimal fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Inferring 3D actions from 2D optical flow between synthesized video frames and initial depth allows for policy learning and execution without requiring explicit action labels.
> - Factorized spatial-temporal convolutions in diffusion models enable training high-fidelity video generation models on 4 GPUs in a single day, dramatically improving computational accessibility.
> - A replanning strategy that re-synthesizes video plans based on current observations is critical for achieving robust, closed-loop control and mitigating accumulated errors in long-horizon tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "LEARNING TO ACT FROM ACTIONLESS VIDEOS THROUGH DENSE CORRESPONDENCES" addresses a crucial challenge in the field of robot learning: achieving generalizable policy execution across diverse tasks, robots, and environments with minimal and readily available data. It primarily contributes to the growing area of **robot learning from videos** and **learning from observation (LfO)**.

Traditionally, robot learning often relies on **imitation learning** (learning from demonstration), which requires pairs of expert state observations and corresponding explicit action labels. Collecting such action labels is notoriously expensive, time-consuming, and robot-specific, limiting the scalability and generalizability of learned policies.

An alternative approach, gaining significant traction, is to leverage **videos as a task-agnostic representation** for both states and actions. Prior work (e.g., Finn & Levine, 2017; Kurutach et al., 2018) explored predicting future image sequences as a form of "visual foresight." More recently, methods like UniPi (Du et al., 2023), a direct predecessor to this work, formulated policy prediction as a text-conditioned video generation problem. These approaches aim to synthesize a video depicting how a task should be executed. However, a significant bottleneck remained: inferring the concrete robot actions from these generated videos. Past solutions either learned **action-specific video prediction models** or **task-specific inverse-dynamics models**, both of which still necessitate extensive action labels for training.

This paper positions itself directly in this landscape by introducing a method, **Actions from Video Dense Correspondences (AVDC)**, that bypasses the need for *any explicit action labels* during the action inference stage. It distinguishes itself from prior video-based methods by proposing a novel mechanism to *directly regress actions from synthesized videos* using **dense correspondences** (optical flow). This innovation moves robot learning closer to **learning from observation**, where policies are learned solely from expert state sequences (videos) without explicit action information. While other LfO methods exist (e.g., Torabi et al., 2019b; Karnan et al., 2022), many still require interacting with the environment or inferring rewards, which AVDC avoids by operating purely on visual data.

Furthermore, the work also contributes to the practical aspect of robot learning by addressing the **high computational costs** associated with training advanced video generation models. Many state-of-the-art models, like UniPi, require massive computational resources (e.g., over 256 TPU pods). AVDC offers an **efficient, open-source framework** that enables training high-fidelity video policy models on significantly fewer resources (4 GPUs within a single day). This practical contribution is vital for democratizing access to cutting-edge robot learning techniques, making them more accessible for academic research and smaller industrial applications.

The integration of **dense correspondences** as a core mechanism for action inference is another key aspect. Dense correspondences have been effectively used in other robotics contexts for inferring object poses and manipulator states (e.g., Florence et al., 2018; Simeonov et al., 2022). This paper innovatively applies this concept to interpret the implicit actions within *synthesized* video plans, converting pixel-level movements into executable 3D rigid transformations for objects or the robot itself.

In summary, this work pushes the boundaries of robot learning from videos by eliminating the need for action labels, significantly reducing computational overhead, and enhancing generalizability across diverse tasks and embodiments. It represents a crucial step towards building truly versatile and data-efficient robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to advance the field of robot learning by enabling the construction of robust and generalizable robot policies from **minimal, actionless video demonstrations**. The authors identify and are motivated to address several critical limitations in current robot learning paradigms:

1. **Eliminate Reliance on Expensive Action Annotations:** A primary motivation is to overcome the significant hurdle of collecting task-specific action labels. Existing video-based policy learning methods, while leveraging images as a task-agnostic representation, still require action labels either to train inverse dynamics models (to translate visual changes into actions) or to learn action-specific video predictors. This reliance makes data collection costly, time-consuming, and hinders scalability and broad applicability across different robots and tasks. The authors explicitly state their aim to infer actions directly from video *without requiring any action labels*.

2. **Enhance Generalizability Across Diverse Robotic Tasks and Environments:** The diversity of robotic tasks (e.g., folding cloth vs. picking and placing), varying state representations, and differing action spaces across robots (e.g., different grippers, motor actuations) present a major challenge. The research aims to construct a single policy capable of handling this diversity. By using images as a "task-agnostic" representation for both state and action, and text for general goal specification, they seek a universal approach to robot learning.

3. **Improve Computational Efficiency and Accessibility:** Current state-of-the-art video generation models, particularly those capable of high-fidelity video synthesis for robotics (e.g., UniPi), demand immense computational resources, often requiring large-scale TPU pods. This high barrier to entry limits research and adoption. A key objective is to develop an **efficient and open-source framework** for video modeling that can be trained on commodity hardware (e.g., 4 GPUs in a single day), thereby making advanced video-based policy learning more accessible to a wider research community and industry.

4. **Leverage the Abundance of Actionless Video Data:** Given that a vast amount of video data (e.g., online videos, human demonstrations) is readily available but lacks explicit action labels, the authors are motivated to tap into this rich resource. By developing a method that can "learn to act from actionless videos," they unlock a massive potential data source for robot training.

5. **Robustness through Closed-Loop Control:** Acknowledging the inherent challenges and potential for accumulated errors in open-loop planning from synthesized videos, the authors are motivated to incorporate a **replanning strategy**. This ensures that the policy can adapt to discrepancies between planned and observed outcomes, leading to more robust task execution.

In essence, the core motivation is to simplify the data requirements for advanced robot learning, reduce computational barriers, and build more flexible and generalizable robotic systems by intelligently bridging the gap between high-level visual plans (synthesized videos) and low-level robot actions, all without the need for expensive, human-annotated action labels.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, **Actions from Video Dense Correspondences (AVDC)**, is a three-stage pipeline designed to learn robot policies from actionless videos. It takes an initial RGBD observation of the environment and a textual task description as input and outputs a sequence of robot commands.

**1. Text-Conditioned Video Generation:**
- **Purpose:** To synthesize a short video sequence that implicitly depicts the desired task execution.
- **Model:** A conditional diffusion model. This model learns to generate a sequence of future frames (img$_{1:T}$) given an initial frame (img$_0$) and a textual task description (txt).
- **Architecture:** A modified U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015; Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) adapted for video generation.
 - **Input Conditioning:** The initial frame (img$_0$) is concatenated channel-wise to all future frames (img$_{1:T}$) to maintain consistency. The text description (txt) is encoded using a pre-trained CLIP-Text encoder (Radford et al., 2021), and its embedding is pooled by a Perceiver (Jaegle et al., 2021) and integrated into the U-Net's time embeddings.
 - **Efficiency Enhancement:** A crucial architectural optimization is the use of **factorized spatial-temporal convolutions** within each ResNet block (He et al., 2016; Ho et al., 2022). This involves applying 2D spatial convolutions independently across time steps, followed by 1D temporal convolutions independently across spatial locations. This factorized approach significantly reduces computational complexity compared to conventional 3D convolutions, enabling training on 4 GPUs within a single day, a notable improvement over prior resource-intensive methods.
- **Training Strategy:** For manipulation tasks (Meta-World), an "adaptable frame sampling" technique is used, sampling the initial frame, the final frame, and T-2 intermediate frames to improve learning efficiency for goal-conditioned tasks. For navigation tasks (iTHOR), consecutive future frames are sampled, with the last frame duplicated if the video segment is shorter than T frames, signaling task completion.

**2. Flow Prediction:**
- **Purpose:** To estimate the pixel-level dense correspondences (optical flow) between consecutive frames of the *synthesized video*. This step is critical for translating visual movement into measurable changes.
- **Model:** An off-the-shelf, pre-trained optical flow prediction model, GMFlow (Xu et al., 2022), which is a transformer-based architecture.
- **Rationale for two-stage approach:** The authors found empirically that directly training diffusion models to predict flow was challenging due to the sparse and less spatially/temporally smooth nature of flow fields. A two-stage approach (video generation, then flow prediction) proved more effective.

**3. Action Regression from Flows and Depths:**
- **Core Insight:** Many robotic actions, especially manipulation and navigation, can be formulated as **rigid 3D transformations (SE(3))** of objects or the robot's viewpoint. Optical flow provides 2D pixel-level movements, and with initial depth information, these can be lifted to 3D transformations.
- **Procedure:**
 1. **Object-centric motion (for manipulation tasks):**
 - An object mask is obtained (either ground-truth or via external segmentation models like Language Segment-Anything).
 - The initial 3D points of the object are computed from the initial RGBD frame and camera intrinsics.
 - Optical flow provides the 2D projection of these points in subsequent synthesized frames.
 - An optimization problem is solved to find the optimal rigid body transformation (rotation and translation) that minimizes the L2 loss between the projected 3D points and the tracked 2D points over time.
 - **Action Inference:** Given these 3D object transformations, off-the-shelf robotics primitives are used. For graspable objects, a grasp point is sampled, and target end-effector poses are computed based on the object's inferred trajectory. For non-graspable objects, push actions are inferred. Subgoals are iteratively computed and reached using a position controller. This avoids learning inverse dynamics from scratch.
 2. **Robot (camera) motion (for navigation tasks):**
 - The duality between object motion and camera motion is leveraged. The same optimization algorithm is applied, but instead of tracking an object, the optical flow of the entire scene is used (or regions of significant flow, excluding static background).
 - The computed transformation `T_t` is then interpreted as camera motion `C_t = (T_t)^-1`.
 - **Action Inference:** An imaginary point 1 meter in front of the robot is tracked. Based on its 3D translation inferred from `C_t`, discrete actions like `MoveForward`, `RotateLeft`, `RotateRight`, or `Done` are selected.
- **Depth Requirement:** Crucially, only the **initial frame's depth map** is required. Subsequent depth maps are not needed, as rigid body transformations can be inferred from 2D flow and initial 3D information. This allows training on RGB-only videos and only requiring an RGB-D camera at execution.
- **Replanning Strategy (Closed-Loop Control):** To combat accumulated errors in open-loop execution, AVDC employs a replanning mechanism. If the robot's movement is minimal over a certain period (e.g., < 1mm over 15 steps) and the task isn't fulfilled, the entire video generation and action prediction pipeline is re-run from the current observation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents a compelling set of findings that highlight the efficacy and practical advantages of the AVDC framework across diverse robotic tasks and environments.

**1. Superior Performance in Simulated Manipulation (Meta-World):**
- AVDC (Full), the complete proposed method, significantly outperformed all baselines. It achieved an average success rate of **43.1%** across 11 complex manipulation tasks, whereas behavioral cloning baselines (BC-Scratch, BC-R3M) achieved only 16.2% and 15.4% respectively, and UniPi (which requires an inverse dynamics model trained with action labels) achieved 6.1%. This demonstrates AVDC's ability to learn complex manipulation skills without relying on explicit action labels.
- **Ablation Studies on AVDC Variants:**
 - **AVDC (Flow)**, a variant that directly attempts to predict optical flow via diffusion, performed poorly (13.7%), validating the two-stage approach of first generating video and then inferring flow. This suggests that diffusion models are better suited for generating coherent visual sequences than potentially sparse and discontinuous flow fields.
 - **AVDC (No Replan)**, the open-loop version of AVDC, achieved only 19.6% success. The substantial performance jump to 43.1% for AVDC (Full) underscores the critical importance and effectiveness of the proposed **replanning strategy** for mitigating accumulated errors and ensuring robust execution in long-horizon tasks (Figure 6).
- **Qualitative Results (Figure 5):** The generated videos and inferred actions qualitatively matched desired task executions, such as picking up a nut and placing it onto a peg, demonstrating the pipeline's interpretability.
- **Failure Modes:** Primary failures were attributed to optical flow tracking errors (especially for small objects or low resolution) and the limitations of off-the-shelf flow models not trained on in-domain data.

**2. Strong Performance in Simulated Navigation (iTHOR):**
- AVDC achieved a respectable **31.3%** average success rate in object navigation tasks across various room types. In stark contrast, both BC baselines (BC-Scratch, BC-R3M) essentially failed, with success rates of 2.1% and 0.4% respectively. This highlights AVDC's capability in learning sequential decision-making for navigation tasks from videos.
- **Qualitative Results (Figure 7):** The framework successfully synthesized navigation videos and accurately inferred discrete actions (MoveForward, RotateLeft/Right, Done) based on scene flow, leading the agent to target objects.

**3. Demonstrating Cross-Embodiment Learning:**
- On the Visual Pusher task, AVDC showed impressive **zero-shot transfer capabilities**. A video diffusion model trained solely on actionless human pushing videos (198 videos) could directly control a simulated robot to perform pushing tasks with a **90% success rate** (Figure 8), without any fine-tuning. This is a significant finding, demonstrating the potential to leverage vast online human video datasets for robot learning.

**4. Real-World Application with Franka Emika Panda Arm:**
- AVDC was successfully deployed on a real-world Franka Emika Panda robot for tabletop manipulation.
- The video generation model, pre-trained on the large Bridge dataset (33,078 robot demonstrations from a different robot model) and fine-tuned with only 20 human demonstrations collected with the target setup, could reliably infer actions for tasks like picking and placing objects (Figure 9, 10).
- **Zero-shot generalization** of the Bridge-trained model to complex real-world kitchen scenarios was observed for video generation, even before fine-tuning.
- **Real-World Failure Modes:** The primary causes of failure in real-world experiments were incorrect plans generated by the video diffusion model (e.g., picking the wrong object or placing it incorrectly) or discontinuities in the generated video (object disappearing), rather than issues with action inference from correct flow.

**5. Computational Efficiency:**
- A major practical contribution: The architectural optimizations (factorized spatial-temporal convolutions) enabled training of high-fidelity video generation models on just **4 GPUs within a single day** for Meta-World and iTHOR. For the larger Bridge dataset, high-quality results were achieved with two days of training. This significantly lowers the computational barrier for robot learning from videos.
- **Inference Efficiency:** While video generation is the most time-consuming step (approx. 10.57 seconds per video), it can be further accelerated (e.g., 10x speedup with only 5.6% drop in Meta-World performance) using techniques like Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM).

**6. Additional Ablation Studies:**
- **First-Frame Conditioning:** The proposed RGB-channel-wise concatenation of the initial frame consistently outperformed frame-wise concatenation in terms of generated video quality (lower MSE).
- **Text Encoder:** The CLIP-Text encoder performed comparably to a larger T5-base encoder, suggesting that CLIP's smaller model is sufficient for goal conditioning in this context.
- **Segmentation Models:** While using external segmentation models (Language Segment-Anything) to extract object masks for action regression led to a performance drop (34.5% vs. 43.1% with ground-truth masks in Meta-World) due to segmentation errors, it still achieved reasonable success, indicating viability for end-to-end systems.

These findings collectively demonstrate that AVDC offers a compelling and practical solution to key challenges in robot learning, particularly regarding data efficiency, generalizability, and computational accessibility.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "LEARNING TO ACT FROM ACTIONLESS VIDEOS THROUGH DENSE CORRESPONDENCES" carries significant implications and potential impact across the fields of robotics, artificial intelligence, and machine learning.

**1. Breaking the Action Label Dependency Barrier:** This is arguably the most impactful contribution. By demonstrating effective robot policy learning without *any explicit action labels*, AVDC addresses a fundamental bottleneck in robot learning. Collecting action annotations for real robots is incredibly expensive, time-consuming, and robot-specific. Eliminating this requirement unlocks a vast, virtually limitless reservoir of actionless video data (e.g., YouTube videos, human demonstrations, passively recorded robot operation) for training, drastically reducing the cost and effort associated with data acquisition. This opens the door for robots to learn from observational data at an unprecedented scale.

**2. Enhanced Generalizability and Transfer Learning:**
- **Task Generalization:** The approach is demonstrated across diverse tasks, from precise table-top manipulation (pick-and-place, assembly) to abstract navigation, suggesting a path toward more versatile robots capable of handling a broader range of real-world challenges.
- **Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The successful zero-shot transfer from human demonstration videos to robot execution is highly significant. This capability allows robots to learn skills by observing humans, even when their physical embodiments (e.g., human hand vs. robot gripper) are different, bridging a major gap in imitation learning.
- **Environment Generalization:** Efficacy in both simulated and real-world environments, coupled with the ability to generalize from a large dataset like Bridge to a different real-world setup with minimal fine-tuning, indicates a strong potential for robust deployment.

**3. Democratizing Advanced Robot Learning:** The development of an open-source framework that enables training high-fidelity video policy models on just **four GPUs within a single day** is a major practical contribution. This drastically lowers the computational barrier that has historically restricted advanced robot learning techniques to well-funded research institutions with access to massive computational clusters (e.g., 256 TPU pods for UniPi). This accessibility will accelerate research and development in academia and make advanced robotic capabilities more attainable for smaller labs, startups, and educational institutions.

**4. Robustness through Vision-Based Closed-Loop Control:** The proposed replanning strategy, which re-synthesizes video plans based on current observations, introduces a crucial layer of robustness. This closed-loop control mechanism allows the robot to correct for accumulated errors, unexpected environmental changes, or inaccuracies in initial planning, making the learned policies more reliable and deployable in dynamic real-world scenarios.

**5. Advancing the Capabilities of Embodied AI:** By allowing robots to learn directly from visual demonstrations of "what to do" rather than "how to do it" (i.e., explicit actions), AVDC contributes to the development of more intuitively programmable and adaptable embodied AI agents. It leverages strong visual foresight capabilities of diffusion models combined with geometric understanding from dense correspondences, moving towards a more holistic, perception-driven form of intelligence in robots.

**6. Fueling Future Research Directions:** The work highlights the power of combining modern generative models (diffusion models) with classical computer vision techniques (optical flow, 3D geometry). It opens several exciting avenues for future research, including:
- Developing more robust, in-domain optical flow estimation, especially for small objects or challenging lighting.
- Integrating sophisticated grasp and contact point prediction modules to enhance real-world manipulation capabilities.
- Exploring methods to learn or infer force information from purely visual cues or minimal interaction.
- Scaling to even larger, more diverse datasets of unstructured videos.

While acknowledging its limitations (e.g., occlusion challenges, sensitivity to rapid lighting changes, robust grasp inference), the significance of AVDC lies in its elegant solution to a critical problem in robot learning, offering a highly generalizable, data-efficient, and computationally accessible pathway for future robotic systems.
