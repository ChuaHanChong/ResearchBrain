# TaRL: Learning General and Physical Rewards from Tactile Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> TaRL learns reinforcement-learning rewards from tactile demonstrations instead of video. A causal transformer reads sequences of 3D fingertip deformation maps and regresses task progress, trained on successful, rewound and failed demonstrations. Used as a shaping reward, it raises nut threading success from 34% to 56% in simulation and real cube pickup from 37% to 97%, and generalizes to unseen object positions.

## Problem

- Sparse task rewards make reinforcement learning on contact-rich manipulation sample-inefficient, while hand-specified dense rewards are hard to design and can induce unintended behavior.
- Visual reward learning grounds rewards in scene appearance, so it misses occluded contact variables such as grasp firmness, applied force direction and when to switch movement patterns.
- Visual progress rewards overfit to scene layout: trained at one object position they collapse at unseen positions, and plateau once the end-effector reaches the object.

## Method

- ==Tactile Reward Learning== replaces RGB video with tactile video in a ==ReWiND-style trajectory-conditioned progress regressor==, needing no ground-truth states, visual observations, goal images or action labels.
- Architecture: a shared ==3-layer CNN== encodes each finger's 3D deformation map into tokens, a ==causal transformer== over 16 subsampled timesteps, and a ==sigmoid MLP head== outputting per-step progress.
- Per-sequence normalization by maximum absolute deformation removes absolute force magnitudes; training loss regresses progress on successful, rewound-successful and failed tactile videos, then rewards plug into ==PPO== or ==IQL==.

## Results

- Trained on 200 successful and 800 failed demonstrations at one object position, TaRL shaping lifts nut threading success from **34%** to **56%** across four Isaac Lab tasks.
- Visual ReWiND's success-versus-failure reward gap drops from **0.5** to **0.1** at unseen positions even with multi-position training, while TaRL's tactile features stay in-distribution under t-SNE.
- Real-world offline IQL with 40+40 teleoperated demonstrations raises cube pickup from **37%** to **97%**, peg pickup by **45 points** and peg insertion by **10 points**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Touch is a complementary reward modality: vision guides reaching and localization, tactile progress rewards supply signal through contact phases where visual rewards flatten out.
> - Rewards built on local contact signals are naturally invariant to scene layout, so a single-position reward model can generalize across object positions and similar object instances.
> - Useful contact rewards did not emerge from tactile-conditioned critics or handcrafted grasp detectors; they had to be learned from success and failure demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In nut threading, a robot must reach and grasp a nut, then make sustained helical contact. Sparse success/failure rewards give little guidance while a robot learns to grasp firmly, make precise contact, and apply force in the right direction. Hand-designed dense rewards are laborious and can encourage unintended behavior. TaRL instead learns that guidance from touch. A model reads tactile deformation over time and supplies a dense estimate of task progress that can be added to the task reward during reinforcement learning (RL).

In simulation, final nut-threading success rose from 34% without TaRL to 56% with it. This is one task's simulation result, not evidence of a general improvement in wall-clock time or of success on every manipulation task.

### 2. Touch becomes a learning signal

TaRL provides a shaping reward: an added, denser learning signal that supplements the task reward. It receives sequences of 3D deformation maps from sensors on the two gripper fingers. It does not need task-state labels, visual observations, or action annotations for reward-model training. A convolutional encoder represents the maps. A causal transformer, whose output at each point uses only observations up to that point, predicts task progress.

The model learns from three kinds of demonstrations:

- Successful demonstrations are labeled as progress increasing over time.
- Failed demonstrations get a zero-progress target.
- A successful sequence is also rewound by joining a forward prefix to a reversed suffix. This teaches the model to lower its progress prediction when motion undoes progress.

After offline training, the model supplies a tactile reward during downstream RL without online fine-tuning. The environment's basic reward already supports reaching, lifting, transport, or placement, but deliberately lacks terms for stable grasp and precise contact. Simulation policies use online PPO; real-world policies use offline IQL.

The formulation comes from [[2505.10911|ReWiND: *Language-Guided Rewards Teach Robot Policies without New Demonstrations* (CoRL 2025)]], which predicts task progress from visual observation sequences. TaRL adopts its trajectory-conditioned progress-regression formulation and model architecture. It replaces RGB video with tactile deformation maps to test the modality rather than propose a new reward-model architecture.

### 3. Where touch changes the comparison

In the nut-threading comparison, the simulated policy receives basic task rewards but no engineered stable-grasp or precise-contact terms, and visual rewards are off. Success rate means the percentage of evaluated policies/attempts judged to complete the task, averaged over three seeds. "Learning efficiency" in this paper refers to success-rate progress over training iterations, not elapsed time or lower added computation.

Against ReWiND, the paper analyzes a successful gear-assembly example. There, the visual reward rises and plateaus when the end-effector reaches the target object. The tactile reward continues to vary across contact phases and grows until task success.

At unseen object positions, ReWiND's progress signal becomes less discriminative even when ReWiND is trained on demonstrations from multiple positions. The reported reward gap between successful and failed trajectories falls from 0.5 to 0.1 on unseen configurations. This measures reward-model quality, not downstream policy success. The authors explain it as tactile observations encoding local interaction rather than global scene layout.

TaRL still improves RL when the actor and critic receive tactile observations. That comparison does not establish that demonstration-trained reward learning is the only possible cause of improvement. The authors also combine tactile and visual rewards on peg insertion, gear assembly, and nut threading. They report faster downstream RL training, with a substantial success increase for peg insertion.

The real-world experiment uses offline IQL. Policies are evaluated at object positions different from those used for TaRL's demonstrations. For cube pickup, final success rose from 37% to 97%.

Real-world cube pickup, peg pickup, and peg insertion: success rate against training iteration without (blue) and with (orange) tactile reward. Point labels show successes out of 30 evaluations.

### 4. Scope

TaRL shows that tactile sequences can supply a contact-level progress signal for RL, without hand-coding every contact cue. The authors train a reward model per task. The box-placement-to-can-placement result is evidence of a specific zero-shot transfer, not a general-purpose reward model for arbitrary new tasks.
