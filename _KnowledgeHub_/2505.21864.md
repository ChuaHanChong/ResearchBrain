# DexUMI: Using Human Hand as the Universal Manipulation Interface for Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The DexUMI framework introduces a method to teach dexterous manipulation skills to robots by using the human hand as a universal interface, combining a robot-specific wearable exoskeleton with a visual adaptation pipeline. This approach enabled successful imitation learning for complex tasks with an 86% average success rate and boosted data collection efficiency by 3.2 times compared to teleoperation.

## Problem

- A significant embodiment gap exists between human and robot hands, hindering direct skill transfer for dexterous manipulation.
- Traditional teleoperation methods are inefficient, lack direct haptic feedback, and struggle with retargeting human motions to diverse robot kinematics.
- Existing methods for learning from human videos often require additional real-robot data or struggle with sim-to-real transfer and inferring precise robot actions.

## Method

- Developed ==robot-specific wearable hand exoskeletons== through an optimization framework, ensuring kinematically feasible human demonstrations that provide natural haptic feedback.
- Implemented a ==software adaptation pipeline== to transform human demonstration videos into robot-centric visual observations, ==inpainting the human hand== with a corresponding robot hand image.
- Integrated various sensors (==joint encoders==, wrist tracker, camera, ==tactile sensors==) into the exoskeleton to capture comprehensive, robot-compatible data.

## Results

- Achieved an average task success rate of **86%** across four challenging, contact-rich manipulation tasks on both underactuated (Inspire Hand) and fully-actuated (XHand) robot hands, with a **3.2x** increase in data collection efficiency over teleoperation.
- Policies trained using relative finger actions consistently showed higher performance and robustness across all evaluated tasks compared to absolute actions.
- The software visual adaptation pipeline was crucial for policy success, as using raw human images or simple masking significantly lowered performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Minimizing the action gap with robot-specific exoskeletons allows human demonstrations to be directly interpretable as robot actions without complex retargeting, while bridging the visual observation gap through software adaptation is equally essential since raw human hand images are insufficient.
> - Training policies with relative finger trajectories leads to more robust and reactive behavior for dexterous tasks, outperforming absolute position control.
> - The effectiveness of tactile sensing is task-dependent and can be significantly improved when combined with reactive action control, especially in tasks with clear force signals and visual occlusion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

DexUMI addresses a fundamental and long-standing challenge in robotics: **transferring dexterous manipulation skills from humans to diverse robotic hands**. Human hands are incredibly versatile, capable of performing a vast array of complex, contact-rich tasks. Replicating this dexterity in robots is a holy grail for robotics, but it is severely hampered by the **embodiment gap**. This gap refers to the significant differences between human and robot hands in kinematics, morphology, sensing capabilities (e.g., tactile), and visual appearance.

The broader research landscape for dexterous manipulation heavily relies on two main paradigms for data collection and skill transfer:

- **Teleoperation:** This involves a human operator controlling a robot remotely, often using motion capture gloves, VR devices, or camera-based tracking. While flexible, teleoperation suffers from issues like spatial observation mismatch, lack of direct haptic feedback, and the difficulty of "retargeting" human hand motions to robots with fundamentally different kinematic structures, especially for highly dexterous, multi-fingered hands. Teleoperation also requires the physical robot to be present during data collection, limiting its flexibility. DexUMI directly tackles these limitations by allowing intuitive, direct human interaction with objects (providing haptic feedback) and designing an exoskeleton that intrinsically constrains human motion to be robot-feasible, thus bypassing complex retargeting challenges and the need for the robot during initial data collection.
- **Learning from Human Hand Videos:** This approach aims to extract skills directly from videos of humans performing tasks. While promising for affordance learning or pose estimation, many such methods still require additional real-world robot data or rely on privileged information (like object pose in simulation) for deployment, struggling with sim-to-real transfer or requiring significant real-robot interaction for fine-tuning. DexUMI aims to provide high-fidelity robot-centric visual data directly, circumventing the challenges of inferring robot actions from raw human videos.
- **Wearable Devices for Data Collection:** This category includes portable hand-held grippers for simpler tasks or specialized motion capture gloves. However, most existing wearable systems are either designed for simple parallel grippers (not multi-fingered dexterous hands) or still depend on optimization-based retargeting and additional real-world robot data (e.g., Dexcap). Some recent "hand-over-hand" systems require the human to physically lift and guide the robot hand, which is less scalable and potentially cumbersome. DexUMI distinguishes itself by developing robot-specific wearable exoskeletons that ensure kinematic compatibility *a priori* and integrate sensors to capture comprehensive robot-compatible data (joint angles, vision, tactile) without needing the physical robot during the *initial* data collection, offering a more scalable and efficient alternative.

DexUMI positions itself as a **universal manipulation interface** by directly addressing the action and observation components of the embodiment gap simultaneously. By providing both a hardware adaptation (exoskeleton) to map human actions to feasible robot actions with direct haptic feedback, and a software adaptation to bridge the visual discrepancy between human and robot hands, DexUMI offers a more seamless and efficient approach to collecting diverse, high-quality training data for dexterous robots. This moves beyond the limitations of existing methods, paving the way for more robust and scalable imitation learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind DexUMI stems from the desire to leverage the inherent dexterity of the human hand as an intuitive and efficient interface for teaching complex manipulation skills to robots. The authors pose a guiding question: **"How can we minimize the embodiment gap, so that we can use the human hand as the universal manipulation interface for diverse robot hands?"**

To answer this, DexUMI sets out with the following key objectives:

1. **Minimize the Action Gap:** Develop a hardware adaptation that allows humans to directly demonstrate manipulation skills while intrinsically ensuring that the recorded motions are kinematically feasible for a target robot hand. This hardware should provide natural haptic feedback to the human demonstrator, making the process intuitive and precise.
2. **Minimize the Observation Gap:** Create a software adaptation pipeline that transforms visual data collected during human demonstrations into a robot-centric view, effectively "inpainting" the human hand with the corresponding robot hand, thus ensuring visual consistency between training and robot deployment.
3. **Enable Efficient and Scalable Data Collection:** Design a framework that significantly improves the efficiency of data collection for dexterous manipulation compared to traditional teleoperation methods, without requiring the physical robot to be present during initial demonstrations.
4. **Support Diverse Robot Hands:** Demonstrate the framework's versatility across different types of dexterous robot hands, specifically showcasing its ability to adapt to both underactuated and fully-actuated multi-fingered systems.
5. **Facilitate Policy Learning for Complex Tasks:** Validate the effectiveness of the collected data by training imitation learning policies capable of performing precise, contact-rich, and long-horizon dexterous manipulation tasks in the real world.

The motivation is to overcome the twin challenges of data scarcity and the embodiment gap, which currently hinder the widespread adoption and capability of dexterous robots. By making data collection more intuitive and directly relevant to robot capabilities, DexUMI aims to accelerate progress in robot learning for complex manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DexUMI employs a two-pronged approach—hardware adaptation and software adaptation—to bridge the embodiment gap.

#### 3.1. Hardware Adaptation: Wearable Hand Exoskeleton

The core of DexUMI's hardware adaptation is a **wearable hand exoskeleton** designed to be robot-specific but developed using a generalizable optimization framework.

- **Exoskeleton Mechanism Design (Sec 3.1):**
 - **Goals:** The design prioritizes two main goals: (1) **Shared joint-action mapping**, meaning the exoskeleton and target robot hand must have identical joint-to-fingertip position mappings and limits for direct action transfer, and (2) **Wearability**, ensuring the exoskeleton is comfortable and allows natural human hand movements, especially addressing thumb pronation-supination.
 - **Optimization Framework:** This is formulated as a bi-level optimization problem. The design parameters (joint positions, linkage lengths) are optimized to maximize the similarity between the fingertip workspaces of the exoskeleton and the robot hand. The objective function minimizes the Euclidean distance between sampled fingertip poses from both workspaces, ensuring the exoskeleton can cover the robot's workspace and that its workspace remains within the robot's capabilities.
 - **Constraints:** Wearability constraints are applied as bounds on design parameters, allowing for modifications like moving the thumb swing joint to avoid human hand collision, while still preserving kinematic relationships for fingertip contact.
 - **Initialization:** For robot hands with detailed URDFs (e.g., XHand), the design can be initialized directly. For complex underactuated hands (e.g., Inspire-Hand with four-bar linkages), motion capture data is used to model finger kinematics, and equivalent general linkage designs are optimized to match these observed behaviors.

- **Sensor Integration (Sec 3.2):** The exoskeleton integrates various sensors to capture all necessary information for policy learning, minimizing the embodiment gap in sensory data.
 - **Joint Capture & Mapping:** Resistive position encoders (Alps encoder) are integrated at every actuated joint of the exoskeleton to precisely record joint angles. A simple regression model is trained for each joint to map exoskeleton encoder values to robot motor values, accounting for non-linearities, using paired data collected by visually overlaying robot and exoskeleton motions.
 - **Wrist Pose Tracking:** An iPhone using ARKit is rigidly mounted to track the 6-DoF wrist pose during data collection. This is used for action recording but not for robot deployment.
 - **Visual Observation:** A wide-angle camera (OAK-1) with a 150° DFoV is mounted under the wrist on both the exoskeleton and the robot hand. Critically, camera poses relative to the wrist frame are identical to maintain visual consistency.
 - **Tactile Sensing:** Tactile sensors are installed on the exoskeleton's fingertips to capture contact information. The *same type* of tactile sensors are used on the exoskeleton as on the target robot hand (e.g., electro-magnetic sensors for XHand, Force Sensitive Resistors for Inspire-Hand) to ensure consistent readings and minimize sensory mismatch.

#### 3.2. Software Adaptation: Bridging the Visual Gap (Sec 4)

A data processing pipeline transforms the human demonstration video into a robot-centric visual input, making it suitable for imitation learning.

- **V.1 Segment Human Hand and Exoskeleton:** The human hand and exoskeleton are segmented from the raw demonstration video using SAM2 (Segment Anything Model 2). A consistent initial hand gesture establishes prompt points for efficient segmentation.
- **V.2 Inpaint Environment Background:** After segmentation, the human hand and exoskeleton pixels are removed. A flow-based inpainting method, ProPainter, is used to refill the missing areas with a realistic environment background.
- **V.3 Record Corresponding Robot Hand Video:** The recorded joint actions (from the exoskeleton encoders) are replayed on the actual robot hand, and a separate video of *only* the robot hand performing these actions is recorded. SAM2 is again used to extract the robot hand pixels from this video, discarding its background. The authors note that an image generation model could eventually replace this step.
- **V.4 Compose Robot Demonstrations:** The final step merges the inpainted background video with the robot-hand-only video. An occlusion-aware compositing approach is used, leveraging the consistent under-wrist camera setup and kinematic similarity. A visible mask (intersection of exoskeleton and robot hand masks) determines which pixels of the robot hand should overwrite the inpainted background, preserving natural occlusion relationships between the hand and objects.

#### 3.3. Imitation Learning

The processed visual observations (DINO-V2 features from inpainted robot-centric images) and tactile sensor readings are fed into an imitation learning policy (a diffusion policy). This policy predicts a sequence of future actions, including 6-DoF end-effector actions and N-DoF hand actions (either absolute or relative joint trajectories), which are then executed by the robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

DexUMI was evaluated through comprehensive real-world experiments on two distinct dexterous robot hands: the **Inspire Hand** (an underactuated 6-DoF hand) and the **XHand** (a fully-actuated 12-DoF hand). Four challenging tasks were used: Cube Pick and Place, Egg Carton Opening, Tea Picking with Tool, and a multi-stage Kitchen task.

- **Overall Effectiveness:** DexUMI demonstrated high task success rates, achieving an average of **86% across four tasks**. This highlights its capability for precise, contact-rich, and long-horizon dexterous manipulation, generalizing across diverse scenarios and different robot hardware.

- **Data Collection Efficiency:** DexUMI significantly improved data collection efficiency. It achieved **3.2 times greater throughput** of successful demonstrations compared to traditional teleoperation methods, underscoring its potential for scaling real-world dexterous manipulation datasets.

- **Relative vs. Absolute Finger Actions:** Policies trained with **relative finger trajectories consistently outperformed** those with absolute finger positions across all tasks.
 - Relative actions exhibited a simpler, unimodal distribution, making them easier for the policy to learn.
 - They enable a more reactive behavior, accumulating delta actions until a key event (e.g., contact) is achieved, which makes contact events more reliable.
 - Absolute actions, relying on static mappings, were less robust to minor errors and hardware imperfections.

- **Impact of Tactile Sensing:** The utility of tactile feedback was nuanced and dependent on sensor quality and task characteristics:
 - **Noisy Tactile Sensors:** For the Inspire Hand (where manually installed FSRs were noisy) and XHand (where electromagnetic sensors could drift under high pressure), tactile feedback generally **degraded or minimally improved performance** when used with absolute actions.
 - **Benefit with Relative Actions:** Interestingly, policies using relative finger trajectories **suffered less performance drop** or could even *benefit* from tactile sensing despite noise, suggesting a synergistic effect with reactive action control.
 - **Clean Force Profiles:** Tactile feedback significantly **improved performance for tasks with clear force signals and visual occlusion**, such as "picking up salt." In this task, tactile sensors provided crucial feedback when fingers contacted the salt bowl, guiding the grasp more reliably than visual cues alone. Conversely, for tasks like "tweezer manipulation" (which involve minimal tactile readings), tactile input did not provide significant benefits.

- **Software Adaptation Importance:** The software adaptation pipeline, which generates high-fidelity robot hand images for training, was crucial. Without it, using raw images with the human exoskeleton or simple green masks resulted in significantly lower success rates, demonstrating the necessity of bridging the visual gap for effective policy learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DexUMI represents a significant advancement in the field of dexterous robot manipulation and imitation learning, with substantial potential impact:

- **Overcoming the Embodiment Gap:** By simultaneously addressing the kinematic (action) and visual (observation) components of the embodiment gap, DexUMI provides a comprehensive solution for transferring human dexterity to robots. This is a critical step towards enabling robots to perform complex tasks traditionally exclusive to human hands.
- **Scalable and Efficient Data Collection:** The framework’s ability to collect high-fidelity robot-centric data 3.2 times faster than teleoperation methods, and crucially, *without requiring the physical robot to be present during initial demonstrations*, marks a paradigm shift. This drastically reduces the time, cost, and logistical overhead associated with creating large, diverse datasets for dexterous manipulation. This efficiency is paramount for advancing data-driven robot learning.
- **Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction:** By using the human hand as a natural interface with direct haptic feedback, DexUMI makes data collection more intuitive and less fatiguing for human operators compared to abstract teleoperation interfaces. This enhances the quality and realism of demonstrations.
- **Generalizability Across Robot Hardware:** The demonstrated success on both underactuated (Inspire Hand) and fully-actuated (XHand) dexterous hands validates the framework's adaptability. This suggests DexUMI can be a "universal" interface, paving the way for easier deployment of advanced manipulation skills across a wider range of robotic platforms.
- **Robust Policy Learning:** The findings regarding relative actions and the strategic use of tactile feedback provide valuable insights for designing more robust and reactive policies for dexterous manipulation, especially in contact-rich and visually challenging scenarios.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** DexUMI lays a strong foundation for future work in several areas, including:
 - **Automated Exoskeleton Design:** The current hardware optimization requires some manual tuning; fully automated generative design could further improve scalability.
 - **Improved Sensor Integration:** Addressing the reliability issues of tactile sensors and exploring advanced vision-based tactile sensors will enhance robot perception.
 - **Synthetic Data Generation:** Eliminating the need for real robot hand replays by using image generation models could further streamline the software adaptation pipeline.
 - **Co-design of Hands and Exoskeletons:** The authors suggest a reverse design paradigm where exoskeletons inform robot hand design, which could lead to inherently more wearable and easily trainable robot hands.

In conclusion, DexUMI presents a powerful and practical solution to a significant bottleneck in robotics. By making the collection of dexterous manipulation data more accessible, efficient, and robust, it moves the field closer to realizing robots capable of performing highly skilled, human-like tasks in complex, unstructured environments.
