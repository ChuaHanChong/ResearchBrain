# Catch It! Learning to Catch in Flight with Mobile Dexterous Hands

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a two-stage reinforcement learning framework to enable a mobile dexterous robot to catch diverse objects thrown in flight. The system, trained entirely in simulation, achieved a 70% tracking success rate and 15-25% catching success rate on a real robot for various sandbag shapes without fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Enabling robots to catch objects in flight demands accurate, agile, and real-time whole-body coordination of a mobile base, a 6-DoF arm, and a 12-DoF dexterous hand within short flight durations.
- The high-dimensional action space associated with a mobile base, arm, and dexterous hand makes learning effective control policies from scratch computationally intensive and often inefficient.
- Robots need to be robust to randomly thrown objects that vary in position, velocity, shape, and size, requiring highly adaptive and generalizable control policies.

## Method

- **Catch It!** uses a ==two-stage reinforcement learning framework== that first trains mobile base and arm control for object tracking, then fine-tunes with dexterous hand control for grasping.
- Integration of a custom-built mobile dexterous manipulator consisting of an omni-mobile base, a 6-DoF arm, and a 12-DoF dexterous hand, replicated in a ==Mujoco simulation== environment.
- Application of sim2real transfer techniques including ==low-pass filtering== on real robot velocity commands, system identification for PID parameter alignment, and extensive ==domain randomization== during training.

## Results

- The proposed two-stage training framework demonstrated superior training efficiency and achieved substantially higher final success rates in simulation compared to a one-stage baseline.
- The learned policy exhibited robust generalization in simulation, maintaining an approximately **80%** catching success rate when tested on **8** unseen object shapes.
- On a real robot, the system achieved approximately **70%** tracking success and **15-25%** catching success for various sandbag shapes in a zero-shot transfer, with the low-pass filter being critical for deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing a complex, high-DoF dynamic manipulation task into sequential sub-tasks (tracking and catching) within a reinforcement learning framework substantially improves training efficiency and overall performance.
> - Precise control over the arm's roll degree of freedom is crucial for optimizing the hand's orientation, which directly impacts the success of dexterous grasping for dynamic objects.
> - Hardware-specific considerations like smoothing velocity commands via a low-pass filter are critical for bridging the sim-to-real gap, enabling zero-shot deployment of complex dynamic policies on real robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical and challenging problem at the intersection of robotic manipulation, mobile robotics, and dynamic control: enabling robots to catch objects in flight. This task, while seemingly simple for humans, presents significant challenges for autonomous systems due to the need for agile, accurate, and real-time whole-body coordination, especially with high-dimensional degrees of freedom (DoF) and diverse object characteristics.

The paper meticulously positions its contributions against existing literature, highlighting key limitations in prior work.
- **Whole-Body Control for Mobile Manipulation:** Earlier research on whole-body control for mobile manipulators largely focused on classical optimization methods (e.g., Model Predictive Control, Quadratic Programming) for static or slower, more predictable tasks like navigate-pick-and-place or door-opening. While effective for these scenarios, these methods often struggle with the complexity and real-time demands of highly dynamic tasks. Learning-based approaches, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), have emerged as promising alternatives for complex, high-DoF systems, but often do not explicitly consider the implicit relationships between actions and optimization, which can impact training efficiency. "Catch It!" improves upon this by strategically decomposing the task, a concept inspired by prior works that sought to enhance RL efficiency.

- **Catching Dynamic Objects with Manipulation:** Previous efforts in dynamic object manipulation (DOM), specifically catching, typically fall into two categories:
 - **Fixed-Base Manipulators:** Many studies have explored catching with robot arms that are stationary, often focusing on precise control and dexterous manipulation. While achieving agility, these systems are inherently limited by a restricted workspace, making them unsuitable for catching objects thrown from distant or unpredictable locations.
 - **Mobile Manipulators with Limited Dexterity:** Some works have integrated mobile platforms to expand the workspace for catching. However, these often utilize simpler grippers or suction cups, which limit their ability to interact with and stably grasp objects of diverse shapes and sizes. They lack the versatility of a dexterous hand for complex grasping.

The "Catch It!" project is unique in its comprehensive integration: it combines a **mobile base** (for extended workspace and agility), a **6-DoF arm** (for reach and precise positioning), and a **12-DoF dexterous hand** (for versatile, stable grasping of diverse objects). This combination addresses the core limitations of previous research by enabling agile, long-distance catching of a wide variety of objects. Furthermore, the use of a **two-stage reinforcement learning framework** is a methodological contribution aimed at improving the efficiency of training such a high-DoF, dynamic system, distinguishing it from purely end-to-end or classical control approaches. By tackling these combined challenges, the work represents a significant step forward in developing highly adaptive and capable robotic systems for dynamic human-robot interaction and object manipulation in unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable robotic systems to proficiently "catch objects in flight," a capability that is commonplace for humans but profoundly challenging for robots. This ambition is driven by several key motivations and aims to overcome specific technical hurdles:

**Motivation:**
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** Equipping robots with the ability to deftly catch thrown objects has the potential to revolutionize human-robot collaboration, particularly in scenarios requiring dynamic object handovers. Imagine a future where robots can seamlessly assist in manufacturing, logistics, or even domestic settings by catching tools or items passed to them from a distance.
- **Improved Operational Efficiency:** By allowing robots to perform agile, long-distance catching maneuvers, tasks that involve object transfers between spatially separated points can be completed significantly faster, leveraging the short airborne duration of the objects. This would streamline workflows and boost productivity in various industrial and service applications.

**Challenges Driving the Objectives:**
The authors identify three primary challenges that necessitate novel solutions for mobile dexterous catching:
1. **Accurate and Agile Whole-Body Control:** The task demands precise, real-time coordination of multiple robot components—the mobile base, the 6-DoF arm, and the 12-DoF dexterous hand. The entire sequence, from detection to grasp, must occur within approximately two seconds (the object's typical flight time), requiring highly responsive and coordinated movements.
2. **High-Dimensional Action Space:** The combined degrees of freedom across the mobile base, arm, and hand result in a vast action space. Optimizing a control policy for such a high-dimensional system from scratch is computationally intensive and often inefficient.
3. **Randomly Thrown and Diverse Objects:** The robot must be robust to variability in real-world scenarios. This includes objects being thrown from unpredictable positions with random velocities, as well as varying significantly in shape and size. The control policy must be highly adaptive and generalize across these diverse conditions.

**Key Objectives (Technical Contributions of "Catch It!"):**
To address these challenges, the authors set forth three core technical objectives:
1. **Develop Unified Whole-Body Control:** To train a single, integrated control policy that simultaneously governs the mobile base, robotic arm, and dexterous hand. This ensures seamless coordination and enables the agile and accurate movements required for successful object catching.
2. **Propose a Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning Framework:** To enhance training efficiency for the complex, high-DoF system. By dividing the task into a "tracking" phase (base and arm control) and a "catching" phase (hand grasping with fine-tuned base/arm), the framework aims to reduce the complexity of the learning problem and accelerate convergence.
3. **Achieve Zero-Shot Sim2Real Transfer:** To demonstrate that the policy trained entirely in simulation can be directly and successfully deployed on a real robot with onboard sensing and computation, minimizing the need for extensive real-world training, which is typically expensive and time-consuming.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Catch It!" system employs a sophisticated learning-based approach, combining a custom-built mobile dexterous manipulator with a two-stage reinforcement learning framework and advanced sim2real transfer techniques.

#### 3.1. System Setup (Hardware and Simulation)

- **Real-World System:** The physical robot consists of a **Ranger Mini V2 omni-mobile base** (for 2D planar movement), a **6-DoF XArm** (for extended reach and precise positioning), and a **12-DoF LEAP Hand** (for dexterous grasping). Onboard computation is handled by a **Thunderobot MIX Mini-PC** equipped with an i7-13620H CPU and an RTX 4060 GPU. Object tracking is performed by an overhead-mounted **RealSense D455 RGB-D camera**, which estimates 3D object positions in real-time.
- **Simulation Environment:** The entire system is meticulously replicated in **Mujoco**, a physics engine suitable for model-based control. A URDF/MJCF model ensures kinematic and physical alignment with the real robot. PID controllers are developed for each component (base, arm, hand) to mimic real-world actuation. Inverse kinematics (Levenberg-Marquardt and Quadratic Programme methods) are used for arm control.

#### 3.2. Task Decomposition and Reinforcement Learning Framework

The core innovation in methodology is a **two-stage reinforcement learning (RL) framework**, designed to address the high dimensionality and complexity of the task:
1. **Tracking Task:** In the initial phase, the primary goal is for the robot's end-effector (palm) to track and reach the flying object. During this stage, only the mobile base and the 6-DoF arm are actively controlled; the dexterous hand remains in a fixed, neutral position. This simplifies the action space and allows the base and arm to learn coordinated movements for intercepting the object's trajectory.
2. **Catching Task:** Once the object is sufficiently close to the palm, the system transitions to the catching task. Here, the 12-DoF dexterous hand is activated to grasp the object, while the base and arm policies (pre-trained in the tracking task) are fine-tuned to optimize the grasping position. This sequential learning approach is formulated as a Partially-Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) and optimized using the **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** algorithm.

#### 3.3. State and Action Space Definition

- **State Space (Observation):** Policy inputs include two consecutive 3D positions of the object and the arm's end-effector (relative to the arm's base), the base's 2D planar velocity, and the hand's joint positions. The use of consecutive object positions allows for implicit velocity estimation.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs include the 2D planar velocity of the mobile base, 12 delta joint positions for the hand, and the 3D delta position alongside the delta roll rotation for the arm's end-effector. Critically, yaw and pitch rotations of the end-effector are excluded from the action space, as they were found to destabilize training.

#### 3.4. Reward Design

A sophisticated, multi-component reward function guides the RL process in both tasks, balancing different aspects of successful catching:
- **Object Position Reward:** Encourages reducing the distance between the palm and the object.
- **Object Precision Reward:** An exponential function of distance, strongly penalizing larger distances to promote high-precision approach.
- **Object Orientation Reward:** Rewards alignment between the palm's Z-axis and the object's velocity vector, ensuring the hand is oriented correctly for impact.
- **Object Touch Reward (Tracking):** Binary reward for successful contact between palm and object.
- **Object Stability Reward (Catching):** Rewards the duration an object is held stably in the hand.
- **Control Penalty:** Penalizes large actions to encourage energy-efficient and smooth movements.
- **Constraint Penalty:** Punishes joint limit violations.

#### 3.5. Sim2Real Transfer Techniques

To bridge the simulation-to-reality gap, several techniques are applied:
- **Low-Pass Filter (LPF):** A crucial addition for the real mobile base. It smooths velocity commands, preventing jerky movements and hardware limitations (e.g., braking during large steering changes).
- **System Identification:** Used to estimate and align PID controller parameters between simulation and the real robot, ensuring similar dynamic responses.
- **Domain Randomization:** Applied extensively during training to enhance policy robustness. This includes randomizing object shapes, sizes, mass, and damping coefficients, as well as PID parameters, gravity, observation noise, action noise, and crucially, the **timing of object throws** to prepare for unpredictable human throws.

#### 3.6. Real Robot Controller Architecture

A **ROS-based multi-process control system** orchestrates the real robot. It collects proprioceptive states (from base, arm, hand) and object position data (from camera), synchronizes them at 30 Hz, and feeds them into the trained policy. The policy inference runs at 25 Hz, matching the simulation control frequency, generating real-time commands for the mobile manipulator.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed two-stage reinforcement learning framework and sim2real transfer techniques in enabling a mobile dexterous manipulator to catch diverse objects in flight.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Two-Stage vs. One-Stage Training:** The two-stage training framework (ours) significantly outperformed the one-stage baseline. It showed superior training efficiency, converging faster, and achieving substantially higher final success rates in both the tracking and catching tasks. This validates the effectiveness of task decomposition for complex, high-DoF learning problems. The one-stage baseline struggled considerably, indicating the necessity of the pre-training step.
- **Impact of Arm Roll Action:** Comparison between "Two-Stage with Arm Roll" (ours) and "Two-Stage without Arm Roll" revealed the importance of the arm's roll degree of freedom. While both achieved high tracking success rates, the ability to control the arm's roll greatly improved the catching success rate. This highlights that fine-tuning the palm's orientation is crucial for optimal grasping, especially with a dexterous hand, contributing to better object orientation reward.
- **Generalization to Unseen Objects:** The policy trained with the two-stage method (ours) exhibited robust generalization capabilities in simulation. When tested on 8 unseen object shapes (e.g., bowls, bottles, cups, breads, and irregular shapes), it maintained a high catching success rate of approximately 80% (with tracking success rates above 90%). This impressive generalization capacity underscores the policy's adaptivity to varied object geometries and trajectories, which is critical for real-world deployment.

#### 4.2. Real Robot Deployment (Sim2Real Transfer)

The policy trained in simulation was successfully deployed directly onto the physical mobile manipulator in a zero-shot manner, without any real-world fine-tuning.
- **Crucial Role of Low-Pass Filter (LPF):** The results demonstrated that the Low-Pass Filter was indispensable for successful real-world deployment. Without the LPF applied to velocity commands, the tracking and catching success rates were drastically low (e.g., 5-15% for tracking, 0-5% for catching). With the LPF, the system achieved a high tracking success rate of approximately 70% across various sandbag shapes (cube, sphere, cylinder, and an irregular combination). This confirms the effectiveness of the LPF in smoothing robot movements and bridging the sim2real gap related to hardware limitations.
- **Real-World Catching Performance:** While the real-world tracking success rate was high, the catching success rate was lower, ranging from 15% to 25% across different sandbag shapes. Despite this, successfully catching objects in flight with a mobile dexterous manipulator in a zero-shot manner is a significant achievement, especially given the system's complexity and dynamic nature.
- **Identified Limitations and Future Improvements in Real World:**
 - **Object Elasticity:** A primary reason for the lower catching success rate compared to simulation was the elasticity of the real-world sandbags. These objects tended to rebound off the robot's palm, especially at high speeds, a dynamic not fully captured or penalized in the simulation.
 - **Object Tracking Errors:** The overhead RGB-D camera, while effective, introduced errors in object position tracking when the object moved rapidly or was occluded by the robot's hand. The authors suggest that integrating a more robust global localization system (e.g., VICON) could significantly improve tracking accuracy and, consequently, catching performance.

In summary, the results validate the overall approach: the two-stage RL framework efficiently learns whole-body control, and the combination of domain randomization, system identification, and an LPF enables a practical zero-shot sim2real transfer for a highly dynamic and complex robotic task.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Catch It!" research represents a notable advance in the field of robotics, particularly in dynamic manipulation and human-robot interaction. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

#### 5.1. Advancing Robotic Capabilities

- **Integration of Complex Systems:** This work successfully integrates and coordinates a mobile base, a 6-DoF arm, and a 12-DoF dexterous hand, a high-DoF system, for a challenging dynamic task. This holistic approach moves beyond traditional fixed-base manipulation or mobile robots with limited grasping capabilities, pushing the boundaries of what integrated robotic systems can achieve.
- **Agile and Real-time Dynamic Manipulation:** By enabling robots to catch thrown objects, the research demonstrates significant progress in agile, real-time dynamic object manipulation. This capability is crucial for robots operating in fast-paced, unstructured environments where objects might be in motion.

#### 5.2. Contributions to Reinforcement Learning and Sim2Real

- **Efficient High-DoF RL Training:** The proposed two-stage reinforcement learning framework offers a compelling solution for efficiently training control policies for high-dimensional robotic systems. By decomposing the complex "catching" task into sequential "tracking" and "grasping" subtasks, the authors show a clear path to overcoming the typical inefficiency of end-to-end RL for such systems. This methodological insight is valuable for the broader RL community.
- **Robust Sim2Real Transfer:** The successful zero-shot sim2real transfer of a complex whole-body control policy to a real robot is a major achievement. The identification and mitigation of critical sim2real gaps (e.g., hardware-specific limitations like the mobile base's steering via the Low-Pass Filter, and the comprehensive use of domain randomization and system identification) provide valuable lessons and techniques for future robotic deployments from simulation.

#### 5.3. Practical Applications and Future Directions

- **Revolutionizing Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability to catch objects dynamically opens up new paradigms for human-robot collaboration. Robots could seamlessly receive tools, components, or packages, making dynamic handovers more efficient and natural in manufacturing, logistics, or even domestic settings. This could lead to smoother, more intuitive, and safer human-robot workspaces.
- **Enhanced Operational Efficiency:** In industrial or warehouse environments, enabling robots to catch objects can significantly improve throughput and efficiency by allowing object transfer over distances without the need for the robot to navigate to pick up an item, thus completing tasks within the short airborne duration.
- **Foundation for More Complex Dynamic Tasks:** This work lays a strong foundation for robots to engage in even more complex dynamic tasks, such as juggling, playing sports, or interacting with unpredictable environments, paving the way for more versatile and autonomous robotic agents.

#### 5.4. Addressing Limitations and Future Work

The authors transparently acknowledge current limitations, particularly regarding the real-world catching success rate due to object elasticity and sensor limitations. This commitment to refining the system by incorporating tactile sensors for better rebound management and exploring more robust global localization systems (like VICON) for object tracking provides clear and actionable directions for future research. This iterative refinement process is critical for moving lab-based successes into robust real-world applications.

In essence, "Catch It!" not only demonstrates a remarkable engineering feat in building and controlling a sophisticated mobile dexterous manipulator but also provides valuable methodological contributions in reinforcement learning and sim2real transfer that will undoubtedly influence future research and development in agile robotic manipulation.
