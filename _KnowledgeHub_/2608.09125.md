# Trajectory Divergence Horizon Decision for Reliable Dual-Arm Surgical Subtask Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> TDHD is a test-time execution-horizon controller for surgical Vision-Language-Action policies. It generates two flow-matching action chunks from slightly perturbed initial noise, measures step-wise L2 divergence, and truncates execution at the first step breaching an absolute or ratio threshold, forcing replanning. On a real da Vinci-like dual-arm platform it lifts end-to-end success from 55% to 60% (needle) and 55% to 80% (tissue).

## Problem

- Existing VLA policies execute fixed-length open-loop action chunks, so accumulated errors under shifting scene conditions risk anatomical damage in millimeter-precision surgical manipulation.
- Prior surgical controllers are rule-based or trained task-by-task, tailored to narrow instruments and scenarios, limiting cross-task reuse and composability at the system level.
- Medical VLA adaptations emphasize single-arm manipulation or perception-centric grounding, leaving coordinated dual-instrument surgical manipulation and its deformable-tissue contact dynamics underexplored.

## Method

- **Trajectory Divergence Horizon Decision**: sample primary noise plus a small perturbed copy, run both through the same deterministic ==flow-matching== velocity field, and measure per-step ==L2 divergence== between the two action chunks.
- ==Dual-threshold truncation rule==: stop at the earliest step where divergence exceeds an absolute limit or its ratio to the previous step exceeds a jump limit, clamped to a minimum-maximum horizon range.
- Real-world da Vinci-like dual-arm benchmark on an RM65-B platform with dVRK end-effectors, exterior plus wrist cameras, and 600 teleoperated demonstrations across needle and tissue suites at 30 Hz.

## Results

- End-to-end success reaches **60%** on needle manipulation and **80%** on tissue manipulation, versus **55%/55%** for the strongest baseline and **10%/20%** for a diffusion baseline.
- Conditional stage gains concentrate late: pick-given-reach rises **85%** to **90%**, regrasp **64.7%** to **66.7%**, resection-given-reach-and-lift **68.8%** to **80%**, with tissue reach improving **80%** to **100%**.
- Ablation over **10** runs beats both maximum-horizon and best-tuned fixed-step execution: needle pick **80%** to **90%**, tissue resection **60%** to **70%**; mean executed lengths shorten to **17.13** steps during resection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sensitivity of a deterministic generative sampler to tiny input-noise perturbations is a cheap, model-agnostic proxy for step-wise action reliability, requiring no extra training.
> - Adaptive horizon control pays off mainly in contact-unstable phases; already-stable stages see no gain, so replanning budget should track interaction difficulty rather than uniform time.
> - Gains were measured only on controlled phantom setups with hand-set thresholds and one backbone, so threshold calibration and cross-model consistency remain unverified cautions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Surgical robotics has become an integral part of modern operative care, contributing to millions of procedures worldwide. With rising clinical workload and an aging population, there is a growing impetus to develop autonomous solutions for repetitive surgical subtasks to alleviate human burden and enhance execution consistency.

Traditional approaches to surgical robot autonomy have typically relied on rule-based designs, geometric constraints, or predefined analytical models. While effective for specific instruments and narrowly defined scenarios, these methods struggle with the inherent complexity of surgical environments, which include deformable soft tissue, evolving scene contexts, and multi-stage workflows.

Data-driven learning methods offer an alternative, encoding diverse manipulation skills and improving generalization. However, many learning-based surgical controllers are developed in a task-by-task manner, limiting their reusability and composability across different procedures. Recent advancements in embodied intelligence, particularly Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, present a promising paradigm for general-purpose robotic manipulation. VLA models integrate visual perception, language grounding, and action generation within a unified framework, allowing them to generate action sequences conditioned on visual observations and language instructions. This integration reduces the need for task-specific engineering and improves cross-task reuse, laying a foundation for more generalized surgical autonomy.

Despite the potential of VLA models, a significant challenge arises from their typical open-loop execution mechanism, which involves fixed-length action sequences. In surgical manipulation, where millimeter-level precision, trajectory smoothness, and consistent end-effector pose are critical, and interactions with nonlinear, viscoelastic soft tissue are common, fixed-horizon open-loop execution can lead to accumulated errors. Time-varying contact conditions or unexpected tissue deformations can cause the robot to commit to pre-planned actions that are no longer appropriate, potentially resulting in anatomical damage. This conflict between VLA's open-loop nature and the stringent demands of surgical environments represents a key limitation addressed by the current work.

Previous research in VLA models for medical applications, such as RoboNurse-VLA and EndoVLA, has explored language-guided assistive policies and multimodal grounding in endoscopic environments. However, these efforts often focus on single-arm manipulation or perception-centric formulations, leaving coordinated multi-instrument surgical manipulation underexplored. This paper contributes to the broader landscape by addressing the reliability of VLA deployment in dual-arm surgical manipulation, specifically tackling the issue of error accumulation in open-loop execution through an adaptive horizon decision mechanism.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized conflict between the inherent open-loop, fixed-length action sequence execution of current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and the stringent precision and safety requirements of surgical manipulation. While VLA models offer a promising path toward unified and composable surgical autonomy, their reliance on executing pre-planned action chunks without real-time adaptive feedback can lead to substantial risks.

Specifically, the authors identify several critical issues:
1. **Accumulated Errors:** Surgical procedures demand millimeter-level precision. Fixed-length open-loop action sequences, if not perfectly aligned with the evolving scene, can accumulate errors over time.
2. **Unpredictable Scene Conditions:** Surgical environments are characterized by deformable soft tissues, dynamic contact forces, and potential instrument exchanges. These factors can rapidly alter scene conditions, rendering pre-planned actions suboptimal or dangerous.
3. **Risk of Damage:** Continuing to execute a rigid, pre-planned action sequence when the target tissue unexpectedly deforms, slips, or changes position can directly lead to severe anatomical damage, compromising patient safety.
4. **Multimodality of Action Distributions:** As the prediction horizon extends, especially in complex tasks like insertion or cutting, the VLA model may face multiple plausible correct movements, leading to multimodal and spatially diffuse predicted action distributions. A fixed-length execution strategy struggles to adapt to this inherent uncertainty.
5. **Trade-off in Horizon Length:** A large fixed execution horizon in VLA models might capture long-range intent but increases exposure to open-loop errors. Conversely, a small fixed horizon limits execution to near-term, lower-uncertainty steps but can introduce motion discontinuities and frequent replanning.

To mitigate these risks and enhance the reliability of VLA model deployment in surgical settings, the key objectives of this work are:
1. **Formulate an Adaptive Execution-Horizon Decision Problem:** To move beyond fixed-length open-loop execution, the research aims to conceptualize surgical VLA deployment as a problem where the execution horizon can be adaptively determined based on real-time conditions.
2. **Propose a Test-Time Mechanism for Action Reliability:** The core objective is to introduce a mechanism, Trajectory Divergence Horizon Decision (TDHD), that estimates the step-wise reliability of a generated action chunk. This mechanism should detect underlying trajectory instability to identify when continued open-loop execution becomes hazardous.
3. **Enable Timely Replanning:** By detecting instability, the TDHD mechanism should effectively truncate the execution horizon at the first unstable step, thereby forcing the system to replan from updated visual observations before spatial errors amplify. This targeted intervention is intended to enhance robustness against rigid-body constraints and unpredictable tissue deformations.
4. **Establish a Real-World Dual-Arm Surgical Benchmark:** To validate the proposed framework, a practical, accessible da Vinci-like dual-arm surgical robotic platform is to be developed. This benchmark includes synchronized multi-view perception and language instructions, along with a dataset of teleoperated demonstrations for diverse needle and tissue manipulation subtasks.
5. **Demonstrate Improved Performance and Reliability:** The ultimate objective is to quantitatively show that the proposed TDHD mechanism, when integrated with VLA policies, consistently improves performance (e.g., success rates) over existing VLA baselines in real-world dual-arm surgical manipulation tasks, particularly in contact-sensitive and later stages of manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology addresses the limitations of fixed-length open-loop VLA execution by introducing an adaptive test-time mechanism called Trajectory Divergence Horizon Decision (TDHD).

**A. VLA Model with Action Chunking**
The core control policy is a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model. At each timestep, the VLA model takes current observations (camera images and robot joint states) and a language instruction as input. Instead of predicting a single next action, it employs an action chunking strategy, outputting a sequence of future actions, A = [a_t, a_{t+1}..., a_{t+H-1}], where H is the chunk length. Each action a_i specifies target positions for the robot arms and instruments. This strategy promotes smooth, continuous movements.

**B. Flow Matching for Action Chunk Generation**
The VLA system generates action chunks using a two-part network structure with flow matching. A large encoder network processes visual and linguistic inputs once per control cycle, caching the resulting representations. A smaller decoder network then predicts robotic motions. Flow matching frames action generation as a deterministic continuous path from a random noise matrix (z ~ N(0, I) at time τ=1) to a valid action sequence (A at τ=0). The decoder predicts the movement direction v_θ(x_τ, τ, c), and by integrating this velocity field, the system derives A: A = z + ∫_0^1 v_θ(x_τ, τ, c)dτ. This process ensures that, given fixed conditioning context, model parameters, and an ODE solver, the generated action chunk A is entirely determined by the initial noise sample z.

**C. Trajectory Divergence Horizon Decision (TDHD)**
TDHD is introduced as a test-time safety gate that adaptively determines the reliable execution horizon (s*) for a generated action chunk. It functions by evaluating the step-wise prediction reliability based on trajectory divergence.
1. **Motivation for Divergence Measurement:** Recognizing the deterministic nature of flow matching, the sensitivity of the generated trajectory to small perturbations in the initial noise sample (z) is used as a proxy for trajectory instability. Significant divergence indicates that the predicted sequence has become unstable, reducing the reliability of subsequent open-loop execution. This divergence reflects both epistemic uncertainty (under-represented states) and aleatoric uncertainty (stochastic deformable tissue contact).
2. **Divergence Quantification:** During each inference pass, two initial noise vectors are sampled: a primary noise vector z_1 ~ N(0, I) and a perturbed counterpart z_2 = z_1 + ϵη, where η ~ N(0, I) is a random direction and ϵ is a small scaling factor. Both z_1 and z_2 are passed through the same learned velocity field via flow matching to produce two distinct action trajectories, A^(1) and A^(2). The step-wise divergence δ_i at each future step i is quantified using the L2 distance: δ_i = ||A^(1)_i - A^(2)_i||_2. A smaller δ_i suggests higher stability, while a larger δ_i indicates reduced prediction reliability.
3. **Adaptive Horizon Decision Rules:** To determine s*, two independent stopping rules are applied, starting from step i = 1:
 - **Absolute Threshold (Abs):** i_abs = min{i ≥ 1 | δ_i > θ_abs}, where θ_abs is a predefined absolute limit.
 - **Ratio Threshold (Jump):** i_rat = min{i ≥ 1 | (δ_i / (δ_{i-1} + ξ)) > r_thresh}, where r_thresh is a ratio limit and ξ is a small positive constant to prevent division by zero.
 The earliest step at which either rule is broken defines ˆs = min(i_abs, i_rat). If all steps are stable, ˆs defaults to the maximum allowed length s_max.
4. **Constrained Execution Horizon:** To prevent motion discontinuities and excessive replanning, the final execution horizon s* is constrained within a physical range [s_min, s_max]: s* = max(s_min, min(ˆs, s_max)).
5. **Replanning:** After executing the selected s* actions, the system captures updated observations and predicts a new action chunk, effectively replanning from the new state. The computational overhead of TDHD is modest, as the vision-language encoder is computed only once per control cycle, with the action decoder evaluated for both z_1 and z_2.

**D. Experimental Setup**
1. **Hardware Platform:** A real-world da Vinci-like dual-arm surgical robotic system was developed using a Realman RM65-B robot, equipped with motorized surgical end-effectors adapted from the da Vinci Research Kit (dVRK). A compliant soft phantom was used for tissue interaction. The perception system included a global external RGB camera and two wrist-mounted RGB cameras (640x480 resolution) for multi-view observation. Teleoperated demonstrations were collected using two Geomagic Touch haptic devices, mapping master-side Cartesian poses to slave manipulators.
2. **Task Definition:** Two task suites were designed:
 - **Needle Manipulation:** (i) Needle Reach (TCP within 5 mm of needle midpoint, midpoint inside gripper jaw projection), (ii) Needle Pick (secure grasp, lifted without dropping, original curvature maintained), (iii) Needle Regrasp (transfer from left to right arm, secure grasp by right, release by left, stable hold).
 - **Soft-Tissue Manipulation:** (i) Tissue Reach (TCP at abnormal point, point inside gripper jaw projection), (ii) Tissue Lift (stable grasp of abnormal region, lifted above phantom surface), (iii) Tissue Resection (left arm traction, right arm cuts, complete severance, lifted without residual attachment).
3. **Dual-Arm Surgical Manipulation Dataset:** 600 successful teleoperated demonstrations were collected (100 per subtask, 80 for training, 20 for testing). Data synchronization was achieved using Network Time Protocol (NTP) at 30 Hz. Observations included multi-view visual data, 6-DoF manipulator joint positions, 6-DoF end-effector poses, gripper motor states, and natural language instructions.

**E. Evaluation Criteria**
Success was defined hierarchically due to task dependencies.
- End-to-end success rate: SR_e2e = P(S1 ∩ S2 ∩ S3).
- Conditional success rates: e.g., SR_Pick|Reach = P(S2 | S1), SR_Final|Reach&Pick = P(S3 | S1 ∩ S2).
Quantitative evaluations involved 20 independent trials for each policy and task setting (5 policies × 6 subtasks × 20 trials = 600 real-world trials), with baselines (π0.5, π0, RDT, Octo) fine-tuned on the collected dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted comprehensive real-world evaluations comparing TDHD (integrated with π0.5) against several VLA baselines (π0.5, π0, RDT, Octo) across two task suites: needle manipulation and tissue manipulation.

**A. Quantitative Results**
The TDHD framework consistently demonstrated improved execution reliability and stage-wise success rates compared to the VLA baselines.

- **Overall Success Rates:**
 - For needle manipulation, the TDHD method achieved a 60% end-to-end success rate, outperforming π0.5 (55%), π0 (50%), RDT (10%), and Octo (0%).
 - For tissue manipulation, TDHD achieved an 80% end-to-end success rate, substantially higher than π0.5 (55%), π0 (45%), RDT (20%), and Octo (5%).

- **Needle Manipulation Task Suite:**
 - **Needle Reach:** Generalist policies like Octo and RDT showed limited precision in localizing the target (Octo 40%, RDT 60%). In contrast, π0.5, π0, and TDHD all achieved a 100% reach success rate.
 - **Needle Pick:** This stage involves stricter physical constraints. TDHD achieved a 90% conditional pick success rate (SR_Pick|Reach), slightly higher than π0.5's 85%. The paper notes that while the numerical gain was modest, fixed-step execution often left the needle tilted, complicating subsequent bimanual alignment. TDHD mitigated this by promoting more stable planar alignment.
 - **Needle Regrasp:** This bimanual task requires precise synchronization of spatial position and vertical alignment. Success rates generally decreased across all methods. TDHD maintained a 66.7% conditional regrasp success rate (SR_Regrasp|Reach & Pick), outperforming π0.5's 64.7%. This robustness was attributed to TDHD's ability to detect early instability via trajectory divergence and truncate execution, enabling corrective replanning and stable dual-arm coordination.

- **Tissue Manipulation Task Suite:**
 - **Tissue Reach:** The target lesion point had limited color contrast, making precise localization sensitive to visual errors. π0.5 achieved 80% and π0 achieved 75% reach success, whereas TDHD maintained a 100% reach performance.
 - **Tissue Lift:** Once the tool was aligned, lifting primarily depended on maintaining appropriate grasping height. Conditional lift success rates were high across methods, with TDHD achieving 100% (SR_Lift|Reach).
 - **Tissue Resection:** This stage presented the greatest difficulty due to continuous tissue deformation under traction, causing dynamic shifts in the cutting boundary. Fixed-step open-loop execution often led to misaligned or incomplete cuts. TDHD's dynamic step selection mitigated error propagation by truncating unstable segments and replanning, resulting in an 80% conditional resection success rate (SR_Resection|Reach & Lift), significantly outperforming π0.5's 68.8% and π0's 64.3%.

**B. Ablation Study**
An ablation study was performed using the π0.5 model to assess the effect of adaptive execution-horizon selection, comparing Fixed s_max, Fixed s_best, and TDHD (Ours).
- **Task-Dependent Gains:** The benefits of TDHD were found to be task-dependent.
 - For needle tasks, TDHD improved Pick success from 80% (Fixed s_best) to 90%, while Reach remained 100% and Regrasp remained 50%.
 - For tissue tasks, TDHD improved Resection success from 60% (Fixed s_best) to 70%, with Reach and Lift both at 90%.
- **Critical Stages:** These results indicate that adaptive horizon control is most beneficial in stages characterized by stronger contact instability, such as needle pick and tissue resection, rather than in inherently stable motion phases.
- **Executed Step Length Analysis:** Analysis of executed step lengths revealed that TDHD truncated execution during delicate contact transitions, while allowing longer horizons in stable motion phases.
 - For needle pick, truncation was mainly triggered during the downward alignment phase, preventing excessive pushing. The subsequent upward lift executed closer to the full 25-step horizon.
 - For tissue lift, execution was near full-horizon (average 23.76 steps), aligning with high success rates.
 - For tissue resection, the shorter average execution length (17.13 steps) reflected more frequent replanning during cutting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes contributions to the field of robotic surgery and embodied intelligence by addressing a fundamental limitation in the deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for safety-critical manipulation tasks.

**Significance:**
1. **Enhanced Reliability for VLA in Surgery:** The paper demonstrates that relying on fixed-horizon open-loop execution for VLA models in surgical settings presents significant risks due to error accumulation and dynamic scene conditions. The proposed Trajectory Divergence Horizon Decision (TDHD) mechanism provides a practical and effective solution by enabling adaptive, test-time control over the execution horizon. This is significant for improving the trustworthiness and safety of VLA models in demanding applications.
2. **Adaptive Control for Complex Interactions:** TDHD's ability to monitor trajectory stability and truncate unreliable action segments is particularly relevant for surgical tasks involving delicate contact transitions, such as grasping rigid objects (needles) and interacting with deformable tissues (resection). The findings highlight that adaptive execution control is crucial for maintaining performance and preventing failures when facing nonlinear tissue responses and unmodeled contact perturbations.
3. **Real-World Dual-Arm Benchmark:** The establishment of a real-world da Vinci-like dual-arm surgical robotic platform with multi-view perception and teleoperated demonstrations is a valuable contribution. This benchmark, covering both rigid-object precision and deformable-tissue interaction, provides an accessible environment for further research into advanced surgical autonomy and the evaluation of learning-based policies.
4. **Improved Performance in Critical Stages:** The consistent performance gains observed with TDHD, especially in the later, contact-sensitive stages of both needle and tissue manipulation, underscore its practical utility. By mitigating prediction drift and facilitating timely replanning, TDHD enables more robust dual-arm coordination and interaction with the surgical environment.

**Potential Impact:**
1. **Advancing Surgical Autonomy:** This research contributes to the long-term goal of developing more autonomous surgical systems. By enhancing the reliability of VLA models, TDHD moves closer to solutions that can assist surgeons with repetitive subtasks, potentially reducing clinical workload and standardizing execution quality.
2. **Safer Robotic Procedures:** The safety gate mechanism offered by TDHD has direct implications for patient safety. By preventing the accumulation of errors and triggering replanning before critical spatial deviations occur, it reduces the risk of iatrogenic damage during robot-assisted procedures.
3. **Generalizability of VLA Models:** While the current study focuses on specific surgical subtasks, the principle of adaptive execution horizon control based on trajectory divergence is generalizable to other complex robotic manipulation scenarios where VLA models are deployed in dynamic and uncertain environments. This could pave the way for more robust and widely applicable general-purpose robotic intelligence.
4. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for future investigations, including optimizing threshold calibration for TDHD, exploring the impact of trajectory ensemble size, and evaluating its consistency across a wider range of VLA architectures and more diverse surgical procedures and anatomies. The established benchmark also serves as a resource for the community to develop and test new methods for learning-based surgical control.
