# TacVLA: Contact-Aware Tactile Fusion for Robust Vision-Language-Action Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Purdue University developed TacVLA, a system that enhances Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by integrating tactile sensing with a contact-aware gating mechanism. This approach achieved an 83.75% average success rate on complex disassembly tasks and 70% on in-box picking, showing improved robustness in visually occluded or human-perturbed environments.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models rely heavily on visual and linguistic information, leading to failures in scenarios with severe visual occlusions or when fine-grained physical properties like contact forces are critical.
- Existing approaches for integrating tactile sensing into VLA often treat tactile observations as dense image-like inputs, increasing computational costs, and naive concatenation can result in inefficient cross-modal interaction during non-contact phases.
- VLA systems typically lack robustness to unexpected physical resistance, dynamic contact variations, or external disturbances due to the absence of direct physical interaction feedback.

## Method

- TacVLA integrates a compact tactile array into a transformer-based VLA policy, utilizing a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)-based encoder to project raw tactile maps into low-dimensional tactile tokens.
- A contact-aware gating module adaptively activates tactile tokens only when physical contact is detected, preventing interference from uninformative tactile data during non-contact phases while maintaining a fixed token structure.
- The system strengthens cross-modal grounding across vision, language, and touch within a pretrained Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone, with an action expert module predicting continuous action sequences conditioned on the fused multimodal tokens.

## Results

- TacVLA achieved an average success rate of 83.75% across four constraint-locked disassembly tasks, outperforming the finetuned Pi0.5 baseline (63.75%) and diffusion-based policies (48.75%).
- In the visually challenging in-box picking task, TacVLA demonstrated a 70% success rate, a notable improvement over the finetuned Pi0.5 baseline (10%) and other diffusion policies (0-5%).
- The system showed enhanced robustness under severe visual occlusion, maintaining over 60% average success (compared to ~30% for the vision-only baseline) and successfully recovering from unexpected human disturbances.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Efficient and effective integration of tactile sensing into VLA models, particularly through a compact tokenization scheme, can substantially enhance robotic manipulation capabilities in visually challenging and contact-rich environments.
> - A contact-aware gating mechanism for multimodal fusion is critical for robust performance, as it selectively leverages tactile information only when physically relevant, preventing performance degradation from uninformative tactile signals during non-contact phases.
> - Adaptive fusion of vision, language, and touch enables robots to achieve higher success rates and greater resilience to visual occlusions and dynamic disturbances in complex, fine-grained manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements with the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models, by integrating pretrained Vision Language Models (VLMs), enable robots to interpret natural language instructions and perceive unstructured environments, thereby bridging the gap between high-level semantic understanding and low-level control policies. They offer a general-purpose backbone for deployment across diverse robotic platforms and real-world scenarios.

However, a predominant limitation of current VLA architectures is their reliance on visual and linguistic modalities alone for perceiving the physical world. This visual dependency poses a critical bottleneck in scenarios involving severe visual occlusions, such as when the robot's end-effector or the manipulated object obstructs the camera's view. Furthermore, for fine-grained, contact-rich manipulation tasks like disassembly or precise assembly, visual signals frequently fail to capture essential physical properties such, as contact forces, friction, and surface textures. Without direct feedback from physical interaction, robots may struggle to dynamically adjust actions in response to unexpected resistance or slippage, potentially leading to suboptimal performance.

To address these limitations, recent research has explored incorporating tactile sensing into VLA-style policies. Tactile feedback provides direct signals of contact events, slippage, and local geometry, which can enhance grasp stabilization and facilitate corrective adjustments in tasks such as insertion, wiping, and fruit peeling. However, existing evaluations often have limited emphasis on highly contact-intensive, fine-grained manipulation tasks that require sustained and precise contact control, such as disassembly. The robustness of these systems under challenging conditions, including visual occlusion, human perturbations, and dynamic contact variations, also remains an area requiring further investigation. Additionally, many existing approaches treat tactile observations as dense image-like inputs, which can increase token length and computational costs in transformer architectures. Simply concatenating tactile tokens with visual and language tokens can also result in inefficient cross-modal interaction, particularly during non-contact phases where tactile data may be uninformative.

TacVLA positions itself within this landscape by specifically addressing the challenges of efficiently and effectively integrating tactile sensing into VLA architectures for robust fine-grained manipulation. It proposes a compact tactile tokenization scheme and, critically, a contact-aware gating mechanism designed to adaptively fuse tactile information, thereby selectively activating tactile tokens only when physical contact is detected. This approach aims to leverage the strengths of tactile sensing while mitigating the computational overhead and potential interference associated with unconditioned multimodal fusion, particularly in contact-rich and visually challenging environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the capabilities of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation, specifically in tasks that are contact-rich, require fine-grained control, and are susceptible to visual occlusions.

The motivation for TacVLA stems from several identified limitations of existing VLA models:

1. **Over-reliance on Visual Information:** Current VLA models predominantly depend on visual input. This dependency becomes a significant vulnerability when visual information is limited or completely occluded, such as when the robot's gripper blocks the camera view or during manipulation inside confined spaces (e.g., in-box picking). Tasks requiring precise interaction within enclosed or visually obstructed environments frequently result in suboptimal performance.
2. **Inability to Capture Fine-Grained Physical Properties:** For intricate manipulation tasks like disassembly or precise assembly, visual signals alone are often insufficient to capture critical physical properties. These include contact forces, pressure distribution, friction, and local surface textures, which are essential for stable grasping, detecting slippage, and executing compliant movements. Without direct feedback on physical interactions, robots struggle to perform dynamic adjustments necessary for successful completion of such tasks.
3. **Lack of Robustness to Physical Interactions and Disturbances:** Existing VLA systems may not robustly handle unexpected physical resistance, changes in contact conditions, or external disturbances (e.g., human interference). The absence of a direct physical sensing modality limits their ability to adapt and recover from such unforeseen events during task execution.
4. **Inefficient Multimodal Fusion:** While some previous work has incorporated tactile sensing, many approaches treat tactile data as image-like inputs, leading to increased token length and computational burden within transformer architectures. Furthermore, simply concatenating tactile tokens throughout an entire trajectory, regardless of contact state, can lead to inefficient cross-modal interaction, as tactile signals are often uninformative during non-contact phases and may introduce irrelevant noise or competition for attention.

To address these motivations, TacVLA aims to:
- Integrate a compact tactile array into a transformer-based VLA policy.
- Develop an efficient tactile tokenization scheme that provides a low-dimensional yet expressive representation without significantly increasing token length.
- Propose a contact-aware gating mechanism that adaptively activates tactile tokens only when physical contact is detected, thereby enabling selective and efficient multimodal fusion and preventing interference from uninformative tactile data during non-contact phases.
- Strengthen cross-modal grounding across vision, language, and touch to enhance robustness and precision in contact-rich and visually challenging manipulation tasks.

By achieving these objectives, TacVLA seeks to enable more robust, precise, and adaptive robotic manipulation capabilities, particularly in scenarios where physical interaction and visual uncertainty are prominent.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TacVLA is designed as a tactile-enhanced extension of a transformer-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework, focusing on efficient multimodal fusion and adaptive grounding for contact-rich manipulation.

**Overall Architecture:**
The TacVLA architecture consists of four main components:
1. **Modality Tokenizers:**
 - **Visual Encoder:** RGB images from both a fixed front-facing camera and a wrist-mounted camera are processed by a SigLIP visual encoder, generating a sequence of visual tokens for each image.
 - **Language Tokenizer:** Natural language instructions and robot proprioception (joint states, gripper position, etc.) are tokenized using the PaliGemma tokenizer.
 - **Tactile Encoder:** A 15x8 tactile array sensor provides raw tactile measurements. These are processed by a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)-based encoder, which projects the tactile map into 36 low-dimensional tactile tokens. Fixed 2D sine-cosine positional embeddings are added to these tokens to preserve spatial structure. This approach provides a compact representation of local contact patterns, global pressure distribution, and contact geometry.
2. **Pretrained VLM Backbone:** The tokenized inputs from all modalities are concatenated into a unified token sequence, $\tilde{z}_t = [z_{vis_t}, z_{lan+pro_t}, \tilde{z}_{tac_t}]$. This sequence is then fed into a pretrained VLM backbone. A non-causal attention mechanism within this backbone allows visual, language, and tactile tokens to freely interact and cross-attend, producing a deeply integrated contextual representation.
3. **Action Expert:** The fused multimodal representation serves as a prefix to an action expert module, which follows the Pi0.5 design. This module is trained with a flow-matching objective and is responsible for predicting continuous action sequences $a_{t:t+H}$ conditioned on the multimodal tokens.
4. **Contact-aware Gating Module:** This is a core innovation of TacVLA, designed to selectively activate tactile tokens based on the physical contact state.

**Contact-aware Gating Tactile Fusion:**
Recognizing that tactile signals are typically uninformative during non-contact phases, the contact-aware gating module aims to prevent unnecessary interference and optimize computational efficiency.
- **Contact Detection:** Physical contact is determined using a threshold-based criterion: contact is declared when the number of taxels (individual sensing elements) exceeding a predefined pressure threshold surpasses a fixed count.
- **Adaptive Gating Mechanism:** Instead of dynamically adding or removing tokens, a fixed token structure is maintained, and tactile tokens are treated as conditional inputs.
 - A binary contact flag $c_t \in \{0, 1\}$ is generated, where $c_t = 1$ only when physical contact is detected.
 - A contact-dependent attention mask $M^{tac}_t = c_t \cdot \mathbf{1}$ is applied to the tactile tokens, where $\mathbf{1}$ is a mask vector of the same length as the tactile token sequence. When $c_t = 0$ (no contact), tactile tokens are effectively masked out from attention computation, preventing them from participating in cross-modal interaction.
 - Additionally, embedding gating is applied, $\tilde{z}^{tac}_t = c_t \cdot z^{tac}_t$, which zeros out tactile tokens when no contact is present. This suppresses any offsets from embedding layers or positional encodings, further reducing the influence of pre-contact sensor noise.
This module enables tactile information to influence policy decisions only when it is physically informative, maintaining a fixed token topology while allowing state-dependent modality routing.

**Training Procedure:**
- **Dataset:** A real-world dataset was collected, comprising synchronized visual observations, language instructions, tactile maps, robot proprioception, and ground-truth action trajectories. The dataset includes 50 human teleoperation demonstrations for each of the four disassembly tasks and one in-box picking task. All data streams were recorded at 10 Hz and temporally aligned.
- **Fine-tuning:** TacVLA is fine-tuned using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) on top of an OpenPI backbone checkpoint (specifically, `pi05 base`).
- **Optimization:** All models are fine-tuned for 10,000 gradient steps with consistent optimization settings. The parameters of the tactile encoder are frozen during this fine-tuning process.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Hardware:** A 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda robotic arm equipped with parallel grippers.
- **Perception:** Two RGB cameras (a fixed front-facing camera for a global scene view and a wrist-mounted camera for close-range observations) and a tactile array sensor (adapted from, with a spatial resolution of 15x8) mounted on one gripper finger.
- **Tasks:**
 - **Constraint-locked Disassembly:** Four tasks requiring fine-grained adjustments based on tactile feedback, involving separating components locked by geometric constraints through specific motions (pressing, twisting, sliding). Examples include "tight shaft," "press clip," "shaft rotation," and "slide pull."
 - **In-box Picking:** A task requiring retrieval of objects from a confined space with limited visual access, emphasizing reliance on tactile sensing.
- **Baselines:** Performance comparison against two diffusion-based baselines (image-based Diffusion Policy and 3D Diffusion Policy, both augmented with tactile input) and a Finetuned Pi0.5 (vision-language only) baseline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted to evaluate TacVLA's performance on contact-rich manipulation tasks, including constraint-locked disassembly and in-box picking, along with robustness evaluations under visual occlusion and human disturbance.

**Overall Performance (Table II):**
- **Disassembly Tasks:** TacVLA achieved an average success rate of 83.75% across the four disassembly tasks, outperforming the finetuned Pi0.5 baseline (63.75%) and the diffusion-based policies (48.75% for Diffusion Policy + Tactile, and 31.25% for 3D Diffusion Policy + Tactile).
 - TacVLA demonstrated consistent performance, achieving 100% on Task 1, 90% on Task 2, 70% on Task 3, and 75% on Task 4.
 - The largest performance gain was observed in Task 4 (slide pull), where TacVLA improved success rate by 45% compared to the Pi0.5 baseline, indicating enhanced stability during fine-grained sliding interactions with partial occlusion. The Pi0.5 baseline frequently became stuck or attempted repeated re-grasping in this task.
- **In-box Picking Task:** In this task, characterized by severe visual challenges (no front camera visibility inside the box, limited illumination, and frequent wrist camera occlusion), TacVLA achieved a 70% success rate. This significantly surpassed the finetuned Pi0.5 baseline (10%) and diffusion policies (0-5%). TacVLA exhibited active exploration and re-grasp attempts, proceeding to lifting only after contact detection, which the baseline failed to achieve reliably.

**Robustness Evaluation:**
- **Block Camera (Fig. 6):** Under severe visual occlusion (front camera physically blocked), TacVLA maintained a significantly higher average success rate (over 60%) across disassembly tasks. The vision-only finetuned Pi0.5 baseline experienced a substantial performance drop, achieving an average success rate of approximately 30%. Task 3 (shaft rotation) showed the largest improvement with TacVLA (+40%), demonstrating its ability to leverage tactile feedback when visual cues are degraded.
- **Human Disturbance (Fig. 5):** When a human operator unexpectedly returned the manipulated object to the box, TacVLA successfully detected the state change, returned to the box, re-grasped the object, and continued task execution. In contrast, the finetuned Pi0.5 baseline failed to recover from such dynamic perturbations.

**Ablation Study (Table III, Fig. 6, Fig. 7):**
- **Effect of Contact-aware Gating:** The ablation study compared TacVLA (with gating) against a variant where tactile tokens were directly concatenated without the contact-aware gating module (Finetuned Pi0.5 + Tactile w/o Gating).
 - **Disassembly:** Removing gating reduced the average success rate from 83.75% to 71.25%. Notably, on Task 3, the no-gating variant performed worse than the Pi0.5 baseline without any tactile input (60% vs. 65%), suggesting that naive tactile fusion can degrade performance. Qualitatively, the no-gating variant showed issues like misalignment, repeated re-grasps, stalled states, and failed lifts, indicating interference from unconditioned tactile tokens.
 - **In-box Picking:** Performance dropped from 70% to 40% without gating, with common failure cases involving repeated unsuccessful grasp attempts. This suggests that unconditional tactile fusion limits effective contact-state information usage under degraded visual conditions.
 - **Block Camera on Disassembly:** Under visual occlusion, removing the gating mechanism resulted in a substantial performance drop of approximately 25% compared to TacVLA. This highlights the critical role of contact-aware gating in leveraging tactile feedback when visual information is unreliable.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in TacVLA addresses significant limitations within the current landscape of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation, demonstrating a method for enhancing their capabilities in challenging scenarios.

The significance of this work can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Bridging Modality Gaps:** TacVLA successfully integrates tactile sensing into VLA models, effectively addressing the limitations of relying solely on visual and linguistic information in contact-rich and visually occluded environments. This provides a more comprehensive perception of the physical world for robots.
2. **Adaptive Multimodal Fusion:** The introduction of a contact-aware gating mechanism is a notable contribution. This adaptive strategy, which selectively activates tactile tokens only during physical contact, offers a computationally efficient and robust approach to multimodal fusion. It prevents the dilution of informative signals by irrelevant tactile data during non-contact phases and mitigates the overhead associated with denser, continuous tactile representations. This mechanism represents a step towards more intelligent and state-dependent sensor utilization in robotics.
3. **Enhanced Robustness and Precision:** The experimental results demonstrate consistent and significant improvements in success rates across diverse contact-rich tasks, including complex constraint-locked disassembly and in-box picking. Crucially, TacVLA exhibits enhanced robustness under challenging conditions such as severe visual occlusion and unexpected human disturbances. This capability is vital for deploying robots in dynamic and unstructured real-world settings where perfect visual conditions cannot be guaranteed.
4. **Blueprint for Future Robotic Systems:** By providing a detailed architecture and empirical validation, TacVLA offers a potential blueprint for developing future robotic manipulation systems that require fine motor skills and adaptability. The compact tactile tokenization and adaptive gating mechanism can inspire further research into more sophisticated and learnable strategies for integrating heterogeneous sensor modalities.
5. **Benchmarking Challenging Tasks:** The paper introduces and evaluates performance on a set of challenging contact-rich disassembly tasks and an in-box picking scenario. These tasks provide valuable benchmarks for assessing the capabilities of VLA models in fine-grained manipulation, particularly concerning physical interaction and robustness to visual uncertainty.

The potential impact of TacVLA extends to various applications where robots need to interact with objects and environments with precision and reliability. This includes advanced manufacturing and assembly lines, service robotics operating in human environments, hazardous environment exploration and intervention, and potentially even surgical robotics where tactile feedback is critical. By enabling robots to better understand and respond to physical interactions, TacVLA contributes to making robotic manipulation more autonomous, reliable, and adaptable to the complexities of the real world.
