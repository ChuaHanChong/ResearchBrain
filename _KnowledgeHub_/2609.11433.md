# Safety-aware Skill Adaptation for Reinforcement Learning in Dynamic Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Dist-GPRL adapts Gaussian-Process-parameterised robot skills in cluttered, dynamic scenes by sequentially updating overlapping local windows of trajectory via-points, correlating raw SAC actions through the GP covariance, and guiding exploration with a HAP safe-subspace prior plus Euclidean distance field clearance and gradient rewards. It reaches 89% and 98% simulated success with 1-5% collisions, transferring to hardware at 90%.

## Problem

- Prior GP-based skill adaptation assumes fixed, collision-free task-configuration via-points and annealed exploration, so it explores only near the conditioned mean and collides in clutter.
- Adapting the entire via-point set at once creates a high-dimensional action space and severe credit assignment problems, causing unstable learning and suboptimal, collision-prone policies.
- Safe-subspace methods are computationally expensive and robot-specific under dynamic scenes; distance fields are rarely combined with structured adaptation of demonstrated skills.

## Method

- ==Windowed local adaptation==: a phase variable selects overlapping contiguous via-point windows (three windows of five 6-DoF via-points, one-point overlap), shrinking the per-step action to 30 dimensions.
- ==GP covariance action transform==: raw policy outputs are premultiplied by a principal submatrix of the global correlation operator, coupling neighbouring via-point updates into temporally coherent trajectory edits.
- Dual safety guidance in the ==SAC== objective: ==HAP soft-min safe-subspace prior== plus cosine/acceleration ==kinematic similarity regulariser==, with ==hyperbolic clearance penalty== and ==EDF gradient-alignment reward==.

## Results

- Simulation over 100 varied task configurations: **89%** success on cube pushing and **98%** on bar manipulation versus **18%** and **44%** for the unwindowed GP baseline, with collisions cut to **5%** and **1%**.
- Hardware bar-removal transfer over 10 episodes reached **90%** success at **10%** collision, beating global adaptation (**50%**), distance-only (**70%**), prior-only (**60%**), and residual ProMP (**0%**).
- Ablations over three seeds show the HAP subspace and covariance transform each raise success, lower collision, and raise episode return; adaptation costs about **19.3 ms** per episode.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing a global trajectory edit into overlapping local windows is a cheap fix for credit assignment, recovering most of the gain before any safety machinery is added.
> - Projecting raw policy actions through a learned prior's covariance is a general way to buy temporal coherence and stability without constraining the policy architecture.
> - Safe-subspace priors and distance-field rewards are complementary, not redundant: priors speed convergence, clearance rewards prevent contact, and neither alone suffices; no formal guarantee results.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the intersection of robot skill learning, probabilistic trajectory representation, reinforcement learning, and safety-aware motion adaptation. Conventional robot programming and learning-from-demonstration methods often produce trajectories that work for a particular task configuration but do not generalize reliably when object locations, start conditions, or obstacles change. Skill adaptation methods seek to modify a demonstrated motion while retaining its useful geometric and kinematic properties.

Several trajectory representations have been used for this purpose. Dynamic Movement Primitives provide compact and stable motion models but have limited capacity to represent trajectory distributions. Gaussian Mixture Models offer probabilistic representations but may be less expressive for complex manipulation trajectories. Probabilistic Movement Primitives are also distributional and expressive, although their integration with reinforcement learning can be less direct. Gaussian Processes are attractive because they provide continuous trajectory models, uncertainty-aware representations, and analytically available derivatives for velocity and acceleration.

Previous Gaussian Process reinforcement learning methods, including the authors’ earlier GPRL framework, adapted sparse trajectory via-points using Soft Actor-Critic. These methods, however, generally modified the complete via-point set at every policy step. They also relied on assumptions such as fixed task observations, collision-free conditioning points, and decreasing exploration during training. Such assumptions are difficult to maintain in dynamic, cluttered environments. A task-relevant observation may itself be close to an obstacle, and unrestricted reinforcement learning exploration can produce collisions or unstable updates.

The paper extends this research direction by combining three ideas: local rather than global trajectory adaptation, action smoothing based on the GP covariance structure, and two complementary forms of safety guidance. The first is a safe-subspace prior generated by the Hausdorff Approximation Planner (HAP). The second is continuous obstacle-clearance feedback from dynamically updated Euclidean Distance Fields. This places the work between structured learning-from-demonstration methods and safe reinforcement learning approaches that use geometric constraints, signed distance fields, or constrained policy optimization.

Unlike methods that impose strict low-level collision constraints or project actions onto a constraint manifold, Dist-GPRL primarily uses priors, regularization, and reward shaping. It therefore aims to improve empirical safety and learning stability without providing formal collision guarantees.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to enable a robot to adapt a demonstrated manipulation skill to new and changing task configurations while satisfying two requirements: maintaining safe clearance from obstacles and preserving the motion characteristics of the demonstration.

The authors identify several specific problems with existing approaches. First, global via-point adaptation creates a high-dimensional action space. In the reported implementation, the trajectory contains 90 via-point variables across six position and orientation dimensions. Updating all of them simultaneously makes credit assignment difficult because the policy cannot easily determine which individual modifications caused a successful interaction or a collision.

Second, independent policy outputs applied directly to via-points can produce temporally inconsistent trajectory changes. A modification at one point may be incompatible with neighboring points, resulting in irregular motions or loss of the demonstrated velocity and acceleration profile.

Third, safety based solely on a task reward or a fixed safe region may be inadequate in dynamic scenes. A precomputed safe subspace can become outdated when obstacles move. Conversely, local clearance information from a distance field can identify nearby obstacles but does not necessarily guide exploration efficiently toward task-feasible regions.

Dist-GPRL is motivated by the idea that these safety mechanisms should be complementary. HAP provides a global or task-dependent bias toward feasible Cartesian regions, while a distance field supplies local, continuously updated obstacle information. The framework also seeks to avoid overly conservative behavior. A policy that simply maximizes clearance may avoid collisions by failing to approach or manipulate the target. Consequently, task completion, safety, exploration direction, and kinematic similarity are optimized together.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Gaussian Process Skill Representation

A demonstrated six-degree-of-freedom trajectory is represented using separate Gaussian Processes for the three position and three orientation dimensions. Sparse via-points act as conditioning variables. Because GP kernels encode temporal correlations, changes to the via-points generate smooth modifications over the full trajectory rather than isolated point movements. Analytical GP derivatives provide the adapted velocity and acceleration profiles.

The adaptation problem is formulated as a Markov decision process. The policy observes the currently active via-point window, the distance-field clearances at those points, the end-effector’s displacement from the manipulated object, the displacement from the goal, and a phase variable indicating the current trajectory location.

#### 3.2. Sequential Local Window Adaptation

Instead of updating every via-point at every reinforcement learning step, Dist-GPRL selects a contiguous local window based on the trajectory phase. The window advances through the trajectory, and neighboring windows overlap by one via-point. This local structure reduces the number of variables being optimized at each step and makes the consequences of an action more temporally localized.

The implementation uses 15 via-points per trajectory dimension, producing 90 variables over six dimensions. It uses three overlapping windows, each containing five six-degree-of-freedom via-points. Thus, the policy produces a 30-dimensional action for each local adaptation step. The overlap is intended to preserve continuity between adjacent adapted segments.

#### 3.3. Covariance-Based Action Transformation

The policy initially produces a raw corrective action for the active window. These outputs are then transformed using a precomputed GP correlation matrix:

\[
S = K(K+\lambda I)^{-1}.
\]

The relevant local submatrix of \(S\) is applied to the raw action. The resulting update modifies neighboring via-points in a correlated manner. The parameter \(\lambda\) controls the degree of smoothing: smaller values preserve more of the original policy action, while larger values produce smoother and more conservative changes.

This transformation uses the temporal structure already encoded by the GP. It is intended to make policy exploration more coherent than independently changing each via-point.

#### 3.4. Kinematic Similarity Regularization

The actor objective includes a regularizer that compares the adapted and demonstrated trajectories. Velocity similarity is measured using cosine similarity, which encourages the adapted trajectory to retain the directionality of the original motion. Acceleration differences are penalized using a norm-based term, encouraging comparable smoothness and dynamic characteristics.

The regularization does not require the adapted trajectory to reproduce the demonstration exactly. Instead, it discourages unnecessary changes in the motion profile while allowing task-dependent geometric deviations.

#### 3.5. HAP Safe-Subspace Prior

The HAP component generates a set of Cartesian positions considered feasible under the current task configuration. Sampled points along the adapted trajectory are compared with nearby points in this safe set. A soft-minimum distance loss biases the actor toward the safe subspace while maintaining differentiability.

This component affects policy exploration through the actor objective rather than acting as a hard constraint. It is therefore a preference for safe and feasible regions, not a guarantee that all generated trajectories remain collision-free.

#### 3.6. Dynamic Distance-Field Guidance

A Euclidean Distance Field represents the clearance between workspace points and obstacle surfaces. It is updated online as moving obstacles change position. The reward includes a hyperbolic penalty for sampled end-effector points whose clearance falls below a chosen safety margin. The penalty grows rapidly near contact, producing a stronger learning signal than a linear distance penalty.

The method also calculates the gradient of the distance field at active via-points. This gradient points in the direction of increasing clearance. A gradient-alignment reward gives higher values when a via-point update is aligned with this locally safer direction.

The total reward combines task completion, clearance-based safety, and gradient alignment. The policy is trained with Soft Actor-Critic, using entropy regularization for exploration and the additional HAP and kinematic terms in the actor loss.

#### 3.7. Experimental Design

The method was evaluated in PyBullet on two dynamic manipulation tasks using a UR5e robot and one expert demonstration per task:

1. **Dynamic Cube Pushing:** A shovel-equipped robot pushes a moving cube toward a goal while avoiding a moving obstacle. Object and obstacle positions vary from the demonstration.
2. **Dynamic Bar Manipulation:** A hook-equipped robot removes one of two bars from a moving conveyor while avoiding the other bar, conveyor rollers, and rails.

The bar-manipulation task was also transferred to a real UR5e robot. Bar locations were detected using ArUco markers, and the real-world distance field was updated using the IDMP framework.

The baselines were the original global GPRL method, ProMP residual reinforcement learning, global Dist-GPRL without local windows and action correlation, windowed adaptation with distance rewards but no HAP prior, and windowed adaptation with HAP but no distance rewards.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

Across 100 simulated runs per task, Dist-GPRL achieved an 89% success rate and a 5% collision rate in dynamic cube pushing. In dynamic bar manipulation, it achieved a 98% success rate and a 1% collision rate. The fifth-percentile minimum clearances were 0.017 m and 0.037 m, respectively.

The original GPRL method performed poorly in both settings, with 18% success and 68% collisions for cube pushing, and 44% success and 54% collisions for bar manipulation. These results support the paper’s claim that global, uncorrelated adaptation is unstable in cluttered environments.

The global variant with safety mechanisms improved performance but remained less reliable than local adaptation. It reached 64% success and 30% collisions in cube pushing, and 78% success and 11% collisions in bar manipulation.

The ablation results clarify the contribution of each component. The distance-reward variant generally produced larger clearances and fewer collisions, but sometimes sacrificed task success. The HAP-only variant improved exploration and performance compared with the unstructured baseline, but did not consistently prevent collisions. Combining both mechanisms provided a better balance between interaction with the target and obstacle avoidance.

The covariance transformation also improved learning behavior across three random seeds. According to the reported training curves, it led to earlier improvements in success, lower collision frequency, and higher episode returns relative to direct local action updates.

The reported computational cost was approximately 19.3 milliseconds for the three adaptation windows on an Intel Core i7-1185G7 processor. This included distance-field queries, local GP conditioning, and policy inference.

#### 4.2. Hardware Results

Ten real-world bar-manipulation trials were conducted with randomly initialized bar positions. Dist-GPRL achieved 90% success, 10% collisions, and a minimum clearance of 0.027 m.

The original GPRL method achieved 10% success and 70% collisions. ProMP residual reinforcement learning avoided many collisions but achieved no successful removals, indicating that excessive conservatism can prevent useful object interaction. The global Dist-GPRL variant achieved 50% success and 20% collisions. The distance-only and HAP-only variants achieved 70% and 60% success, respectively, with collision rates of 20% and 30%.

The hardware results followed the general simulation pattern: local adaptation, correlated updates, and combined safety guidance were associated with more reliable task completion than the individual components or global adaptation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper contributes a structured method for making reinforcement-learning-based skill adaptation more compatible with dynamic manipulation environments. Its main significance lies in combining probabilistic trajectory structure with local action updates and geometric safety feedback. The approach reduces the effective adaptation problem without abandoning the global smoothness properties of Gaussian Processes.

The results suggest potential value for collaborative and industrial robots that must reuse demonstrated skills under changing object locations and moving obstacles. The use of dynamically updated distance fields is particularly relevant where obstacle geometry changes during execution. The one-shot transfer from simulation to hardware also indicates that the learned adaptation policy can operate with real sensor-derived geometric information, although the hardware evaluation is limited in scale.

The method has important limitations. Its safety mechanisms are soft and empirical, so they do not guarantee collision avoidance. Performance depends on accurate task observations, obstacle detection, distance-field updates, and suitable hyperparameters. The authors also identify GP extrapolation as a problem, particularly near trajectory boundaries, where adapted via-points can generate erratic motion. Sensor noise and uncertainty in the estimated distance field are not modeled explicitly. Finally, the experimental evidence comes from two tasks, one robot platform, and only ten hardware trials.

Future work would need to address these issues through explicit safety constraints, uncertainty-aware perception and distance fields, improved boundary handling for GP trajectories, and larger real-world evaluations. Within those limits, Dist-GPRL provides evidence that local, covariance-coupled adaptation can improve the stability and practical safety of reinforcement-learning-based robot skill modification.
