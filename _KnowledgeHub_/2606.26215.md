# TaskNPoint: How to Teach Your Humanoid to Hit a Backhand in Minutes

> [!summary] Summary
> TaskNPoint introduces a "coach-learner" protocol that enables humanoid robots to learn dynamic loco-manipulation skills, such as tennis or soccer, from a single human video demonstration and a defined "interaction window". This method allows policies to generalize zero-shot to novel target locations and velocities with high success rates (e.g., 93% in simulation, 100% for slow targets in hardware) while requiring under an hour of training on a single GPU.

## Problem

- Existing robot learning methods for dynamic loco-manipulation are either data-intensive (requiring massive simulations or large datasets) or struggle with generalization and extensive reward engineering.
- Reinforcement learning often yields unnatural behaviors due to biases from hand-tuned reward functions.
- Humanoid policies for sports are typically highly specialized and do not efficiently generalize to new goal locations or integrate whole-body coordination.

## Method

- A "coach-learner" protocol where a human coach provides single video demonstrations, defines discrete skills, identifies a crucial "interaction window," and specifies spatio-temporal goals.
- Robot learner uses kinematically retargeted human motions as reference to train policies in simulation with randomized target sampling to achieve zero-shot generalization.
- Policies are trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) using an asymmetric actor-critic architecture and a reward function balancing trajectory tracking with end-effector goal achievement.

## Results

- Achieved high simulation success rates: **93%** GSR for ballistic hitting and **98%** for box pick-and-place, generalizing over 3D volumes of target locations.
- Demonstrated robust hardware deployment on a Unitree G1 robot, achieving **100%** success for slow (0-4 m/s) tennis and soccer targets, and 45-**70%** for fast (4-8 m/s) targets.
- Policies were trained efficiently, requiring fewer than 10 human demonstrations and under an hour on a single GPU, without task-specific reward tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic skills often hinge on a short, critical "interaction window," with the surrounding motion serving to correctly execute this crucial phase.
> - Abstracting dynamic tasks as spatio-temporal goals, defined by 3D conditioning points and contact times, simplifies complex motion learning and allows for efficient, parallel training of discrete actions.
> - A "coach-learner" paradigm, leveraging minimal human expert input (single demonstrations) and simulation-based practice, can effectively teach complex, generalized dynamic skills to humanoids.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics research aims to enable robots to perform complex, dynamic loco-manipulation skills. A significant challenge in this area is how to efficiently and robustly teach these skills to robots. Current approaches often face limitations in terms of data efficiency, generalization to novel scenarios, and the need for extensive reward engineering or task-specific tuning.

The paper identifies two main directions in existing research for humanoid loco-manipulation:
1. **Massive Simulations and Reward Optimization:** This approach uses reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize a reward function through large-scale simulations. While data-efficient in terms of human demonstrations, it frequently leads to unnatural or inefficient behaviors due to biases introduced by hand-tuned rewards.
2. **Learning from Massive Human Motion Datasets:** This method utilizes general whole-body controllers that track arbitrary trajectories derived from large human motion datasets. While bypassing reward engineering, these methods often require expensive and ad-hoc data cleaning to ensure sufficient quality for effective training.

Furthermore, research into sport-playing humanoids has demonstrated impressive behaviors, but these policies are typically highly specialized and tuned to a specific sport. They may also decouple upper and lower body control, which does not fully address whole-body coordination. Such approaches often do not match the data efficiency observed in human learning, where a coach provides discrete skills and demonstrations for refinement.

TaskNPoint positions itself as an alternative that addresses the limitations of these existing paradigms. It proposes a novel abstraction methodology and training pipeline that draws inspiration from how humans learn dynamic skills, particularly in sports. By focusing on a "coach-learner" division of labor, the work aims to enable humanoid robots to acquire dynamic skills from minimal human input (single video demonstrations), generalize these skills to new target locations and trajectories, and do so with high efficiency (short training times on a single GPU) without requiring per-task reward tuning. This approach seeks to provide a general framework for dynamic humanoid motions that is both data-efficient and robust, covering a wide task space without extensive data augmentation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from an observation about human learning of dynamic skills, particularly in sports: humans typically learn by working with a coach who defines a skill, demonstrates it, highlights critical interaction moments, and specifies the goal, followed by practice. This contrasts with learning solely from vast quantities of observational data. The authors argue that this "coach-learner" paradigm is a suitable strategy for teaching dynamic loco-manipulation skills to humanoid robots.

A core insight motivating TaskNPoint is the "peculiar structure" of dynamic sports skills. For instance, in actions like a tennis backhand, the outcome is not determined by the entire body motion, but by a short, crucial portion of the trajectory—termed the "interaction window" (e.g., the racket's travel around ball contact). The remainder of the motion (backswing, footwork, follow-through) serves to correctly execute this critical interaction window, coordinating control, physics, and morphology. This suggests that learning dynamic skills can be decomposed into mastering a set of distinct actions and practicing each until the interaction window is executed correctly.

Based on this, the key objectives of the TaskNPoint protocol are:

- **Formalize a "coach-learner" division of labor:** Define explicit roles for a human coach and a robot learner. The coach provides expert knowledge, including:
 - A discrete set of skills (e.g., forehand, backhand).
 - A single demonstration per skill.
 - Identification of the "interaction window" for each skill.
 - Specification of the goal for each skill (e.g., ball placement, or simplified to impact quality for this work).
- **Enable efficient learning in simulation:** The robot learner is tasked with filling in the complete action trajectory, generalizing the skill to a continuum of possible target points and velocities, and acquiring robustness to perturbations through practice in a physics-accurate simulation environment.
- **Achieve zero-shot generalization:** A crucial objective is to allow a single demonstration to generalize zero-shot to unseen goal locations by employing randomized target sampling during training.
- **Demonstrate practical applicability and efficiency:** The protocol aims to teach complex dynamic skills (e.g., tennis, soccer, pick-and-place) to a humanoid robot from short human video demonstrations, requiring minimal training time (under an hour on a single GPU) and no per-task reward tuning, leading to successful hardware deployment.
- **Develop a dynamic task abstraction:** To frame dynamic tasks as spatio-temporal goals, simplifying the learning problem by dividing sports into clear, discrete actions trainable in parallel simulation.

In essence, the motivation is to leverage the structural properties of dynamic skills and human learning paradigms to overcome the data intensity, lack of generalization, and reward engineering challenges prevalent in current robot learning methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TaskNPoint introduces a structured training protocol involving a human coach and a robot learner. The methodology is divided into three main stages: motion acquisition from human demonstrations, policy learning in simulation, and motion selection and planning for deployment.

#### 3.1. Motion Acquisition

This stage focuses on extracting and processing reference motions from human demonstrations.
- **Motion Reconstruction:**
 - The process begins with collecting monocular video demonstrations of human actions.
 - State-of-the-art off-the-shelf monocular human pose estimation methods, specifically PromptHMR, are used to recover 3D human pose estimates in the form of SMPL-X parameters (translation, body pose, global orientation, and shape) for each frame.
 - To address common failure modes in monocular reconstruction, such as foot skating and translation drift, multi-view videos are utilized where available. For these, per-view pose estimates are fused into a maximum-likelihood consensus pose. This fusion models each per-camera joint estimate as a Gaussian observation, with covariance elongated along the camera's depth axis to account for monocular depth ambiguity. These estimates are then lifted into a shared world-coordinate frame for fusion.
- **Kinematic Retargeting and Goal Identification:**
 - The reconstructed human motion trajectories are kinematically retargeted to the robot's morphology using GMR (Goal-oriented Motion Retargeting) to adapt the human motion to what the robot can execute.
 - During this process, the human coach identifies the crucial "interaction window" and its corresponding timestamp ($t^*$). At this $t^*$, the position ($p^*$), velocity direction ($\nu^*$), and orientation ($n^*$) of the end-effector (e.g., tennis racket, foot, hand) are extracted. This tuple ($p^*, \nu^*, n^*, t^*$) constitutes the nominal goal ($G^*$) for the motion.
 - For training purposes, target points $p$ are randomized around the nominal target $p^*$ with a defined covariance $\Sigma$ to cover a 3D volume of possible target locations. Velocities and orientations are similarly randomized. This randomized goal generation allows a single demonstration to generalize across a continuum of target points.
 - The collection of all retargeted demonstrations forms an "action library" ($A$).

#### 3.2. Policy Learning

With the action library established, a policy is learned that computes a trajectory based on a chosen goal, aiming for robustness to novel targets.
- **Simulator and Algorithm:** The policy is trained in the MJlab simulator using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm with an Asymmetric Actor-Critic architecture. Standard hyperparameters are used for reproducibility (e.g., discount factor $\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$, learning rate $1e-3$).
- **Observations:** The policy's actor network receives proprioceptive inputs (history of joint positions, joint velocities, angular velocity, projected gravity vector, and previous action) and the chosen goal (target positions and orientations in the robot's local frame, target velocities in the world frame, and the reference trajectory of the chosen action). The critic network receives additional privileged observations, including the robot's center of mass position and velocity, and each robot link's positions and orientations.
- **Actions:** The actor outputs position setpoints for each joint, which are then tracked by PD controllers at 200 Hz.
- **Rewards:** The reward function has two main components:
 - **Trajectory Tracking:** Based on established methods, it rewards tracking key point positions, orientations, and velocities of the robot, and penalizes self-collisions, joint limit violations, and high action rates.
 - **Goal Achievement:** This component specifically rewards the end-effector for achieving the target position, velocity, and orientation within the identified "interaction window" ($\Omega$). Rewards are exponentially weighted, decreasing with the square of the error. A key aspect is the absence of additional per-task reward tuning, relying solely on scaling achievement rewards based on the contact window size.
- **Action Goals and Curriculum:** During training, motions are sampled from the action library. A confidence region $C_i(\delta)$ is defined around each nominal target to cover the workspace. A curriculum is implemented using reference state initialization to focus on more difficult motion phases. After successful completion, the environment is paused for a randomized period before sampling a new motion and goal, allowing the policy to learn recovery behaviors and transitions between motions.

#### 3.3. Motion Selection and Planning

For hardware deployment, a planning stack determines which action to execute and provides the goal to the policy.
- **State Estimation and Prediction:** An OptiTrack motion-capture system tracks the dynamic object (e.g., ball, box). A Kalman filter estimates the object's position and velocity, which are then propagated forward using a hybrid rigid-body model (ballistic flight with bounces) to predict the object's trajectory.
- **Action Selection:** For each incoming object trajectory, the system evaluates all candidate actions in the action library. It selects the action ($A_{i^*}$) whose nominal contact point ($p^*_{i^*}$) best matches the predicted object trajectory, identifying the time ($\hat{t}_{i^*}$) of closest approach.
- **Triggering:** To prevent oscillations in action choice, once the predicted time-to-contact drops below the selected motion's lead time, the action is locked and initiated. The spatial target continues to update until contact. The velocity direction and orientation for the target are chosen heuristically.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates TaskNPoint across various dynamic loco-manipulation tasks using a Unitree G1 humanoid robot in both simulation and hardware. The results aim to demonstrate the framework's effectiveness in task space coverage, robustness to novel scenarios, and hardware deployment without extensive tuning.

#### 4.1. Task Space Coverage

- **Abstraction Efficacy:** TaskNPoint's motion abstraction allows a large task space to be covered with a limited number of actions. The task space is defined as a 3D volume centered around the demonstration's contact point, in contrast to previous approaches that might constrain it to a heuristic plane.
- **Simulation Performance:** In simulation, the approach achieves a Generalized Success Rate (GSR) of 93% for ballistic hitting (tennis) and 98% for box pick-and-place. These figures are based on randomized initial conditions for trajectories and box positions, using metrics consistent with comparable works. This indicates that the policy is robust to variations in 3D target locations.
- **Data Efficiency:** Successful actions over this 3D motion volume are achieved from fewer than 10 human demonstrations, without the need for extensive data augmentations or reward tuning, differentiating it from many current methods.

#### 4.2. Hardware Results

- **Deployment Platform:** The controller was deployed on a 27-DOF Unitree G1 humanoid robot, with the policy running onboard and OptiTrack motion capture data fed via ethernet at 50Hz.
- **Task Performance:** The robot successfully returned balls thrown and kicked at speeds up to 8 m/s and at lateral distances up to 2m, and performed pick-and-place tasks for boxes placed randomly within a 3m radius.
- **Success Rates (Table 2):**
 - **Slow Targets (0-4 m/s):** 100% success rate for tennis and soccer, and 60% for box pick-and-place. The box pick-and-place failures were attributed to imperfect grasps, not general motion failure.
 - **Fast Targets (4-8 m/s):** 45% success rate for tennis and 70% for soccer.
- **Robustness:** The robot demonstrates graceful degradation in performance with increasing ball speed. Even when missing a target, the robot remained standing, tolerated pushes, and adapted to varied room and lighting conditions. Failures on fast targets were primarily traced to inaccuracies in the perception stack (estimation of ball trajectories), rather than limitations of the learned policy itself.

#### 4.3. Generalizing to Novel Tasks

- **Box Pick-and-Place:** The framework's abstraction of motion as a 3D conditioning point and contact time is shown to be generalizable beyond sports tasks. Its application to a box pickup task, where boxes are placed in novel locations, further validates this generality.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Parameter Robustness:** Ablation studies on parameters such as phase density (length of interaction window reward), target nominal distance, target standard deviation, and motion retiming indicate that the policy is robust to considerable variations.
- **Reward Density:** Even with a sparse, single-frame reward for goal achievement, the system exhibits dense behavior around the target phase, suggesting robustness in hit timing (Figure 9).

#### 4.5. Training on In-the-Wild Data

- **Scalability:** The ability of TaskNPoint to handle a larger repertoire of motions was tested by retraining the policy on 34 distinct motions derived from MLE-estimated human poses from in-the-wild multi-view tennis data.
- **Performance with Increased Complexity:** An overall success rate of 91% was achieved for 34 motions, with an average target position error of 4.9cm. This indicates that the method scales effectively to more complex motion libraries without significant loss in quality.

In summary, TaskNPoint demonstrates that dynamic skills can be taught to humanoids with minimal human demonstration and computational resources. It achieves high generalization across target locations and robust hardware deployment, making it a competitive approach against methods requiring more data or extensive tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TaskNPoint presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robot learning by proposing a novel abstraction methodology and training pipeline for dynamic skills. The work's significance lies in its departure from traditional robot learning paradigms by explicitly adopting a "coach-learner" division of labor inspired by human learning.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Data Efficiency:** The approach demonstrates the ability to teach complex dynamic loco-manipulation skills (such as tennis, soccer, and object manipulation) from single, short human video demonstrations. This substantially reduces the amount of human expert data required, addressing a critical bottleneck in robot learning.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Policies are trained in under an hour on a single GPU, indicating a high level of computational efficiency compared to methods relying on massive simulations or large datasets.
- **Zero-Shot Generalization:** By employing randomized target sampling during training, TaskNPoint enables learned skills to generalize zero-shot to unseen goal locations and novel incoming trajectories, enhancing the practical applicability of the learned policies.
- **Reduced Reward Engineering:** The framework largely eliminates the need for extensive, per-task reward tuning, simplifying the policy development process and reducing human intervention.
- **Robustness in Dynamic Environments:** The methodology yields policies that are robust to uncertainties and perturbations, allowing the robot to fail gracefully (e.g., miss a ball but remain standing) and tolerate varied environmental conditions during hardware deployment.
- **Unified Abstraction:** The abstraction of dynamic motions as spatio-temporal goals (defined by 3D conditioning points and contact times) proves generalizable across diverse tasks, from hitting balls to picking up boxes.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Accelerated Robot Skill Acquisition:** TaskNPoint offers a scalable and robust paradigm that could significantly accelerate the process of teaching diverse and complex dynamic actions to humanoid robots. This could make advanced robotic capabilities more accessible to a wider range of researchers and applications.
- **Broader Applicability:** The ability to learn from minimal demonstrations and generalize to new targets opens doors for humanoids to perform a wider array of tasks in unstructured and dynamic environments, including complex sports, service robotics, and manufacturing.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework provides a strong foundation for future research. The authors highlight areas such as approaching environmental conditioning for motion and target selection, and developing more targeted modeling of key interactions (e.g., incorporating explicit models of ball-racket interaction).
- **Addressing Limitations:** The paper acknowledges limitations, such as the current lack of force feedback in training and the assumption of ground-truth estimator accuracy. Future work could integrate force feedback into the learning loop or model the perception stack during training to enhance robustness against real-world sensing errors. Relaxing the hierarchical abstraction to allow the policy to learn from direct interaction with objects is also a potential direction.

In conclusion, TaskNPoint presents a principled and efficient approach to teaching dynamic skills to humanoid robots, bridging the gap between data-intensive and heavily tuned methods. Its potential to enable rapid and generalized skill acquisition could significantly advance the capabilities and deployment of humanoid robots in complex, real-world scenarios.
