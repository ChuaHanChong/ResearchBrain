# Revisiting Reward Design and Evaluation for Robust Humanoid Standing and Walking

> [!summary] Summary
> The Dynamic Robotics and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory at Oregon State University developed a quantitative and reproducible real-world benchmarking framework for humanoid standing and walking. They also proposed and evaluated a reinforcement learning controller trained with a minimally constraining reward function, which achieved perfect disturbance rejection and superior in-place rotation accuracy on the Digit humanoid robot compared to a clock-based RL controller and a manufacturer-supplied controller.

## Problem

- Humanoid standing and walking control lacks standardized, repeatable, and quantitative real-world evaluation methods, hindering objective comparison and progress.
- Current sim-to-real reinforcement learning approaches for locomotion often use highly prescriptive reward functions that are complex to design, brittle, and limit adaptability to disturbances.
- Integrating standing capabilities into walking controllers frequently requires separate designs or complex heuristics, as distinct state spaces complicate unified control.

## Method

- Introduced a low-cost, quantitative, and ==reproducible real-world benchmarking framework== with five key metrics to evaluate humanoid standing and walking performance.
- Developed a unified ==reinforcement learning controller==, trained using **PPO** and ==domain randomization==, capable of both standing and walking without separate policies.
- Designed a ==minimally constraining reward function==, notably avoiding clock-based inputs or reference trajectories, focusing on high-level objectives like single foot contact for locomotion.

## Results

- The refined "Single Contact++ RL" controller achieved **perfect disturbance rejection** against all impulses tested (up to **258 N @ 500 ms**) on the Digit humanoid robot.
- RL controllers demonstrated significantly lower lateral drift and angular error during in-place rotation compared to the manufacturer-supplied Agility Controller.
- The manufacturer-supplied Agility Controller showed higher energy efficiency compared to the learned reinforcement learning controllers, which appeared "stompy."

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Systematic, quantitative real-world benchmarking is essential for identifying subtle controller weaknesses and iteratively improving reinforcement learning policies for robotics.
> - Minimally constraining reward functions can enable reinforcement learning algorithms to discover more robust and adaptable behaviors for humanoid locomotion than highly prescriptive designs.
> - Quantitative evaluation reveals important performance trade-offs, such as learned policies excelling in disturbance rejection and rotational accuracy but potentially having lower energy efficiency compared to model-based controllers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust standing and walking (SaW) capabilities is a foundational challenge in humanoid robotics. Unlike wheeled robots that possess inherent stability, bipedal humanoids are intrinsically unstable, making even basic locomotion a complex control problem. Historically, model-based control approaches (e.g.,) have been employed, but recent advancements have seen a significant shift towards data-driven methods, particularly **sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL)**. This approach trains controllers in simulation, leveraging domain randomization to facilitate transfer to the physical robot.

Despite impressive video demonstrations from various sim-to-real RL approaches (e.g.,), the broader research landscape suffers from several critical shortcomings that this paper directly addresses:

- **Lack of Repeatable Quantitative Evaluation:** A major impediment to progress in bipedal locomotion is the absence of standardized, repeatable, and quantitative real-world evaluation metrics. Most prior works rely on qualitative video demonstrations or non-reproducible human-applied disturbances. This makes it challenging to objectively compare different methods, understand their real-world trade-offs, and gauge actual progress. The current paper aims to fill this gap by proposing a low-cost benchmarking procedure.
- **Reward Function Design Challenges:** In sim-to-real RL, the design of effective reward functions is paramount. However, many existing approaches utilize highly prescriptive reward functions (e.g., clock-based rewards, reference-motion imitation,). While these can yield impressive behaviors, they are often expensive to design, brittle, require extensive tuning for different robots or behaviors, and may impose unnecessary constraints. For instance, handling unexpected disturbances might require the robot to deviate significantly from a "desired" reference trajectory, penalizing potentially life-saving recovery steps. Moreover, integrating standing capabilities into walking controllers often involves separate designs or complex heuristics for smooth transitions, as distinct state spaces cause complications. This paper challenges this paradigm by exploring a "minimally constraining" reward function and a single controller for both standing and walking.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap:** While RL offers powerful learning capabilities, the transition from simulation to the real world (sim-to-real transfer) remains a persistent challenge. Inconsistencies between simulated and real physics can lead to unexpected behaviors or performance degradation, as evidenced by the velocity overshooting observed in their initial controller. Robust evaluation methods are essential to identify and mitigate this gap.

By providing a much-needed framework for quantitative benchmarking and by exploring an alternative, less constrained approach to reward design, this work directly contributes to improving the rigor and efficiency of research in humanoid locomotion. It shifts the focus from "impressive demonstrations" to "measurable performance," fostering a more scientific and systematic development cycle for advanced humanoid controllers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper is driven by two fundamental problems in the field of humanoid standing and walking (SaW) control: the lack of systematic evaluation and the potential suboptimality of current reward function designs. Its key objectives are directly motivated by these challenges:

1. **To Propose a Quantitative and Reproducible SaW Benchmarking Method:** The primary motivation is to address the critical gap in evaluating real-world performance of SaW controllers. Prior works largely rely on qualitative video demonstrations or non-repeatable human-induced disturbances, which hinders objective comparison and understanding of controller trade-offs. The authors aim to introduce a low-cost, quantitative benchmarking procedure that allows for repeatable experiments to measure metrics such as disturbance recovery, command following accuracy, and energy efficiency. This objective is crucial for enabling measurable and sustained progress in the field.

2. **To Revisit and Construct a Minimally Constraining Reward Function for SaW Controllers:** Current RL approaches for bipedal locomotion often employ highly prescriptive reward functions that explicitly dictate preferred gait characteristics (e.g., periodic clock signals, reference trajectories). The authors hypothesize that such constraints might be counter-productive, limiting the robot's ability to flexibly adapt to disturbances or seamlessly transition between behaviors. Their motivation is to design a reward function that is "minimally constraining," allowing the reinforcement learning algorithm greater freedom to discover robust and adaptable behaviors without hand-engineered dictates that might conflict with real-world exigencies like disturbance rejection. This also aims to enable a single controller to natively handle both standing and walking, avoiding the complexities of switching between separate controllers.

3. **To Experimentally Verify the Utility of the Benchmarking Framework and Compare Controllers:** A key objective is to demonstrate that their proposed benchmarking framework can effectively identify weaknesses in controllers and guide targeted improvements. They also aim to use this framework to rigorously evaluate and compare their newly designed, minimally-constrained controller against a manufacturer-supplied controller and a state-of-the-art clock-based RL controller on the Digit humanoid robot. This objective serves to validate the utility of their proposed methodology and provide concrete, quantitative insights into the performance trade-offs among different control paradigms.

In essence, the paper aims to lay the groundwork for a more rigorous, data-driven, and iterative approach to developing humanoid locomotion controllers, shifting the paradigm from ad-hoc tuning and qualitative assessment to systematic experimentation and quantitative improvement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research is two-fold: first, the development of a novel quantitative benchmarking procedure, and second, the design and training of a new reinforcement learning-based SaW controller with a minimally constraining reward function.

#### 3.1. Quantitative SaW Performance Benchmark

The authors propose a set of five metrics designed to be low-cost, reproducible, and easily implementable:

- **Disturbance Rejection (Metric 1: Standing Fall Percentage):** To standardize disturbance testing, a custom impulse application device is introduced. This device uses a weight suspended by magnets, released for a precisely controlled duration (∆t), generating a fixed-magnitude force (F) on the robot's base. This allows for repeatable application of impulses in various directions (limited to X and Y for standing tests). The metric is the percentage of successful trials (robot does not fall) over multiple attempts for a given impulse (F, ∆t).
- **Command Following (Metrics 2 & 3: In-Place Rotation Accuracy):** To assess control precision without expensive motion capture systems, two metrics are proposed for in-place rotation:
 - **Angular Error:** The difference between the commanded angular rotation (based on angular velocity ωz and duration ∆t) and the actual rotation.
 - **Lateral Drift:** The distance of the furthest foot from a predefined starting circle after rotation, measuring positional accuracy.
- **Command Following (Metric 4: Velocity Accuracy):** For linear locomotion, the robot is commanded to walk at a constant velocity (v) for a specific duration (∆t). The actual distance traveled is manually measured and compared to the commanded distance (d_c = v · ∆t). This metric assumes symmetrical acceleration and deceleration phases to simplify measurement.
- **Energy Efficiency (Metric 5: Energy Efficiency):** Energy consumption is approximated by computing the work done by the actuators (W = ∫ τ_t · ω_t dt) using the robot’s estimated motor torques and angular velocities. The metric is reported as estimated average energy usage per meter traveled during walking, normalizing for different travel distances. Standing measurements are omitted due to significant motor losses not accounted for by this approximation.

#### 3.2. SaW Training and Reward Design

The core of their control approach is a **single, unified controller** for both standing and walking, trained using sim-to-real reinforcement learning.

- **Controller Architecture:** A (64, 64) two-layer Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent neural network serves as the policy.
- **Inputs:** The network receives the current robot state (motor/joint positions and velocities, torso orientation) and a user command vector `c_u = [c_x, c_y, c_yaw]` specifying desired linear and angular velocities (where `` signifies standing).
- **Output:** The controller outputs joint-space PD setpoints for all 20 actuators, operating at 50Hz, with PD controllers running at 2kHz.
- **Training Framework:**
 - **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used, augmented with a mirror loss to encourage symmetric policy behavior.
 - **Domain Randomization:** Standard techniques similar to prior work are applied to improve sim-to-real transfer.
 - **Episodic Training:** Each episode lasts 16 simulated seconds or terminates upon robot fall. User commands are uniformly sampled from five categories (standing, sagittal walk, lateral walk, in-place rotation, omnidirectional walk) every 2-6 seconds.
 - **Simulated Disturbances:** Random pushes (200N-800N, 20ms) are applied with a 1% chance per frame during training to foster disturbance rejection.
- **Minimally Constraining Reward Design:** The authors systematically construct a reward function by progressively adding terms based on observed intermediate policy behaviors, aiming to avoid over-prescription:
 - **Basic Command Following:** Rewards for matching linear and angular velocities/orientations. Initially, this led to undesirable "hopping" behavior.
 - **Single Foot Contact:** This crucial term, applied for non-standing commands, rewards 1 if only one foot is in contact (with a 0.2-second grace period for stance/swing overlap). For standing, it is a constant 1, implicitly rewarding stable two-foot contact without penalizing necessary recovery steps. This was found to be the most reliable way to induce walking instead of hopping.
 - **Sim-to-Real and Style Terms:** Additional terms are included to improve visual properties and, more importantly, stability during sim-to-real transfer, and to guide the RL towards acceptable solutions:
 - `base height`: Maintain consistent torso height.
 - `feet air time`: Regularize stepping frequency by penalizing touchdown but rewarding time in air.
 - `feet orientation`: Encourage straight foot orientation (except when rotating).
 - `feet position`: Loosely constrain foot positions during stance.
 - `arm position`: Prevent unwanted arm movements while allowing for balancing.
 - `base acceleration`: Reduce jerky base motions.
 - `action difference`: Promote smooth action transitions.
 - `torque`: Minimize energy consumption.
 - **No Clocks:** A key design decision is to explicitly avoid clock-based inputs or reference trajectories, which simplifies transitions between standing and walking and provides the policy maximum freedom for disturbance rejection.

#### 3.3. Comparative Evaluation

The proposed benchmark is used to evaluate three controllers on the Digit humanoid robot:
1. **Single Contact RL:** Their initial controller trained with the minimally-constrained reward function.
2. **Clock Based RL:** A state-of-the-art controller using a clock-based reward function.
3. **Agility Controller:** The manufacturer-provided controller.

Following initial evaluation, the authors demonstrated the iterative utility of their benchmark by training **Single Contact++ RL**, an improved version of their controller. This involved adjusting the simulated disturbance distribution (closer to real-world test ranges) and increasing the duration of command windows during training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation on the Digit V3 humanoid robot yielded several significant findings:

#### 4.1. Disturbance Rejection While Standing (Metric 1)

- **Initial Comparison:** The initial **Single Contact RL** controller generally outperformed the **Clock Based RL** controller in disturbance rejection, particularly in the x-direction (forward/backward pushes).
- **Unexpected Behaviors:** The **Single Contact RL** showed unexpected asymmetries and non-monotonic behavior in the y-direction (lateral pushes). For instance, in some cases, a higher impulse push was handled more easily than a lower one. This surprising result underscored the crucial need for systematic and quantitative real-world testing, as such inconsistencies would likely go unnoticed in qualitative demonstrations.
- **Benchmark-Guided Improvement (Single Contact++ RL):** The identified weaknesses motivated the training of **Single Contact++ RL**. By adjusting the range and duration of random pushes in simulation training to better match the real-world test distribution (e.g., [20N, 200N] force and [200ms, 500ms] duration uniformly across dimensions), and by increasing the duration of command windows, the improved controller achieved **perfect disturbance rejection** for all tested impulses, including the maximum force and duration the pulling device could deliver (258 N @ 500 ms). This strongly validates the utility of the proposed benchmarking approach for iteratively guiding policy improvements.

#### 4.2. Command Following (Metrics 2, 3, & 4)

- **In-Place Rotation Accuracy (Metrics 2 & 3):**
 - **Lateral Drift:** Both RL controllers (**Single Contact RL** and **Clock Based RL**) exhibited substantially lower lateral drift during in-place rotation compared to the model-based **Agility Controller**. The Agility Controller showed significant drift, up to 2 meters in a 30-second test, highlighting a weakness in its rotational precision.
 - **Angular Error:** The RL controllers also demonstrated much lower angular error, indicating higher accuracy in achieving desired rotations compared to the Agility Controller. This is attributed to architectural differences, where their controller directly tracks a rotating heading.
 - **Single Contact++ RL's Superiority:** The **Single Contact++ RL** controller consistently performed best across all rotation tests, with minimal drift and angular error. This improvement is hypothesized to stem from the longer command windows used during its training, which likely encouraged the policy to minimize cumulative drift over extended periods.
 - **Clock-Based Controller Anomaly:** Interestingly, the **Clock Based RL** had the largest drift for the 1-second rotation test, which the authors attribute to sudden changes in clock signals during the transition from standing to walking mode.
- **Velocity Accuracy (Metric 4):**
 - **Agility Controller:** Undershot the target 1 m/s velocity, achieving an average of 0.74 m/s.
 - **Clock Based RL:** Was the most accurate, closely tracking the commanded 1 m/s at 1.04 m/s.
 - **Single Contact RL:** Significantly overshot the commanded velocity at 1.13 m/s. This revealed a **sim-to-real inconsistency**, as this overshooting was not observed in simulation. The authors suggest that the explicit constraints imposed by clock-based rewards might inadvertently aid in better sim-to-real transfer for velocity tracking, warranting further investigation.
 - *(Note: Velocity and energy metrics for Single Contact++ RL were not recorded due to hardware failure close to submission.)*

#### 4.3. Energy Efficiency (Metric 5)

- The **Agility Controller** was found to be more energy-efficient than both RL controllers, even after normalizing for distance traveled. Visually, the RL controllers appeared "stompy," suggesting higher impact forces.
- The authors hypothesize that the higher energy usage in RL controllers might be due to them anticipating disturbances, leading to more forceful and impactful ground contacts. This finding suggests a clear direction for future improvements in reward function design, specifically emphasizing the minimization of ground contact forces for better energy efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper makes several significant contributions that have the potential to impact the field of humanoid robotics:

1. **Pioneering Standardized Real-World Benchmarking:** The most impactful contribution is the proposal and demonstration of a low-cost, quantitative, and repeatable benchmarking framework for humanoid Standing and Walking (SaW) controllers. This addresses a critical void in the field, where progress has often been demonstrated through qualitative videos or non-reproducible experiments. By providing clear metrics for disturbance rejection, command following, and energy efficiency, this work enables:
 - **Objective Comparison:** Researchers can now objectively compare different control methods and reward designs, fostering healthy competition and clearer understanding of trade-offs.
 - **Measurable Progress:** Future work can demonstrate tangible, quantitative improvements, accelerating the development cycle for robust humanoid locomotion.
 - **Reproducibility:** The simple, open-sourced design of the impulse application device democratizes rigorous testing, allowing more labs to conduct comparable experiments.

2. **Validation of Minimal Reward Design Philosophy:** The paper successfully demonstrates that a "minimally constraining" reward function, devoid of explicit clock signals or complex reference trajectories, can lead to highly robust and adaptable humanoid controllers. This challenges the prevalent paradigm of highly prescriptive reward designs in RL for locomotion. The success of the Single Contact++ RL in handling strong disturbances suggests that providing the learning algorithm with more freedom, guided by broad behavioral objectives, can yield superior real-world robustness. This simplifies reward engineering and potentially generalizes better to unforeseen scenarios.

3. **Guiding Iterative Controller Improvement:** The research exemplifies an iterative development cycle where initial benchmark results clearly highlight weaknesses (e.g., asymmetric disturbance rejection in Single Contact RL). These insights then directly inform targeted modifications to the training process (e.g., adjusting simulated disturbance distributions, longer command windows), leading to measurable and significant improvements (Single Contact++ RL). This "benchmark-guided improvement" paradigm is crucial for practical robot development.

4. **Revealing Practical Trade-offs and Future Research Directions:** The quantitative comparisons between their RL controllers, a clock-based RL controller, and a manufacturer-provided controller uncover important trade-offs:
 - While RL excels in drift-free rotation and disturbance rejection, it currently lags in energy efficiency compared to the manufacturer's controller.
 - The sim-to-real gap for velocity control (overshooting in Single Contact RL) suggests areas for future domain randomization or reward function refinement.
 - The "stompy" nature and higher energy consumption of learned policies provide concrete motivation for adding new reward terms (e.g., emphasizing smaller ground contact forces, motion smoothness) in future iterations.

In conclusion, this paper does more than just present a new humanoid controller; it provides foundational tools and methodologies that can fundamentally change how humanoid locomotion research is conducted. By emphasizing systematic, quantitative real-world evaluation and encouraging a re-evaluation of prescriptive reward design, the authors pave the way for a more robust, efficient, and scientifically rigorous path towards truly capable humanoid robots in diverse real-world applications.
