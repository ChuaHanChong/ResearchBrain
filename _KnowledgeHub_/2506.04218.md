# Pseudo-Simulation for Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> Pseudo-simulation, a novel evaluation paradigm for autonomous driving, combines real-world data with pre-generated synthetic observations to assess AV robustness and error recovery. This two-stage approach achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.89 with closed-loop simulation while reducing planner inferences by 6 times compared to standard interactive methods.

## Problem

- Real-world AV testing is costly, risky, and lacks reproducibility for large-scale evaluation.
- Closed-loop simulators struggle with visual realism and scenario diversity, being computationally intensive for vision-based end-to-end AV systems.
- Open-loop evaluation is scalable but fails to account for distribution shifts, compounding errors, or causal confusion as it operates under expert-aligned conditions.

## Method

- A two-stage ==pseudo-simulation== process is introduced, using an initial real-world observation followed by pre-generated synthetic observations for subsequent steps.
- High-fidelity synthetic camera inputs are generated for sampled viewpoints using a modified ==Multi-Traversal Gaussian Splatting (MTGS)== neural rendering approach.
- Performance is evaluated using an ==Extended PDM Score (EPDMS)== that includes penalties for rule violations and weighted subscores, aggregated across stages using a Gaussian-weighted average for synthetic observations and multiplicative combination for the final score.

## Results

- **Pseudo-simulation** showed a strong Pearson correlation (**r=0.89**) and R^2 value (**0.8**) with closed-loop simulation results, significantly outperforming traditional open-loop metrics.
- The method required approximately **6 times** fewer planner inferences per scenario compared to standard closed-loop simulation, demonstrating substantial computational efficiency.
- Analysis on the NAVSIM v2 leaderboard revealed that planners exhibit a general performance drop from real to synthetic observations, confirming pseudo-simulation's ability to expose sensitivity to distribution shifts and new failure modes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evaluating AVs on systematically perturbed, synthetically rendered future states serves as a robust and efficient proxy for complex closed-loop behaviors.
> - Gaussian weighting for aggregating scores from synthetic observations and multiplicative combination of stage scores are critical for high correlation with closed-loop simulations.
> - Despite some perception degradation on synthetic views, the rendered data maintains sufficient fidelity to preserve critical information needed for planning, enabling accurate performance assessment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust and reliable autonomous driving systems necessitates rigorous and comprehensive evaluation methods. Existing paradigms for evaluating Autonomous Vehicles (AVs) typically fall into two main categories: closed-loop and open-loop evaluation, with real-world testing representing a specialized form of closed-loop evaluation.

Real-world closed-loop evaluation, while providing definitive feedback on system performance, is constrained by high costs, safety risks, and a lack of reproducibility. These limitations restrict its utility for large-scale benchmarking required to demonstrate system robustness. To mitigate these issues, closed-loop evaluation is frequently conducted within simulation environments. Simulators offer controlled scenario generation, reproducibility, and structured metrics, facilitating rapid iteration. However, graphics-based simulators often struggle with achieving sufficient visual realism and behavioral diversity, particularly for vision-based end-to-end AV systems. Manual construction by 3D artists limits the complexity and range of scenarios, and the inherently sequential nature of simulation-based evaluation is computationally intensive, requiring frequent, correlated frame sequences. Data-driven planning simulators (e.g., nuPlan, Waymax, Nocturne, GPUDrive) have improved scalability by leveraging recorded traffic data but generally operate at the trajectory level, without directly supporting sensor-based agents. Efforts to bridge this gap through data-driven sensor simulation using image-based rendering or neural rendering face challenges related to photorealism and runtime efficiency.

In contrast, open-loop evaluation assesses planning performance by comparing predicted trajectories against expert demonstrations in pre-recorded datasets. This approach utilizes real sensor data, avoiding the complexities of interactive simulation, which makes it scalable and efficient. However, open-loop methods operate under expert-aligned conditions, meaning they do not account for distribution shifts that occur when an AV deviates from the demonstrated path. Consequently, they do not test the AV's ability to recover from compounding errors or address causal confusion—situations where the AV's actions lead to unexpected future states. Metrics commonly used in open-loop evaluation, such as average displacement error (ADE), often correlate poorly with real-world performance and can favor trivial baselines. Prior work like NAVSIM v1 offered a structured open-loop benchmarking framework with simulation-based metrics but was still limited to expert-aligned initial observations.

This paper introduces "pseudo-simulation" as a novel evaluation paradigm designed to integrate the scalability and data-driven nature of open-loop evaluation with the comprehensive assessment of robustness and error recovery traditionally associated with closed-loop testing. It aims to address the limitations of existing methods by operating on real datasets augmented with pre-generated synthetic observations, thereby approximating potential future states without the computational overhead and realism challenges of online interactive simulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to propose and validate pseudo-simulation as an efficient and comprehensive evaluation paradigm for autonomous driving systems. The core motivations stem from the inherent limitations of existing evaluation methods:

- **Addressing Limitations of Real-World Evaluation:** Real-world testing is costly, inherently risky due to safety concerns, and often lacks reproducibility, which hinders its utility for iterative development and large-scale benchmarking required to prove system robustness.
- **Improving Realism and Efficiency of Closed-Loop Simulation:** Current closed-loop simulators, particularly graphics-based ones, struggle to achieve sufficient visual realism and scenario diversity needed for vision-based end-to-end AV systems. They are also computationally intensive and sequential, making them slow for large-scale evaluation. The manual construction of scenarios further limits their scope. The objective is to achieve the benefits of closed-loop testing, such as assessing compounding errors and causal confusion, without requiring computationally expensive, online interactive simulation.
- **Overcoming Deficiencies of Open-Loop Evaluation:** While efficient and scalable, open-loop evaluation primarily assesses performance under expert-aligned conditions. It fails to account for distribution shifts that occur when an AV deviates from the expert's path, and thus does not evaluate the AV's ability to recover from errors or mitigate causal confusion. The motivation is to introduce a mechanism within a data-driven framework that probes these recovery capabilities and robustness to minor perturbations.
- **Combining Strengths of Existing Paradigms:** The work is motivated by the desire to merge the scalability and real-data foundation of open-loop evaluation with the comprehensive, robustness-testing aspects of closed-loop evaluation. This means leveraging real-world data while introducing systematic perturbations to evaluate how an AV reacts to deviations from its intended path.
- **Enabling Standardized and Efficient Benchmarking:** A key motivation is to facilitate more efficient experimentation cycles for AV developers and to encourage the prioritization of closed-loop robustness in model design. This includes establishing a standardized benchmark (NAVSIM v2) with a public leaderboard to foster community-wide, consistent evaluation of new methodologies.

In summary, the objective is to create an evaluation method that is scalable, data-driven, and capable of identifying failure modes related to compounding errors and causal confusion, while being more efficient and reproducible than real-world testing and more comprehensive than traditional open-loop evaluation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Pseudo-simulation operates as a two-stage evaluation process, integrating real and pre-rendered synthetic data.

#### 3.1. Stage 1: Initial Observations

The first stage involves evaluating the AV's performance based on an initial real-world observation from a test dataset.
- **Planning Task:** The AV generates a 4-second trajectory (sequence of waypoints) based on multi-view camera images, ego status features (velocity, motion history), and a driving command (e.g., left, straight, right).
- **BEV Simulation:** The predicted trajectory is executed using a kinematic bicycle model and an LQR controller at 10Hz for a 4-second horizon. Crucially, this simulation incorporates reactive traffic: background vehicles respond to the ego agent using a rule-based Intelligent Driver Model (IDM), while pedestrians and static obstacles follow recorded trajectories.
- **Extended PDM Score (EPDMS):** Performance is measured using a metric that combines multiplicative penalties for rule violations and a weighted average of several subscores. Penalty terms include No At-fault Collisions (NC), Drivable Area Compliance (DAC), Driving Direction Compliance (DDC), and Traffic Light Compliance (TLC). Weighted average terms include Time to Collision (TTC), Ego Progress (EP), Lane Keeping (LK), History Comfort (HC), and Extended Comfort (EC). A novel filtering mechanism is introduced: if a rule violation is also committed by the human expert driver in the same scene, the penalty is disregarded, aiming to avoid penalizing contextually justified maneuvers or label noise.

#### 3.2. Stage 2: Synthetic Observations

In the second stage, the AV's behavior is inferred on pre-generated synthetic observations. The scoring pipeline from Stage 1 is repeated for each synthetic observation, and these scores are aggregated based on their proximity to the Stage 1 outcome. This stage aims to evaluate robustness to variations near the intended path.
- **Scenario Pre-generation:** Synthetic observations are generated as a data pre-processing step, independent of any specific planner's Stage 1 output.
 - **Start Point Sampling:** Viewpoints are sampled around the human expert driver's observed endpoint (after 4 seconds in the scene). This sampling covers a region laterally (every 0.5m up to 2.0m) and longitudinally (every 5.0m, spanning physically plausible stopping to maximum reachable distances given accelerations). This results in a varying number of potential states per scenario.
 - **Heading and History Generation:** For each sampled start point, a plausible heading and motion history are generated by matching to the nearest trajectory in a human driving dataset. Filtering criteria are applied to discard candidate trajectories with significant deviations in velocity, acceleration, or heading.
 - **Rejection Sampling:** Remaining start points that violate EPDMS multiplicative constraints (NC, DAC, DDC, TLC) are removed. Scenes are discarded if fewer than five valid synthetic observations remain.
- **Neural Reconstruction and Rendering:** To generate high-fidelity synthetic camera inputs for the sampled viewpoints, a modified version of Multi-Traversal Gaussian Splatting (MTGS) is employed. Unlike standard MTGS, which uses multiple nearby driving traversals, this variant uses only a single traversal, expanding the usable data pool. Accurate initial camera pose estimates are obtained via LiDAR registration and bundle adjustment, followed by camera pose optimization during MTGS training. Scenes with significant sensor failures or low visual quality after reconstruction are filtered.
- **Score Aggregation:** The Stage 2 synthetic observations are scored using EPDMS, yielding a set of scores {s_i^2}. These are combined with the Stage 1 score (s_1) to produce a final combined score (s_combined). The aggregation function A_1 combines s_1 and an aggregated Stage 2 result s_2 multiplicatively (s_combined = s_1 * s_2). The function A_2 aggregates the individual Stage 2 scores {s_i^2} using a Gaussian-weighted average, where weights (w_i) are inversely proportional to the squared Euclidean distance between the i-th Stage 2 start point (x_i) and the ego agent's Stage 1 endpoint (x̂), with a kernel variance (σ^2). This weighting prioritizes synthetic futures that are closer to the AV's initially predicted path.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented three key findings regarding pseudo-simulation: its correlation with closed-loop evaluation, new insights from a public leaderboard, and the fidelity of its neural rendering.

#### 4.1. Alignment with Closed-Loop Evaluation

- **Strong Correlation:** Pseudo-simulation demonstrated a strong correlation with closed-loop simulation results obtained from the nuPlan simulator, which supports reactive rollouts for privileged planners. For a diverse set of 83 planners (37 rule-based, 46 learned), pseudo-simulation achieved a Pearson correlation coefficient (r) of 0.89 and an R^2 value of 0.8. This correlation was particularly evident among learned planners.
- **Comparison to Open-Loop Metrics:** In contrast, traditional open-loop evaluation metrics, such as average displacement error (ADE) and nuPlan's open-loop score (OLS), showed significantly lower correlation with closed-loop scores (R^2 = 0.7 for the best open-loop approach in a direct comparison, and nearly no correlation in broader contexts as reported by prior work).
- **Two-Stage vs. Single-Stage:** The proposed two-stage pseudo-simulation setup (2x4s) achieved higher alignment (r = 0.89, R^2 = 0.8) compared to single-stage open-loop baselines (1x8s: r = 0.83, R^2 = 0.7; 1x4s: lower). This indicates that the introduction of synthetic observations and multi-stage evaluation is effective in capturing closed-loop behaviors.
- **Exposure of Failure Modes:** Pseudo-simulation exposed a wider range of potential failures and typically resulted in lower average EPDMS values compared to closed-loop scores (CLS), suggesting its ability to reveal edge cases that might be missed during standard testing.
- **Impact of Weighting and Aggregation:**
 - **Gaussian Weighting:** An empirical study on the Gaussian weighting in Stage 2 indicated that smaller kernel variances (σ^2 = 0.05 and 0.1) yielded the highest correlations with closed-loop scores. Simple averaging, k-nearest neighbors (k-NN), and hybrid k-NN/Gaussian weighting were less effective.
 - **Multiplicative Aggregation:** For combining Stage 1 and Stage 2 scores, multiplicative aggregation showed higher linear and rank correlation than arithmetic mean or hybrid strategies. This was attributed to the binary nature of many EPDMS subscores, making multiplication a suitable method for representing compounding failures over two segments.
- **Efficiency:** Despite its comprehensive nature, pseudo-simulation required significantly fewer planner inferences per scenario (13 on average) compared to standard closed-loop simulation in nuPlan (80 inferences for an 8-second rollout at 10Hz), representing a 6x reduction in environment interactions. Even with reduced synthetic observation density (e.g., 25%, approximately three Stage 2 observations per scene), the correlation to closed-loop scores remained above 0.85, demonstrating efficiency gains without substantial loss of reliability.

#### 4.2. New Challenges and Insights from the Leaderboard

- **NAVSIM v2 Leaderboard:** The authors established a public NAVSIM v2 leaderboard featuring challenging driving scenarios (e.g., unprotected turns, dense traffic) using a subset of nuPlan called 'navhard' (450 Stage 1 and 5462 Stage 2 observations). This aims to standardize evaluation and prevent inconsistencies.
- **Performance Drop in Stage 2:** Analysis of various planners (baselines and community submissions) on the navhard dataset showed a general drop in subscores from Stage 1 (original real observations) to Stage 2 (synthetic observations) for all methods. This indicates that planners are sensitive to the distribution shifts introduced by the synthetic perturbations, highlighting the value of pseudo-simulation in evaluating robustness.
- **Revealed Failure Modes:** The evaluation revealed specific failure modes previously overlooked. For instance, the PDM-Closed planner, a top-performing privileged planner, achieved the highest overall EPDMS but exhibited lower performance in comfort metrics (HC and EC). This suggests a trade-off in its design that pseudo-simulation effectively identified. A new baseline, NavFormer, employing a BEVFormer encoder, showed competitive performance against established image-only and privileged planners.

#### 4.3. Neural Rendering Fidelity

- **Downstream Task Performance:** To assess the visual fidelity of synthetic observations, an end-to-end planner (LTF model) trained only on real data was evaluated on real and synthetic 'navhard' views.
 - **Perception (mIoU):** A drop in mIoU for BEV segmentation (from 46.0 on real Stage 1 to 37.6 on synthetic Stage 1 views) was observed.
 - **Planning (EPDMS):** Despite the mIoU degradation, planning performance remained largely stable (EPDMS decreased slightly from 62.3 to 61.0). This suggests that the synthetic observations preserved critical information required for planning, even if semantic segmentation fidelity saw a reduction.
 - **Perturbed Inputs:** When evaluated on perturbed synthetic Stage 2 inputs, mIoU dropped marginally (37.6 to 36.9), but EPDMS declined substantially (61.0 to 44.2). This larger EPDMS drop was attributed primarily to the planner's sensitivity to distribution shifts from the perturbed states, rather than rendering artifacts causing perception inaccuracies.
- **Novel View Synthesis (NVS) Ablation:** An ablation study using the LPIPS metric showed that the authors' MTGS-based neural rendering variant with LiDAR registration, bundle adjustment, and pose optimization achieved the best perceptual similarity score (LPIPS of 0.253), outperforming a baseline (Street Gaussians, 0.354) and their own method without pose optimization (0.322). This quantitative result supports the claim of sufficient visual fidelity for planning evaluation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Pseudo-simulation represents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous driving by addressing fundamental limitations in existing evaluation paradigms.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Bridging the Gap:** It effectively bridges the gap between the scalability and real-world data foundation of open-loop evaluation and the comprehensive robustness assessment of closed-loop evaluation. This hybrid approach offers a more balanced trade-off between efficiency and evaluative depth.
- **Early Detection of Failure Modes:** By introducing systematic perturbations through pre-rendered synthetic observations, pseudo-simulation can expose an AV's sensitivity to distribution shifts and its ability to recover from compounding errors. This allows for the detection of critical failure modes that might be missed by open-loop methods and are costly or dangerous to find in real-world or traditional closed-loop simulations.
- **Quantitative Validation:** The strong correlation (R^2 = 0.8) with computationally expensive closed-loop simulations provides quantitative evidence for its effectiveness as a proxy for more complex interactive testing.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Operating with pre-generated synthetic data rather than online interactive rendering significantly reduces the computational cost and accelerates evaluation cycles, enabling AV developers to iterate on models more rapidly. A 6x improvement in efficiency compared to nuPlan's closed-loop simulation is a notable advantage.
- **Standardization and Benchmarking:** The release of the NAVSIM v2 framework and a public leaderboard on the 'navhard' dataset establishes a standardized testbed for the community. This facilitates consistent comparison of methodologies and promotes research focus on challenging scenarios.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Accelerated AV Development:** By providing a more efficient yet robust evaluation tool, pseudo-simulation can significantly accelerate the development and refinement of AV decision-making systems. Faster feedback loops enable quicker iteration on model improvements.
- **Enhanced System Robustness:** The method rigorously tests an AV's sensitivity to perturbations and its error recovery capabilities. This directly encourages the development of more robust AVs, better equipped to handle real-world uncertainties and deviations from ideal conditions.
- **Improved Safety:** By identifying and characterizing previously unknown failure modes and weaknesses in AV algorithms during the development phase, pseudo-simulation contributes to building safer autonomous systems before extensive real-world deployment.
- **Research Catalyst:** The public benchmark and the insights gained from comparing various planners can serve as a catalyst for new research directions, prompting developers to focus on aspects like error recovery, robustness to distribution shifts, and comfort metrics, which might have been deprioritized by traditional open-loop scores.
