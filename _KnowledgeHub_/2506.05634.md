# AutoQD: Automatic Discovery of Diverse Behaviors with Quality-Diversity Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper "AUTOQD" from the University of Southern California introduces an iterative method for automatically generating behavioral descriptors in Quality-Diversity optimization, which enables the discovery of diverse, high-performing policies in continuous control tasks. This approach leverages policy occupancy measures and random Fourier features to create stable behavioral representations, demonstrating superior performance in policy discovery and adaptability to environmental changes across various Gymnasium environments.

## Problem

- Traditional Quality-Diversity (QD) optimization relies on manually designed behavioral descriptors (BDs), requiring extensive domain expertise and limiting the scope of discoverable behaviors.
- Developing effective BDs becomes increasingly challenging with task complexity, creating a bottleneck for applying QD algorithms.
- Prior unsupervised behavior discovery methods often lack theoretical grounding for their representations or struggle with scalability when generating a large number of diverse policies.

## Method

- **AutoQD** efficiently embeds policy occupancy measures into a finite-dimensional vector space using Random Fourier Features (RFFs), where Euclidean distances reliably approximate Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between behaviors.
- An iterative algorithm alternates between discovering diverse policies using a QD optimizer (CMA-MAE) and refining low-dimensional behavioral descriptors via Calibrated Weighted PCA (cwPCA) applied to the archive of policy embeddings.
- The cwPCA dynamically weights policies by their fitness, ensuring descriptors focus on variations among high-quality behaviors, and calibrates projections to a fixed range for grid-based archives.

## Results

- AutoQD consistently achieved superior Ground-Truth QD Scores compared to five baselines in environments such as Ant, Swimmer, and BipedalWalker, often producing the most diverse policy populations.
- Generated policy populations demonstrated enhanced adaptability to environmental dynamics in BipedalWalker, achieving the highest Area Under the Curve for both friction (**1429.66**) and mass (**295.65**) changes.
- The automatically learned behavioral descriptors exhibited high stability, with variance often orders of magnitude lower than autoencoder-based methods, and maintained competitive performance even with as few as two rollouts per policy for BD estimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy occupancy measures provide a complete and theoretically sound characterization of policy behavior in fully-observable Markov Decision Processes.
> - Random Fourier Features offer a computationally tractable way to approximate kernel-based distances (MMD) between high-dimensional occupancy measures, enabling verifiable policy embeddings.
> - An adaptive and iterative refinement of behavioral descriptors, learned from the evolving archive of policies, facilitates open-ended discovery of meaningful diversity without requiring predefined human specifications.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) and evolutionary computation, has traditionally focused on optimizing for a single, often predefined, objective to find an optimal solution. However, this approach frequently overlooks the potential utility of diverse solutions, which could offer robustness or novel capabilities in varied contexts. Quality-Diversity (QD) optimization emerged to address this limitation by generating collections of solutions that are both high-performing and behaviorally diverse. QD has demonstrated success across various domains, including robot locomotion, game content generation, protein design, and image generation.

Within sequential decision-making tasks, the application of QD principles is known as Quality-Diversity Reinforcement Learning (QD-RL). Here, behavioral diversity is significant for two main reasons: it provides robustness against changing environmental conditions by offering alternative successful policies, and it is crucial for open-ended learning, where the objective extends beyond solving specific problems to continuously discovering new capabilities and behaviors.

A fundamental challenge in QD algorithms, particularly in QD-RL, is the reliance on hand-crafted behavioral descriptors (BDs). These descriptors are functions that map policies to low-dimensional vectors, characterizing their behavior (e.g., foot contact patterns for a bipedal robot). Developing effective BDs requires substantial domain knowledge, which becomes increasingly difficult with task complexity. Furthermore, hand-crafted BDs inherently limit the scope of diversity to predefined dimensions, potentially preventing the discovery of unforeseen or highly novel behavioral variations.

Prior research has attempted to mitigate this reliance on manual BDs. Some methods use human data or proxy objectives, such as state reconstruction via autoencoders (e.g., Aurora, LSTM-Aurora), to define BDs. Other approaches within the broader RL literature employ information-theoretic objectives to train a fixed number of policies to be maximally different or distinguishable (e.g., DIAYN, DADS, SMERL, DoMiNo). However, these often either lack theoretical grounding for their behavioral representations, require prior training, or struggle with scaling to a large number of diverse policies.

AutoQD contributes to this landscape by introducing a theoretically grounded approach to automatically generate behavioral descriptors. It leverages the mathematical equivalence between policies and their occupancy measures to create representations that inherently characterize policy behavior. By embedding these occupancy measures into a finite-dimensional space where distances reflect meaningful behavioral differences, AutoQD aims to overcome the limitations of hand-crafted descriptors and facilitate the discovery of genuinely diverse and high-quality policies in an unsupervised manner. This positions AutoQD as a contribution to the fields of unsupervised reinforcement learning and quality-diversity optimization, offering an alternative to existing methods by providing a principled mechanism for behavior representation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a theoretically grounded method for automatically generating behavioral descriptors (BDs) for Quality-Diversity (QD) optimization in sequential decision-making settings. This aims to address a critical limitation in current QD algorithms, which heavily depend on manually designed BDs.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and desirable outcomes:
1. **Elimination of Domain Expertise Requirement:** Traditional QD methods necessitate significant domain knowledge to design effective BDs. As tasks become more complex, this manual engineering becomes increasingly difficult and time-consuming. AutoQD seeks to remove this bottleneck, making QD optimization more accessible and applicable to a wider range of problems without requiring expert insights into specific behavioral patterns.
2. **Unconstrained Discovery of Diverse Behaviors:** Hand-crafted BDs inherently constrain the search for diversity to predefined notions. This can lead to overlooking novel, unexpected, or potentially superior behavioral variations that do not align with human-specified dimensions. By automatically generating BDs, AutoQD aims to enable a more open-ended exploration of the behavior space, fostering the discovery of a broader spectrum of policies.
3. **Theoretically Principled Behavior Characterization:** The research is motivated by the insight that occupancy measures provide a complete characterization of a policy's behavior in fully-observable Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). Leveraging this one-to-one correspondence offers a strong theoretical foundation for representing policy behavior, distinguishing it from prior heuristic approaches.
4. **Enabling Robustness and Open-Ended Learning:** The broader goals of QD-RL – achieving robustness through diverse policies that can adapt to changing conditions and fostering open-ended learning that continuously discovers novel capabilities – are intrinsically linked to the ability to effectively characterize and discover diverse behaviors. Automatic and principled BD generation is seen as a crucial step towards realizing these goals more effectively.

To achieve these motivations, the paper outlines four specific contributions:
1. **Efficient Policy Embedding:** Develop a method to efficiently embed occupancy measures of policies into a vector space, using data from sampled trajectories.
2. **Formal Approximation Guarantees:** Provide a formal proof demonstrating that the distances between these policy embeddings reliably approximate the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) between their corresponding occupancy measures.
3. **Iterative Algorithm Design:** Propose an iterative algorithm that alternates between the process of discovering diverse policies using QD optimization and refining the automatically generated behavior descriptors based on the current archive of policies.
4. **Empirical Validation:** Empirically demonstrate that this approach can discover diverse, high-performing policies in continuous control tasks without requiring any predefined behavioral descriptors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AutoQD operates through a three-step iterative process: (1) embedding policies into a high-dimensional space where distances approximate behavioral differences, (2) extracting low-dimensional behavioral descriptors (BDs) from these embeddings, and (3) using these descriptors with a Quality-Diversity (QD) algorithm to discover diverse policies.

#### 3.1. Policy Embedding via Random Features

The core of AutoQD's methodology relies on representing policy behaviors through **occupancy measures**. An occupancy measure, `ρ_π(s, a)`, quantifies the expected discounted visitation frequency of state-action pairs `(s, a)` when following a policy `π`. Under standard assumptions in fully-observable MDPs, there is a one-to-one correspondence between a Markovian policy and its occupancy measure, making it a comprehensive characterization of policy behavior.

To quantify the differences between these occupancy measures, AutoQD utilizes the **Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)**. MMD is a metric that measures the distance between two probability distributions by comparing statistics of their samples in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). The MMD with a characteristic kernel (e.g., Gaussian kernel) is zero if and only if the two distributions are identical.

A key challenge is that the Gaussian kernel corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature map. To create computationally tractable finite-dimensional embeddings, AutoQD approximates the Gaussian kernel using **Random Fourier Features (RFFs)**.
- A `D`-dimensional random feature map, `ϕ(s, a)`, is defined as:
 `ϕ(s, a) = sqrt(2/D) * [cos(w_1^T * [s; a] + b_1)..., cos(w_D^T * [s; a] + b_D)]`,
 where `w_i` are sampled from a Gaussian distribution `N(0, σ^-2 * I)` and `b_i` are sampled from `U(0, 2π)`. The kernel width `σ` controls the scale of similarity.
- For a policy `π`, its embedding `ψ_π` is derived from sampled trajectories. Specifically, `ψ_π` is calculated as the empirical mean of the discounted sum of RFFs for state-action pairs encountered in `n` trajectories:
 `ψ_π = (1/n) * sum_{j=1 to n} (1 - γ) * sum_{t=0 to T} γ^t * ϕ(s_j,t, a_j,t)`.
 This `ψ_π` leverages all collected transitions in a trajectory, and it is theoretically shown to have the same expectation as an embedding obtained from i.i.d. samples from the occupancy measure, potentially reducing variance.
- **Theorem 1** formally proves that the Euclidean distance between these embeddings, `||ψ_π1 - ψ_π2||`, reliably approximates the true MMD between their corresponding occupancy measures `ρ_π1` and `ρ_π2` with high probability. The approximation error decreases exponentially as the number of samples `n` and the embedding dimension `D` increase.

#### 3.2. The AutoQD Algorithm

AutoQD integrates these policy embeddings with a state-of-the-art blackbox QD algorithm, CMA-MAE (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Map-Annealing, Fontaine and Nikolaidis, 2023), in an iterative fashion.

- **Dimensionality Reduction and Behavioral Descriptors:** The high-dimensional policy embeddings `ψ_π` are projected into a lower `k`-dimensional space to serve as behavioral descriptors `desc(π)`. This projection is performed using an affine transformation: `desc(π) = Aψ_π + b`, where `A` and `b` are parameters of the transformation.
- **Calibrated Weighted PCA (cwPCA):** The parameters `A` and `b` are dynamically derived by applying Calibrated Weighted Principal Component Analysis (cwPCA) to the embeddings of policies currently residing in the QD archive.
 - **Weighting:** Policies in the archive are weighted by their fitness scores, normalized such that better-performing policies have a greater influence on the principal components. This biases the descriptors to capture variations among high-quality behaviors.
 - **PCA:** Standard PCA is then applied to these weighted, centered embeddings to identify the `k` principal components, which form the basis for the affine map `A`. PCA is chosen for its ability to preserve the geometry of the embedding space and for not introducing non-linear distortions.
 - **Calibration:** The projected dimensions are then scaled to a fixed range, typically `[-1, 1]`, by mapping the 5th and 95th percentiles of the projections to these bounds. This ensures compatibility with the fixed-bound grid-based archives used in QD algorithms, allowing for uniform coverage and stable archive boundaries.
- **Iterative Optimization:** The AutoQD algorithm (Algorithm 1) alternates between two main phases:
 1. **QD Optimization Step:** Using the current `k`-dimensional behavioral descriptors `desc(π)`, CMA-MAE samples new policy parameters, evaluates their fitness and descriptors, and attempts to add them to the archive. The archive maintains the best-performing policy for each cell in the discretized behavior space. CMA-MAE then updates its internal search distributions based on improvements made to the archive.
 2. **Descriptor Refinement Step:** Periodically, according to a predefined schedule, the algorithm pauses QD optimization. It then gathers all policy embeddings `ψ_π` from the current archive, re-computes the cwPCA to derive new `A` and `b` parameters, and updates the behavioral descriptor function `desc(π) = Aψ_π + b`. This adaptive refinement allows the behavior space to evolve, focusing on meaningful variations as the population of discovered policies expands and improves.

By integrating theoretically sound policy embeddings with an adaptive dimensionality reduction technique and a robust QD optimizer, AutoQD aims to automatically discover diverse and high-performing policies without requiring any prior domain-specific knowledge for behavior characterization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

AutoQD's performance was evaluated against five baselines across six standard continuous control tasks from the Gymnasium library, including five MuJoCo environments (Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, Swimmer, Walker2d) and BipedalWalker. The baselines included: RegularQD (with hand-crafted BDs), Aurora (autoencoder on states), LSTM-Aurora (LSTM on trajectories), DvD-ES (evolutionary diversity), and SMERL (RL-based skill discovery). Performance was assessed using three metrics: Ground-Truth QD Score (GT QD Score, evaluating quality and diversity against expert-defined BDs), Vendi Score (VS, quantifying population diversity), and Quality-Weighted Vendi Score (qVS, combining quality and diversity).

#### 4.1. Policy Discovery

- **Overall Superiority:** AutoQD consistently demonstrated superior performance across most environments. In terms of GT QD Score, AutoQD significantly outperformed all baselines in Ant, Swimmer, and BipedalWalker. It also achieved competitive or leading results for qVS and VS in many tasks.
- **Exceptions and Analysis:**
 - **HalfCheetah:** AutoQD achieved a high Vendi Score, indicating significant diversity, but a relatively lower qVS and GT QD score compared to some baselines. Analysis revealed that AutoQD discovered numerous policies that moved by "sliding" through subtle joint movements. While novel and diverse, these behaviors resulted in slower movement and consequently lower rewards.
 - **Walker2d:** AutoQD ranked second in this domain. It achieved high mean fitness but underperformed RegularQD in terms of diversity, with a tendency to focus on gait variations dominated by lower joints rather than behaviors involving upper-body movement.
- **Trade-off between Quality and Diversity:** An additional analysis (Figure 5 in Appendix F) showed that AutoQD achieved 5 out of 6 points above the diagonal in a normalized quality-diversity trade-off plot, suggesting an effective balance. It often produced the most diverse populations, sometimes by sacrificing some mean quality. In contrast, methods like DvD-ES often achieved high mean quality but with limited diversity.
- **Comparison to Gradient-based Methods:** An extended result (Table 8 in Appendix F) showed that a gradient-based QD algorithm, PGA-ME, when provided with ground-truth BDs, often achieved the highest maximum objective, indicating a potential avenue for future work combining AutoQD's descriptors with more powerful QD optimizers.

#### 4.2. Adaptation to Different Dynamics

A key motivation for discovering diverse populations is their potential for adaptability to changing environmental conditions. This was evaluated in the BipedalWalker environment by scaling the friction coefficient and altering the robot mass.

- **Robustness Across Variations:** AutoQD's generated populations maintained relatively high performance across both friction and mass variations. It achieved the highest Area Under the Curve (AUC) for both friction (1429.66) and mass (295.65) changes, indicating greater adaptability compared to all baselines (Table 2).
- **Number of Successful Policies:** Further analysis focused on the count of "successful" policies (those maintaining a significant fraction `p` of the best performance in the unaltered environment).
 - At a strict threshold (`p=0.9`), AutoQD consistently included a larger number of successfully adapted policies across friction changes (Figure 4) and mass changes (Figure 6 in Appendix G).
 - At a relaxed threshold (`p=0.7`), while LSTM-Aurora showed many successful policies in certain ranges, AutoQD and DvD-ES proved more capable as environmental changes became more pronounced.
- **Distribution of Returns:** Box plots of policy returns under varying dynamics (Figures 7 and 8 in Appendix G) illustrated that while AutoQD's population might exhibit a lower mean return, it had "longer tails" toward high returns. This suggests that while many policies might fail to adapt, a crucial subset of policies discovered by AutoQD were exceptionally effective at adapting to the altered conditions, aligning with the goal of producing a robust repertoire.

#### 4.3. Stability of Learned Behavior Descriptors

- **Low Variance:** An ablation study on descriptor stability (Figure 11 in Appendix I) demonstrated that AutoQD's learned descriptors are highly stable. The variance of descriptor outputs from multiple rollouts of the same policy was often orders of magnitude lower than those produced by Aurora or LSTM-Aurora, and comparable to hand-crafted BDs from RegularQD.
- **Robustness to Rollout Count:** AutoQD's performance was robust to the number of rollouts used for BD estimation. Even with as few as two rollouts per policy, AutoQD maintained competitive performance, suggesting its embeddings allow for accurate BD estimates with minimal data (Table 9 in Appendix I).

#### 4.4. Effect of Embedding and Behavior Space Dimensions

- **Behavior Descriptor Dimension:** Varying the behavior descriptor dimension (`k` from 1 to 4) showed consistent improvement in QD Score and Vendi Score with increasing `k`. However, the mean objective decreased, suggesting that while higher dimensions capture more diversity, the fraction of high-quality solutions can decrease as the behavior space complexity grows (Figure 10 in Appendix H).
- **Embedding Dimension:** AutoQD's performance was relatively robust to the Random Fourier Feature embedding dimension (`D` from 10 to 1000). While 100 dimensions were used in main experiments, even 10 features yielded competitive results (Figure 9 in Appendix H).

#### 4.5. Qualitative Analysis of Behaviors

Visualizations (Figures 12-15 in Appendix J) showcased the diverse behaviors discovered by AutoQD in Swimmer, BipedalWalker, HalfCheetah, and Walker2d. These included varied locomotion strategies (e.g., S-shaped vs. U/∩-shaped swimming, different BipedalWalker gaits) and illustrated that AutoQD could identify distinct, if sometimes suboptimal (as in HalfCheetah's sliding), behavioral patterns without explicit guidance.

In summary, AutoQD demonstrated a notable ability to automatically discover diverse and high-performing policy repertoires across several continuous control tasks. It consistently matched or exceeded baselines, particularly in its capacity to generate populations robust to dynamic environment changes, while relying on a theoretically grounded and empirically stable method for behavioral descriptor generation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of AutoQD carries several significant implications and potential impacts for the fields of reinforcement learning, quality-diversity optimization, and open-ended learning:

1. **Overcoming the Behavioral Descriptor Bottleneck:** AutoQD directly addresses a fundamental limitation of existing QD algorithms—the necessity of hand-crafted behavioral descriptors (BDs). By automatically generating these descriptors, AutoQD removes a significant barrier to entry, making QD optimization more accessible and broadly applicable to complex domains where domain expertise for BD design is scarce or difficult to formulate. This significantly reduces the manual effort and expert knowledge traditionally required.

2. **Theoretically Principled Foundation:** Unlike many prior unsupervised behavior discovery methods that rely on heuristics or proxy objectives, AutoQD is grounded in the theoretical understanding of occupancy measures as a complete characterization of policy behavior and the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) as a robust metric for distribution comparison. This theoretical backing provides a strong foundation for the reliability and meaningfulness of the learned behavioral representations.

3. **Discovery of Novel and Unanticipated Behaviors:** By not constraining the search to predefined behavioral dimensions, AutoQD facilitates a more open-ended exploration of the policy space. This capability can lead to the discovery of novel, unexpected, or highly specialized behaviors that might be missed by human-designed descriptors. Such discoveries can be crucial for innovation in robotics, complex system control, and adaptive agents.

4. **Enhanced Adaptability and Robustness:** The empirical results demonstrate that the diverse populations generated by AutoQD lead to policies that are more adaptable and robust to variations in environmental dynamics. This is highly significant for real-world applications where conditions are often uncertain or subject to change. The ability to quickly identify a high-performing policy from a diverse archive for altered environments contributes to the development of more resilient intelligent systems.

5. **Catalyst for Open-Ended Learning:** The capacity for automated behavior discovery is a key enabler for open-ended learning systems. By continuously uncovering new skills and capabilities without explicit human guidance, AutoQD can contribute to agents that incrementally build complex behavioral repertoires over extended periods, mirroring aspects of natural evolution and intelligence development.

6. **Broader Applications of Policy Embeddings:** The policy embeddings derived by AutoQD, which capture meaningful behavioral differences, have potential applications beyond QD optimization. These could include:
 - **Imitation Learning:** Facilitating the transfer of behaviors by comparing new policies to a library of known behaviors.
 - **Inverse Reinforcement Learning:** Aiding in inferring reward functions by analyzing behavioral similarities.
 - **Policy Analysis:** Enabling clustering, visualization, and deeper understanding of learned policies in complex tasks.
 - **Skill Transfer:** Providing a basis for identifying transferable skills across different tasks or environments.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, which also suggest avenues for future impact:
- **Sample Efficiency:** In highly stochastic environments, accurately estimating policy embeddings may require many trajectories, impacting sample efficiency. Future work could explore more sample-efficient estimation techniques.
- **Low-Dimensional Behavior Spaces:** The method might, in some cases, concentrate exploration on stable but simple behaviors, hindering the discovery of more complex ones, particularly when constrained to low-dimensional BDs. Investigating higher-dimensional descriptors, adaptive dimensionality, or mechanisms like "extinction events" could mitigate this.
- **Kernel Bandwidth Sensitivity:** The choice of kernel bandwidth for RFFs influences embedding sensitivity. Dynamic adaptation of this parameter during training could yield better results.
- **Scalability Challenges:** As AutoQD relies on existing QD optimizers (like CMA-MAE), it inherits their scalability challenges with very large policy networks or extremely high-dimensional behavior spaces. Future integration with more scalable gradient-based QD methods is a promising direction, despite current stability challenges.
- **Pure Reward Optimization:** While QD prioritizes both quality and diversity, it may sometimes lag behind methods purely focused on maximizing reward. Continued improvements in QD algorithms are expected to narrow this gap.

In conclusion, AutoQD represents a significant step towards autonomous and principled behavior discovery, moving beyond the traditional reliance on human-designed behavior descriptors. Its theoretical foundation, empirical performance, and potential for enabling more adaptive and open-ended learning systems make it a notable contribution with a broad potential impact on the development of more capable and intelligent artificial agents.
