# SAE as a Crystal Ball: Interpretable Features Predict Cross-domain Transferability of LLMs without Training

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces the SAE-based Transferability Score (STS), a metric that leverages sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to predict how Large Language Models (LLMs) will transfer capabilities across domains after post-training, without requiring actual fine-tuning. The STS achieves high correlations (e.g., Pearson > 0.7) with observed performance shifts across various models and tasks.

## Problem

- A lack of predictive understanding exists regarding how post-training (e.g., SFT, RLHF) impacts the cross-domain transferability of LLMs.
- Current transferability analyses are primarily post-hoc, requiring expensive and time-consuming fine-tuning before evaluating performance shifts.
- Post-training often leads to model shifts and performance trade-offs, degrading capabilities in untargeted domains, with the underlying mechanisms being poorly understood.

## Method

- The research employs ==Sparse Autoencoders== (SAEs) to disentangle the highly polysemantic internal representations of LLMs into interpretable, monosemantic features.
- It identifies the specific SAE dimensions most affected by post-training through In-Context Learning (ICL), which acts as a proxy for actual fine-tuning.
- Two variants of the SAE-based Transferability Score (STS) are proposed (STS_act and STS_ICL) to quantify the correlation between these predicted feature shifts and downstream domain performance, computed on the pre-trained model without fine-tuning.

## Results

- The STS consistently achieved strong Pearson correlations, often exceeding **0.7** and reaching up to **0.90**, with actual absolute performance changes across Qwen2.5, Llama3, and Gemma2 models fine-tuned on diverse tasks.
- The method demonstrated generalizability, with STS_ICL correlations of **0.77** and **0.71** for code generation and health dialogue training domains, respectively.
- STS was applied to optimize data mixture, enabling a more balanced performance across various domains by strategically allocating additional training data to domains predicted to suffer degradation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Post-training, such as supervised fine-tuning, primarily induces changes in a small, identifiable subset of an LLM's monosemantic SAE features, rather than uniformly across all features.
> - In-Context Learning can reliably identify these task-relevant feature shifts in SAE space, demonstrating significant overlap with shifts caused by actual fine-tuning.
> - The monosemanticity of SAE features is crucial for accurate identification of relevant shifts, as raw, polysemantic model features exhibit more uniform and less informative changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge in the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Models (LLMs): understanding and predicting the effects of post-training. While pre-training on vast datasets endows LLMs with remarkable general capabilities, adapting them to specific tasks through post-training (e.g., supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reinforcement learning (RL) with human feedback) is crucial for optimal performance. However, this adaptation process inevitably introduces "model shifts" that can significantly alter performance across various domains, often leading to performance improvements in the target task at the cost of degradation in others. This phenomenon, often referred to as "catastrophic forgetting" or "capability trade-offs," is a widely observed but poorly understood aspect of LLM development.

Current research on transferability in LLMs largely focuses on *post-hoc* analysis—examining transfer effects after the training process has already been completed (e.g., Huan et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Chu et al., 2025). This approach, while informative, is inherently limited; it cannot guide the design of post-training strategies *before* expensive and time-consuming fine-tuning begins.

This paper directly tackles this limitation by proposing a *predictive* framework. It draws heavily on the emerging field of **mechanistic interpretability**, particularly the use of **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)**. SAEs are gaining traction as a powerful tool for disentangling the highly polysemantic and entangled internal representations of LLMs into more interpretable, "monosemantic" features, where each dimension corresponds to a specific natural concept (Cunningham et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). By leveraging SAEs, the authors aim to "open up the black box" of LLM post-training, moving beyond empirical observations to a more principled, feature-level understanding.

The work sits at the intersection of several key research areas:
- **LLM Fine-tuning and Transfer Learning:** Understanding how model adaptations influence generalization and specialization.
- **Mechanistic Interpretability:** Utilizing tools like SAEs to make the internal workings of neural networks transparent.
- **Efficient AI Development:** Providing methods to optimize the resource-intensive process of LLM adaptation.

By offering a predictive and interpretable metric, the paper contributes a valuable tool to the LLM research ecosystem, moving the field closer to principled design choices for post-training rather than relying on trial-and-error.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the current lack of a predictive understanding regarding how post-training impacts the cross-domain transferability of LLMs. While post-training (including supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning) is essential for adapting pre-trained models to specific tasks, it frequently leads to unintended consequences, such as performance degradation in domains not directly targeted by the fine-tuning data. This trade-off is often observed but the underlying mechanisms linking model feature shifts to performance changes remain "poorly understood." Without this understanding, developers and researchers lack the ability to foresee which domains will benefit or suffer from a given post-training strategy, thus limiting the interpretability and principled design of these crucial processes.

Given this challenge, the key objectives of the paper are:

1. **To develop an interpretable method to predict post-training transferability without requiring actual training.** The central goal is to create a metric that can reliably forecast performance shifts across domains *before* expensive and time-consuming fine-tuning or reinforcement learning is performed.
2. **To leverage Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) to achieve this predictive capability.** The authors propose using the monosemantic features extracted by SAEs as a window into the internal representations of LLMs, believing that these disentangled features can reveal how specific capabilities are altered during post-training.
3. **To identify "shifted dimensions" within SAE representations that are most affected by post-training.** The hypothesis is that post-training primarily modifies a small subset of features related to the target task. A crucial sub-objective is to identify these shifted dimensions *prior to* the actual fine-tuning process.
4. **To quantify the correlation between these shifted dimensions and various downstream domains.** By understanding how task-relevant features relate to different domains, the paper aims to predict the magnitude of performance change.
5. **To validate the proposed metric (SAE-based Transferability Score, STS) empirically** across diverse models and domains, demonstrating its accuracy and reliability in forecasting performance shifts induced by supervised fine-tuning.
6. **To explore the applicability of this framework to reinforcement learning settings,** taking an initial step towards a broader understanding of post-training effects.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need to transform the "black box" of LLM post-training into a more transparent, predictable, and resource-efficient process, ultimately enabling more informed decisions in LLM development and deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology revolves around leveraging Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) to gain interpretable insights into LLM internal representations, thereby enabling the prediction of cross-domain transferability without the need for actual fine-tuning. The approach can be broken down into several key steps:

#### 3.1. Analyzing SFT-Induced Changes in SAE Features

The first step is an empirical observation of how Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) affects the internal features of an LLM. The authors hypothesized that SFT, being task-specific, would primarily modify a small subset of SAE dimensions related to the target capability.
- **Experiment Setup:** They fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct on LIMO, a mathematical reasoning dataset. SAE features were extracted from the residual streams of a specific layer (layer 25) both before and after SFT.
- **Observation:** They found that SFT indeed caused changes largely concentrated in a small subset of SAE dimensions. For instance, the top-100 most changed dimensions accounted for 25% of the total shift (Figure 1a).
- **Validation:** To link these shifts to model capability, they progressively "zeroed out" the SAE dimensions with the largest changes and observed a rapid drop in performance on math tasks, while zeroing random dimensions had little effect (Figure 1b). This confirms that SFT targets specific, interpretable features crucial for the task.

#### 3.2. Identifying Shifted Dimensions via In-Context Learning (ICL)

A crucial challenge is identifying these shifted dimensions *before* performing SFT. The authors propose using In-Context Learning (ICL) as a proxy for SFT, building on prior work showing similarities between ICL and SFT behaviors (Wang et al., 2023; Mosbach et al., 2023).
- **Approach:** Instead of fine-tuning, they use supervised answers from the target SFT dataset (LIMO) as demonstrations for ICL. They then identify the SAE dimensions that undergo the largest changes during ICL.
- **Validation:** Empirically, they demonstrate a substantial overlap (e.g., 57% for the top 100 dimensions) between the SAE features shifted by ICL and those shifted by actual SFT (Figure 2a).
- **Necessity of SAEs:** They compare this approach using raw model dimensions (before SAEs) and find significantly reduced overlap. This highlights the critical role of SAEs' monosemanticity in disentangling features, making it possible to accurately identify relevant shifts (Figure 2a, 2b). Raw features, being polysemantic, show more uniform shifts, making identification difficult.

#### 3.3. Defining the SAE-based Transferability Score (STS)

With a method to predict shifted dimensions *before* training, the next step is to quantify how these shifts correlate with different downstream domains. Due to SAEs' monosemanticity, activation values in specific dimensions can reflect their correlation with semantic concepts relevant to a domain.
- **Identification of Shifted Dimensions (D_N):** First, the `N` dimensions with the largest changes are identified by comparing SAE features extracted *without* context to those extracted with ICL demonstrations (using the SFT training data's answers as context).
 `D_N = TopN(E_{x_i} ||h_j(x_i; Θ) - h_j(x_0, y_0..., x_t, y_t, x_i; Θ)||²)`
- **STS_act (Activation-based Transferability Score):** This metric quantifies the average activation of the identified shifted dimensions (`D_N`) when processing inputs from a downstream domain (`~T`).
 `STS_act(~T) = E_{~x_i} Σ_{j∈D_N} h_j(~x_i; Θ)`
 A higher `STS_act` suggests stronger correlation and thus a larger expected performance change.
- **STS_ICL (ICL-based Transferability Score):** An alternative metric that leverages ICL within the downstream domain itself. It measures how much the presence of domain-specific ICL demonstrations (ground-truth question-answer pairs from the downstream domain) influences the activation of the identified shifted dimensions (`D_N`).
 `STS_ICL(~T) = E_{~x_i} Σ_{j∈D_N} ||h_j(~x_0, ~y_0..., ~x_m, ~y_m, ~x_i; Θ) - h_j(~x_i; Θ)||²`
 This metric captures how strongly the shifted features are modulated by task-relevant information in the downstream domain.

Crucially, both STS metrics are computed using the *pretrained model* (`Θ`) and SAE encoder (`h`), without any actual fine-tuning of the LLM itself, making them predictive.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings across empirical verifications, ablation studies, and an application case.

#### 4.1. Predicting Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Transferability

The primary finding is that the proposed SAE-based Transferability Score (STS) accurately predicts the cross-domain transferability of SFT without requiring the fine-tuning process.
- **High Correlation with Performance Shifts:** Extensive experiments across multiple LLMs (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama3-8B-Instruct, and Gemma2-9B-Instruct) fine-tuned on a mathematical reasoning dataset (LIMO) showed that STS values strongly correlate with actual absolute performance changes across different MMLU-Pro domains. Pearson correlation coefficients frequently exceeded 0.7, with some cases reaching as high as 0.90 (e.g., STS_act on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) and STS_ICL consistently showing correlations above 0.75 across models (Figure 3, Table 6).
- **Stability of STS_ICL:** STS_ICL generally demonstrated more stable and robust correlations compared to STS_act, suggesting that leveraging in-context learning to estimate domain-feature correlation provides a more reliable prediction.
- **Generalizability Across Training Domains:** Further experiments with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct trained on diverse datasets (a code generation dataset and a health dialogue dataset) also showed strong correlations (0.77 and 0.71, respectively) between STS_ICL and actual performance shifts (Table 1). This indicates the broad applicability of the method.
- **Overlap Validation:** The central hypothesis—that ICL-induced feature shifts substantially overlap with SFT-induced shifts in SAE space—was consistently validated across these diverse training domains, with overlaps of 62% for code and 57% for health-trained models (Table 1).

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies provided crucial insights into the methodological design choices.
- **Importance of Monosemanticity:** Varying SAE hidden dimensions (16k vs. 131k) and sparsity levels (L0 norm) demonstrated that stronger monosemanticity (typically associated with higher sparsity and appropriately sized hidden layers) leads to more accurate predictions (Figure 4a, 4c, Table 12). This confirms that the disentangled, interpretable features from SAEs are essential for the method's effectiveness.
- **Robustness Across Layers:** STS remained consistently correlated with performance changes across different transformer layers (e.g., layers 15, 20, 25 in Qwen2.5-7B), indicating the robustness of the approach to layer selection (Figure 4b).
- **Selection of Shifted Dimensions:** The accuracy of prediction declined when smaller-shifted dimensions were included in the calculation, emphasizing the importance of focusing on the dimensions with the largest changes (Figure 4d).
- **Necessity of Shifted Dimension Identification:** Critically, simply probing raw or SAE feature activations *without* identifying the specific "shifted dimensions" showed negligible correlation with actual performance shifts (Figure 4e, Table 13). This underscores that the core innovation is not just using SAEs, but using them to pinpoint the *changes* in specific, interpretable features.

#### 4.3. Application: A Data Mixture Principle

The paper demonstrated a practical application of STS for optimizing data mixture strategies during post-training.
- **STS-Guided Data Augmentation:** By using STS to predict which domains are most susceptible to performance degradation (e.g., engineering with a larger predicted drop vs. law with a smaller predicted drop), more additional data can be allocated to at-risk domains.
- **Balanced Performance:** When additional data was mixed according to the ratio of their corresponding STS values, the resulting fine-tuned model achieved a more balanced performance across different domains (Figure 5). This showcases STS as an interpretable guide for mitigating uneven performance shifts.

#### 4.4. Explorations on Reinforcement Learning (RL)

An initial exploration into extending STS to reinforcement learning settings revealed both potential and current limitations.
- **Low Initial Correlation:** Directly applying the STS metric (as in SFT) to RL scenarios (using GRPO on Math-LightEval) showed low correlation with performance changes (Figure 6b).
- **Challenge in Shifted Feature Estimation:** The root cause was identified as the difficulty in accurately estimating shifted features in RL due to the lack of ground-truth answers for ICL demonstrations. The overlap between estimated and actual RL-shifted dimensions was substantially lower than for SFT (Figure 6a).
- **Validation of Core Idea:** However, when the *actual* RL-shifted dimensions were used (instead of ICL-estimated ones), STS *did* show a strong correlation with performance changes (Figure 6b). This indicates that the core mechanism of STS (correlating feature shifts with domains) holds, but improved methods for identifying shifted features in RL contexts are needed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "SAE AS A CRYSTAL BALL" offers a significant contribution to the field of Large Language Model development and interpretability, with far-reaching potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Predictive Post-Training Optimization:** The most immediate and impactful contribution is the ability to predict cross-domain transferability of LLMs *before* undertaking the computationally expensive and time-consuming process of supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning. This shifts the paradigm from reactive, post-hoc analysis to proactive, predictive strategy design. Developers can now anticipate performance trade-offs and allocate resources more efficiently, saving substantial computational costs and development cycles.

2. **Enhanced Interpretability of LLM Adaptation:** By grounding the transferability predictions in Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), the method provides a layer of interpretability that is typically absent in black-box LLMs. Understanding *which specific, monosemantic features* are most affected by post-training, and how these features correlate with different downstream domains, offers mechanistic insights into *why* certain capabilities improve or degrade. This moves beyond merely observing performance changes to understanding the underlying internal mechanisms.

3. **Principled Design of Post-Training Strategies:** The STS metric can serve as an invaluable tool for guiding the design of more effective post-training strategies.
 - **Data Curation:** It can inform data mixture ratios for fine-tuning datasets, allowing developers to strategically augment data in domains predicted to suffer performance drops, thereby mitigating unintended side effects (as demonstrated in the data mixture application).
 - **Selective Fine-Tuning:** The identification of "shifted dimensions" could inspire more targeted fine-tuning approaches, where only a subset of relevant model parameters (or SAE-related parameters) are updated, potentially leading to more efficient and less destructive adaptation.
 - **Model Selection:** It could help in selecting base pre-trained models that are inherently more robust to post-training shifts in critical domains.

4. **Advancing Mechanistic Interpretability:** The work further validates the utility of SAEs beyond simple feature visualization. It demonstrates that SAEs can be powerful diagnostic tools for understanding dynamic model behavior (i.e., how features change) and linking these changes to observable outcomes (i.e., transferability). This could spur further research into developing and applying advanced interpretability techniques for practical engineering challenges in AI.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The initial exploration into reinforcement learning, while identifying current limitations in feature shift estimation for RL, points to a clear and important avenue for future work. Overcoming this challenge would significantly broaden the applicability of the STS framework to a wider range of post-training paradigms, including alignment with human preferences.

In conclusion, "SAE AS A CRYSTAL BALL" introduces a practical, interpretable, and predictive framework for understanding LLM transferability, poised to significantly influence how LLMs are developed, fine-tuned, and deployed in real-world applications.
