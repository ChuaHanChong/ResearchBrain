# Learning Safe-Stoppability Monitors for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces PRISM, a simulation-driven framework that learns a neural monitor to predict if a humanoid robot can safely execute a predefined emergency stop. It quantifies safe-stoppability as a probability and achieves efficient learning by iteratively focusing data collection on critical state-space regions, resulting in high accuracy for unsafe state detection (up to 99.38%) on a Unitree G1 humanoid while significantly reducing data volume compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Traditional emergency stops for humanoid robots are often catastrophic, leading to falls and secondary collisions due to their complex dynamics and underactuated nature.
- Precisely characterizing the "safe-stoppable envelope" (SSE) for high-dimensional humanoid robots is computationally intractable and unsafe to perform with real-world failure trials.
- Existing control methods improve robustness but do not explicitly quantify the probability of successfully reaching a designated safe terminal posture (Minimum Risk Condition or MRC) while actively avoiding intermediate failures.

## Method

- The work formalizes safe-stoppability as a policy-dependent stochastic reach-avoid condition, defining a "stoppability value function" that quantifies the probability of safely reaching an MRC under a specified fallback policy.
- A neural monitor is learned using PRISM (Proactive Refinement of Importance-sampled Stoppability Monitors), a simulation-driven framework that approximates the true stoppability function to enable proactive safety assessment.
- PRISM employs an iterative importance sampling strategy to efficiently collect data by concentrating sampling in regions of high uncertainty or systematic error near the predicted SSE boundary, mitigating data imbalance.

## Results

- PRISM achieved comparable monitor performance to uniform training baselines while utilizing over 40% less total trajectory data, demonstrating its data efficiency in characterizing the safe-stoppable boundary.
- The monitor's safety confidence profiling accurately identified complex, state-dependent dynamic vulnerabilities, showing precise temporal alignment of "unsafe" probability spikes with ground truth manipulation phases.
- Real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid validated the monitor's conservative decision boundary, achieving a high unsafe prediction accuracy of 94.1%, which is desirable for safety-critical hardware applications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Safe-stoppability for humanoid robots can be rigorously defined and evaluated as a policy-dependent stochastic reach-avoid probability, enabling a quantifiable approach to emergency safety.
> - Iterative importance sampling, guided by a progressively refined neural monitor, significantly enhances data efficiency in characterizing high-dimensional and complex safe operational envelopes.
> - While robust to internal joint parameter variations, the monitor's performance is notably sensitive to external ground friction changes, indicating how certain environmental interactions introduce novel failure modes requiring specific consideration for sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of humanoid robots in dynamic, human-centered environments necessitates robust safety mechanisms. A fundamental aspect of robot safety is the emergency stop (E-stop), which traditionally involves cutting power to prevent harm. However, for humanoid robots, this approach is often catastrophic due to their underactuated nature and reliance on ground contacts for stability. Abrupt power loss can lead to falls and secondary collisions, escalating rather than mitigating risk. This challenge highlights a gap in current safety protocols, which are largely designed for fixed-base industrial manipulators.

Existing research in humanoid control has focused on enhancing robustness and disturbance handling through methods such as capture-point control, push recovery, step timing adjustment, and optimization-based whole-body control. While these techniques improve a robot's ability to maintain balance and recover from perturbations, they do not explicitly characterize the set of states from which a predefined emergency behavior can reliably drive the robot to a safe, terminal configuration.

In control theory, safety is frequently approached as an invariance problem, aiming to keep system trajectories within a safe set. However, fail-safe behavior, particularly for humanoids, is fundamentally a reachability problem: the system must reach a designated safe terminal condition (Minimum Risk Condition or MRC) while actively avoiding intermediate failures. While theoretical frameworks like reachability and reach-avoid analysis provide strong guarantees, their computational complexity scales poorly with the high-dimensional dynamics of humanoids, rendering exact computation impractical. Data-driven approximations of these sets have emerged, often evaluating a fixed policy by estimating success probabilities, but efficiency in sampling critical boundary regions remains a challenge.

Furthermore, leveraging simulation for robot learning and evaluation is a common strategy to improve data efficiency and reduce real-world risks. Despite the benefits of large-scale parallel simulators, a significant sim-to-real gap persists due to mismatches in physical parameters and dynamics. Domain randomization is a standard technique to mitigate this gap, but its effectiveness for critical safety behaviors still requires careful validation.

This work integrates and extends these areas by formalizing emergency stopping for humanoids as a policy-dependent safe-stoppability problem. It addresses the computational intractability and data inefficiency of characterizing safe-stoppable regions by introducing a simulation-driven, data-efficient learning framework. By focusing on learning a neural monitor and employing iterative importance sampling, the research aims to bridge the gap between theoretical safety guarantees and practical, scalable deployment of humanoids with verifiable fail-safe capabilities in complex environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the inadequacy of traditional emergency stop (E-stop) mechanisms for humanoid robots. Unlike industrial manipulators, which can simply cut power, a humanoid robot performing an E-stop must execute a complex, predefined fallback controller. This controller is designed to preserve the robot's balance, regulate its contacts, and guide it towards a Minimum Risk Condition (MRC), typically a dynamically stable terminal posture. The necessity of such a controlled shutdown introduces a critical safety question: From which instantaneous robot states can this fallback controller be executed safely and reliably?

The primary objectives and underlying motivations are detailed as follows:

1. **Formalize Safe-Stoppability for Humanoids:**
 - **Motivation:** The concept of safe E-stops for humanoids requires a precise definition that accounts for their complex dynamics and the need for controlled, rather than abrupt, shutdown.
 - **Objective:** To formalize safe-stoppability as a policy-dependent stochastic reach-avoid condition. This involves defining a "stoppability value function" (V_stop(x)) that quantifies the probability of successfully reaching an MRC while avoiding intermediate failures (e.g., falls, collisions, joint limit violations) under a specified fallback policy. This formulation acknowledges the inherent stochasticity of robot dynamics and environmental interactions.

2. **Develop a Proactive Safe-Stoppability Monitor:**
 - **Motivation:** Beyond reacting to external E-stop triggers, a robot's ability to anticipate and prevent unsafe situations is crucial for robust operation in human-centered environments.
 - **Objective:** To learn an online neural monitor, $\hat{V}_{stop}(x)$, that accurately approximates the true stoppability function $V_{stop}(x)$. This monitor should enable the robot to continuously evaluate its current state. If the predicted stoppability falls below a predefined confidence threshold ($\alpha$), the monitor can proactively trigger the fallback policy, thereby preventing the robot from entering states where a safe stop is no longer possible. This transforms the E-stop from a reactive measure into a proactive runtime assurance mechanism.

3. **Address Challenges in Characterizing the Safe-Stoppable Envelope (SSE):**
 - **Motivation:** Precisely delineating the SSE for high-dimensional, contact-rich humanoid robots is inherently challenging due to computational, safety, and data efficiency constraints.
 - **Objectives and Approach:**
 - **Computational Intractability:** Exact reach-avoid computation for nonlinear, hybrid dynamics is analytically intractable and numerically prohibitive. The objective is to use data-driven, simulation-based approaches to approximate this boundary.
 - **Safety and Impracticality of Hardware Data:** Directly learning from hardware is unsafe and costly, as failure trials can damage the robot. The approach utilizes large-scale simulation as a "state replay engine" to efficiently obtain binary safety labels for diverse states without hardware risk.
 - **Data Imbalance and Inefficiency:** Safe-to-stop states are common, while failure-to-stop events are rare, particularly near the critical boundary between stoppable and unstoppable regions. Naive data collection wastes resources on trivially safe states. The objective is to introduce a data-efficient learning strategy called PRISM (Proactive Refinement of Importance-sampled Stoppability Monitors). PRISM employs an iterative, importance-guided sampling framework that concentrates data collection in regions of high uncertainty or systematic error near the predicted SSE boundary, significantly improving data efficiency by targeting informative states.

4. **Demonstrate Sim-to-Real Transferability:**
 - **Motivation:** For any simulation-based safety mechanism to be practical, its efficacy must transfer reliably to real-world hardware, despite the inherent sim-to-real gap.
 - **Objective:** To demonstrate the deployment of the pretrained monitor on a real humanoid platform (Unitree G1). This involves mitigating sim-to-real discrepancies by initializing simulated fallback rollouts with real-robot state logs and incorporating targeted domain randomization during simulation. The aim is to ensure the learned monitor is robust and reliable in a physical environment.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to establish a framework for verifiable safe-stop capabilities for bipedal humanoids, a crucial step towards their adoption in industrial and human-centered environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces PRISM (Proactive Refinement of Importance-sampled Stoppability Monitors), a simulation-driven framework designed to learn a neural safe-stoppability monitor for humanoid robots under a predefined fallback controller. The methodology combines formal problem definition, efficient data generation in simulation, and an iterative refinement strategy to overcome the challenges of characterizing safe-stoppable regions.

**A. Problem Formulation: Safe-Stoppable Envelop (SSE) and Stoppability Monitor**

1. **System Model:** The humanoid robot's state $x_t \in X$ evolves under stochastic dynamics $x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t, w_t)$, where $w_t$ represents disturbances and modeling uncertainties. A nominal task policy $\pi_{nom}(x_t)$ guides task execution, generating a nominal state distribution $X_{nom}$.
2. **Safety and Terminal Conditions:** A safe set $X_{safe} \subset X$ is defined, encompassing admissible conditions (e.g., no falls, no forbidden collisions, no joint limit violations). A subset of $X_{safe}$, the Minimum Risk Condition (MRC), $X_{terminal}$, is a predefined stable standing posture where the robot minimizes risk during an emergency.
3. **Stoppability Value Function:** The core concept is the stoppability value function, $V_{stop}(x_{trig})$, defined as the probability that the fallback policy $\pi_{fallback}$ successfully reaches $X_{terminal}$ within a maximum time $T_{max}$, while avoiding any intermediate safety violations (i.e., staying within $X_{safe}$), given an instantaneous trigger state $x_{trig}$.
4. **Safe-Stoppable Set (SSE):** For a given confidence threshold $\alpha \in (0,1]$, the $\alpha$-safe-stoppable set, $X^\alpha_{\pi_{fallback}}$, comprises all states $x$ for which $V_{stop}(x) \ge \alpha$. States with $V_{stop}(x) < \alpha$ are considered "Unstoppable."
5. **Monitor Objective:** The goal is to learn a neural network monitor, $\hat{V}_{stop}(x; \theta)$, that approximates $V_{stop}(x)$. This monitor should accurately estimate safe-stop success probability over $X_{\pi_{nom}}$ and maintain high reliability in safety-critical regions, specifically minimizing false-safe predictions (classifying an unstoppable state as stoppable).

**B. Simulation-Based Stoppability Estimation**

1. **Efficient Safe-Stop Outcome Labeling:** Binary supervisory labels are generated by simulating rollouts of the fallback policy. From sampled trigger states $x_{trig}$ (initially from $X_{nom}$), the $\pi_{fallback}$ is executed in the SPARK simulator. A label $y=1$ (Safe) is assigned if the robot reaches $X_{terminal}$ within $T_{max}$ without violating $X_{safe}$. Otherwise, $y=0$ (Unsafe). Each such rollout provides a deterministic binary label, but statistical theory indicates that optimizing for binary cross-entropy on these labels drives the neural network to recover the conditional expectation, i.e., the true probability $V_{stop}(x)$.
2. **Learning the Stoppability Monitor:** A neural network, $\hat{V}_{stop}(x; \theta)$, is trained using a weighted binary cross-entropy loss. The weighting function $\omega(\cdot)$ is used to address potential class imbalance in the training dataset. At runtime, the monitor's output $\hat{V}_{stop}(x)$ is compared against the threshold $\alpha$ to make a binary decision about stoppability.

**C. Iterative Refinement via Importance Sampling (PRISM Algorithm)**

The core of the data-efficient approach is PRISM, an iterative framework designed to overcome the challenge of class imbalance and the high cost of labeling.

1. **Initial Dataset Construction:** The process begins by collecting $N_0$ nominal trajectories from $\pi_{nom}$. States are uniformly sampled along these trajectories, and fallback rollouts in simulation generate initial labels, forming dataset $D^{(0)}$. An initial monitor $\hat{V}^{(0)}_{stop}$ is trained on this data.
2. **Iterative Data Refinement:** In each iteration $k$:
 - **Nominal Trajectory Generation:** A new batch of nominal trajectories is generated.
 - **Residual Computation:** The current monitor $\hat{V}^{(k)}_{stop}$ is evaluated on a fixed validation set $D_{val}$, and residuals $r^{(k)}_i$ (prediction errors) are computed.
 - **Importance Region Definition:** A quantile threshold $q^{(k)}_{1-\delta}$ (e.g., 90th percentile of errors) is used to define an "importance region" $T^{(k)}$. This region comprises states where the monitor's prediction error exceeds the calibrated tolerance, focusing on systematically mispredicted or uncertain areas.
 - **Sampling Distribution Construction:** A mixed sampling distribution $p^{(k)}_{mix}(x)$ is constructed as a convex combination of the nominal distribution $p^{(k)}_{nom}(x)$ and an importance-refined distribution $p^{(k)}_{imp}(x)$ (which is proportional to $p^{(k)}_{nom}(x)$ but restricted to $T^{(k)}$). A balancing parameter $\beta$ controls the emphasis on global coverage versus boundary refinement.
 - **Targeted Sampling and Labeling:** States are sampled according to $p^{(k)}_{mix}(x)$. In practice, this is achieved by dynamically adjusting the temporal sampling stride along newly collected nominal trajectories: dense sampling in segments where states fall into the importance region $T^{(k)}$, and sparse sampling elsewhere.
 - **Dataset Update and Retraining:** The newly labeled states are added to the aggregated training buffer $D^{(k+1)}$, and the monitor $\hat{V}^{(k+1)}_{stop}$ is retrained. This iterative process allows for progressive expansion of nominal coverage while concentrating sample density near the critical stoppability boundary.

**D. Sim-to-Real Deployment Considerations**

To enable robust real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid:
1. **Triggering State Distribution:** Real-robot state logs from nominal task execution are used to initialize fallback rollouts in simulation. This mitigates discrepancies in the distribution of states from which an E-stop might be triggered.
2. **Dynamics Mismatch:** Targeted domain randomization (e.g., varying friction, damping, motor gains) is applied during simulated fallback rollouts to improve the monitor's generalization to real-world physics variations.
3. **Real-World Monitoring:** An Apple Vision Pro AR headset is used to monitor and reconstruct real-world trials, allowing for data collection and collision checking in a virtual overlay to enhance safety during physical experiments.
4. **Terminal Condition:** The MRC $X_{terminal}$ is defined as a stable standing posture. The fallback policy $\pi_{fallback}$ is trained to reach this MRC from arbitrary initial states.

This comprehensive methodology, combining formal definition, simulation-based labeling, iterative importance sampling, and sim-to-real transfer strategies, aims to provide a robust and data-efficient solution for humanoid safe-stoppability monitoring.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings derived from experiments conducted in both simulation and on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid, validating the efficacy and efficiency of the PRISM framework.

**A. Data-Efficient Iterative Monitor Learning**

A quantitative comparison (Table I) was performed between PRISM and two full-buffer uniform training baselines.
- **Performance under equivalent data volume:** When compared to a 30-trajectory baseline (matching PRISM's data volume at Iteration 17), PRISM demonstrated improved prediction accuracy for critical unsafe states.
- **Data collection footprint:** PRISM achieved comparable performance (e.g., in overall prediction accuracy and unsafe prediction accuracy) to a 54-trajectory baseline while utilizing over 40% less total trajectory exposure. This suggests that PRISM effectively disentangles monitor accuracy from the sheer volume of data, highlighting its data-efficient boundary characterization.

**B. Qualitative Study: Safety Confidence and Spatial Profiling**

1. **Safety Confidence Profiling (Figure 4):** Analysis of the monitor's continuous output logits over a complete 7500-step nominal loco-manipulation trajectory revealed:
 - During steady-state walking (e.g., "Transfer," "Leave" phases), the safety confidence fluctuated in the 'safe' (deep negative) region, indicating a high stability margin.
 - During manipulation phases (e.g., "Pick," "Place"), significant probability spikes occurred into the 'unsafe' (positive) domain. These spikes showed precise temporal alignment with the ground truth, demonstrating the monitor's ability to identify complex, state-dependent dynamic vulnerabilities.
2. **Spatial State-Space Profiling (Figure 5):** Visualization of the monitor's predictions in a 2D Cartesian (x,y) projection for both real-world and simulation environments indicated:
 - Consistent topological structures for the decision boundaries (interfaces between safe and unsafe regions). This structural similarity across environments supports the monitor's sim-to-real transferability and its physical grounding in identifying spatially induced failures.

**C. Robustness to Dynamics Perturbations**

An ablation study (Table II) investigated the zero-shot generalization of a single monitor (trained on 40 nominal trajectories) under domain randomizations.
- **Internal Joint Parameters:** The monitor exhibited robustness to variations in internal joint parameters (damping and PD gains). These parameters primarily affect high-frequency transient control responses and do not fundamentally alter the robot's global whole-body kinematic envelope during an E-stop.
- **External Ground Friction:** Conversely, the monitor was notably sensitive to changes in external ground friction. Excessively high friction prevented natural kinetic energy dissipation through micro-slipping, leading to aggressive deceleration that converted linear momentum into angular tipping momentum around the foot edges. This introduced novel tripping failure modes, identified as out-of-distribution (OOD) states for the monitor, and resulted in a reduction in unsafe prediction accuracy.

**D. Sample Stride Sensitivity**

An ablation study on temporal sampling resolution (Table III) using a class-balanced dataset demonstrated:
- **Accuracy vs. Cost:** Decreasing the temporal stride (i.e., denser sampling) led to improved overall prediction accuracy and reduced false-safe errors by enhancing coverage near the stoppability boundary.
- **Trade-off:** This accuracy improvement, however, incurred a proportional increase in data collection time, empirically substantiating the need for data-efficient strategies like PRISM over blanket dense sampling.

**E. Effect of Decision Threshold α**

An ablation study on the decision threshold $\alpha$ (Table IV) revealed the trade-off between operational conservatism and task efficiency:
- **Conservatism:** A lower $\alpha$ (e.g., 0.47) resulted in a highly conservative monitor, significantly elevating the Unsafe prediction accuracy (up to 99.38%). This extreme safety margin, however, implied a utility penalty (lower safe accuracy).
- **Utility:** A higher $\alpha$ prioritized nominal task utility, allowing the robot to operate closer to the boundary but potentially at the cost of a slightly reduced unsafe prediction accuracy. This tuning capability allows for balancing safety requirements with operational flexibility.

**F. Real-World Experiments**

Validation on the physical Unitree G1 humanoid using a hold-out test set of 5 unseen nominal trajectories (Table V) showed:
- **Conservative Boundary:** The PRISM-trained monitor inherently learned a more conservative decision boundary, reflected by a lower Safe accuracy (70.0%) but a high Unsafe accuracy (94.1%).
- **Safety Maximization:** For safety-critical hardware deployments, where false negatives are significantly more costly than false positives, this conservative prediction margin is desirable. The results validate PRISM's ability to maximize operational safety while minimizing the need for hazardous real-world data collection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of humanoid robotics by addressing a critical challenge in their safe deployment, particularly in human-centered environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Verifiable Fail-Safe Operation for Humanoids:** The work formalizes the concept of "safe-stoppability" as a policy-dependent stochastic reach-avoid property, moving beyond simplistic power-cut E-stops that are unsuitable for humanoids. By providing a data-driven method to characterize the safe-stoppable envelope (SSE), it offers a verifiable framework for ensuring that a humanoid can reliably transition to a minimum-risk condition during an emergency. This capability is a significant step towards meeting stringent industrial safety requirements (e.g., relevant to Safety Integrity Levels or SIL).

2. **Proactive Safety and Runtime Assurance:** The developed neural monitor facilitates a shift from reactive to proactive safety. Instead of merely responding to an external E-stop trigger, the monitor continuously assesses the robot's current state. This allows for autonomous triggering of emergency behaviors *before* the robot enters a state from which a safe stop is no longer guaranteed, thereby preventing catastrophic failures like falls or collisions. This proactive runtime assurance mechanism enhances overall system reliability and safety.

3. **Data Efficiency and Reduced Real-World Risk:** The PRISM framework, through its iterative importance sampling strategy, significantly improves data efficiency. By concentrating simulation-based data collection on safety-critical boundary regions (where the monitor's uncertainty or error is high), it reduces the need for exhaustive and often redundant exploration of trivially safe states. This targeted approach minimizes the reliance on expensive and potentially hazardous real-world failure trials, making the characterization of complex safety envelopes more practical and scalable.

4. **Improved Sim-to-Real Transferability:** The methodology incorporates strategies to mitigate the sim-to-real gap, such as initializing simulated fallback rollouts with real-robot state logs and employing domain randomization. The consistent topological structure of decision boundaries observed across simulation and real-world experiments validates the monitor's physical grounding and transferability, which is crucial for real-world deployment.

5. **Industrial Relevance and Scalability:** By demonstrating the ability to achieve high prediction accuracy for unsafe states (exceeding 99% in certain configurations by tuning the conservatism threshold $\alpha$), the framework suggests potential for meeting industrial safety standards like SIL 2. This capability is vital for scaling the deployment of bipedal humanoids in manufacturing and other complex environments where human-robot collaboration is expected.

6. **Foundation for Future Safety Systems:** The research lays a foundation for future advancements in humanoid safety. The characterized SSE can be used to systematically refine fallback controllers, progressively enlarging the robot's operational envelope. Furthermore, the concept of a safe-stoppability monitor could be integrated into multi-stage emergency behaviors or layered fallback architectures, where secondary recovery policies might first steer the system back into the SSE before executing a complete stop. This iterative co-design between monitoring and control holds promise for enhancing broader recoverability and runtime safety.
