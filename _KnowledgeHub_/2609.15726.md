# Bench2Dex: Benchmarking Visuo-Tactile Bimanual Dexterous Manipulation Across Dexterous Hands

> [!summary] Summary
> Bench2Dex is an Isaac Lab simulation benchmark for visuo-tactile bimanual dexterous manipulation spanning 12 dexterous hands, 26 long-horizon tool-use and articulated tasks, and about 1.3K human-teleoperated demonstrations. A surface-aligned ray-casting interface converts local contact geometry into a shared image-like tactile map across heterogeneous hands. Evaluating ACT, Diffusion Policy, pi-0.5 and GR00T N1.5, matched-scene success drops sharply under controlled scene perturbations.

## Problem

- Most manipulation benchmarks are built around one fixed platform, gripper or hand design, so their protocols cannot compare dexterous policies across differing arm-hand morphologies.
- Vision-based tactile signals depend closely on fingertip shape and sensor layout, so contact data recorded on one dexterous hand does not transfer to another.
- No prior benchmark jointly combines multiple bimanual dexterous embodiments, teleoperated collection, vision-based tactile sensing, long-horizon tool use, and articulated-object interaction in one setting.

## Method

- A unified **surface-aligned tactile interface** casts rays from precomputed contact-surface points along inward normals, encoding first-hit penetration depth as 8-bit 240x240 tactile images across twelve hands.
- Teleoperation stack pairs a **Manus** glove with ==ARKit== wrist tracking, ==DexPilot== retargeting over 21 hand keypoints, and ==Pinocchio== closed-loop ==inverse kinematics==, merged into absolute bimanual joint commands.
- Evaluation suite defines stable reach-and-stop success rate, ==latched stage completion rate== for partial long-horizon progress, and seven perturbations grouped into ==invariance== and ==equivariance== channels.

## Results

- Across **20,800** evaluation episodes, GR00T N1.5 leads matched scenes with **48.5%** success, ahead of ACT **29.5%**, pi-0.5 **27.3%**, and Diffusion Policy **12.9%**.
- Under combined perturbation every policy degrades: GR00T reaches **19.8%** and pi-0.5 **19.7%**, with perturbed-to-matched retention ratios of **40.9%** and **72.1%** respectively.
- Latched stage completion exceeds success rate by **15.5** to **19.0** points for all policies, and **three** of **26** settings yield zero perturbed success everywhere.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Splitting robustness into invariance and equivariance axes localizes failure: pretrained VLA policies resist nuisance shifts better, scratch-trained policies tolerate geometric shifts comparatively better.
> - Aggregate success and per-task win counts disagree, so a single leaderboard number hides which policy actually wins more individual settings under shift.
> - A shared simulated contact representation enables cross-hand study before tactile hardware converges, but remains uncalibrated against physical sensors, leaving real-world transfer unverified.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Bench2Dex addresses a gap at the intersection of dexterous manipulation, bimanual robot learning, tactile sensing, teleoperation, and policy generalization. Existing manipulation benchmarks such as Meta-World, RLBench, LIBERO, RoboCasa, and ManiSkill provide standardized environments and controlled evaluation, but most are based on fixed robot embodiments and parallel-jaw grippers. They generally do not support comparisons among heterogeneous multi-finger hands or provide vision-based tactile observations.

Other projects have focused specifically on dexterous or bimanual manipulation. Bi-DexHands studies reinforcement learning with two Shadow Hands, DexMimicGen supports demonstration generation for several dexterous embodiments, DexJoCo includes tool-use tasks across two embodiments, and DexVerse provides a larger task collection for a single dexterous platform. These efforts address portions of the problem but do not combine multiple dexterous hands, bimanual tasks, teleoperated demonstrations, tactile sensing, and controlled distribution shifts in one evaluation pipeline.

The tactile-learning literature has developed both physical sensors, including GelSight and DIGIT, and simulators such as TACTO and Taxim. Recent work also investigates visuo-tactile representation learning, tactile-conditioned diffusion policies, tactile-language-action models, and tactile benchmarks. However, tactile observations are strongly dependent on sensor geometry, fingertip shape, elastomer structure, and sensor layout. Consequently, tactile data collected from one hand is not directly comparable with data from another hand.

Bench2Dex is positioned as a simulation framework for studying this cross-embodiment problem. Its central design choice is not to simulate any particular physical tactile sensor. Instead, it defines a shared surface-aligned representation based on contact geometry and penetration depth. This makes the observations structurally comparable across hands while explicitly leaving hardware-specific calibration and sim-to-real validation for future work.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to establish a common benchmark for bimanual dexterous manipulation across diverse robot-hand designs. The benchmark is intended to support both demonstration-based policy learning and controlled evaluation of task completion, partial progress, robustness, safety, and generalization.

The motivation has several components. First, dexterous manipulation involves contact-rich interactions that are difficult to infer from external cameras. Examples include maintaining a grasp, stabilizing an object with one hand while manipulating it with the other, transferring an object between hands, using tools, and interacting with articulated objects. Tactile sensing can provide information about contact location and interaction state that is unavailable or ambiguous in vision.

Second, dexterous hands differ substantially in kinematics, finger count, contact-surface geometry, thumb configuration, and sensor arrangement. A policy may fail on a new hand because its observation and action spaces differ, rather than because the underlying manipulation strategy is inadequate. A benchmark that uses matched tasks across many embodiments can help separate algorithmic limitations from embodiment-specific effects.

Third, long-horizon bimanual tasks require more informative evaluation than a binary terminal-success measure. A policy may complete several valid stages and then fail at a later operation. Bench2Dex therefore introduces stage-level progress measurements in addition to stable final success.

Finally, the authors seek to distinguish two forms of generalization. Invariance concerns changes that should not alter the correct action, such as lighting, background, tabletop texture, distractors, or camera pose. Equivariance concerns changes in task geometry, such as object pose or table height, where the policy should modify its actions appropriately. Separating these axes is intended to make robustness failures easier to interpret.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Benchmark Scope and Task Design

Bench2Dex is built in Isaac Lab and contains 26 long-horizon bimanual tasks, 12 robot embodiments, and approximately 1,300 human-teleoperated demonstrations. The embodiments combine different arms and hands, including Panda, UR5, xArm7, IIWA7, RM65, and JAKA arms paired with hands such as Allegro, Shadow, Schunk, LEAP, Sharpa, Orca, RH5DG2, RH56DFX, Revo2, Wuji, Ability, and DexHand021.

Tasks cover object arrangement, tool use, pouring, articulated-object interaction, object transfer, sorting, container loading, piano playing, puzzle assembly, and multi-stage household activities. Examples include opening a refrigerator, transferring and pouring a wine bottle, placing objects into a microwave, loading a cleaner into a box, and performing a sequence of piano key presses.

#### 3.2. Teleoperation and Data Collection

Human operators control the simulated robots using Manus gloves and ARKit wrist tracking. The Manus system provides a 25-node hand skeleton, which is converted to 21 MediaPipe-style keypoints. DexPilot retargeting maps these hand motions to target joint angles for each supported dexterous hand. ARKit provides wrist translation and orientation, while a Pinocchio-based closed-loop inverse-kinematics controller converts the desired wrist pose into arm joint targets. Hand and arm commands are combined into a full bimanual joint-position action.

During online teleoperation, the system records the action, robot state, object state, task metadata, and evaluator signals. Each trajectory begins from a home configuration to reduce discontinuities and reset teleoperation filters and tracking references.

The remaining sensing modalities are generated through offline deterministic replay. Recorded joint trajectories and initial object states are replayed with the same simulation seed. Six camera viewpoints provide RGB and depth observations: chest, overhead, stereo-left, stereo-right, left-wrist, and right-wrist cameras. The replay process also produces segmentation labels, 2D and 3D bounding boxes, occupancy grids, object states, and tactile observations. The resulting synchronized records are stored in HDF5 format.

#### 3.3. Unified Tactile Representation

For each hand, the authors reconstruct the intended contact surfaces, such as rubber pads, elastomer surfaces, fingertip shells, or force-sensor covers. These surfaces are represented as regular grids of points with inward-facing surface normals.

During replay, rays are cast from each surface point toward the interior of the finger. If a task object has penetrated beyond the nominal contact surface, the first-hit distance measures contact penetration depth. A reverse-ray consistency check removes geometrically invalid samples. The signal is stored as a binary contact mask, a metric depth map, and a compact 8-bit image-like tactile map at 240 × 240 resolution. A piecewise quantization scheme gives higher precision to small contact depths, followed by Gaussian smoothing.

This interface preserves the local geometry of each hand while providing a common data structure. It is therefore a representation-level standardization rather than a sensor-level physical simulation.

#### 3.4. Policies and Evaluation

The study evaluates ACT, Diffusion Policy, π0.5, and GR00T N1.5. ACT and Diffusion Policy are trained from scratch, while π0.5 and GR00T N1.5 are fine-tuned from publicly available checkpoints. The reported baseline policies use multi-view RGB and proprioceptive observations; they do not use the tactile modality in the experiments summarized in the main results. The benchmark is nevertheless designed to support future tactile-conditioned policies.

The primary metric is stable success rate, requiring the terminal predicate to remain true for a default dwell time of 0.5 seconds. The latched stage completion rate measures the fraction of dependency-valid task stages reached at any point during the episode. Additional metrics cover success-conditional completion time, safe success, drops, high-speed violations, tool selection, grasp-related behavior, and motion characteristics.

Each of the 26 task–embodiment settings is evaluated with 50 rollouts for each of four channels—None, Equi., Inv., and Full—across four policies, producing 20,800 evaluation episodes. None reproduces the matched anchor scene. Equi. changes object pose and table height. Inv. changes background, texture, lighting, distractors, and camera pose. Full changes both groups.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Under matched scenes, GR00T N1.5 achieves 631 successes out of 1,300 rollouts, corresponding to 48.5%. ACT records 383 successes, or 29.5%; π0.5 records 355, or 27.3%; and Diffusion Policy records 168, or 12.9%. GR00T has the highest observed success on 23 of the 26 task–embodiment settings, while π0.5 leads on two and ties GR00T on one.

Performance decreases under the combined Full perturbation channel. GR00T records 258 successes, or 19.8%, while π0.5 records 256, or 19.7%. ACT obtains 169 successes, or 13.0%, and Diffusion Policy obtains 50, or 3.8%. Although GR00T has a slightly higher aggregate rate, π0.5 leads on more individual tasks under Full perturbation, indicating that aggregate and task-level rankings do not always coincide.

The four-channel mean success rates are 28.3% for GR00T, 20.3% for π0.5, 18.0% for ACT, and 7.1% for Diffusion Policy. The corresponding mean latched stage completion rates are 47.3%, 36.1%, 33.5%, and 23.0%. The higher LSCR values show that policies frequently make partial, dependency-valid progress without completing the entire task. For example, on Jigsaw Puzzle Assembly, some policies have zero stable successes but nonzero stage completion. This demonstrates that binary success alone would conceal meaningful differences in behavior.

The generalization results are policy dependent. All four policies perform best in the None condition. ACT and Diffusion Policy show higher success under Equi. than Inv., whereas π0.5 and GR00T show the reverse pattern. The observed Full-to-None success-rate ratios are 44.1% for ACT, 29.8% for Diffusion Policy, 72.1% for π0.5, and 40.9% for GR00T. These comparisons are descriptive rather than causal because the perturbation channels are sampled distributions and are not necessarily paired by identical difficulty.

Task-level performance is heterogeneous. Microwave Bowl Loading has the highest combined Full-condition success across the four policies. Jigsaw Puzzle Assembly, Fridge Fruit Shelf Sorting, and Soup Serving produce no Full-condition successes for any evaluated policy. Other tasks reveal policy-specific strengths, such as ACT leading Citrus Plate Loading, π0.5 leading Tool Box Loading, and GR00T leading Gaming Desk Setup.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

Bench2Dex provides a unified infrastructure for studying dexterous manipulation across multiple hand morphologies. Its contributions are methodological as well as empirical: it links teleoperation, multimodal replay, contact-geometry-based tactile sensing, executable task evaluation, and structured robustness testing. The shared tactile format could facilitate comparisons of tactile encoders and visuo-tactile policies that would otherwise be confounded by incompatible sensor layouts.

The benchmark may also support research on cross-embodiment transfer, policy adaptation, stage-aware learning, tactile-conditioned action generation, and failure analysis. Its separation of invariance and equivariance factors offers a framework for diagnosing whether policies are sensitive to nuisance appearance changes or unable to adapt to altered task geometry.

Several limitations constrain interpretation. The tactile observations are simulated and are not calibrated against matching physical sensors. They should not be treated as replacements for real tactile measurements. The evaluated policies do not use tactile input, so the experiments establish baseline manipulation performance rather than demonstrating a benefit from touch. Tasks and embodiments are not fully factorially crossed, which prevents isolation of embodiment effects. The 50 rollouts per evaluation cell are not independent retraining replicates and therefore do not measure between-training variability. Finally, the results are obtained entirely in simulation, leaving transfer to physical hardware unresolved. Future work will need hardware-specific calibration, real-world validation, and experiments that directly compare visual-only and visuo-tactile policies across embodiments.
