# MOBIUS: A Multi-Modal Bipedal Robot that can Walk, Crawl, Climb, and Roll

> [!summary] Summary
> MOBIUS is a multi-modal bipedal robot developed by researchers from MERL, ETH Zurich, and UCLA, capable of walking, crawling, rolling, and dynamic free climbing using a single, non-reconfigurable morphology. It successfully performs pinch-grasp pull-ups supporting its full body weight, leverages reinforcement learning for robust locomotion, and employs a multi-modal planner that shows crawling is more energy-efficient (65 J/m) than bipedal movement (104 J/m).

## Problem

- Most existing legged robots are highly specialized for particular locomotion regimes, limiting their adaptability across diverse and complex terrains.
- Few robotic systems achieve dynamic free climbing in vertical, discrete environments without sacrificing general ground mobility or relying on less dexterous adhesion/passive grasping.
- Designing a single, non-reconfigurable robotic platform capable of multiple locomotion and manipulation modalities presents challenges in balancing conflicting kinematic, force, and actuator requirements.

## Method

- Developed **MOBIUS**, a 10.3 kg bipedal robot with 6-DoF arms featuring two-finger spine-based grippers and 4-DoF legs, designed for both robust locomotion and dexterous manipulation.
- Implemented a unified hybrid control stack combining ==model-free reinforcement learning (RL)== for dynamic locomotion, an ==adaptive admittance controller with a Reference Governor== for safe, contact-rich manipulation, and a ==Mixed Integer Quadratic Constraint Programming (MIQCP) planner== for autonomous mode selection.
- Utilized a co-design approach for hardware and control, incorporating passive features like curved back rails for fall recovery and balancing limb kinematics for versatility across modes.

## Results

- MOBIUS successfully demonstrated bipedal walking, crawling, rolling (achieving **0.7 m/s**, **600%** faster than bipedal), and dynamic free climbing, including pinch-grasp pull-ups from a dead-hang supporting its full body weight (**9 out of 10** trials successful).
- Reinforcement learning policies provided greater robustness to external disturbances (stable up to **0.25 m/s** impulse for bipedal motion vs. **0.05 m/s** for model-based control) and improved velocity tracking across various locomotion directions.
- The MIQCP planner effectively selected locomotion modes, identifying crawling as the most energy-efficient (**65 J/m**) compared to bipedal (**104 J/m**), and the Reference Governor improved force tracking by **45%** while ensuring safety during contact-rich tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Tightly integrating hardware morphology with advanced control and planning architectures enables a single robotic platform to achieve a wide range of diverse and challenging multi-modal tasks, including dynamic free climbing.
> - Extensive domain randomization in simulation is crucial for successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of reinforcement learning policies, leading to significantly higher robustness against external disturbances in bipedal locomotion.
> - A high-level planner combining path planning with discrete mode selection can optimize for exploration, goal reaching, and energy efficiency, revealing the inherent trade-offs between different locomotion modes (e.g., crawling's energy efficiency versus rolling's speed).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has produced various specialized platforms for locomotion, including bipeds, quadrupeds, and hopping robots, each excelling in particular environments or tasks. While these robots demonstrate high capabilities within their specific domains, their specialized nature often limits their adaptability across diverse and complex terrains. For instance, bipedal robots are designed for human-made environments, while quadrupeds offer stability on uneven ground. Concurrently, advances in robotic manipulation have enabled "loco-grasping" behaviors, allowing robots to move and interact with objects. However, a limited number of systems address the specific challenge of free climbing in discrete, vertical environments, with most relying on adhesion or passive spine-based grasping, which can restrict dexterity and dynamic capabilities. Existing climbing robots often sacrifice general ground mobility to achieve their climbing specialization.

MOBIUS aims to address these limitations by developing a single, non-reconfigurable robotic platform capable of multiple locomotion and manipulation modalities. This approach contrasts with prior multi-modal robots that often achieve versatility through mechanical reconfiguration, part swapping, or mode-specific appendages, which can increase complexity and mass. By integrating bipedal walking, crawling, rolling, and dynamic free climbing, the work seeks to expand a robot's interaction capabilities, workspace, and traversability without altering its fundamental morphology. This research contributes to the development of highly adaptable robots for applications such as search and rescue, inspection, and exploration, where transitioning seamlessly between varied terrains and tasks is beneficial.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce MOBIUS, a unified robotic platform designed to perform bipedal walking, crawling, rolling, and dynamic free climbing within a single, non-reconfigurable morphology. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Overcoming Specialization Limitations:** Existing legged robots typically excel in a single locomotion regime, which restricts their adaptability across continuous and discrete terrain. The authors aim to create a robot that can transition smoothly between various modes to handle diverse environmental challenges without requiring hardware changes.
2. **Enabling Complex Loco-Manipulation:** While progress has been made in loco-grasping, the extreme case of dynamic free climbing with dexterous manipulation in vertical environments remains largely unaddressed by versatile platforms. MOBIUS is motivated to demonstrate grasping-based climbing and pull-ups using finger grippers on a bipedal robot, a capability that few, if any, mid- to large-scale bipedal robots have achieved from a full dead-hang configuration.
3. **Balancing Performance Trade-offs:** The design of a multi-modal robot inherently involves conflicting requirements regarding kinematics, force capability, and actuator selection. The project is motivated to develop a morphology and control system that balances efficiency, stability, and manipulation across different modes. For instance, the robot must support its full body weight on a single limb during bipedal walking while also being capable of power-intensive and dexterous contact-rich climbing.
4. **Integration of Morphology, Planning, and Control:** A core motivation is to demonstrate the importance of tightly integrating hardware design with advanced control and planning architectures. This includes combining reinforcement learning for dynamic locomotion, adaptive admittance control for compliant manipulation, and high-level planners for autonomous mode selection, all within a cohesive software stack. This integration is expected to enable autonomous transitions between modes without manual intervention.

In summary, the key objectives are to design, develop, and validate a robot that achieves a wide range of locomotion and manipulation tasks, including challenging vertical mobility, using a single, unchanging hardware configuration, supported by an integrated and adaptive control system.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of MOBIUS involved a co-design approach integrating hardware morphology, low-level control, and high-level planning.

#### 3.1. Hardware Design and System Mechanisms

MOBIUS is a bipedal robot weighing 10.3 kg, designed for multi-modal operations. Its mechanical architecture includes:
- **Limbs:** Two 6-DoF arms equipped with two-finger spine-based grippers and two 4-DoF legs with flat feet. This hybrid parallel mechanism is intended to withstand impact loads during bipedal locomotion while providing dexterity for climbing. The grippers serve as both dexterous end-effectors for manipulation and load-bearing contacts during crawling and climbing.
- **Actuators:** High gear-ratio spur gear actuators are used for compactness and weight efficiency. Fatigue analysis using AGMA formulations informed the design to mitigate risks associated with repeated impact loads, with additional bearings integrated to reduce stress concentrations.
- **Passive Features:** Curved back rails are incorporated to passively guide the robot into recoverable postures after backward falls, reducing the need for active control in such scenarios and enabling rolling locomotion.
- **Kinematics:** Limb kinematics are balanced to provide both velocity and force isotropy for bipedal and crawling configurations, while maintaining dexterity for climbing and manipulation.

#### 3.2. Software Architectures for Planning and Control

The software architecture is a unified hybrid control stack operating at various frequencies, coordinating different modules for distinct tasks.

#### 3.2.1. Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Locomotion

- **Application:** Model-free reinforcement learning (RL) is used primarily for bipedal locomotion, where the robot's asymmetric mass distribution (forearms are 55% heavier than the lower body) complicates model-based control. It is also applied to crawling locomotion.
- **Formulation:** Locomotion is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), with policies trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- **Training:** Training occurs in two stages: initial training on flat terrain, followed by fine-tuning on rough terrain. Domain randomization (mass, friction, actuator gains/biases, observation delays) and disturbances (e.g., simulated kicks) are extensively used to improve sim-to-real transferability.
- **Observation and Action Space:** The observation vector includes estimated trunk state (orientation, angular/linear velocity), joint encoder values, previous action, desired linear and angular velocities, and a history of 15 past observations. Actions consist of target joint positions, filtered using a moving average for hardware smoothness.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function encourages stability (penalizing vertical motion, pitch/roll rates), tracking accuracy (rewarding planar/yaw velocity tracking, standing still), and smoothness (penalizing action changes, foot slip), while also promoting proper stepping (rewarding foot air time).

#### 3.2.2. Grasping Force Control

- **Mechanism:** An admittance controller is employed for contact-rich tasks, such as pull-ups, mapping measured wrench to desired motion. It is built upon an auto-tuning framework that adapts controller gains online to track profiles, minimize slip, and avoid kinematic singularities.
- **Safety Guarantees (Reference Governor):** To ensure safety, a Reference Governor (RG) module is integrated. The RG modifies the commanded reference in real-time to prevent state and control limit violations. It operates based on a precomputed Maximal Output Admissible Set (MOAS), which defines safe operating regions by simulating closed-loop control laws offline and retaining trajectories that satisfy position, velocity, and wrench constraints. Online, the RG queries a KD-Tree of the MOAS to find the closest admissible point and adjust references if the current state is outside the safe set.

#### 3.2.3. High-Level Multi-Modal Planning

- **Problem Formulation:** A Mixed Integer Quadratic Constraint Programming (MIQCP) approach is used for high-level planning, enabling joint optimization of grid-based path planning and discrete mode selection. This allows the robot to autonomously select locomotion modes (crawling, biped, rolling) over a given time horizon.
- **Objective Function:** The planner's objective balances exploration (maximizing visited grid cells), goal reaching (minimizing distance to goal), and energy efficiency (penalizing costly modes). Crawling is penalized least, biped more, and rolling most due to localization instability, biasing the planner towards crawling by default.
- **Constraints:** The MIQCP includes constraints for grid cell visitation, state propagation based on the active mode's step set, terrain-dependent mode restrictions (e.g., crawling on rough terrain), and obstacle avoidance (rectangular forbidden regions).

#### 3.3. Simulation and Hardware Integration

- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Policies trained in the high-fidelity MuJoCo simulation are transferred to hardware.
- **Control Frequencies:** RL policies run at 60 Hz, outputting smoothed joint positions for 300 Hz low-level PID motor controllers. The admittance controller also operates at 300 Hz.
- **State Estimation:** A robust real-time state estimator fuses T265 VIO-SLAM (200 Hz) with the OptiState algorithm (leveraging joint encoders, IMU, single-rigid body model, and vision transformer autoencoder for depth images).
- **Visual-Servo Control:** An object detection model (YOLOv8) runs at 30 Hz with an RGB-D camera to track handle centroids for pre-manipulation positioning during climbing tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results validate MOBIUS's multi-modal capabilities across various terrains and tasks.

#### 4.1. Simulation and Hardware Integration

The sim-to-real transfer for RL policies was successful without zero-shot adjustments, attributed to comprehensive domain randomization. The state estimation system provided robust real-time control across all modes. The MIQCP planner solved 20m by 20m maps for 20-second trajectories in approximately 10-11 seconds using Gurobi.

#### 4.2. Bipedal Locomotion Performance

- **Robustness:** The RL policy demonstrated higher robustness to external disturbances compared to a model-based controller. While the model-based controller failed at approximately 0.05 m/s impulse disturbance, the RL policy maintained stability up to 0.25 m/s. This difference is attributed to the RL policy's implicit capture of MOBIUS's complex, asymmetric dynamics.
- **Velocity Tracking:** Hardware experiments showed the RL policy improved velocity tracking accuracy for lateral, backward, and rotational commands compared to the model-based approach. The RL policy exhibited better disturbance rejection and motion decoupling, with the robot achieving up to 0.1 m/s laterally and 0.25 rad/s in yaw. However, the RL policy showed slightly worse forward x-velocity tracking compared to the model-based approach.

#### 4.3. Crawling Locomotion Analysis

- **Velocity Tracking:** For crawling, both model-based and RL approaches yielded comparable results, though RL generally improved tracking for sideways and backward motions.
- **Challenges:** The RL policy's tracking for crawling was noted as less effective than for bipedal locomotion, potentially due to the complex contact dynamics of the gripper-equipped arms used as front limbs and simulator inaccuracies in modeling their contact behavior. Crawling also operates at higher speeds (up to 0.4 m/s laterally, 0.6 rad/s in yaw), making tracking more dynamically challenging. Asymmetry in limb morphology (arms vs. legs) was identified as a contributing factor.

#### 4.4. Rolling Locomotion

- **Speed and Efficiency:** Rolling was the fastest locomotion mode, achieving an instantaneous speed of 0.7 m/s, which is 600% faster than bipedal walking and 75% faster than crawling. It was also found to be more energy-efficient than bipedal mode.
- **Stability and Control:** Rolling is described as the least stable mode due to its passive nature during the roll, which impedes accurate state estimation and perfect control.

#### 4.5. Multi-Modal High-Level Planning Results

- **Energy per Visited Cell (EVC):** The MIQCP planner was evaluated using EVC as a metric for balancing exploration, goal reaching, and energy consumption.
- **Optimal Configuration:** Low EVC was achieved when exploration and goal objectives were equally weighted, with an average mode-switch rate of approximately 25% per time step.
- **Mode Comparison:** When restricted to single modes, crawling achieved the lowest EVC (5.11 J/cell), significantly outperforming bipedal (26.89 J/cell) and rolling (21.96 J/cell) locomotion. Rolling reached the goal fastest due to its speed but provided limited coverage due to sensor limitations during the roll. Bipedal locomotion had the highest failure rate due to requiring longer horizons for its limited step distance.
- **Energy Efficiency:** Bipedal motion required approximately 104 J/m, higher than crawling at 65 J/m, primarily due to increased potential energy costs and higher per-actuator loads in an elevated posture. Crawling distributed effort across more limbs, reducing peak actuator loads.

#### 4.6. Vertical Mobility

- **Vertical Climbing on a Kid’s Slide:** MOBIUS successfully climbed a children’s slide by pinch-grasping side rails and stepping upward. 8 out of 10 trials were successful; failures related to foot advancement. A visual-servo controller aligned end-effectors to handles, followed by open-loop trajectory execution.
- **Pinch Grasp Pull-up:** MOBIUS performed pinch-grasp pull-ups on aluminum bars, supporting its full body weight from a dead-hang. Spine-based engagement provided a dominant shear force of 124.2 N perpendicular to the bar, with minimal reliance on friction. 9 out of 10 trials were successful.
- **Auto-Tuning Admittance Control and Safety:** Auto-tuning improved force tracking error by 45% during pull-ups. The Reference Governor (RG) successfully enforced safety, keeping end-effector position and velocity within kinematic limits during force control, whereas without RG, constraint violations occurred.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MOBIUS platform demonstrates the feasibility of a multi-modal bipedal robot capable of a wide range of locomotion and manipulation tasks, including walking, crawling, rolling, and dynamic climbing, within a single, non-reconfigurable morphology. The integration of a compact, fatigue-resistant hardware design with high-DoF limbs and a cohesive control stack (combining model-free reinforcement learning, adaptive admittance force control, and MIQCP-based high-level decision making) represents a contribution to robot versatility.

This work suggests that tightly co-designing morphology, control, and planning can enable robots to adapt to diverse and complex environments, potentially expanding their utility in applications such as search and rescue, inspection, and exploration, where varied terrain and tasks are common. The successful demonstration of dynamic free climbing and pinch-grasp pull-ups by a bipedal robot with finger grippers highlights an advancement in dexterous loco-manipulation for challenging vertical environments. The use of a Reference Governor for safety in contact-rich tasks also provides a method for ensuring reliable operation in scenarios involving high forces and potential constraint violations.

Despite its demonstrated versatility, the system involves trade-offs. The dual use of grippers for both grasping and locomotion may increase mechanical complexity and wear. The reliance on reinforcement learning controllers, while effective, can be sensitive to novel surfaces if domain randomization is insufficient. State estimation for rolling locomotion was noted as unreliable. Additionally, the MIQCP-based planner operates offline, which limits real-time reactivity in dynamic environments, and bipedal locomotion remains relatively energy-intensive. Addressing these limitations in future work, such as real-time replanning with vision, long-horizon autonomy, and hardware refinements for energy efficiency, could further enhance the impact of such multi-modal robotic systems.
