# Transformers without Normalization

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces Dynamic Tanh (DyT), a simple element-wise function designed as a drop-in replacement for normalization layers in Transformers. Models equipped with DyT achieve performance comparable to or exceeding their normalized counterparts across a wide range of tasks including computer vision, large language models, and speech processing, often without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## Problem

- Modern deep neural networks, especially Transformers, are widely believed to require normalization layers (e.g., Layer Normalization, RMSNorm) for stable and effective training, which can complicate architectures and potentially limit optimization.
- Previous attempts to train deep models without normalization often involve complex initialization schemes, architectural modifications, or extensive hyperparameter tuning, frequently resulting in performance trade-offs or fragile training processes.
- Understanding the precise functional role that makes normalization layers indispensable beyond statistical stabilization remained an open question, hindering simpler alternatives.

## Method

- The paper introduces ==Dynamic Tanh (DyT)==, a simple element-wise operation defined as `DyT(x) = γ * tanh(αx) + β`, where `α` is a ==learnable scalar== and `γ`, `β` are learnable per-channel vectors.
- DyT serves as a direct ==drop-in replacement== for normalization layers like Layer Normalization (LN) and RMSNorm within Transformer architectures, requiring no statistical computation.
- The `tanh` function provides the observed ==S-shaped squashing effect== on extreme values, while the learnable `α` parameter dynamically scales the input to adapt to varying activation ranges.

## Results

- Transformers equipped with **DyT** achieve performance comparable to or slightly surpassing normalized counterparts across diverse tasks, including supervised and self-supervised computer vision (ViT, ConvNeXt, MAE, DINO), diffusion models (DiT), large language models (LLaMA **7B**-**70B**), speech (wav2vec 2.0), and DNA sequence modeling, often without extensive hyperparameter re-tuning.
- **DyT** consistently outperforms other normalization-free methods like Fixup, SkipInit, and σReparam in empirical evaluations on ViT and MAE, demonstrating superior performance with greater simplicity.
- Ablation studies confirm that both the `tanh` squashing function and the learnable `α` parameter are essential for stable training and strong performance, with `α` values correlating with the inverse of input activation standard deviations in trained models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Layer Normalization (LN) and RMSNorm, despite their statistical computation, collectively exhibit an S-shaped, tanh-like input-output mapping across activations in trained deep networks, particularly in deeper layers.
> - The learnable scalar parameter 'α' in DyT dynamically scales input activations similar to how normalization layers adjust magnitudes based on variance, effectively learning to track the inverse of the standard deviation (1/std) of inputs.
> - The functional squashing of extreme activation values via a smooth non-linearity like `tanh`, combined with adaptive scaling, appears to be a critical mechanism for stable training that DyT successfully emulates, rather than complex statistical normalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Executive Summary

This paper, "Transformers without Normalization," introduces Dynamic Tanh (DyT), a remarkably simple, element-wise operation designed as a drop-in replacement for ubiquitous normalization layers in Transformer architectures. DyT, defined as `DyT(x) = tanh(αx)` (where α is a learnable scalar), is inspired by the empirical observation that Layer Normalization (LN) and Root Mean Square Normalization (RMSNorm) often produce S-shaped, tanh-like input-output mappings in deep networks. The authors demonstrate that Transformers equipped with DyT can achieve performance comparable to or even surpassing their normalized counterparts across a wide spectrum of tasks, including supervised and self-supervised computer vision, diffusion models, large language models, speech processing, and DNA sequence modeling, often without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning. This work challenges the long-held belief that normalization layers are indispensable for stable and effective training of modern deep neural networks, providing novel insights into their functional role.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 2.1. The Ubiquity and Perceived Indispensability of Normalization Layers

Normalization layers, beginning with Batch Normalization (BN) in 2015, have become fundamental components in almost all modern neural networks. Their widespread adoption is attributed to significant empirical benefits: accelerating and stabilizing training convergence, improving generalization, and mitigating issues like exploding/vanishing gradients and internal covariate shift. Layer Normalization (LN), introduced in 2016, and Root Mean Square Normalization (RMSNorm) are particularly prevalent in Transformer architectures, which dominate various fields like natural language processing and increasingly, computer vision.

The conventional understanding is that normalization layers are not just beneficial but "crucial, if not indispensable," for training deep networks, especially as models become wider and deeper. This belief is implicitly reinforced by the trend where novel architectures often seek to replace attention or convolution layers but almost universally retain normalization layers. Their role has been theorized to include stabilizing gradient flow, reducing sensitivity to initialization, moderating outlier eigenvalues, auto-tuning learning rates, and smoothing the loss landscape. Recent work has also highlighted the strong non-linearities introduced by LN, which enhance representational capacity and mimic biological neuron saturation.

#### 2.2. Previous Efforts to Train Without Normalization

Despite their perceived necessity, there has been a continuous line of research exploring ways to train deep models without explicit normalization layers. These efforts generally fall into a few categories:
- **Initialization-based Methods:** Techniques like Fixup and SkipInit aim to stabilize training by carefully adjusting initial parameter values to control gradient and activation magnitudes. These methods attempt to create conditions at initialization that mimic the stabilizing effects of normalization.
- **Weight-Normalization-based Methods:** Approaches like `σReparam` impose constraints on network weights throughout training to maintain stable learning dynamics. This typically involves reparameterizing weights or controlling their spectral norm.
- **Alternative Architectures/Activations:** Some works propose entirely new activation functions (e.g., in Self-Normalizing Neural Networks) or modifications to block structures that inherently reduce the reliance on normalization.
- **Heavy Regularization/Hyperparameter Tuning:** In some cases, normalization-free training has been achieved but often requires significant tuning of learning rates, extensive data augmentation, and regularization techniques (e.g., adaptive gradient clipping, dropout), suggesting a more fragile training process.

While these methods have shown some success, they often involve complex modifications to initialization, architecture, or training protocols, and in many cases, come with a performance trade-off compared to normalized counterparts.

#### 2.3. Position of Current Work

This paper directly challenges the "indispensable" notion by offering a remarkably simple, drop-in replacement for normalization layers. Unlike many prior attempts that involve intricate initialization schemes or reparameterizations, DyT is an element-wise operation that directly aims to replicate the *observed functional behavior* of normalization layers, specifically their S-shaped, squashing effect on extreme values, rather than their statistical computation. By focusing on emulation of behavior rather than statistical normalization, the authors propose a much simpler alternative that reportedly matches or exceeds performance with minimal architectural or hyperparameter changes. This approach marks a significant shift in how the role of normalization is understood and potentially implemented.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **demonstrate that Transformers can be trained effectively without traditional normalization layers** (Layer Normalization or RMSNorm). More specifically, the authors aim to:

1. **Challenge Conventional Wisdom:** Directly refute the widely held belief that normalization layers are indispensable for the stable and high-performance training of deep neural networks, particularly Transformers.
2. **Propose a Simple Alternative:** Introduce a conceptually and computationally simpler element-wise operation, Dynamic Tanh (DyT), as a direct replacement for normalization layers.
3. **Validate Across Diverse Settings:** Empirically demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of DyT-equipped Transformers across a broad range of tasks, model sizes, and domains (e.g., vision, language, speech, genomics; supervised, self-supervised, generative learning). This aims to show that DyT is a universal solution, not just a niche one.
4. **Gain Insights into Normalization:** By observing what DyT successfully emulates, the authors seek to shed light on the core functional properties of normalization layers that are crucial for deep network training, beyond just statistical stabilization.

The motivation for this work stems from a crucial empirical observation:
- **Observation of LN Behavior:** The authors analyze the input-output mappings of Layer Normalization layers in pre-trained Transformer models (ViT, wav2vec 2.0, DiT). They find that while LN performs linear transformations per token, when viewed across all activations, its aggregate behavior strongly resembles an S-shaped curve, akin to a `tanh` function. This non-linear squashing effect on extreme values, rather than just mean-centering and variance scaling, is hypothesized to be a critical functional aspect of normalization. This observation directly inspires the design of DyT.

By providing a simpler, yet equally effective, alternative, the research also implicitly motivates potential future benefits such as architectural simplification, reduced memory overhead (in certain unoptimized scenarios), and potentially more interpretable network dynamics, though the authors note these are not fully realized speedups with current highly optimized libraries.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work lies in the empirical observation of normalization layer behavior and the subsequent design of a simple function to mimic that behavior.

#### 4.1. Observation of Layer Normalization Behavior

The authors conducted an empirical analysis of Layer Normalization (LN) layers in trained Vision Transformers (ViT), wav2vec 2.0 (a speech Transformer), and Diffusion Transformers (DiT). For each model, they sampled mini-batches and plotted the input and output values of several LN layers (before the affine transformations).
- **Key Insight:** While LN performs linear operations (mean subtraction and division by standard deviation) *per token*, the collective input-output relationship across all activations of the input tensor was consistently observed to be an **S-shaped curve**, highly resembling a `tanh` function.
- **Linear vs. Non-linear Regions:** Earlier LN layers showed more linear mappings, but deeper layers exhibited prominent S-shapes. The central part of the curve (inputs near zero) remained largely linear, while extreme input values were significantly "squashed" into a less extreme range.
- **Role of Squashing:** This non-linear squashing of extreme values, which cannot be achieved by a simple affine transformation, is hypothesized to be the indispensable property of normalization layers.
- **Detailed Visualization:** Further analysis by coloring points by tokens and channels revealed that while each token's activations are linearly transformed, different tokens have different variances, leading to varying slopes and collectively forming the S-shaped curve. Similarly, different channels exhibit widely varying input ranges, with only a few channels displaying extreme values that get heavily squashed.

#### 4.2. Dynamic Tanh (DyT) Formulation

Inspired by the observed tanh-like behavior, the authors propose **Dynamic Tanh (DyT)** as a direct, element-wise replacement for normalization layers.
- **Definition:** `DyT(x) = γ * tanh(αx) + β`
 - `tanh(.)`: The hyperbolic tangent function, which naturally produces the S-shaped curve and squashes inputs into the range `(-1, 1)`.
 - `α` (alpha): A **learnable scalar parameter** that scales the input `x` before the `tanh` function. This "dynamic" scaling allows the function to adjust its sensitivity to different input ranges, mimicking how normalization layers adapt to varying input statistics (e.g., smaller α for larger input ranges, and vice versa, similar to 1/std).
 - `γ` (gamma) and `β` (beta): Learnable per-channel vector parameters, identical to the affine scaling and shifting parameters found in traditional normalization layers. These allow the output to be scaled back to any desired range.
- **Core Principle:** Unlike normalization layers, DyT does not compute any statistics (mean, variance) across dimensions or batch elements. It processes each input element `x` independently. Its effectiveness comes from `α` learning an appropriate scaling and `tanh` providing the necessary non-linear squashing of extreme values.

#### 4.3. Integration and Initialization

- **Drop-in Replacement:** Integrating DyT into existing architectures is straightforward: one DyT layer directly replaces one normalization layer (LN or RMSNorm) within attention blocks, FFN blocks, and before final linear projections.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning:** A key claim of the paper is that DyT often requires minimal to no tuning of hyperparameters that were originally optimized for normalized models, highlighting its plug-and-play nature and suggesting similar learning dynamics.
- **Initialization:**
 - `γ` is initialized to all ones, and `β` to all zeros, following standard normalization layer practice.
 - `α` is generally initialized to `0.5` for most non-LLM models, which is found to be robust.
 - For Large Language Models (LLMs), a more nuanced initialization strategy for `α` is found to be beneficial:
 - Larger models require smaller `α` initial values.
 - `α` for DyT layers within attention blocks benefits from higher initial values, while `α` for FFN blocks and final layers benefits from lower initial values.
 - An additional learnable scalar parameter, initialized to `sqrt(d_model)`, is added after the initial embedding layer in LLMs to prevent activation magnitudes from being too small.

This simple yet effective methodology directly translates the observed behavior of complex normalization layers into a minimalistic, element-wise function, providing a strong empirical counter-argument to the perceived indispensability of normalization.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents extensive empirical validation of DyT across a wide array of deep learning tasks and architectures, demonstrating its competitive or superior performance compared to traditional normalization layers.

#### 5.1. Performance Across Diverse Tasks

- **Supervised Learning in Vision (ViT, ConvNeXt on ImageNet-1K):** DyT models (ViT-B/L, ConvNeXt-B/L) achieved slightly better or comparable top-1 classification accuracies than their LN counterparts. Training loss curves for DyT and LN models were highly aligned, suggesting similar convergence behaviors.
- **Self-Supervised Learning in Vision (MAE, DINO on ImageNet-1K):** DyT consistently performed on par with LN for both MAE and DINO pretrained Vision Transformers, highlighting its effectiveness across different self-supervised objectives.
- **Diffusion Models (DiT on ImageNet-1K):** DyT-based Diffusion Transformers (DiT-B/L/XL) achieved comparable or improved Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) scores, indicating high-quality image generation.
- **Large Language Models (LLaMA 7B-70B on The Pile):** DyT replaced RMSNorm in LLaMA models. After careful `α` initialization (as detailed below), DyT achieved comparable training loss and average zero-shot performance on 15 `lm-eval` tasks across all four model sizes (7B, 13B, 34B, 70B). Loss curves showed close alignment throughout training.
- **Self-Supervised Learning in Speech (wav2vec 2.0 on LibriSpeech):** DyT performed comparably to LN for both Base and Large wav2vec 2.0 models, demonstrating its applicability in audio processing.
- **DNA Sequence Modeling (HyenaDNA, Caduceus on GenomicBenchmarks):** DyT maintained performance comparable to LN for long-range DNA sequence classification, showcasing its versatility across biological sequence data.

Across these diverse settings, a recurring theme is that DyT models often achieve their strong performance *without tuning the training hyperparameters* from the original normalized architectures, underscoring its ease of integration.

#### 5.2. Analysis of DyT Components (Tanh and Alpha)

- **Role of Squashing Function:** Ablation studies confirmed that the squashing function within DyT is essential for stable training. Replacing `tanh` with an identity function (no squashing) led to unstable training and divergence. Among `tanh`, `hardtanh`, and `sigmoid`, `tanh` performed the best, likely due to its smoothness and zero-centered properties.
- **Role of Learnable Alpha (α):** Removing the learnable `α` parameter resulted in performance degradation across all squashing functions. This highlights `α`'s critical role in scaling the input activations appropriately, which is crucial for overall model performance.

#### 5.3. Insights from Alpha Values

- **During Training:** The learnable `α` parameter dynamically tracks the `1/std` (inverse of standard deviation) of the input activations throughout training. This suggests that `α` effectively learns to scale activations in a manner similar to how normalization layers adjust input magnitudes based on their variance.
- **After Training:** In trained networks, there's a strong correlation between the final `α` values and the `1/std` of the input activations. Layers with higher `1/std` (meaning smaller input standard deviation) correspond to larger `α` values, and vice versa. Deeper layers generally exhibited activations with larger standard deviations, consistent with observations in other deep networks. This analysis reinforces that `α` acts as a collective normalization mechanism for the entire input activation, while the `tanh` function handles the non-linear suppression of extreme values.

#### 5.4. Comparison with Other Normalization-Free Methods

DyT was compared against popular initialization-based methods (Fixup, SkipInit) and weight-normalization-based methods (`σReparam`) on ViT and MAE tasks.
- **Superior Performance:** DyT consistently outperformed all other tested normalization-free methods. Fixup and SkipInit often required significantly lower learning rates to prevent divergence and still achieved lower accuracy. `σReparam` was closer but still slightly inferior to DyT.
- **Simplicity Advantage:** Beyond performance, DyT stands out for its simplicity and minimal need for hyperparameter tuning, unlike other methods that often demand specific initialization schemes or careful learning rate searches.

#### 5.5. Alpha Initialization Specifics for LLMs

While non-LLM models were largely insensitive to `α`'s initial value (0.5 generally sufficient), LLMs showed notable performance gains with careful tuning:
- **Model Size Dependency:** Larger LLM models (e.g., LLaMA 34B, 70B) required smaller initial `α` values for optimal performance compared to smaller models.
- **Location-Specific Initialization:** Optimal performance in LLMs was achieved by initializing `α` with higher values for DyT layers within attention blocks and lower values for DyT layers in FFN blocks and before the final linear projection. This suggests different scaling needs depending on the module type.
- **Width Dependency:** Model width was found to be the primary determinant for optimal `α` values, with wider networks benefiting from smaller `α` initializations. Model depth had negligible influence. This sensitivity in LLMs is hypothesized to relate to their excessively large widths compared to other models.

Overall, the empirical results are robust and comprehensive, strongly supporting the paper's central claim about the dispensability of traditional normalization layers when replaced by DyT.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in "Transformers without Normalization" carry significant implications for the field of deep learning:

#### 6.1. Challenging Conventional Wisdom

The most profound significance is the direct challenge to the long-held belief that normalization layers are "indispensable" for training modern deep neural networks, especially the dominant Transformer architecture. By demonstrating that a simple, element-wise operation like DyT can match or exceed performance, this work prompts a re-evaluation of the fundamental mechanisms underpinning stable and effective deep learning. It suggests that the crucial role of normalization might not be solely about batch statistics or intricate gradient flow stabilization, but rather about the **functional squashing of extreme activation values** combined with an adaptive scaling. This offers a simpler conceptual model for understanding what truly makes deep networks trainable.

#### 6.2. Practical Implications

- **Architectural Simplification:** DyT simplifies the neural network architecture by replacing complex statistical computations (mean, variance) with a straightforward function. This could lead to more elegant and potentially more interpretable network designs.
- **Ease of Adoption:** The "drop-in replacement" nature of DyT, coupled with its general lack of hyperparameter tuning requirements (except for specific nuances in LLMs), makes it highly practical for researchers and practitioners. It suggests that a fundamental architectural change can be implemented with minimal effort.
- **Potential for Efficiency:** While the paper notes that DyT currently offers no speedup over highly optimized normalization layers (due to existing library optimizations like `torch.compile`), its element-wise nature inherently avoids reduction operations. This theoretical simplicity could unlock future optimization opportunities on specialized hardware or via custom kernel fusion, potentially leading to faster inference or training in certain deployment environments. It also means potentially lower memory footprint as no statistics need to be stored/computed.
- **New Research Avenues:** This work opens up new research directions to further understand the role of non-linearities and scaling in deep networks. It could inspire exploration of other simple, learnable element-wise functions that replicate beneficial properties currently attributed to more complex layers. It also encourages re-examining the precise impact of `tanh`-like squashing on generalization and optimization landscapes.

#### 6.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors acknowledge important limitations:
- **Scope of Normalization Layers:** The study primarily focuses on replacing LN and RMSNorm, which are prevalent in Transformers. Preliminary experiments suggest DyT struggles to directly replace Batch Normalization (BN) in classic ConvNets (e.g., ResNet-50, VGG19), indicating that DyT may not be a universal replacement for all types of normalization layers across all architectures. This suggests differences in how BN versus LN/RMSNorm contribute to network dynamics, and further research is needed to understand if and how DyT (or a variant) could adapt to BN-heavy models.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Despite its conceptual simplicity, DyT does not offer a speedup over well-optimized normalization layers when compiled by current deep learning frameworks. This means the primary benefit is architectural and conceptual simplicity, not immediate performance gains in terms of wall-clock time on standard hardware setups. Future work could focus on custom kernel development or fusion techniques to realize potential speedups.

In conclusion, "Transformers without Normalization" is a significant contribution that redefines our understanding of normalization layers. By proposing DyT, a simple and effective alternative, the paper not only offers a practical tool for building deep networks but also sparks a critical re-examination of the foundational assumptions in deep learning architecture design.
