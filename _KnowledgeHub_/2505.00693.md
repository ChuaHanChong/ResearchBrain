# Robotic Visual Instruction

> [!summary] Summary
> Li et al. introduce Robotic Visual Instruction (RoVI), an object-centric, hand-drawn symbolic language, and the Visual Instruction Embodied Workflow (VIEW) pipeline to guide robotic tasks. This approach enables robots to interpret precise 2D visual instructions and translate them into 3D actions, achieving an 87.5% success rate on unseen real-world manipulation tasks, surpassing language-conditioned baselines.

## Problem

- Natural language instructions for robots often lack spatial precision, leading to ambiguity, verbosity, and unsuitability in quiet environments.
- Existing visual instruction methods, such as goal images or full trajectories, are often impractical or unintuitive for users to provide.
- There is a need for a communication method that balances user-friendliness, interpretability, and direct spatio-temporal alignment for robotic manipulation tasks.

## Method

- Researchers designed **Robotic Visual Instruction** (RoVI), a hand-drawn symbolic language using object-centric primitives (arrows, circles) augmented with colors and numbers to encode spatial and temporal information.
- ==The RoVI Book== dataset, comprising 15,000 image-text question-answer pairs, was curated to fine-tune Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for robust RoVI comprehension, enabling learning with minimal computational resources.
- The Visual Instruction Embodied Workflow (VIEW) pipeline was developed, consisting of a VLM for high-level planning, a specialized keypoint module to extract 2D spatial-temporal constraints, and a keypoint-conditioned low-level policy to execute 3D actions.

## Results

- The VIEW pipeline, particularly with LLaVA-13B fine-tuned on RoVI Book, achieved an **87.5%** success rate across 8 unseen real-world tasks, outperforming language-conditioned policies like Voxposer (**43.8%**) and CoPa (**45%**).
- In challenging simulated cluttered environments, VIEW reached a **76.6%** success rate, significantly outperforming end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models such as RT-1-X (**20%**) and Octo-language (**3%**).
- A specialized YOLOv8-based keypoint module achieved a mean average precision (mAP) of **1.00** for RoVI symbol detection and mean distance errors of **6.83** pixels, demonstrating superior performance over general open-vocabulary detectors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Object-centric, hand-drawn visual symbols offer a precise, intuitive, and silent alternative to natural language and existing visual modalities for robotic instruction, effectively conveying both path and end-state information.
> - Specialized VLM fine-tuning on a curated dataset like RoVI Book, coupled with a dedicated keypoint extraction module, is crucial for robust interpretation and execution of novel visual instructions by robots.
> - A structured "Geometric Style" for drawing RoVI symbols significantly improves VLM comprehension and overall task performance compared to a casual "Loose Style".

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) has increasingly relied on natural language as a primary communication medium, largely driven by advancements in large language models (LLMs). This approach allows for intuitive interaction, converting verbal or textual commands into executable robotic actions. However, natural language inherently lacks spatial precision, leading to ambiguity and verbosity when describing precise object positions, directions, or distances. Furthermore, verbal communication is often inappropriate in quiet public environments such as libraries or hospitals.

Alternative visual modalities, including goal images, trajectories, and subgoal images, offer more direct means of conveying spatio-temporal information. However, existing visual methods present their own practical challenges for users. Goal images require users to provide the robot's end state, which can be counter-intuitive, while full trajectory inputs demand users to imagine and draw the entire motion path of the end-effector, diminishing readability and ease of use.

This work addresses the limitations of both natural language and existing visual modalities by introducing Robotic Visual Instruction (RoVI). RoVI positions itself as a novel HRI paradigm that leverages object-centric, hand-drawn symbolic representations to command robots. It seeks to combine the precision of visual cues with the user-friendliness lacking in prior visual methods, while overcoming the spatial ambiguity and contextual limitations of natural language. The research also integrates with advancements in Vision-Language Models (VLMs), adapting them to interpret these novel visual instructions and generate precise robotic actions. Furthermore, it builds upon existing work in visual prompting and keypoint constraints for manipulation, specifically by extracting keypoints directly from user-drawn symbols rather than environmental objects or model-generated prompts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and validate Robotic Visual Instruction (RoVI) as a novel paradigm for guiding robotic tasks. This paradigm utilizes object-centric, hand-drawn symbolic representations to encode spatial-temporal information into human-interpretable 2D sketches.

The motivation for developing RoVI stems from several limitations observed in current human-robot interaction methods:

- **Ambiguity and Verbosity of Natural Language:** Natural language, while intuitive, struggles to convey precise spatial details (e.g., exact position, direction, distance) for robotic task definition. This often results in instructions that are vague or excessively lengthy, hindering accurate robotic execution.
- **Inappropriateness in Specific Settings:** Verbal communication with robots is unsuitable in environments requiring quiet, such as libraries or hospitals, necessitating a silent alternative for human-robot interaction.
- **User-Unfriendliness of Existing Visual Modalities:** While visual inputs like goal images or complete trajectories offer spatial information, they are often impractical for users. Goal images require foreseeing and inputting the exact final state, which contradicts natural operational sequences. Similarly, drawing an entire end-effector trajectory is challenging for users to accurately imagine and depict.
- **Need for Object-Centric, Spatio-Temporally Aligned Instructions:** The research identifies a gap for a communication method that optimally balances user-friendliness, interpretability, and direct spatio-temporal alignment for robotic manipulation tasks.

To enable robots to understand and execute tasks based on RoVI, a secondary objective is to develop a robust workflow. This workflow, termed Visual Instruction Embodied Workflow (VIEW), is designed to interpret RoVI inputs, decode spatial and temporal constraints from 2D pixel space, and translate them into executable 3D action sequences for robotic manipulation.

A third objective is to curate a specialized dataset. This dataset is intended to fine-tune small Vision-Language Models (VLMs), enabling them to effectively learn RoVI capabilities with minimal computational resources and facilitating their deployment on edge devices.

In summary, the research aims to establish RoVI as a precise, intuitive, and silent alternative for robotic instruction, supported by a VLM-driven pipeline and a dedicated dataset, thereby enhancing robotic generalization and robustness in various manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research comprises the design of the Robotic Visual Instruction (RoVI) paradigm, the creation of a specialized dataset called RoVI Book, and the development of the Visual Instruction Embodied Workflow (VIEW) pipeline to enable robots to interpret and act upon RoVI.

#### 3.1. Robotic Visual Instruction (RoVI) Design

RoVI is a hand-drawn symbolic representation based on two visual primitives: arrows and circles. These primitives are used to define three object-centric motions: moving from A to B, rotating an object, and picking up/selecting.

- **Arrows:** Denote trajectory and temporal sequence. An arrow is decomposed into a Tail (starting point $p_0$), Shaft (waypoints $\{p_1, \dots, p_{n-1}\}$), and Head (endpoint $p_n$). These are 2D coordinates extracted by a keypoint module. The starting point indicates the grasp position, and the endpoint signifies the action’s goal.
- **Circles:** Highlight key interaction areas on objects. The center point $p_0$ represents an affordance center for actions such as grasping, rotation pivots, or pressing.
- **Colors and Numbers:** Colors convey temporal sequencing (e.g., green for the first step, blue for the second, pink for the third), allowing for multi-step task decomposition. Numbers label different embodiments for dual-arm systems.
- **Drawing Settings:** RoVI is drawn using a stylus on a tablet or PC. Two drawing styles were investigated: a "Loose Style" (casual, hand-drawn) and a "Geometric Style" (structured, with standard triangle arrowheads and circles) for clearer VLM interpretation.

#### 3.2. RoVI Book Dataset

To facilitate VLM understanding of RoVI, a dataset named RoVI Book was curated.
- **Size and Content:** It comprises 15,000 image-text question-answer pairs. Each instance includes an image of the initial task observation annotated with RoVI, a simple default query, and an answer generated by GPT-4o. The answers provide RoVI analysis, task names, fine-grained planning steps, and Python code functions.
- **Source and Coverage:** The original tasks and images are adapted from the Open-X Embodiment dataset. The dataset covers 64% single-step and 36% multi-step tasks across five fundamental manipulation skills: moving, rotating, picking up, opening, and closing drawers/cabinets.
- **Data Augmentation:** To enhance generalization, RoVI annotations undergo augmentation, introducing 3–8 visual variants per task, with variations in paths, drawing styles, and line thickness.

#### 3.3. Visual Instruction Embodied Workflow (VIEW)

The VIEW pipeline transduces 2D RoVI instructions into action sequences for robotic manipulation. It consists of three main components:

1. **Vision-Language Model (VLM) ($f_\theta$):**
 - Takes as input the hand-drawn RoVI ($v$), an initial observation image ($X$), and a system-provided default prompt.
 - Interprets RoVI through a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning process, producing a natural language response ($y_{language}$) and executable Python code functions ($y_{code}$).
 - The language response includes coarse-grained task predictions and fine-grained planning with sub-goal sequences, which are then converted into functions like `move` or `grasp`.

2. **Keypoint Module ($f_\delta$):**
 - Decodes spatiotemporal information from RoVI.
 - Decomposes multi-step tasks into single-step tasks based on color identifiers.
 - A trained YOLOv8 model is used to extract 2D keypoints ($p_0$ for starting point, $p_i$ for waypoints, $p_n$ for endpoint) from RoVI symbols.
 - This module is trained on a specialized dataset of 2,000 images focused on identifying RoVI symbol components.

3. **Keypoint-Conditioned Low-Level Policy ($\pi$):**
 - Maps the 2D keypoints extracted from RoVI to 3D coordinates ($p'_i \in R^3$) using depth data from an RGB-D camera.
 - These 3D keypoints form a sequence of desired end-effector poses in SE(3) space. The initial pose ($e_0$) is obtained via a grasp module based on $p_0$.
 - Categorizes actions into translation (e.g., move, push) and rotation (e.g., flip, adjust knob).
 - At each time step, the robot observes its current end-effector pose and the target keypoint, then minimizes a cost function $L_i(t)$ to move towards the target.
 - The cost function is a weighted sum of translational ($\delta_{trans}(t) = \|e_t - e_i\|$) and rotational ($\delta_{rot}(t) = |\theta_t - \theta_i|$) costs, where weights are determined by the action type. The robot transitions to the next keypoint once $L_i(t)$ falls below a threshold $\epsilon$.

#### 3.4. Model Training and Implementation

- **VLMs:** GPT-4o and LLaVA-13B were used. LLaVA-7B and 13B models were fine-tuned using LoRA on the RoVI Book dataset (one epoch, learning rate 2e-4).
- **Keypoint Module:** YOLOv8 was trained for keypoint extraction.
- **Grasp Module:** AnyGrasp was used to filter grasp poses.
- **Robotic Systems:** UFACTORY X-Arm 6 and UR5 robotic arms with two-finger grippers, and RealSense D435 cameras (top-down and third-person views) were used for real-world experiments. Robotic arms operated at 20 Hz with end-effector delta control.
- **Tasks:** 11 novel tasks were designed (8 real, 3 simulated), including single-step, multi-step, trajectory-following, and cluttered environment scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated RoVI and the VIEW pipeline across generalization capabilities, VLM comprehension, and component impact.

#### 4.1. Generalization to In-the-Wild Manipulation (Real-World)

- **Performance:** VIEW, particularly with LLaVA-13B fine-tuned on RoVI Book, achieved an 87.5% success rate in 8 unseen real-world tasks. VIEW-GPT4o achieved 82.5%.
- **Comparison with Baselines:** Language-conditioned policies like Voxposer (43.8%) and CoPa (45%) exhibited lower success rates. These baselines struggled with tasks requiring precise spatial alignment (e.g., 'move the lemon close to and below the potato'), cluttered environments, and trajectory following.
- **Reasoning:** The superior performance of VIEW was attributed to RoVI's pixel-level precision and the keypoint module's robustness. The keypoint module focuses on RoVI symbols rather than open-vocabulary object detection, making it less susceptible to environmental variations or distractors. VIEW also demonstrated improved performance on long-horizon tasks (Tasks 6-8) by decomposing them into color-cued individual steps, simplifying temporal reasoning.
- **Spatiotemporal Alignment:** VIEW methods showed higher spatiotemporal alignment scores compared to baselines across tasks.

#### 4.2. Comparative Study in Simulation

- **Performance:** In three challenging simulated tasks within cluttered environments, VIEW achieved a 76.6% success rate, outperforming end-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models such as RT-1-X (20%), Octo-goal-image (13.3%), and Octo-language (3%).
- **Qualitative Analysis:** RoVI was observed to be the only instruction format effectively conveying both path information and the end state. Goal image policies were effective for end states but deficient in path description, while natural language policies resulted in inconsistent paths and end states with limited spatial precision.

#### 4.3. RoVI Comprehension by Modern VLMs

- **Large Models (In-Context Learning):** Advanced large models like GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5 Pro, and Claude 3.5-Sonnet demonstrated a strong ability to comprehend RoVI-conditioned manipulation tasks through in-context learning, even without explicit training on a specialized dataset. GPT-4o achieved the highest combined real-world (81%) and simulated (97%) task and planning success rates.
- **Small Models (Fine-tuning):** Models with fewer than 13 billion parameters generally failed to comprehend RoVI effectively without fine-tuning. However, LLaVA-13B fine-tuned on the RoVI Book dataset performed well on long-sequence multi-step tasks (Task 8), indicating the dataset's effectiveness for learning such capabilities.
- **Error Breakdown:** LLaVA-13B, despite sometimes having lower 'task and planning' prediction rates, showed strong action execution performance. This was attributed to the RoVI Book dataset's effectiveness in reducing errors related to the execution function, making it robust to potential perception or language interpretation inaccuracies.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Drawing Style:** A comparative study between "Loose Style" (casual, hand-drawn) and "Geometric Style" (structured, with standard components) drawing revealed that the more structured geometric style yielded superior VLM comprehension. For instance, the geometric style achieved an average task/subgoal prediction accuracy of 0.8 compared to 0.74 for the loose style.
- **Keypoint Module:** The proposed keypoint module, a trained YOLOv8 model specialized for RoVI symbols, significantly outperformed general open-vocabulary detection models (GDINO, OWL-ViT, OWL-V2) in extracting task-relevant keypoints. It achieved lower mean distance errors (e.g., 6.83 pixels vs. hundreds of pixels for baselines) and a mean average precision (mAP) of 1.00, demonstrating the benefit of specialized detection for RoVI interpretation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents RoVI as a novel human-robot interaction paradigm that offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on robotics and HRI:

- **Addressing HRI Limitations:** RoVI offers a spatially precise, intuitive, and silent alternative to natural language and existing visual instruction methods. This addresses the ambiguity and verbosity inherent in natural language commands, the user-unfriendliness of goal images and trajectories, and the social constraints of verbal communication in quiet environments. This broadens the applicability of robotic systems to a wider range of settings and user needs.
- **Enhanced Robotic Task Execution:** The VIEW pipeline enables robots to effectively interpret complex visual instructions and translate them into precise 3D actions. This leads to improved performance in tasks requiring fine spatial alignment, trajectory following, and multi-step operations, even in cluttered or dynamic environments, where traditional methods often struggle.
- **Leveraging and Advancing VLMs for Robotics:** The work demonstrates the capability of Vision-Language Models to understand and reason with novel visual symbolic languages. By showing that even smaller VLMs can be effectively fine-tuned with a specialized dataset (RoVI Book), it opens avenues for developing more efficient and deployable robotic control systems.
- **Contribution of a Specialized Dataset:** The creation of the RoVI Book dataset provides a valuable resource for the research community. This dataset is designed to train models specifically for RoVI comprehension, facilitating future research in visual instruction for robotics and supporting the development of lightweight models for edge deployment.
- **Practical Implications for Robot Deployment:** The robustness and generalization capabilities demonstrated by VIEW indicate its potential for real-world deployment. The system's ability to handle unseen tasks and environments with disturbances suggests a more versatile and adaptable robotic assistant.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** RoVI establishes a new direction for visual instruction in robotics. Future work, including scaling the RoVI Book dataset and exploring diverse free-form drawing styles, can further equip models with a deeper understanding of human visual communication of dynamic movements, potentially leading to more advanced and adaptable human-robot collaboration. The work also points towards the possibility of efficiently training smaller models for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices.
