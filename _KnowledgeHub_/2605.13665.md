# Robot Squid Game: Quadrupedal Locomotion for Traversing Narrow Tunnels

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework called SQUID enables quadrupedal robots to autonomously navigate challenging 3D confined environments like tunnels by combining procedural environment generation with a multi-expert policy distillation strategy. The system achieved higher success rates, better efficiency, and lower collision rates in simulations compared to baseline methods, and demonstrated successful real-world deployment on a Unitree Go2 robot with up to 80% success in difficult tunnel geometries.

## Problem

- Quadrupedal robots struggle to navigate complex 3D confined environments such as tunnels and collapsed structures, which require 360-degree awareness and precise limb/body coordination.
- Existing locomotion strategies often rely on oversimplified training environments, leading to policies that are specialized and lack generalization to diverse, irregular tunnel geometries.
- Current methods are sensitive to real-world sensory noise, hindering robust performance during deployment in unpredictable confined spaces.

## Method

- The SQUID framework utilizes a procedural generation system to create diverse 3D tunnel geometries (e.g., triangular, circular, gap) with varying difficulty, preventing policies from overfitting to simplified training setups.
- Four specialized 'expert' policies are trained independently using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with privileged state information, each tailored to a specific tunnel class.
- These expert policies' knowledge is consolidated into a single vision-based student policy via Dataset Aggregation (DAgger), enabling navigation using depth images and proprioception while robust against sensor noise.

## Results

- SQUID consistently achieved the highest success rates across all tunnel configurations and difficulty levels in simulation, outperforming both a Vanilla RL agent and a Hierarchical Planner.
- In simulation, SQUID demonstrated superior traversal efficiency (faster completion times), lower collision frequency, and greater energy efficiency compared to baseline methods.
- Real-world deployment on a Unitree Go2 robot showed successful transfer, achieving **70%** success in circular, **80%** in gap, and **60%** in sloped triangular tunnels, validating performance under realistic sensor noise.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generating diverse and complex tunnel geometries procedurally is critical for training generalizable locomotion policies that can adapt to a wide range of real-world confined spaces.
> - Decoupling perception from core locomotion by using multi-expert distillation with privileged teachers enhances robustness and generalization by allowing the student policy to effectively learn from vision.
> - A class-balanced replay buffer during distillation is essential to retain specialized behaviors from all experts, preventing bias towards any single tunnel geometry and ensuring comprehensive skill transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics has experienced advancements in recent years, with quadrupedal platforms demonstrating capabilities in navigating unstructured outdoor terrains. Prior research has primarily focused on ground-level obstacle negotiation, including adaptation to diverse terrains like sand and mud, and maintaining stability on uneven surfaces. End-to-end systems using egocentric vision have shown capabilities in traversing features such as stairs and stepping stones, while learning-based methods have enabled locomotion across terrains with sparse footholds.

However, a capability gap persists in confined three-dimensional (3D) environments, such as tunnels, caves, or collapsed structures. These environments impose 360° navigation challenges, requiring precise coordination of body posture, limb articulation, and environmental awareness to avoid collisions with ceilings, floors, and lateral obstructions. Applications like mine shaft inspections or urban disaster response necessitate robots operating in tunnel-like spaces characterized by irregular cross-sections, tight turns, and potentially limited visual accessibility.

Existing methods for navigating confined spaces generally fall into two categories: classical/hierarchical planning and reinforcement learning (RL) techniques.
- **Classical and Hierarchical Planning Approaches**: These methods, such as those employing perceptive whole-body planning with elevation mapping or sampling-based approaches with reachability abstraction, often rely on predefined motion primitives, handcrafted foothold safety heuristics, or can be computationally intensive. These characteristics can limit their generalization to irregular geometries and dynamic terrains.
- **Reinforcement Learning for Confined-Space Locomotion**: RL has been utilized to enhance adaptability in unstructured environments. Some RL frameworks combine classical waypoint planning with low-level policies for obstacle avoidance, while others use 3D volumetric representations for navigation. End-to-end RL has been demonstrated for dynamic skills. However, these approaches have faced limitations including reliance on simplified environmental models for training, the generation of highly specialized policies that require significant retuning for new tunnel shapes, sensitivity to sensory noise, coordination challenges between hierarchical layers, and difficulties in consolidating specialized skills across diverse geometries due to monolithic training frameworks.
- **Privileged Learning and Policy Distillation**: This area has advanced simulation-to-reality transfer by separating perception from control, typically by training teacher policies with full state observability and distilling these skills into vision-based student policies. However, single-policy architectures often encounter difficulties with conflicting skill requirements in multi-constraint spaces.
- **Procedural Environment Generation**: This technique has become a component for training robust policies in varied settings. While existing generators have created diverse terrains and obstacles, they have often lacked the specific geometric diversity required for complex tunnel-like constraints.

This work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the identified limitations, particularly the challenges of adaptability, generalization, and robustness to sensor noise in confined 3D environments. It proposes a framework that aims to overcome oversimplified training, reduce policy specialization, and mitigate sensitivity to sensory noise, thereby enhancing the capabilities of quadruped robots in complex, spatially constrained settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robust quadrupedal locomotion for autonomous traversal of confined 3D environments, such as tunnels, caves, and collapsed structures. This objective is motivated by the recognized challenges that current robotic locomotion strategies face in such complex scenarios, where spatial constraints demand advanced navigation capabilities beyond simple ground-level obstacle avoidance.

The authors identify and aim to address three core limitations prevalent in existing methods for navigating confined spaces:
1. **Oversimplified Training:** Current approaches often fail to capture the real-world structural diversity of confined environments. This leads to policies that are not sufficiently adaptable to varied geometries encountered during deployment. The research seeks to overcome this by generating diverse tunnel geometries during training.
2. **Specialized Policies and Retuning:** Existing methods frequently result in highly specialized policies that require substantial retuning or retraining when confronted with new tunnel shapes or configurations. The objective is to develop a more generalizable policy that can operate across a wide range of tunnel structures without extensive modifications.
3. **Sensitivity to Sensory Noise:** Many current strategies are sensitive to sensory noise, which can undermine performance when deployed in real-world scenarios. The authors aim to improve the robustness of the locomotion policy against imperfections and noise inherent in real-world sensor data.

To achieve these objectives, the research is grounded in two central observations. First, explicitly modeling the geometric variability of real-world confined 3D spaces through diverse tunnel generation is considered critical for preventing policies from overfitting to simplified training setups. Second, decoupling perception-handling strategies from core locomotion is identified as key to achieving robust generalization. This involves training specialized "expert" policies with privileged information (focusing solely on locomotion) and subsequently integrating perception as their expertise is transferred to a unified student policy. This approach is intended to reduce the need for policy retuning and mitigate the impact of noisy real-world sensors.

The overarching motivation is to advance the capabilities of quadruped robots for critical applications such as search-and-rescue missions, where robots must navigate collapsed structures to locate survivors, and infrastructure inspection (e.g., mine shafts, pipelines), where robots need to operate in hazardous, confined spaces to assess structural integrity. By developing a method that achieves consistent traversal across complex spatial constraints, the research endeavors to bridge the gap between structured simulation training and unpredictable, confined 3D spaces in real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named SQUID (Skill-fused Quadrupedal locomotion Using Imitation and Distillation), is an Reinforcement Learning (RL) approach that combines procedural environment generation with policy distillation to enable robust quadrupedal navigation through confined tunnels. The system architecture progresses through three distinct stages: procedural generation of diverse tunnel geometries, training of specialized expert policies, and distillation of these multiple experts into a single vision-based student policy.

**A. Procedural Tunnel Generation**
This stage addresses the limitation of static and oversimplified training environments by dynamically generating diverse tunnel configurations. The system constructs 3D blocks with hollowed-out pathways for the robot. Four primary tunnel classes are defined, each with tunable difficulty parameters (e.g., edge length, radius, gap width, shelf angle, rotation, Z-normal flip) to systematically vary spatial constraints:
- **Equilateral Triangle Tunnel (△):** Features a sharp, angular interior, demanding body rotation and limb adjustments. Difficulty increases as edge length decreases.
- **Full-Circle Tunnel (⃝):** Offers a uniformly enclosed structure. Difficulty increases as the radius decreases.
- **Half-Circle Tunnel (T):** Introduces complexity through random Z-normal flips, requiring adaptation to inverted terrain. Difficulty increases as the radius decreases.
- **Gap Tunnel (⊢⊣):** Consists of a central void with elevated side shelves, posing challenges for stable foothold selection. Difficulty increases as gap width widens or shelf angles increase.

These primitives aim to capture core geometric factors for confined-space locomotion, including sharp angles, clearance variation, base inclination, and rotational asymmetry. Key environmental factors such as tunnel width, height, curvature, and inclination vary continuously across training episodes. To improve adaptability, tunnel configurations are sequentially connected during training, allowing the robot to progress from simpler to more complex environments in a curriculum-based approach.

**B. Privileged Teacher Policies**
Four independent teacher policies (one for each tunnel class) are trained using an RL framework, specifically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). These "expert" policies leverage privileged state information from the simulator to learn locomotion strategies tailored to their specific tunnel class.
- **Observation Space:** The observation space for teacher policies includes proprioceptive and exteroceptive measurements: base linear and angular velocities, gravity vector orientation, joint positions and velocities, previous actions, and a 108-dimensional grid representing terrain measurements around the robot's base.
- **Action Space:** Each expert policy outputs a 12-dimensional vector of desired joint positions (three per leg), which are then converted into motor torques by a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller to ensure stable learning and efficient locomotion.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function is a weighted sum of individual terms designed to encourage efficient, stable, and collision-free locomotion while minimizing energy consumption. Key terms include:
 - Linear and angular velocity tracking to match target motion commands.
 - Vertical and horizontal angular velocity penalties to maintain stable body movement.
 - Joint motion and torque penalties for smooth and efficient actuation.
 - Collision penalty for contacts with tunnel walls.
 - Step duration reward based on leg air time to promote structured footstep timing.

**C. Student Policy Using Distillation**
After the expert policies are trained, their knowledge is consolidated into a single vision-based student policy using a policy distillation framework. Unlike the experts, the student policy learns to navigate using depth images and historical proprioception, making it suitable for real-world deployment.
- **Method:** Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) is employed. This iterative imitation learning approach collects on-policy rollouts under the student policy and queries the expert policies for corrective supervision.
- **Student Observations:** The student policy uses a convolutional neural network (CNN) encoder to process 64x48 pixel depth images and a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) to maintain temporal context from proprioceptive history.
- **Tunnel-Conditioned Distillation:** During training, each sample is associated with a tunnel class label. The student is supervised by the expert corresponding to that tunnel class, with the objective defined by an action-matching loss.
- **Class-Balanced Replay Buffer:** To prevent bias towards any single tunnel geometry and retain specialized behaviors from all experts, a class-balanced replay buffer is maintained, and mini-batches are sampled proportionally across the four tunnel types during student updates. Depth images are augmented with random noise and dropout during distillation to enhance robustness against sensor imperfections.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the SQUID framework through simulation-based trials and real-world deployments, comparing its performance against baselines and conducting ablation studies.

**A. Comparison with Baselines (Simulation)**
SQUID was compared against two baselines: Vanilla Reinforcement Learning (V-RL), a single PPO agent trained simultaneously on all tunnel classes, and a Hierarchical Planner (HP), a two-layer system with a high-level planner and a low-level controller. Experiments were conducted across four tunnel classes (triangle, circle, half-circle, gap) at low, medium, and high difficulty levels defined by geometric parameters.
- **Success Rate:** SQUID consistently achieved the highest success rates across all tunnel configurations and difficulty levels. Its advantage was particularly noticeable in triangular and gap tunnels, which demand precise body articulation for constrained navigation. HP performed adequately in structured tunnels like the circle but struggled with environments requiring adaptive motions, such as the gap tunnel. V-RL exhibited poor generalization, frequently failing in complex geometries and at higher difficulty levels.
- **Traversal Efficiency, Collision Avoidance, and Energy Consumption:**
 - **Completion Time:** SQUID demonstrated faster traversal speeds compared to HP, which exhibited cautious movement. V-RL occasionally stalled in tight spaces. SQUID maintained a balance between speed and stability.
 - **Collision Frequency:** SQUID recorded the lowest collision rates, effectively maintaining clearance from tunnel walls and adapting to asymmetric structures. HP encountered difficulties in tunnels requiring whole-body posture adaptation, while V-RL frequently collided due to erratic foot placements.
 - **Energy Consumption:** SQUID achieved greater energy efficiency through smoother gait transitions and reduced corrective movements. HP consumed more energy due to slower traversal, and V-RL was the least efficient, characterized by frequent stops and unstable gaits.

**B. Ablation Studies**
Ablation experiments were conducted to assess the contribution of key SQUID components to the overall policy performance. Success rates under different settings were reported.
- **Single Teacher vs. Multiple Teachers:** Training a student policy with only a single expert (e.g., the circular tunnel expert) resulted in moderate success in similar environments but failed to generalize to tunnels with asymmetric constraints (e.g., triangular or gap tunnels). This indicated that policies trained with a single teacher overfit to specific geometries, validating the need for the multi-expert approach.
- **Procedural Generation Disabled:** A student policy trained in a fixed, non-procedural environment achieved reasonable success in familiar scenarios but failed in tunnels with unexpected variations in curvature, slope, or orientation. This confirmed the role of procedural generation in promoting generalization by exposing the policy to a wide range of environmental constraints.
- **Two-Layer Elevation Map vs. Ground Elevation Map:** Teacher policies trained with a two-layer elevation map (concatenating floor and ceiling elevations) failed to converge due to conflicting optimization objectives, leading to unstable posture adjustments. In contrast, policies trained with only ground elevation maps successfully learned stable locomotion strategies. This suggested that explicitly encoding ceiling constraints might increase learning complexity without improving performance, necessitating the student policy's reliance on depth maps for exteroception.
- **Reward Shaping Simplifications:** Removing the collision penalty resulted in erratic movements and frequent contacts with tunnel walls. Eliminating the vertical velocity penalty led to increased hopping behaviors, compromising stability and traversal efficiency. These findings underscored the importance of the structured reward function in ensuring safe and effective locomotion.

**C. Real-World Deployment**
The SQUID policy was deployed on a Unitree Go2 quadruped in a controlled tunnel testbed consisting of modular 1 m tunnel segments representing high-difficulty confined geometries: a narrow circular tunnel (r = 0.20 m), an equilateral triangular tunnel with a 40° inclined base (l = 0.60 m), and a gap tunnel with a 0.50 m-wide opening. The robot utilized a 4D LiDAR for depth images and proprioceptive inputs.
- **Success Rates:** Defining a successful trial as traversing at least 80% of the tunnel length without external recovery, the policy achieved a 70% success rate in the circular tunnel, 80% in the gap tunnel, and 60% in the sloped triangular tunnel (identified as the most challenging due to combined tight clearance and roll perturbation from the incline).
- **Transferability:** These results indicated that the distilled policy successfully transferred to real hardware, maintaining functionality under realistic sensor artifacts (depth noise, missing returns) and construction tolerances at high difficulty settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, SQUID, that advances the capabilities of quadrupedal robots in navigating complex and spatially constrained 3D environments.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Addressing Key Limitations:** The work directly addresses critical limitations of prior approaches in legged robotics for confined spaces, including oversimplified training environments, the generation of highly specialized policies, and sensitivity to real-world sensory noise.
- **Novel Methodological Integration:** It introduces a novel integration of procedural environment generation with a multi-expert teacher-student policy distillation paradigm. This combination allows for comprehensive training across diverse geometric constraints and effective knowledge transfer from privileged expert policies to a single, vision-based student policy suitable for deployment.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The procedural generation system significantly increases the diversity of training environments, fostering a policy that generalizes more effectively to unseen tunnel configurations. The distillation process, coupled with sensor augmentation, contributes to the robustness of the student policy against real-world sensor imperfections.
- **Improved Performance Metrics:** Through rigorous simulation and real-world testing, SQUID demonstrates superior performance across key metrics, including higher success rates, faster traversal times, fewer collisions, and improved energy efficiency, especially in challenging and constricted environments.
- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful deployment on a physical Unitree Go2 robot, using only onboard depth sensing and proprioception, validates the effectiveness of the training and distillation pipeline for practical applications, bridging the gap between simulation and reality.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Search and Rescue Operations:** The ability of quadruped robots to autonomously traverse collapsed structures and narrow passages could enhance the effectiveness and safety of search-and-rescue missions in urban disaster zones, allowing for faster location of survivors or assessment of structural damage in hazardous environments.
- **Infrastructure Inspection and Maintenance:** The framework could enable autonomous inspection of critical infrastructure such as pipelines, ventilation systems, mine shafts, and other confined industrial spaces. This could reduce the need for human entry into dangerous areas, improve inspection efficiency, and facilitate proactive maintenance.
- **Exploration of Unstructured Environments:** Robots equipped with SQUID's locomotion capabilities could be deployed in natural confined spaces like caves or burrows for scientific exploration, environmental monitoring, or geological surveying, accessing areas otherwise inaccessible or dangerous for humans.
- **Advancements in Legged Robotics:** The methodology, particularly the multi-expert distillation with procedural generation, offers a blueprint for training highly adaptable and robust legged robots. This could spur further research in areas such as skill learning, generalization across diverse tasks, and robust real-world deployment for complex robotic systems.
- **Increased Autonomy and Versatility:** By expanding the operational domain of legged robots into previously challenging 3D confined spaces, this work contributes to the development of more autonomous and versatile robotic platforms capable of addressing a wider array of real-world challenges.

Future work directions identified by the authors include extending the procedural generator to explicitly model unstructured clutter (e.g., debris, rocks, irregular protrusions) and evaluating performance in tight-turn junction networks, further enhancing the framework's applicability to complex real-world scenarios.
