# Action-Conditional Video Prediction using Deep Networks in Atari Games

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper presents deep neural network architectures capable of predicting future image frames in complex, action-dependent Atari game environments for extended periods. The models successfully generate visually realistic long-term predictions and are shown to be useful for controlling pre-trained reinforcement learning agents and enhancing exploration strategies for model-free agents.

## Problem

- Developing models that can predict future visual observations in complex, action-dependent environments like Atari games, which have high-dimensional data and intricate object dynamics.
- Enabling model-based reinforcement learning from raw pixels, as previous video prediction methods often lacked external control inputs or struggled with long-term, high-dimensional sequences.
- Addressing the challenge of predicting the consequences of an agent's actions, which is crucial for planning and efficient learning in reinforcement learning scenarios.

## Method

- Developed two deep neural network architectures (==Feedforward Encoding== and ==Recurrent Encoding with LSTM==) that follow an ==encoding-transformation-decoding== paradigm.
- Integrated action information into the prediction process using ==multiplicative interactions== within the transformation layers, allowing the model to learn different transformations for different actions.
- Employed ==deconvolutional layers== for decoding the action-conditioned features back into high-resolution pixel space to generate the predicted next frame.

## Results

- Proposed deep architectures generated visually-realistic frames for up to **30-500 steps**, outperforming baselines in Mean Squared Error (MSE) over **100-step** predictions.
- When used to replace emulator frames, a pre-trained DQN controller achieved significantly better-than-random scores, indicating the semantic usefulness of the predictions for control.
- An 'informed exploration' strategy, using the predictive model to guide a DQN agent towards least-visited states, improved DQN's performance in **three out of five** games, notably QBert.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The models learned to disentangle objects directly controlled by the agent (high variance factors) from background or autonomously moving objects (low variance factors).
> - Implicitly, the models discovered meaningful relationships between actions, showing positive correlations for similar actions and negative for opposing ones.
> - Curriculum learning with multi-step prediction was effective in stabilizing training and reducing compounding errors for long-term video generation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a crucial and challenging problem at the intersection of deep learning, computer vision, and reinforcement learning: learning to predict future visual observations conditioned on an agent's actions.

**In the context of Video Prediction:** Before this work, research in video prediction using deep networks primarily focused on modeling simple, often synthetic, video data (e.g., bouncing balls, small patches, handwritten digits) where future frames largely depend on past frames. While advancements were made with architectures like Recurrent Temporal Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RTRBMs), structured RTRBMs (sRTRBMs), higher-order gated autoencoders, and LSTMs for sequence generation, these approaches rarely incorporated external control inputs or scaled to high-dimensional, complex naturalistic scenes. This paper significantly pushes the boundary by tackling *action-conditional* prediction on *high-dimensional* Atari game frames for *long-term* sequences, a problem far more complex than predicting simple, unmanipulated video. The authors explicitly state that, "To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to make and evaluate long-term predictions on high-dimensional video conditioned by control inputs."

**In the context of Reinforcement Learning (RL):** The work is fundamentally motivated by vision-based RL problems. RL paradigms are broadly categorized into model-free and model-based approaches. Model-free methods (like Q-learning and policy gradients, notably Deep Q-Networks or DQN) directly learn policies or value functions without explicitly modeling the environment's dynamics. While successful, they often require extensive interaction with the environment. Model-based RL, on the other hand, aims to learn a model of the environment (how states transition and rewards are generated given actions) which can then be used for planning, simulation, and more efficient learning. This paper directly contributes to model-based RL by proposing a method to learn the visual dynamics model from raw pixels. The Arcade Learning Environment (ALE), with its Atari 2600 games, serves as a popular and challenging benchmark for general AI agents, characterized by high-dimensional visual observations, partial observability, and delayed rewards. DeepMind's DQN had achieved remarkable, human-level performance on many Atari games using a model-free approach. This paper takes a step towards integrating model-based learning into this complex visual domain, providing a complementary direction to the purely model-free successes. Previous attempts to learn transition models for Atari data often focused on patch-based predictions or only 1-step accuracy, lacking the global context and long-term predictive power demonstrated here.

**In the context of Deep Learning Architectures:** The paper leverages the successes of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for spatial feature extraction and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), for temporal modeling. It also incorporates "deconvolution" (transposed convolution) for generating images from latent representations, a technique that was gaining prominence around the time of this paper's publication for image generation tasks. The use of multiplicative interactions for integrating action variables into the transformation layer is another sophisticated deep learning technique employed. The paper thus demonstrates the power of combining these advanced neural network components in a novel architecture tailored for action-conditioned sequence generation.

Overall, this work is a crucial step towards building more intelligent and efficient agents by enabling them to predict the future consequences of their actions in rich, dynamic visual environments. It bridges the gap between sophisticated deep learning architectures and the challenging requirements of model-based reinforcement learning, setting the stage for future advancements in planning and exploration for visually-driven AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of this research is to develop and evaluate deep neural network architectures capable of predicting future image frames in complex, action-dependent visual environments. Specifically, the authors focus on Atari games from the Arcade Learning Environment (ALE), which serve as a challenging testbed for vision-based reinforcement learning (RL) problems.

The key motivations behind this work are multifaceted:

- **Enabling Model-Based Reinforcement Learning:** A fundamental tenet of intelligent behavior is the ability to anticipate outcomes. In reinforcement learning, this translates to learning a model of the environment's dynamics – how states transition and rewards are generated based on actions. For vision-based RL, this means predicting future visual observations (image frames) given current observations and chosen actions. Such a predictive model is essential for model-based RL approaches, allowing agents to plan, simulate future trajectories, and potentially learn more efficiently than purely model-free methods.
- **Addressing the Complexity of Vision-Based RL:** Atari games, despite being "non-natural scenes," present significant challenges for visual prediction. They involve:
 - **High-dimensional data:** Frames are large (210x160 RGB pixels).
 - **Complex dynamics:** Tens of objects can be present, with direct control over one or more objects and indirect influence on others.
 - **Object dynamics:** Objects can enter, depart, or interact in non-trivial ways (e.g., collisions).
 - **Deep partial observability:** The full state of the game is not directly observable from a single frame, requiring the model to infer underlying dynamics.
 - Traditional video prediction methods often struggled with these complexities or did not account for control inputs.
- **Beyond Simple Video Prediction:** Previous work on video prediction mostly focused on scenarios where the next frame is highly predictable solely from past frames (e.g., bouncing balls). In many real-world applications (e.g., autonomous driving, robotics), future observations are profoundly influenced by the agent's actions. This paper aims to tackle this critical missing piece by explicitly modeling the action-conditional dependency.
- **Evaluating Practical Utility for Control:** The ultimate goal for a predictive model in RL is not just to generate visually pleasing frames, but for these predictions to be *useful for control*. The authors aim to demonstrate this utility in two ways:
 - By testing if a pre-trained model-free controller (DQN) can still perform effectively when fed predicted frames instead of real emulator frames.
 - By showing that the predictive model can facilitate more efficient exploration for a model-free RL agent, guiding it towards novel and potentially rewarding states.
- **Understanding Learned Representations:** A secondary, but important, objective is to analyze what meaningful features and structures the deep networks implicitly learn about the environment's dynamics. This includes understanding how action information is encoded and whether the model can distinguish between objects directly controlled by the agent versus those that move autonomously or are indirectly influenced. Such insights contribute to the broader understanding of how deep learning models build internal representations of the world.

In summary, the authors are motivated to advance the state of the art in learning dynamic models from high-dimensional visual data, with a strong emphasis on the practical application and benefits for building more intelligent and sample-efficient reinforcement learning agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper proposes and evaluates two deep neural network architectures for action-conditional video prediction, both following an "encoding-transformation-decoding" paradigm.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The goal is to learn a function $f: x_{1:t}, a_t \rightarrow x_{t+1}$, where $x_t$ is the image frame at time $t$, $a_t$ is the action taken at time $t$, and $x_{1:t}$ represents the history of frames up to time $t$.

#### 3.2. Proposed Architectures (Encoding-Transformation-Decoding)

Both architectures share the core components but differ in their encoding approach:

- **Encoding Layers:** These layers extract spatio-temporal features from the input frames.
 - **Feedforward Encoding (Figure 1a):** This variant takes a fixed history of previous frames (e.g., the last 4 frames) concatenated along the channel dimension as input. A stack of convolutional layers directly extracts spatio-temporal features, followed by a fully-connected layer to produce a high-level feature vector $h_{enc,t}$. This is conceptually an "early-fusion" approach, allowing convolutional filters to learn temporal correlations directly from the raw pixel history.
 - **Recurrent Encoding (Figure 1b):** This variant processes one frame at a time. A CNN extracts spatial features from the current frame, which are then fed as input to a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network. The LSTM's recurrent connections model the temporal dynamics and maintain a memory cell $c_t$ to retain information from a deep history of inputs. The output of the LSTM's hidden state, $h_{enc,t}$, serves as the encoded feature. This approach is better suited for capturing long-term dependencies.
 - *Shared Architecture Details:* Both encoding parts consist of 4 convolutional layers followed by a fully-connected layer (2048 units). ReLU activations are used throughout.

- **Action-Conditional Transformation Layers:** This is the critical component that injects action information into the prediction process. The encoded feature vector $h_{enc,t}$ is transformed into an action-conditioned feature $h_{dec,t}$.
 - The authors use **multiplicative interactions** (Equation 3), where the action $a_t$ modulates the transformation of $h_{enc,t}$. This allows the model to learn different transformations for different actions.
 - To make the 3-way tensor (weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n \times a}$) scalable for high-dimensional features, they approximate it by factorizing it into three matrices (Equation 4: $h_{dec,t} = W_{dec}(W_{enc}h_{enc,t} \odot W_a a_t) + b$). This factorization allows for weight sharing across actions by mapping them to a common factor space ($f$ factors), which can be advantageous if common temporal dynamics exist across different actions. The number of factors is set to 2048.

- **Decoding Layers:** These layers map the action-transformed feature $h_{dec,t}$ back into the pixel space to generate the predicted next frame $\hat{x}_{t+1}$.
 - A fully-connected layer reshapes $h_{dec,t}$ into a 3D feature map.
 - Multiple **deconvolutional layers** (also known as transposed convolutions) are then used to effectively "upsample" and reconstruct the image from this feature map. This is considered an "inverse" operation of convolution, efficiently generating higher-resolution images. The decoding layers consist of 4 deconvolution layers, with the last layer outputting 3 channels for RGB images.

#### 3.3. Training Method: Curriculum Learning with Multi-Step Prediction

- To address the issue of compounding prediction errors when predicting high-dimensional images over multiple steps, the authors employ a **curriculum learning** strategy.
- The model is trained to minimize the average squared error over $K$-step predictions (Equation 6), where the network's own predictions are used as input for subsequent steps.
- Training proceeds in multiple phases, gradually increasing the prediction horizon $K$ (e.g., from 1-step to 3-step, then 5-step predictions). This "easier to harder" progression helps stabilize training.
- Optimization is performed using Stochastic Gradient Descent with Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) and RMSProp optimizer. Gradients are clipped to prevent explosion.

#### 3.4. Data and Preprocessing

- Game-play video datasets are generated using a replication of DeepMind's DQN agent playing Atari games with an $\epsilon$-greedy policy (with $\epsilon = 0.3$ for random actions), ensuring diverse gameplay.
- Approximately 500,000 training frames and 50,000 test frames are collected per game.
- Images are RGB (210x160), normalized by subtracting mean pixel values and dividing by 255. Actions are one-hot encoded.

#### 3.5. Baselines for Comparison

Two baselines are used to validate the proposed architectures:
- **Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP):** A simple feedforward neural network that takes the last frame as input, with action concatenated to a hidden layer. This serves as a baseline for complex temporal and spatial modeling.
- **No-Action Feedforward (naFf):** Similar to the proposed feedforward encoding model but the transformation layer *does not* receive action input. This isolates the contribution of action-conditioning.

#### 3.6. Evaluation Metrics

- **Qualitative:** Visual inspection of generated video sequences.
- **Quantitative:** Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and ground-truth frames.
- **Usefulness for Control:**
 - **Emulator Replacement:** A pre-trained DQN controller is tested by being fed frames generated by the predictive model instead of the actual game emulator. Performance is measured by average game score.
 - **Informed Exploration:** The predictive model is used to simulate potential next states for different actions, allowing the DQN agent to prioritize exploration towards least-visited states. Impact on game score and exploration patterns (heatmaps of visited states) is analyzed.
- **Analysis of Learned Representations:**
 - **Action Similarity:** Cosine similarity of learned action factors is analyzed.
 - **Distinguishing Controlled/Uncontrolled Objects:** Factors with high variance across actions are hypothesized to correspond to controlled objects, while low-variance factors correspond to background/uncontrolled objects. This is empirically tested.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a thorough evaluation of the proposed architectures' capabilities in generation quality, practical utility for RL, and interpretability of learned features.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that the proposed action-conditional video prediction architectures achieve state-of-the-art results for long-term prediction on high-dimensional Atari game frames and provide practical utility for reinforcement learning.

#### 4.1. Evaluation of Predicted Frames

- **Qualitative Evaluation (Prediction Video):** The proposed models are able to generate "visually-realistic" frames for extended periods, ranging from 30 to 500 steps depending on the game. They successfully predict complex local translations (e.g., vehicle movement) and interactions between objects (e.g., collisions). Crucially, they can also predict global context changes, such as an object reappearing at the bottom of the screen after crossing the top boundary (Figure 2). In contrast, the MLP baseline quickly diverges, producing blurry or meaningless images, while the `naFf` baseline (without action input) fails to predict the movement of the agent-controlled object.
 - **Limitations:** The models struggled with accurately predicting very small objects (like bullets in Space Invaders) due to the relatively low contribution of their pixel errors to the overall squared error loss. They also had difficulty with stochastic elements, such as the random appearance of new objects in Seaquest, though they still generated new objects with reasonable shapes and movements.

- **Quantitative Evaluation (Squared Prediction Error):** Both proposed architectures consistently outperform the MLP and `naFf` baselines in terms of Mean Squared Error (MSE) over 100-step predictions across all tested Atari games (Figure 3). While the MSE gap between their models and `naFf` wasn't always massive, this is attributed to the controlled object often occupying only a small part of the high-dimensional image.

#### 4.2. Relative Strengths and Weaknesses of Feedforward and Recurrent Encoding

- **Feedforward Encoding:** Demonstrated better performance in predicting *precise spatial transformations*, likely because its convolutional filters learn temporal correlations directly from raw pixel data in concatenated frames. However, it struggles with very *long-term dependencies* due to its fixed input window.
- **Recurrent Encoding:** Proved superior in modeling *arbitrarily long-term dependencies* by leveraging the LSTM's memory (e.g., predicting when Space Invaders enemies would move after 9 steps of inactivity). However, it sometimes exhibited small pixel translation errors that could compound over time, suggesting less precise spatial encoding compared to the feedforward approach.

#### 4.3. Evaluating the Usefulness of Predictions for Control

- **Replacing Real Frames with Predicted Frames as Input to DQN:** When a pre-trained DQN agent was fed frames generated by the predictive model (instead of the actual game emulator), its performance inevitably decreased. However, the game scores remained significantly better than random play, especially when predictions were re-initialized with true frames every few steps (Figure 5). Crucially, the proposed architectures yielded much better scores than the baselines for deep predictions, indicating that their ability to accurately predict the controlled object's movement (despite overall pixel error) was highly beneficial for control. This suggests the learned representations are semantically useful for decision-making.

- **Improving DQN via Informed Exploration:** The paper introduced an "informed exploration" strategy where, instead of choosing random actions, the agent uses the predictive model to simulate next frames for all possible actions and then chooses the action that leads to a least-visited state. This method improved DQN's performance in three out of five games, with a notable improvement in QBert (Table 1, Figure 6). This provides concrete evidence of the practical utility of the predictive model in enhancing model-free RL agents, especially during the early stages of learning (Figure 11).

#### 4.4. Analysis of Learned Representations

- **Similarity among Action Representations:** The models implicitly learned meaningful relationships between actions. Analyzing the cosine similarity between learned "action factors" (from the factorized multiplicative interaction layer) showed positive correlations for actions with similar effects (e.g., "up" and "up+fire") and negative correlations for opposing actions (e.g., "up" and "down") (Figure 7). This demonstrates that the model automatically discovers a structured representation of the action space.
- **Distinguishing Controlled and Uncontrolled Objects:** By analyzing the variance of different action factors, the authors showed that the model implicitly learned to disentangle objects affected by the agent's actions from other background or autonomously moving objects. High-variance factors were primarily responsible for transforming the agent-controlled object, while low-variance factors handled the rest of the scene (Figure 8). This is a remarkable emergent property, indicating that the network learns a form of "contingency awareness" by observing how different parts of the image change in response to actions.

In summary, the main findings highlight the effectiveness of the proposed deep architectures in generating long-term, action-conditional video predictions. More importantly, they demonstrate that these predictions are not merely visually plausible but are also *useful for controlling* reinforcement learning agents and that the models learn *meaningful, disentangled representations* of the environment's dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in the field of artificial intelligence, particularly at the intersection of deep learning, computer vision, and reinforcement learning. Its contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Pioneering Long-Term Action-Conditional Video Prediction:** This paper is recognized as one of the first to successfully demonstrate and rigorously evaluate long-term (up to 100+ steps) action-conditional video prediction on high-dimensional, complex visual data like Atari games. Previous efforts in video prediction were largely confined to simpler, non-interactive scenarios or very short prediction horizons. This work establishes a robust framework for addressing a crucial aspect of predictive modeling for intelligent agents.

- **Foundation for Model-Based Reinforcement Learning in Complex Visual Domains:** While model-free deep reinforcement learning (e.g., DQN) has achieved remarkable successes in Atari, this paper provides a strong foundation for developing *model-based* RL agents that can learn environment dynamics directly from raw pixels. The ability to predict future states given actions is fundamental for planning, model predictive control, and sample-efficient learning, which often remains a challenge for purely model-free methods in complex environments. This research paves the way for more efficient and robust RL systems that can reason about future outcomes.

- **Enhancing Model-Free RL through Informed Exploration:** Beyond a pure model-based approach, the paper concretely demonstrates how a learned predictive model can directly benefit existing model-free RL algorithms. The "informed exploration" strategy, where the agent uses predicted frames to choose actions that lead to less-visited states, significantly improved DQN's performance in several games. This highlights a practical and immediate impact, showing how predictive capabilities can address the notorious exploration-exploitation dilemma in RL, potentially leading to faster and more effective learning, especially in environments where reward signals are sparse or delayed.

- **Generalizability of Architectures:** The proposed encoding-transformation-decoding architectures are "domain-independent," meaning they are not tailored to specific Atari games but rather designed to learn general principles of visual dynamics. This suggests that the approach can be broadly applied to a variety of other vision-based RL problems, such as robotics (predicting sensor readings based on motor commands) or autonomous driving (predicting future road scenes based on steering and acceleration inputs). This opens up new avenues for building predictive models in real-world complex systems.

- **Insights into Learned Internal Representations:** The analysis of the learned representations is particularly impactful from a scientific standpoint. The findings that the model automatically learns:
 - **Meaningful action similarities:** Providing a structured embedding of the action space.
 - **Disentanglement of controlled vs. uncontrolled objects:** Implicitly identifying which parts of the environment are directly influenced by the agent's actions.
 This level of understanding about what deep neural networks learn about their environment's causal structure is invaluable. It moves beyond treating these models as black boxes and provides insights that could inform the design of more interpretable, efficient, and robust AI systems.

- **Directions for Future Research:** The paper explicitly points towards future work on integrating reward prediction into these models and evaluating their performance in full model-based RL settings. This paper serves as a critical stepping stone, providing the visual dynamics prediction component that is necessary for such advanced model-based planning.

In conclusion, "Action-Conditional Video Prediction using Deep Networks in Atari Games" is a foundational paper that pushes the boundaries of predictive modeling in AI. Its successful demonstration of long-term, action-conditioned visual predictions, coupled with its proven utility for reinforcement learning and its insights into learned representations, establishes a strong precedent for future research in building intelligent agents that can anticipate and plan in complex, dynamic visual worlds.
