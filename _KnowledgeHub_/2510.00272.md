# BC-MPPI: A Probabilistic Constraint Layer for Safe Model-Predictive Path-Integral Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from University College London, IIT, CNRS, Inria, and IRISA introduced Bayesian-Constraints MPPI (BC-MPPI), a method that integrates probabilistic safety guarantees into Model Predictive Path Integral control by learning constraint satisfaction with Bayesian Neural Networks. The approach improves safety margins, reduces collisions, and enhances target tracking in complex obstacle avoidance tasks compared to standard MPPI, while maintaining sample efficiency.

## Problem

- Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control struggles with robustly satisfying hard constraints due to its Monte-Carlo nature, limiting its use in safety-critical applications.
- Traditional penalty-based constraint handling in MPPI often leads to brittle tuning, unreliable performance, and undesirable constraint breaches.
- The engineering burden of hand-crafting differentiable cost and constraint functions for complex, non-linear tasks limits the effectiveness of traditional MPC in such scenarios.

## Method

- **BC-==MPPI==** integrates a probabilistic safety layer by using Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs) to model each hard constraint, providing a predictive mean and epistemic uncertainty of satisfaction.
- The joint probability of satisfying all constraints for a candidate trajectory is computed by multiplying individual BNN-predicted feasibility probabilities.
- This joint feasibility probability is used to reshape the standard MPPI importance weights, exponentially down-weighting unsafe trajectories to bias sampling towards safe regions without explicit rejection.

## Results

- BC-MPPI consistently achieved target goals and demonstrated superior safety performance, with significantly fewer collisions, compared to MPPI-penalty, which often failed to complete tasks.
- In scenarios with stationary obstacles, BC-MPPI maintained larger average distances from obstacles than Classic MPPI, and it resulted in fewer collisions in the most complex dynamic environments.
- BC-MPPI exhibited more consistent target tracking and achieved these safety improvements with a lower sample rejection rate, indicating more efficient utilization of sampled trajectories at a modest computational cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Probabilistic constraint models, learned offline by BNNs, can be effectively integrated into MPPI's sampling distribution to enhance safety without requiring explicit gradient computations or complex penalty tuning.
> - By down-weighting rather than rejecting unsafe trajectories, BC-MPPI improves sample efficiency and preserves exploratory capabilities compared to methods that discard entire rollouts.
> - The modular nature of the BNN safety layer allows for independent verification and runtime monitoring, providing a pathway towards certifiable autonomous systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Model Predictive Control (MPC) has become a widely adopted control strategy in robotics, capable of optimizing finite-horizon costs while explicitly respecting constraints. Its strength lies in using a predictive model to manage complex robotic behaviors. However, its effectiveness in highly non-linear, contact-rich, or perception-driven tasks is often limited by the engineering burden of hand-crafting differentiable, well-conditioned, and sufficiently rich analytical cost and constraint functions.

Stochastic sampling-based Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control emerged as an alternative to traditional MPC, particularly for problems with non-linear dynamics and non-differentiable objectives. MPPI relies on Monte-Carlo rollouts to estimate path integrals, thereby avoiding explicit gradient computations. This characteristic allows it to achieve state-of-the-art performance in complex scenarios such as aggressive autonomous driving, agile quadrotor flight, and contact-rich locomotion. Recent advancements, including GPU acceleration and differentiable programming frameworks, have enabled real-time MPPI operation. Techniques like low-pass filtering and learned importance sample priors have further addressed issues like action noise and stability.

Despite MPPI's advantages in handling non-linearities, it inherently struggles with hard constraint satisfaction due to its Monte-Carlo nature. Naive penalty costs for constraint violations in MPPI often result in brittle tuning and occasional, undesirable constraint breaches. This deficiency poses a significant challenge for safety-critical robotic applications that require formal guarantees for collision avoidance, torque limits, or stability conditions.

Previous efforts to incorporate constraints into stochastic optimal control include methods like Cross-Entropy Motion Planning (CEM) and Robust CEM Planning, which bias sampling distributions to satisfy constraints. Within MPPI, softer constraint handling has been explored through risk-aware objective functions, such as Conditional Value-at-Risk (RA-MPPI), which reduces crashes but does not eliminate violations entirely. Hard constraint enforcement has been attempted via projection or real-time filtering, using techniques like control-barrier functions (CBF) in Shield-MPPI or for stochastic hybrid systems. While these methods offer guarantees, they can sometimes over-constrain the optimizer and compromise solution optimality.

A third category of approaches focuses on shaping the sampling distribution itself to favor feasible, low-risk rollouts. Examples include Robust MPPI, Dynamic Risk-Aware MPPI (which uses sample rejection based on collision probabilities), and Constrained Covariance-Steering MPPI. The work presented in this paper, Bayesian-Constraints MPPI (BC-MPPI), aligns with this distribution-shaping philosophy. It distinguishes itself by integrating a probabilistic constraint model directly into the sampling law, modulating sampling weights without requiring an explicit sample rejection step. This approach aims to provide a principled constraint-handling layer that mitigates the long-standing limitation of MPPI in guaranteeing constraint satisfaction, particularly in safety-critical contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to bridge the gap between Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control's ability to handle complex, non-linear dynamics and its inherent difficulty in providing robust, hard guarantees on constraint satisfaction. The authors aim to introduce a method that embeds a principled constraint-handling layer directly within the MPPI framework, specifically targeting safety-critical robotic applications.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations:
- **MPPI's strengths and weaknesses:** MPPI offers significant advantages over traditional MPC for highly non-linear and non-convex problems by being gradient-free and leveraging stochastic sampling. However, its Monte-Carlo nature means that guaranteeing every sampled trajectory respects state and input limits is challenging, and common approaches like penalty costs often lead to unreliable performance with constraint violations.
- **Requirement for safety guarantees:** In many advanced robotic applications, especially those operating in human environments or performing critical tasks (e.g., autonomous driving, agile flight, industrial manipulation), formal guarantees on safety—such as collision avoidance, adherence to torque limits, or maintaining stability—are paramount. The current lack of such guarantees in MPPI limits its practical deployment in these safety-critical domains.
- **Engineering burden of traditional MPC:** While traditional MPC can handle hard constraints, defining the necessary differentiable and well-conditioned cost and constraint functions for highly complex or perception-driven tasks represents a substantial engineering challenge.
- **Limitations of existing MPPI constraint methods:** Previous attempts to integrate constraints into MPPI often involve "soft" penalty terms, which can result in residual violations, or "hard" filtering/projection methods, which can reduce optimality. Sample rejection, while effective, might be computationally inefficient or limit exploration.

To address these issues, the authors propose Bayesian-Constraints MPPI (BC-MPPI). Their objective is to develop a lightweight safety layer that learns a probabilistic description of task constraints and integrates this knowledge directly into the MPPI sampling process. This integration is designed to reshape the proposal distribution by exponentially down-weighting trajectories likely to violate constraints, thereby pushing the sampling distribution towards the safe subset of trajectories without explicit sample rejection or extensive hand-tuning of penalty costs. The ultimate goal is to enable MPPI to preserve safety margins and satisfy prescribed probabilities of violation while maintaining trajectory optimality, thereby enhancing its applicability in certifiable autonomous systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, Bayesian-Constraints MPPI (BC-MPPI), integrates a probabilistic safety layer with the standard Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control. The core idea is to reshape the MPPI sampling distribution by weighting candidate trajectories based on their predicted feasibility, as determined by learned Bayesian surrogate models of the constraints.

#### 3.1. Background on Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)

MPPI operates by solving a non-linear, non-convex optimal control problem through stochastic sampling. At each replanning step, it minimizes a cost function (Equation 1) over a finite horizon. This cost comprises a terminal cost and a sum of instantaneous costs along the trajectory, subject to system dynamics and an input parametrization.
1. **Sampling and Weighting:** MPPI draws *K* perturbations ($\Delta\theta_k$) from a Gaussian distribution ($N(0, \Sigma)$) around a nominal parameter vector ($\bar{\theta}$). For each perturbation, the system dynamics are rolled out to generate a candidate trajectory, and its total cost ($J_k$) is evaluated. Each trajectory is then assigned an importance weight ($\omega_k$) using an exponentiated cost function (Equation 3), which includes a temperature parameter ($\lambda$) to balance exploration and exploitation. These weights are normalized and used to update the nominal parameter vector ($\theta^*$) through a weighted average of the perturbations (Equation 4).
2. **Execution:** The first control input ($u^*$) from the optimized sequence corresponding to $\theta^*$ is applied to the robot (Equation 5) and held constant until the next replanning cycle. Modern MPPI implementations leverage parallel computation on GPUs to evaluate thousands of trajectories in sub-millisecond times, enabling real-time control.

#### 3.2. Probabilistic Constraint Satisfaction in BC-MPPI

BC-MPPI extends the classical MPPI framework by incorporating a probabilistic constraint satisfaction mechanism, inspired by Constrained Bayesian Optimization (CBO).
1. **Bayesian Surrogate Models:** Each hard constraint, $c_i(\theta) \le 0$, is modeled by a Bayesian surrogate model, specifically a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN). This BNN returns both a predictive mean ($\mu_i(\theta)$) and an epistemic uncertainty measure (standard deviation, $\sigma_i(\theta)$) for constraint satisfaction.
2. **Feasibility Probability:** Assuming additive Gaussian noise, the probability of a single constraint being satisfied (feasible) is calculated as $Pr[c_i(\theta) \le 0] = \Phi(-\mu_i(\theta)/\sigma_i(\theta))$, where $\Phi$ is the normal cumulative distribution function (CDF).
3. **Joint Feasibility Weighting:** For a candidate trajectory $\theta_k$, the joint probability of satisfying all $I$ constraints is computed by multiplying the individual constraint probabilities: $\prod_{j=1}^{J} Pr[c_j(\theta_k) \le 0]$.
4. **Reshaping the Sampling Distribution:** This joint feasibility probability is then integrated into the MPPI weighting scheme. The standard MPPI importance weight ($\mu_k$) is augmented by this joint feasibility probability, yielding a new weight $\tilde{\mu}_k = \exp(-(J_k - \rho)/\lambda) \times \prod_{j=1}^{J} Pr[c_j(\theta_k) \le 0]$.
 - If a surrogate model predicts high probability of constraint satisfaction for a sample, its factors $Pr[c_j(\theta_k) \le 0]$ are close to 1, and its weight $\tilde{\mu}_k$ remains close to the classic MPPI weight $\mu_k$.
 - If any constraint is likely to be violated, at least one factor becomes vanishingly small, effectively driving $\tilde{\mu}_k$ towards zero. This down-weights unsafe trajectories without explicitly discarding them, preserving the gradient-free nature of MPPI.

#### 3.3. Simulation Experiments

The proposed BC-MPPI method was evaluated in the MuJoCo physics engine using a quadrotor tasked with point-to-point flight amidst obstacles.
1. **Baselines:** BC-MPPI was compared against two baselines:
 - **MPPI-penalty:** A simplified MPPI version where constraint violations are handled solely by adding a large penalty term (1e3) to the objective function if a constraint is violated, based on the L1 distance to obstacles (Equations 8 and 9). No explicit rejection rule is applied.
 - **Classic MPPI:** Standard MPPI as described in Section 3.1, which implicitly rejects high-cost samples but has no explicit constraint layer.
2. **Scenarios:** A range of scenarios was designed, varying in complexity:
 - Static and moving obstacles.
 - Number of obstacles ranging from 3 to 15.
 - Most complex settings involved randomized obstacle trajectories (circular, diagonal, sinusoidal) and target positions at each iteration.
3. **BNN Training:**
 - A Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) served as the surrogate model for BC-MPPI.
 - It was trained offline using a dataset of 1000 simulated rollouts.
 - Each sample in the dataset included the initial system state (13 dimensions), a 25-step sequence of control inputs (100 dimensions), and a scalar constraint-violation term (average penalty terms across the horizon). Total input dimensionality was 113.
 - Rollouts were generated with a 2:2:1 ratio of circular, diagonal, and sinusoidal obstacle trajectories to ensure data richness.
 - The dataset was shuffled, stored offline, split 7:3 for training and testing, and all features were standardized.
 - The BNN achieved a mean squared error of 1.07 and an R² score of 0.08 on the held-out test set, deemed sufficient for probabilistic weighting.
4. **Metrics:** Performance was assessed using both computational and trajectory quality metrics:
 - **Computational:** Simulation runtime and control frequency.
 - **Trajectory Quality:** Average distance from obstacles, total distance from the target, and number of collisions.
 - **Rejection-based methods:** Rejection rate (percentage of sampled rollouts discarded).

This methodical approach allowed for a systematic comparison of BC-MPPI's performance against established and penalty-based methods under varying task complexities, focusing on its ability to enhance safety while maintaining control efficacy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The simulation experiments provided a comparative analysis of BC-MPPI against MPPI-penalty and Classic MPPI across various obstacle avoidance scenarios for a quadrotor.

#### 4.1. BC-MPPI vs. MPPI-penalty

- **Target Achievement:** MPPI-penalty consistently failed to reach the target across all tested scenarios, regardless of obstacle number or motion. This suggests that a simple penalty term in the objective function is insufficient for ensuring goal achievement in the presence of constraints.
- **Safety Performance:** MPPI-penalty trajectories exhibited larger average distances from the target but, paradoxically, reduced distances from obstacles. This indicates that the controller often drove the quadrotor into closer proximity with obstacles as it struggled to find a path to the target. The number of collisions significantly increased with a higher number of obstacles, confirming the inadequacy of penalty-based shaping for enforcing safety.
- **Computational Performance:** MPPI-penalty achieved shorter simulation runtimes and higher control frequencies compared to BC-MPPI, particularly in stationary-obstacle settings. However, this computational efficiency came at the cost of safety and task completion, as evidenced by high collision rates and failure to reach the target. The results underscore that raw penalty terms were unable to effectively prevent unsafe rollouts from dominating the sampling distribution.

#### 4.2. BC-MPPI vs. Classic MPPI

- **Safety Margins (Stationary Obstacles):** In scenarios with stationary obstacles and low-to-moderate numbers of obstacles, BC-MPPI consistently maintained larger average distances from obstacles compared to Classic MPPI (Figure 4a). This finding supports the effectiveness of the Bayesian surrogate in biasing the sampling distribution towards safer trajectories.
- **Safety Margins (Moving Obstacles):** In more complex scenarios involving moving obstacles with randomized circular, diagonal, and sinusoidal trajectories, the safety margin advantage of BC-MPPI narrowed. Both methods yielded similar average obstacle clearances (Figure 4b).
- **Target Tracking:** Despite comparable obstacle clearances in complex scenarios, BC-MPPI consistently tracked the target more closely than Classic MPPI (Figure 5). Although the absolute difference in average distance to the target was small (on the order of centimeters), this effect was observed consistently across trials, indicating a more precise goal-seeking behavior.
- **Collision Rate and Sample Efficiency:** In the most complex environments, BC-MPPI produced fewer collisions compared to Classic MPPI. Crucially, BC-MPPI achieved this while also maintaining a lower rejection rate overall (Figure 6). This result suggests that BC-MPPI utilizes samples more efficiently: instead of discarding unsafe rollouts entirely (as Classic MPPI might effectively do for high-cost samples), it probabilistically down-weights them, thereby preserving potentially useful exploration while enhancing safety.
- **Computational Performance:** Classic MPPI generally exhibited higher control frequencies across almost all scenarios, making it more responsive to dynamic changes. However, this responsiveness was associated with a higher rejection rate and greater variability in trajectory quality, implying a trade-off where Classic MPPI prioritized speed while BC-MPPI prioritized consistent safety and goal-tracking at a modest computational cost.

In summary, BC-MPPI demonstrated superior safety performance and more reliable target tracking compared to MPPI-penalty, which largely failed. Against Classic MPPI, BC-MPPI achieved clearer safety margins in simpler scenarios, reduced collisions in complex environments, and maintained better target adherence with more efficient sample utilization, albeit with a slight reduction in control frequency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Bayesian-Constraints MPPI (BC-MPPI) presents a significant advancement in transforming Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) from a purely stochastic, gradient-free controller into a closed-loop scheme that incorporates an explicit and probabilistic understanding of safety. This development has several implications for the field of autonomous systems, particularly concerning verification and validation (V&V).

One key aspect of BC-MPPI's significance lies in its amenability to V&V processes. The Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) surrogate model, which learns and predicts constraint satisfaction probabilities, is developed offline using a finite, version-controlled dataset. This characteristic allows the BNN to function as a static artifact that can undergo traditional software engineering practices such as unit testing, regression testing with new data, and even formal probabilistic checks (e.g., ensuring posterior variance remains within acceptable bounds across the reachable state space). Furthermore, at runtime, each trajectory is assigned a single scalar weight, $w(\theta) = \prod_{j} Pr[c_j(\theta) \le 0]$, which monotonically reflects the level of constraint satisfaction. This scalar value can be readily monitored by a lightweight runtime guard. If the weight falls below a certified threshold, control can be safely handed over to a pre-verified, simpler autonomous mode, thereby enhancing the overall system's robustness and certifiability.

The experimental results underscore the architecture's ability to balance computational cost with safety guarantees. The stark failure of MPPI-penalty to reach targets and its susceptibility to collisions in even simple scenarios demonstrate that merely adding penalty terms to the objective function is inadequate for enforcing hard safety requirements in practice. In contrast, BC-MPPI consistently achieved target goals across all scenarios, highlighting its practical effectiveness.

When compared to Classic MPPI, BC-MPPI demonstrated comparable or superior obstacle clearance and more consistent target tracking. Crucially, it led to a reduction in collisions within the most complex environments. These improvements were achieved with a lower rejection rate, indicating that BC-MPPI's probabilistic weighting strategy is more sample-efficient than explicit sample rejection. This efficiency is vital for maintaining exploration capabilities while ensuring safety.

While BC-MPPI incurred a modest increase in simulation runtimes and a reduction in control frequencies due to the surrogate evaluation and weighting, this trade-off is often acceptable in safety-critical applications. The baselines' higher responsiveness was frequently accompanied by higher collision rates and reduced reliability, illustrating that a higher control frequency alone does not equate to safety. For applications where verifiable safety is paramount, the slight increase in computational overhead is justified by the measurable reduction in constraint violations and the ability to offer probabilistic safety guarantees.

The architecture of BC-MPPI also facilitates a clear separation of concerns, which is beneficial for complex system design and management. Low-level dynamics can remain within optimized, handwritten code. The probabilistic surrogate model and its weighting rule are encapsulated in a self-contained module, allowing for independent verification using standard software engineering methodologies (code review, static analysis, continuous integration). High-level mission logic can then rely on the scalar safety weight as a verifiable contract, simplifying compositional reasoning about the entire autonomous system. This modularity could streamline the development and certification processes for sophisticated autonomous robots.

Looking forward, the insights gained from this work can drive further research into optimizing surrogate model accuracy, scaling BC-MPPI to higher-dimensional robotic systems, and integrating it with embedded hardware. The potential for automatic coverage metrics for training datasets, formal co-analysis of surrogate models with temporal-logic mission specifications, and the design of incremental re-training pipelines for dynamic environments further solidify BC-MPPI's role as a foundational step towards certifiable and truly autonomous systems.
