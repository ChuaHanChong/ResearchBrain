# Inferring and Executing Programs for Visual Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford University and Facebook AI Research developed a visual reasoning model that infers and executes programs for Visual Question Answering. This model achieved 96.9% overall accuracy on the CLEVR dataset with strong supervision, and 95.4% accuracy using only 2.5% of the ground-truth program supervision.

## Problem

- Existing visual reasoning systems and VQA models struggle with compositional understanding, often exploiting dataset biases rather than genuinely reasoning about novel object interactions.
- Prior Neural Module Networks (NMNs) relied on hand-designed syntactic parsers and hand-engineered modules, limiting their generalizability to complex natural language questions.
- Traditional 'black-box' deep learning architectures lack interpretability, making it difficult to understand the step-by-step reasoning process for visual tasks.

## Method

- A ==neural program generator== (==LSTM sequence-to-sequence== model) translates natural language questions into ==abstract syntax trees== representing compositional reasoning programs.
- A ==neural execution engine==, implemented as a ==module network== with generic neural modules, executes the generated programs on visual features extracted from an image.
- A semi-supervised training approach combines initial supervised learning with ==reinforcement learning (REINFORCE)== for joint finetuning, reducing the reliance on extensive ground-truth program annotations.

## Results

- The model achieved **96.9%** overall accuracy on the CLEVR dataset with strong supervision and **95.4%** with only **18K (2.5%)** ground-truth programs, outperforming prior VQA baselines.
- It demonstrated strong generalization to new attribute combinations (CLEVR-CoGenT), novel question types (longer reasoning chains), and free-form human-posed questions (CLEVR-Humans).
- Visualizations confirmed that modules consistently attended to relevant objects and regions, and adapted their attention based on changes in question semantics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling visual reasoning as the inference and execution of compositional programs significantly enhances understanding compared to direct input-to-output mappings.
> - Both the program generator and the generic neural modules for execution can be learned effectively with limited program supervision, indicating the system's ability to abstract compositional logic.
> - The learned modules demonstrate specialized functions (e.g., filtering, querying, spatial relations) and attend to relevant image regions, offering a degree of interpretability into the reasoning process.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of computer vision is actively pursuing methods for visual reasoning, particularly in tasks such as Visual Question Answering (VQA). Conventional approaches to visual recognition often rely on direct input-to-output mappings using "black-box" architectures like deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) combined with recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or LSTMs. While effective for tasks like image classification or object detection with fixed categories, these models exhibit limitations when confronted with complex, compositional queries that require understanding novel object interactions or attribute combinations. Existing VQA systems based on such architectures have been observed to primarily exploit dataset biases rather than genuinely learning compositional reasoning, as highlighted by their performance on diagnostic datasets like CLEVR.

This work builds upon and addresses limitations of prior research in several areas:
- **Visual Question Answering (VQA):** It tackles the core challenge in VQA of enabling models to perform explicit visual reasoning beyond surface-level correlations. Previous VQA models have been criticized for their inability to generalize to out-of-distribution questions or to genuinely reason about spatial relationships and disentangled representations.
- **Reasoning-Augmented Models:** The paper aligns with efforts to augment neural networks with explicit reasoning components. While some approaches, such as Neural Turing Machines or Memory Networks, focus on long-term memory, this work specifically targets compositional reasoning within the visual domain.
- **Neural Module Networks (NMNs):** This research is a direct advancement of NMNs, which incorporate compositional reasoning by dynamically assembling neural networks (modules) based on a question's syntactic structure. Prior NMNs, however, relied on hand-designed, off-the-shelf syntactic parsers and hand-engineered modules. These parsers often proved inadequate for complex natural language questions, limiting the generalization and practical applicability of such models.
- **Semantic Parsers and Program Induction:** The approach relates to semantic parsing, which maps natural language to logical forms, and program induction, which learns programs from input-output pairs. However, this work differentiates itself by learning both the program generator (akin to semantic parsing) and the execution engine (interpreting low-level operators), whereas previous methods often assumed fixed semantics or predefined low-level operators. Additionally, it applies these concepts to complex visual inputs rather than simpler algorithmic tasks.

By developing a model that learns both to generate and execute compositional programs without heavy reliance on manual heuristics, this paper aims to provide a more flexible and generalizable solution for visual reasoning, pushing beyond the limitations of purely end-to-end black-box systems and earlier, more constrained module network designs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that current visual reasoning systems, particularly those for VQA, struggle with tasks requiring compositional understanding. Many modern approaches learn a direct mapping from inputs (image, question) to outputs (answer) without explicitly modeling the underlying reasoning process. This "black-box" nature leads to models that often exploit statistical biases in datasets rather than developing robust reasoning capabilities, thereby failing to generalize to novel scenarios or complex queries. The ability to perform compositional reasoning, where an understanding of individual components (objects, attributes, actions) is combined to interpret novel interactions, is considered a hallmark of human intelligence and is crucial for applications such as robotic navigation, autonomous driving, and general visual intelligence.

Given these limitations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Explicitly Model Compositional Reasoning:** To move beyond direct input-output mappings by designing a model that explicitly constructs and executes a step-by-step reasoning plan or "program" to answer visual questions. This program is intended to represent the compositional steps required to derive an answer.
2. **Develop a Learnable Program Generator:** To create a neural network-based program generator that can infer the correct compositional program directly from a natural language question. This objective specifically aims to overcome the reliance of prior module networks on fixed, hand-tuned syntactic parsers, which proved ineffective for complex questions.
3. **Implement a Learnable Execution Engine:** To build a neural network-based execution engine that can interpret and execute the generated program on an image to produce an answer. A key aspect is the use of a generic, universal architecture for the individual neural modules that comprise the execution engine, thereby avoiding hand-engineering specific modules for different functions.
4. **Enable Training with Limited Program Supervision:** To devise practical training procedures that can effectively learn both the program generator and execution engine. Recognizing the expense of annotating ground-truth programs, a specific objective is to enable strong performance even with minimal ground-truth program supervision, primarily through semi-supervised learning and reinforcement learning (REINFORCE) for joint finetuning.
5. **Demonstrate Robust Generalization:** To evaluate the model's ability to generalize across various challenging scenarios:
 - **Performance on a Diagnostic Benchmark:** Achieve state-of-the-art accuracy on the CLEVR dataset, which is designed to test compositional reasoning and mitigate dataset biases.
 - **Compositional Generalization:** Assess generalization to novel object-attribute combinations (e.g., CLEVR-CoGenT dataset) not seen during training.
 - **Structural Generalization:** Evaluate generalization to question types with novel program structures, specifically from "short" to "long" reasoning chains.
 - **Linguistic Generalization:** Test the model's ability to adapt to and answer free-form, human-posed natural language questions, which often exhibit greater linguistic variety and ambiguity than algorithmically generated ones.
6. **Enhance Model Interpretability:** To provide a degree of interpretability by demonstrating how the modules in the execution engine attend to specific visual regions and perform their intended functions during the reasoning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed model for visual reasoning consists of two interconnected neural network components: a program generator and an execution engine.

#### 3.1. Programs

The core of the approach involves defining a domain-specific language for reasoning. Programs are represented as abstract syntax trees composed of functions from a predefined vocabulary `F`. Each function `f` has a fixed arity (unary or binary, `n_f ∈ {1, 2}`). A special constant `Scene` represents the visual features of the input image, serving as a leaf node in the program tree. The system learns the *semantics* (how to execute) these functions, while the *syntax* (how to build valid programs) is fixed.

#### 3.2. Program Generator (π(q))

This component translates a natural language question `q` into a program `z`.
- **Architecture:** It is implemented as a standard Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) sequence-to-sequence model.
 - **Encoder:** Receives the question as a sequence of words. Words are converted to 300-dimensional learned embeddings. A two-layer LSTM with 256 hidden units processes this sequence. The final hidden state of the second LSTM layer summarizes the question into a fixed-length vector.
 - **Decoder:** Takes the encoder's output and sequentially generates the program. At each timestep, it receives an embedding of the previously generated function (or a `<START>` token) and the encoder's output. A two-layer LSTM with 256 hidden units processes these, and its hidden state is used by a linear projection to predict a distribution over all possible functions.
- **Program Serialization:** Programs, being tree structures, are serialized into sequences using a prefix traversal to enable LSTM processing.
- **Error Handling:** During inference, generated sequences are converted to syntax trees. If a sequence is too short (functions lack sufficient children), `Scene` constants are padded. If too long (functions lack parents), unused functions are discarded.

#### 3.3. Execution Engine (φ(x, z))

This component executes the generated program `z` on the input image `x` to produce an answer `a`.
- **Architecture:** It functions as a neural module network. For each function `f` in the predefined vocabulary `F`, there is a corresponding neural network module `m_f`. The program `z` dictates how these modules are assembled into a question-specific neural network `m(z)`. The outputs of "child modules" feed into their "parent modules" following the program's structure.
- **Module Design:** In contrast to prior NMNs, a generic architecture is used for all modules, simplifying the design and training.
 - **Input Features:** The initial visual features for the `Scene` module are extracted from the `conv4` layer of a ResNet-101 model, pre-trained on ImageNet and kept fixed during execution engine training. These features are further processed by four convolutional layers.
 - **Unary Modules:** Each unary module (e.g., `filter`, `query`) is a standard residual block with two 3x3 convolutional layers, maintaining the C×H×W feature map shape.
 - **Binary Modules:** Binary modules (e.g., `intersect`, `equal`) concatenate their two input feature maps along the channel dimension, use a 1x1 convolution to project back to the original channel count, and then feed the result into a residual block.
- **Output Classifier:** The final feature map produced by the root module of the assembled network is flattened and passed through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) classifier to output a distribution over a fixed set of possible answers `A`.
- **Batch Normalization:** Batch Normalization was omitted from modules due to issues with single-example batch sizes during execution engine training (each program in a minibatch is processed individually).

#### 3.4. Training Procedures

The model supports different training regimes:
- **Supervised Training (with ground-truth programs):**
 - **Program Generator:** Trained independently as a sequence-to-sequence model using (question, ground-truth program) pairs, optimizing cross-entropy loss.
 - **Execution Engine:** Trained independently using (image, ground-truth program, answer) triplets. Gradients are computed via backpropagation through the assembled module network.
- **Semi-Supervised Learning (practical approach, primary method):** Designed for scenarios with limited or no ground-truth programs for the full dataset.
 1. **Initial Program Generator Training:** A small set of (question, ground-truth program) pairs is used to train an initial program generator via supervised learning.
 2. **Execution Engine Training with Predicted Programs:** The program generator is then fixed, and the execution engine is trained on a larger dataset of (image, question, answer) triplets. For each question, the fixed program generator predicts a program, which is then used by the execution engine.
 3. **Joint Finetuning with REINFORCE:** Both the program generator and execution engine are jointly finetuned on the full (image, question, answer) dataset without further ground-truth program supervision. Since the program generator's argmax operation is non-differentiable, REINFORCE is employed to estimate gradients. The reward signal for the program generator's output is the negative zero-one loss of the execution engine, combined with a moving-average baseline to reduce variance.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details (from Supplementary Material)

- **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer is used throughout training, with specific learning rates and batch sizes for each stage (e.g., 5e-4 for program generator, 1e-4 for execution engine, 5e-5 for joint training, all with batch size 64).
- **Early Stopping:** Training for all components employs early stopping based on validation set accuracy.
- **Moving Average Baseline:** For REINFORCE, an exponentially decaying moving average of past rewards (decay factor 0.99) serves as the baseline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the proposed model across several dimensions, primarily using the CLEVR dataset and its variants.

#### 4.1. Performance on the CLEVR Benchmark (Table 1)

- **Strong Supervision:** When trained with full ground-truth program supervision ("Ours-strong", 700K programs), the model achieved an overall accuracy of 96.9%, which surpassed human performance (92.6%) and significantly outperformed all baseline VQA models (e.g., CNN+LSTM+SA+MLP: 73.2%). This indicated that the explicit compositional approach, given perfect program supervision, is highly effective.
- **Semi-Supervision:** The model demonstrated efficiency in learning with limited program supervision. Using only 18K ground-truth programs (approximately 2.5% of the total available) for initial program generator training, the "Ours-semi" model achieved 95.4% overall accuracy, closely approaching the fully supervised variant. Even with 9K ground-truth programs, it reached 88.6%. This suggests the model's ability to generalize the underlying compositional structure from a small set of examples.
- **Program Generator Accuracy (Figure 4):** The program generator achieved near-perfect accuracy (over 95%) in correctly predicting ground-truth programs after training with only approximately 20K supervised programs. This indicated that the program generator learned the syntax and mapping rules for a large number of unique programs (450K in CLEVR) from a small subset.

#### 4.2. Interpretability and Module Functionality (Figure 3)

- Visualizations, depicting the norm of the gradient of predicted answer scores with respect to the final feature map, provided insight into the model's attention mechanisms. The model consistently attended to relevant objects and regions in the image, even for complex queries involving spatial relationships, conjunctions, and disjunctions.
- The visualizations illustrated that specific modules performed their intended functions. Minor changes in the question (e.g., swapping "purple" for "blue," "left" for "right," or "and" for "or") led to distinct changes in module attention and the final predicted answer, demonstrating that individual modules learned specialized operations like localization and set operations without direct supervision of their intermediate outputs.

#### 4.3. Generalizing to New Attribute Combinations (CLEVR-CoGenT) (Figure 5)

- On the CLEVR-CoGenT dataset, designed to test compositional generalization by swapping color-shape correlations, the model trained on Condition A initially outperformed baselines on Condition B, but still showed a drop in accuracy compared to Condition A.
- Crucially, when finetuned on a small amount of Condition B data (around 10K questions, 1K images) *without ground-truth programs*, the model learned the new attribute combinations effectively. It then significantly outperformed all similarly trained baselines on Condition B, suggesting its compositional nature allowed for rapid adaptation of attribute semantics from limited data.

#### 4.4. Generalizing to New Question Types (Long Questions) (Table 2, Figure 6)

- When the program generator was initially trained only on "short" questions (mean program length < 16), the model's performance on "long" questions dropped considerably (55.3% accuracy) compared to its performance on short questions (95.9%). This indicated a bias towards generating shorter programs.
- After *joint finetuning* on the combined dataset of short and long questions (without ground-truth programs), the model's accuracy on long questions significantly improved to 77.8%. It then outperformed baselines that were trained on the entire dataset. This demonstrated the effectiveness of finetuning, using REINFORCE, in enabling the program generator to learn to produce longer, more complex programs corresponding to the question's intent.

#### 4.5. Generalizing to Human-Posed Questions (CLEVR-Humans) (Table 3, Figure 7)

- A new dataset, CLEVR-Humans, consisting of free-form human-posed questions for CLEVR images, was collected to evaluate generalization to more linguistically diverse and ambiguous questions.
- When trained on synthetic CLEVR questions and then finetuned *only the program generator* on the CLEVR-Humans dataset (without program supervision for human questions), the model's accuracy improved from 54.0% to 66.6%. This performance significantly exceeded all baseline models (e.g., CNN+LSTM+SA+MLP: 57.6%).
- The model demonstrated an ability to map novel words (e.g., "box") present in human questions to its existing module vocabulary and learned to approximate question intent even when exact semantics were not directly expressible by its fixed set of modules. However, the model sometimes failed on questions requiring skills outside its module inventory.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of visual reasoning and visual question answering, particularly through its systematic approach to incorporating explicit compositional reasoning.

1. **Enhanced Visual Reasoning Capabilities:** The model significantly improves performance on complex VQA tasks, demonstrating that explicitly modeling the reasoning process as a program leads to superior accuracy compared to black-box, end-to-end architectures. This addresses a fundamental limitation of many contemporary vision-language models.
2. **Generalization to Novelty:** The work highlights the model's robust generalization capabilities across various challenging scenarios. Its ability to generalize to new attribute combinations (CLEVR-CoGenT), novel program structures (long questions), and human-posed natural language questions (CLEVR-Humans) suggests a higher degree of understanding and adaptability than previously achieved by either purely black-box models or earlier, heuristically-driven module networks.
3. **Data Efficiency and Practicality:** The semi-supervised training approach demonstrates that high performance can be achieved with a relatively small amount of ground-truth program supervision. This reduces the substantial annotation burden typically associated with collecting such detailed data, making the approach more practical for real-world applications where exhaustive program labels are infeasible.
4. **Interpretability:** By explicitly generating programs and visualizing module-specific attention, the model offers a degree of interpretability into its decision-making process. This contrasts with the opaque nature of many end-to-end deep learning models, providing insights into *how* the model arrives at an answer rather than just *what* the answer is. Such interpretability is valuable for debugging, trustworthiness, and understanding learned capabilities.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The generic design of the modules and the learnable program generator provide a flexible framework for future extensions. The discussion section outlines clear paths for further research, such as automatically identifying and learning new modules or incorporating Turing-complete sets of modules (e.g., with control-flow operators like if/then/else and loops) to handle an even broader range of reasoning tasks. This provides a strong direction for integrating symbolic representations more deeply into neural architectures.
6. **Impact on Real-World Applications:** The demonstrated abilities—especially in compositional generalization and adapting to human language—have potential implications for developing more intelligent computer vision systems for applications such as autonomous systems (robots, self-driving cars) that need to interpret complex queries about dynamic visual environments, surveillance systems requiring sophisticated scene understanding, and interactive AI agents.

In summary, this research underscores the effectiveness of structured, explicit reasoning within neural networks for complex visual tasks, moving closer to building machines that can reason and understand the visual world in a more compositional and adaptable manner.
