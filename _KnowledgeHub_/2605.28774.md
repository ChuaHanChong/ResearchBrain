# Agent Explorative Policy Optimization for Multimodal Agentic Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Agent Explorative Policy Optimization (AXPO) is introduced to bridge the "Thinking-Acting Gap" in multimodal agentic reasoning, a challenge where standard reinforcement learning under-trains tool use. This method notably improves vision-language model performance in tool-intensive tasks, with an 8B AXPO-trained model achieving superior Pass@4 scores compared to a 32B baseline.

## Problem

- Current multimodal agentic reasoning models struggle with effectively integrating external tool use, often due to an inherent asymmetry between internal "thinking" and external "acting."
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods like GRPO are suboptimal in addressing this "Thinking-Acting Gap," leading to under-attempted tool usage and suppressed learning signals for tool-calling behaviors.
- Empirical analysis revealed tool use is under-attempted (~30% of rollouts) and tool-using subgroups are frequently "all-wrong" (~40% of cases), hindering effective learning from RL.

## Method

- Introduces Agent Explorative Policy Optimization (**AXPO**), an RL algorithm built upon ==GRPO==, designed to specifically address the identified "Thinking-Acting Gap."
- Employs prefix-fixed resampling to concentrate exploration on the tool-call boundary, generating multiple tool-using continuations from a fixed thinking prefix.
- Utilizes uncertainty-based ranking to prioritize resampling from prefixes with low confidence in their tool-call tokens and applies a binary recovery reward to credit prefixes leading to successful resampled continuations.

## Results

- AXPO consistently improved performance, achieving average Pass@1 gains of +1.1 to +**1.8** percentage points and Pass@4 gains of +1.8 to +**2.8** percentage points across different model sizes (2B, 4B, 8B).
- The 8B AXPO-trained model demonstrated enhanced parameter efficiency, outperforming the 32B Base model on Pass@4 (**75.8%** vs. **75.1%**) and reaching **99%** of its Pass@1 performance.
- AXPO effectively closed the "Thinking-Acting Gap" during training, increasing the tool-use rate by **28** percentage points and reducing the "all-wrong" rate for tool-using subgroups by approximately **17** percentage points.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The "Thinking-Acting Gap" is characterized by under-attempted and frequently incorrect tool use, where standard RL methods fail to provide adequate learning signals for tool-call tokens.
> - Targeted exploration focused on the tool-call boundary, achieved through prefix-fixed resampling, is more effective than increasing overall rollout budgets or uniform exploration for improving tool-using capabilities.
> - By concentrating stochasticity and applying separate advantage calculations for shared prefixes and resampled continuations, AXPO can effectively restore positive learning signals for tool-using behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) has advanced significantly, demonstrating extended reasoning capabilities that enable models to solve complex problems through internal thought processes. However, many real-world tasks necessitate interaction with external resources, such as retrieving current information via web search, performing precise numerical computations with code interpreters, or discerning fine-grained visual details using image manipulation tools. This integration of external tools with internal reasoning is termed "Multimodal Agentic Reasoning."

Current approaches to developing agentic reasoning models typically involve a two-stage post-training pipeline: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on expert demonstrations, followed by Reinforcement Learning (RL) using verifiable outcome rewards. While SFT effectively teaches the mechanics of tool use, it often struggles to generalize to scenarios not covered by the training demonstrations, leading to performance plateaus. RL methods, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), are employed to refine tool utilization by providing a training signal beyond imitation.

This paper identifies a specific challenge within agentic reasoning, termed the "Thinking-Acting Gap." This gap arises from an inherent asymmetry: "thinking" is the model’s default, self-contained mode, offering internal control and error correction, whereas "tool use" (acting) is an auxiliary, high-variance behavior where a brief token sequence triggers an unpredictable external response. Existing RL methods like GRPO are observed to be suboptimal in addressing this asymmetry, as they exhibit reduced learning signals for tool-using behaviors. This research aims to develop a targeted RL approach that specifically addresses this gap, thereby enhancing the model's proficiency in tool-integrated reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the performance of vision-language models in multimodal agentic reasoning tasks by addressing a specific challenge identified as the "Thinking-Acting Gap."

The motivation stems from the observation that while VLMs with extended reasoning can solve complex problems, many real-world scenarios require external tools. The paper posits that current agentic reasoning systems, particularly those trained with standard RL methods like GRPO, exhibit a structural asymmetry between "thinking" (internal, self-contained reasoning) and "tool use" (auxiliary, high-variance interaction with external environments). This asymmetry constitutes the "Thinking-Acting Gap."

The authors diagnose this gap through two empirical symptoms observed during GRPO training:
1. **Under-attempted Tool Use:** Tool use remains a minority behavior. Only approximately 30% of rollouts attempt tool use, a rate that remains largely unchanged across RL training steps (Figure 3a). This indicates that GRPO does not sufficiently incentivize the model to explore tool-using trajectories.
2. **Proneness to "All-Wrong" Tool-Using Subgroups:** When tool use is attempted within a group of rollouts for a single question, the tool-using subgroup (all rollouts that include tool use) is "all-wrong" (none are correct) in approximately 40% of cases (Figure 3b). In contrast, "no-tool" subgroups exhibit an all-wrong rate of about 25%. This phenomenon significantly suppresses the learning signal for tool-call tokens: in all-wrong groups, the group-normalized advantage collapses to zero, while in mixed-success groups (where no-tool rollouts succeed but tool-using ones fail), tool-call tokens receive a negative advantage. Consequently, the model receives insufficient positive feedback to learn effective tool use in scenarios where it is most needed.

A further motivation is the observation that tool calls, even with a fixed thinking prefix, can lead to substantively diverse actions. By fixing a failed rollout's thinking prefix and resampling continuations, the authors demonstrate that the resulting tool calls do not collapse to a single semantic class, but rather occupy multiple distinct clusters (2.9–3.4 clusters out of 16 samples on average for different tool types) (Figure 3c). This suggests that the tool-call boundary is a genuine divergence point, ripe for targeted exploration, and that the thinking prefix alone does not fully determine the subsequent action.

These diagnostic observations motivate the development of an RL algorithm that specifically targets and closes this Thinking-Acting Gap by concentrating exploration on the tool call itself, rather than relying on uniform sampling or other general RL improvements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology addresses the Thinking-Acting Gap by proposing Agent Explorative Policy Optimization (AXPO), an RL algorithm built upon a standard SFT-then-RL pipeline using GRPO.

#### 3.1. Agentic Reasoning Preliminaries

The work focuses on Vision-Language Models (VLMs) (specifically Qwen3-VL-Thinking) that perform agentic reasoning. This involves an iterative process where the model generates a thinking segment, followed by an action (a tool call formatted as JSON), and then receives an observation (tool output). This continues until a final answer or turn limit. The tool set includes a Python interpreter, a web search engine (Tavily), and an image zoom-in tool.

#### 3.2. Baseline: RL Training with GRPO

The baseline training pipeline involves Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on expert trajectories, followed by Reinforcement Learning (RL). For RL, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is used. In GRPO, for a given input, the policy generates $N$ rollouts. Each rollout receives a binary reward based on answer correctness. A group-normalized advantage is computed for each rollout and uniformly applied to every token in that rollout to update the policy via a PPO-clip surrogate objective, regularized by a KL penalty towards a fixed reference policy.

#### 3.3. Diagnosing the Thinking-Acting Gap

The empirical analysis (detailed in Section 2.2 and Figure 3) establishes the existence and characteristics of the Thinking-Acting Gap:
- **Tool use is under-attempted:** Across RL training steps, approximately 20-35% of rollouts involve at least one tool call, indicating a persistent under-representation of tool-using behavior (Figure 3a).
- **Tool-using subgroups are prone to being all-wrong:** When tool use occurs, the "tool-using subgroup" (rollouts within a group that invoke a tool) fails to yield any correct answers in about 40% of questions, compared to 25% for "no-tool subgroups" (Figure 3b). This leads to non-positive advantage signals for tool-call tokens, suppressing learning.
- **Tool calls admit substantive resampling:** Analysis shows that fixing a thinking prefix and resampling continuations can produce diverse and semantically distinct tool calls (Figure 3c). This indicates the tool-call boundary is a critical divergence point for exploration.

#### 3.4. Agent Explorative Policy Optimization (AXPO)

AXPO is designed to overcome the Thinking-Acting Gap through targeted exploration at the tool-call boundary.

#### 3.4.1. Tool-call Resampling

Instead of uniformly scaling rollouts, AXPO concentrates stochasticity on the continuation from a chosen thinking prefix that already commits to a tool call. For a source tool-using rollout ($\tau_{src}$), AXPO fixes its thinking prefix ($t_{src}^1$, which includes the `<tool_call>` tag) and draws $K$ new continuations ($y_{res}^k$) from the policy conditioned on this prefix. These resampled trajectories ($\tau_{res}^k$) share the same prefix but diverge at the tool call, ensuring all samples are tool-using.

**Theoretical Motivation (Proposition 1):** The paper provides a theoretical argument that prefix-fixed resampling dominates raw sampling in recovering correct tool-using rollouts. By construction, every resampled continuation is tool-using (effectively setting the tool-use rate $q \rightarrow 1$), eliminating the $(1-q)$ waste factor present in raw sampling where non-tool rollouts cannot teach the tool call. This significantly increases the probability of generating at least one correct tool-using rollout from the fixed prefix.

#### 3.4.2. Resample Candidate Selection

- **Trigger:** AXPO only resamples from groups where the tool-using subgroup is non-empty and entirely wrong. These are the cases where GRPO would yield non-positive advantage, and a single successful recovery can significantly impact the learning signal.
- **Budget Allocation:** The extra resampling cost is capped at a fraction ($r=0.25$) of the standard rollout budget per step. Questions are prioritized breadth-first.
- **Uncertainty-based Prefix Ranking:** Among triggered prefixes, AXPO prioritizes resampling from those with the lowest confidence (mean policy probability) over the tool-call tokens in the source rollout. This focuses exploration on uncertain or "hard" tool calls. Confidence is used as a tractable proxy for predictive entropy (justified in Appendix B.9).

#### 3.4.3. Advantage Calculation

To avoid contradictory gradients for shared prefix tokens, AXPO decomposes the advantage streams:
- **Resampled Continuations:** For each selected prefix $t_{src}^1$, the $K$ resampled continuations form an independent GRPO group. A per-prefix GRPO advantage ($\hat{A}_{res}^k(t_{src}^1)$) is computed for each continuation, but applied *only* to the continuation tokens ($y_{res}^k$), masking the prefix tokens.
- **Source Trajectory Prefix:** The prefix tokens of the original source rollout ($t_{src}^1$) are updated using a binary recovery reward, $r_{prefix}(t_{src}^1)$. This reward is 1 if at least one of the $K$ resampled continuations is correct, and 0 otherwise. This recovery reward replaces the original (zero) reward of the failed source rollout within its GRPO group, ensuring the prefix is credited positively when resampling leads to success. The resulting advantage ($\hat{A}_{prefix}(t_{src}^1)$) is applied *only* to the source prefix tokens, masking the continuation.

The combined objective function for a selected prefix (Equation 5) sums the PPO-clip surrogate losses for the source prefix (with recovery reward) and all successful resampled continuations. Non-triggered groups and rollouts not selected for resampling retain the standard GRPO loss.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Base Models:** Qwen3-VL-Thinking (2B, 4B, 8B). Qwen3-VL-32B-Thinking is used as an inference-only baseline and SFT teacher.
- **Training Data:** SFT uses 64K correct trajectories from ViRL, fvqa, and PyVision-RL. RL uses a filtered subset of SFT data (15.5K problems) plus 22K hard questions from MMFineReason-hard (total ~37K).
- **Benchmarks:** Nine multimodal benchmarks, grouped into Reasoning (MathVision, DynaMath, Math-VR), Perception (V*, VisualProbe, HR-Bench-4K, HR-Bench-8K), and Search (HR-MMSearch, MMSearch).
- **Metrics:** Pass@1 (average accuracy over 4 rollouts) and Pass@4 (at least one correct rollout out of 4).
- **Baselines:** Base (no training, with tools), GRPO (RL from base), SFT (SFT only), SFT + GRPO (standard SFT-then-RL).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides comprehensive results demonstrating the effectiveness of AXPO in improving multimodal agentic reasoning.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **AXPO vs. SFT + GRPO:** SFT + AXPO consistently outperforms SFT + GRPO across all tested model sizes and on average. Specifically, on average Pass@1, AXPO achieves gains of +1.1 pp (2B), +1.4 pp (4B), and +1.8 pp (8B). For Pass@4, the gains are +2.8 pp (2B), +2.3 pp (4B), and +1.8 pp (8B) (Table 1, Table 5).
- **Parameter Efficiency:** At 8B parameters, SFT + AXPO surpasses the 32B Base model on Pass@4 (75.8% vs. 75.1%) and achieves 99% of its Pass@1 performance, demonstrating a 4x reduction in parameters for comparable or superior performance (Figure 1).
- **Benchmark Concentration:** The improvements are most pronounced on tool-intensive benchmarks. For the 8B model, SFT + AXPO gains +2.9 pp on Perception tasks and +1.3 pp on Search tasks over SFT + GRPO, compared to +0.6 pp on Reasoning tasks. This indicates that AXPO effectively enhances performance in scenarios where tool interaction is critical.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study on the 8B model confirms that all proposed components of AXPO contribute to its performance (Table 2):
- **Prefix Fixing:** Removing prefix fixing (i.e., rolling out from scratch) degrades performance significantly, dropping the average Pass@1 to 52.1% (compared to 53.9% for full AXPO), highlighting the importance of concentrating exploration.
- **Resampling Target:** Resampling no-tool rollouts instead of all-wrong tool-using rollouts also reduces performance (average Pass@1 of 51.7%), demonstrating the need to target the identified gap.
- **Uncertainty-based Ranking:** Eliminating uncertainty-based prefix selection by using random ranking leads to a drop in average Pass@1 to 51.0%, indicating that intelligent budget allocation is crucial.
- **Prefix Credit:** Removing the binary recovery reward for the source prefix results in an average Pass@1 of 51.4%, showing the necessity of providing a positive signal to the prefix that leads to successful tool use.
- **Separated Advantage Group:** Merging resampled continuations into the source group's advantage calculation (instead of separate groups) leads to 51.2% average Pass@1, confirming that distinct advantage streams for prefixes and continuations are beneficial.

#### 4.3. Comparison to Alternative RL Recipes

AXPO outperforms other RL training strategies on the 8B model (Table 3):
- **Reward Shaping:** Methods using tool penalty or tool bonus perform below the SFT + GRPO baseline (average Pass@1 of 46.0% and 50.8% respectively).
- **Increased Rollout Budget:** Doubling the GRPO rollout budget (2x compute) yields an average Pass@1 of 52.4%, which is still lower than SFT + AXPO's 53.9%, demonstrating that targeted exploration is more effective than simply increasing raw compute.
- **Alternative RL Algorithms:** RLTF (43.3%), CISPO (47.8%), and ARPO (51.3%) all perform below AXPO. Notably, ARPO, which uses mid-trajectory branching after tool observations, is outperformed by AXPO, suggesting that addressing issues at the tool-call boundary (AXPO's focus) is more critical than after tool output (ARPO's focus).

#### 4.4. Narrows the Thinking-Acting Gap During RL Training

- **Training Dynamics (Figure 4):** AXPO successfully reverses the diagnostic symptoms of the Thinking-Acting Gap. Under SFT + AXPO, the tool-use rate significantly climbs by +28 pp over training (compared to flat ~30% for GRPO). Concurrently, the all-wrong rate for tool-using subgroups is suppressed, sitting ~17 pp below GRPO at every step. This suppression is attributed to the per-step restoration rate, where AXPO's resampling flips approximately 12% of all-wrong tool-using subgroups into successful ones, restoring a positive learning signal.
- **Test-time Dynamics (Figure 5):** Post-training analysis shows that AXPO simultaneously increases the tool-attempt rate and improves conditional Pass@1 (Figure 5a), a characteristic not observed in other training stages. Furthermore, on questions where both SFT + GRPO and SFT + AXPO invoke a tool, AXPO demonstrates improvements in Pass@1 across all three tool families (Python, Zoom-In, Search), indicating an enhancement in the quality of tool interaction (Figure 5b).

#### 4.5. Generalization to an Unseen Tool

AXPO's tool-using policy shows better transfer to an image-search tool not included in its training set (approximated via GPT-5.4 at inference time) (Table 8). When image search is enabled, AXPO gains +4.6 pp in Pass@1 (45.0% to 49.6%), whereas GRPO gains +2.8 pp (44.0% to 46.8%). This suggests that AXPO's induced exploration fosters a more generalized understanding of tool interaction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work identifies and addresses a fundamental challenge in the post-training of agentic reasoning models, termed the "Thinking-Acting Gap." By demonstrating that standard RL methods like GRPO under-train tool use due to suppressed learning signals at tool-call tokens, the paper highlights a critical limitation of existing approaches.

The proposed Agent Explorative Policy Optimization (AXPO) offers a targeted and efficient solution. By concentrating exploration precisely at the tool-call boundary through prefix-fixed resampling and uncertainty-based prefix selection, AXPO effectively restores learning signals for tool-using behaviors. The theoretical justification (Proposition 1) and comprehensive empirical validation across diverse multimodal benchmarks and model scales support its efficacy.

The significance of AXPO lies in several areas:
- **Improved Agentic Capabilities:** AXPO enhances both the frequency and quality of tool use, leading to higher accuracy in complex multimodal tasks. This directly translates to more capable and reliable AI agents that can effectively leverage external tools.
- **Parameter Efficiency:** The ability of an 8B AXPO-trained model to surpass a 32B Base model on Pass@4, while achieving comparable Pass@1, demonstrates significant parameter efficiency. This has implications for deploying high-performing agentic models with reduced computational resources.
- **Efficiency in Learning:** AXPO achieves its gains by strategically allocating compute, outperforming methods that simply increase the overall rollout budget. This suggests a more efficient learning process by focusing exploration where it is most impactful.
- **Generalizability:** The improved ability of AXPO to generalize to unseen tool types suggests that the learned policy for tool interaction is more robust and less narrowly fitted to the training environment.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identification of the Thinking-Acting Gap provides a new lens for analyzing agentic RL. AXPO opens avenues for further research into more sophisticated exploration strategies, integration with richer and more complex tool environments, and scaling these methods to even larger foundation models, potentially combining tool-call branching with post-observation branching.

In summary, this research contributes a novel understanding of challenges in agentic RL and provides a demonstrably effective method for improving the integration of thinking and acting in multimodal models, with potential benefits for real-world applications requiring robust and intelligent interaction with diverse tools.
