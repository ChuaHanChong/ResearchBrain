# Context Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Context Language Models (CLMs) let a language model manage its own context. The live context is mirrored as a file that the model can freely edit with Bash, so no harness-defined compaction is needed. Used zero-shot, CLMs beat state-of-the-art context-management baselines at lower compute on deep research, coding, and day-long agent-swarm tasks. Skill evolution, success-gated reinforcement learning, and Suffix Cache Reuse serving improve them further.

## Problem

- Most agent harnesses compact history with fixed, human-designed policies, such as at a length threshold or every turn, and these lose or hallucinate information even on simple retention tasks.
- Action-based methods like AutoCompact, Context-as-a-Tool and ACM give models more control, but they still limit them to a human-defined set of compaction, offloading and retrieval actions.
- Recursive Language Models decide what to read into context but leave the live context append-only, and standard serving reuses only matching prefixes, so mid-context edits are costly.

## Method

- **Context-as-a-file**: the live context is mirrored to an editable file, and Bash edits to it sync into the next turn. This turns append-only context updates into arbitrary model-controlled ones and extends naturally to multi-agent swarms.
- **In-context steering and skill evolution**: natural-language instructions or a ==skill document== shape the context-management policy without weight updates, and a ==prompt-evolution loop== with external or self proposers optimizes that document.
- ==Stepwise GRPO== with a ==success-gated efficiency advantage== that re-ranks only successful trajectories by prefix-reuse FLOPs, plus **Suffix Cache Reuse** serving that keeps the cached states of tokens that survive an edit.

## Results

- On BrowseComp-Plus with Qwen3.6-27B at a 32K context limit, CLM scores **59.4%**. That is **11.4%** relative above Codex-style summarization while using **21.5%** fewer prefix-reuse FLOPs; on TBLite it scores **73.7%** versus **67.0%**.
- On 12-hour EdgeBench-10, CLM reaches **44.6** at **179 PFLOPs** versus **42.3** at **437** for summarization. It also beats OpenEvolve on all four math problems and gives **65%** greater agent-swarm speedup.
- RL lifts the Qwen3.5-9B CLM from **28.8%** to **42.5%** on BrowseComp-Plus, using **1.34** versus **2.19 PFLOPs** for the trained summary harness. Skill evolution adds up to **35.9 points**, and Suffix Cache Reuse cuts server compute by **35%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Giving models unrestricted control over their working context beats hand-designed compaction rules, a Bitter Lesson pattern: remove human-defined action spaces and let better strategies emerge.
> - Moving a harness function into model behavior makes it learnable both in context and in weights, so prompt evolution and reinforcement learning can optimize it directly.
> - Rewarding efficiency only among successful trajectories avoids reward hacking through needless edits, a reusable recipe for jointly optimizing accuracy and cost in agentic RL.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses context management for long-horizon language-model agents. In conventional language-model systems, the conversation or agent history is generally append-only. Once the context becomes too large, an external harness decides when to summarize, truncate, retrieve, or move information into external memory. These operations are typically implemented through fixed rules or a restricted collection of predefined tools.

Recent research has increased model involvement in context management. Harnesses such as Codex-style summarization and MEM1 prescribe particular update procedures, while systems such as Self-Compact, AutoCompact, Context-as-a-Tool, ACM, Sculptor, and related methods allow models to choose among human-designed actions. Recursive Language Models give models programmatic access to long inputs, but the live interaction context remains largely read-only and continues to grow.

The proposed Context Language Models, or CLMs, extend this progression by giving the model unrestricted read–write access to its live context. The context is represented as a file, and the model can append to it, rewrite portions of it, delete material, create summaries, or define procedures for future edits. The central claim is that context management should be treated as an intrinsic model capability rather than as an external control policy. This places the work at the intersection of long-context inference, agent memory, tool-using language models, multi-agent systems, reinforcement learning, prompt or skill evolution, and efficient model serving.

The paper also connects context management to systems research. Arbitrary edits disrupt standard prefix-based key-value cache reuse because an edit early in a sequence normally requires all subsequent tokens to be recomputed. The authors therefore introduce a serving method called Suffix Cache Reuse, intended to reduce this cost.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to determine whether language models can manage their own live context more effectively than fixed harnesses or restricted context-management interfaces. The authors are motivated by several limitations of existing systems.

First, fixed summarization can remove information that later becomes necessary, introduce factual distortions, or preserve irrelevant material. Second, predefined actions constrain the strategies available to the model. A task may require selective verbatim retention, fine-grained in-place editing, temporary working notes, or coordination across several agents, whereas a tool interface may expose only summarization or retrieval. Third, context management has usually been optimized separately from the model’s reasoning process, even though the information available at each turn directly affects subsequent reasoning.

The paper therefore seeks to test whether unrestricted context editing allows models to discover strategies that are difficult to specify manually. It also investigates whether those strategies can be learned in three ways: through direct natural-language instructions, through textual evolution of reusable “skills,” and through reinforcement learning that internalizes context-management behavior in model parameters.

A further objective is to evaluate the computational consequences of model-controlled editing. An edit may improve reasoning while increasing inference cost by invalidating cached states. The authors introduce prefix-reuse FLOPs to measure the trajectory-wide cost of decoding, prefilling, and re-prefilling under standard serving. They then investigate whether cache mechanisms can preserve efficiency despite arbitrary context changes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. CLM Formulation and Implementation

A standard language model is represented as producing a new context by appending generated output:

\[
c_{t+1}=c_t \oplus f_{\mathrm{LM}}(c_t).
\]

A CLM instead directly constructs the next context:

\[
c_{t+1}=f_{\mathrm{CLM}}(c_t).
\]

The implementation mirrors the live context into a file whose path is provided to the model. The model can use ordinary shell or programming commands, particularly Bash, to edit the file. Any modification is immediately synchronized with the model’s live context. If the model does not edit the file, generated tokens are appended in the usual manner. This design allows the model to use general-purpose programming operations rather than a fixed set of context tools.

The same mechanism supports multiple agents. Each agent can have a separate synchronized context file, allowing agents to be created, terminated, and coordinated through shared files and workspace artifacts.

#### 3.2. ContextBench Diagnostic

The authors introduce ContextBench to isolate context management from general reasoning and factual knowledge. It contains four synthetic tasks:

- **Needle Retention**, which tests selective verbatim preservation.
- **Sudoku Sketchpad**, which requires repeated, precise in-place updates to a board.
- **KV Store**, which tests offloading and exact retrieval of large key–value records.
- **Log Triage**, which tests storage, retrieval, and aggregation of logs.

The context limit is 32,000 tokens, while input pressure reaches up to 24 times the context capacity. Baselines include an unmodified harness, Codex-style summarization, Context Folding, Recursive Language Models, Self-Compact, and ACM. The benchmark grades information retained in the context itself, not merely information stored in external files.

#### 3.3. Long-Horizon Evaluation

The paper evaluates CLMs on coding, deep research, mathematical discovery, repository optimization, and multi-agent software optimization. The main comparisons use Qwen3.6-27B, Qwen3.5-9B, Claude 4.6 Sonnet, GPT-5.6-Sol, and other models depending on the experiment.

The coding and research evaluations use TerminalBench 2.1, TBLite, and BrowseComp-Plus. Open-discovery tests include four mathematical optimization problems, ten EdgeBench repository tasks lasting up to 12 hours, and a six-repository Software World task lasting more than 24 hours. The authors compare CLMs with summary-based systems, action-based context managers, Recursive Language Models, and specialized evolutionary workflows such as OpenEvolve.

#### 3.4. Learning Context-Management Strategies

For in-context steering, the authors add single-sentence instructions that request particular behaviors, such as compacting at a specified token threshold, compacting at semantic task boundaries, or backing up the context before editing.

For skill evolution, an external proposer or the agent itself generates candidate textual instructions based on rollout traces. Candidates are evaluated on training and development splits, and the final skill is tested once on held-out data.

For reinforcement learning, the authors use stepwise GRPO. The outcome advantage of a complete trajectory is assigned to each constituent model call. They add a success-gated efficiency advantage: among successful trajectories, lower prefix-reuse FLOPs receive higher reward. This avoids rewarding cheap but unsuccessful trajectories or indiscriminate context deletion.

#### 3.5. Suffix Cache Reuse

Standard prefix caching reuses only tokens before the first mismatch. After an in-context edit, unchanged suffix tokens are normally recomputed. Suffix Cache Reuse identifies surviving spans after the edit, relocates their cached states, adjusts positional encodings where necessary, and reuses them. The method limits the number of relocated spans to control approximation error. It is implemented for both full-attention and hybrid models with linear-attention layers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

On BrowseComp-Plus, CLMs using Qwen3.6-27B achieve 59.4% accuracy, exceeding the strongest comparison baseline by 11.4% relative while using 21.5% fewer prefix-reuse FLOPs. On TerminalBench 2.1, CLMs match the strongest baseline while using approximately 29.5% fewer FLOPs. On TBLite, CLMs reach 73.7% compared with 67.0% for the cited summary baseline, using about 91% of its FLOPs.

On mathematical optimization, CLMs achieve the best reported best-of-run score on all four tasks compared with OpenEvolve and OpenEvolve-Agent. The reported improvements include up to 16.8% on the Heilbronn problem and 3.0% on circle packing, although these experiments use one run per method and therefore provide limited information about variance.

For EdgeBench-10, Qwen3.6-27B CLM reaches a score of 44.6 using 179 prefix-reuse PFLOPs per trial, compared with 42.3 and 437 PFLOPs for summary-based compaction. With Claude 4.6 Sonnet, CLM reaches 51.0 compared with 42.3 for summarization. In Software World, a six-agent CLM swarm produces 65% greater downstream speedup than a summary-based swarm at the same spending level.

The qualitative analysis shows that CLMs develop behaviors not explicitly hard-coded into the harness. Examples include maintaining scoreboards for subagents, creating an internal “notes” role, defining reusable compaction functions, deleting irrelevant search results, and preserving untried ideas in optimization logs. These behaviors indicate that the model is not merely invoking a fixed compression operation.

Natural-language instructions reliably alter behavior. Instructions about compaction thresholds, semantic boundaries, and backups change the timing and form of context edits. Textual evolution produces substantial gains on ContextBench. In assisted evolution, held-out KV Store accuracy rises from 38.3% to 74.2%; across development tasks, improvements include increases from 45.3% to 65.8% on Sudoku Sketchpad and from 0% to 100% on Log Triage.

Reinforcement learning improves Qwen3.5-9B CLM performance on BrowseComp-Plus from 28.8% to 42.5%, a 47.6% relative improvement, while reducing compute from 1.52 to 1.34 PFLOPs per question. The trained CLM slightly exceeds the similarly trained summary harness, which reaches 42.1% at 2.19 PFLOPs.

Suffix Cache Reuse preserves task accuracy in the reported BrowseComp-Plus experiment while reducing empirical server-side compute to 65% of standard SGLang, corresponding to approximately 35% savings. The paper notes that some of this benefit also applies to ordinary chat serving because prior reasoning tokens are often stripped between turns.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper proposes a shift in the abstraction used for long-horizon agents: context is treated as a mutable computational artifact rather than a passive transcript or a resource controlled by an external harness. If the results generalize, CLMs could simplify agent architectures by moving context policies into model behavior and could support more flexible single-agent and multi-agent workflows.

The ability to learn context-management skills in prompts, evolve them textually, or internalize them through reinforcement learning may also make context policies reusable across tasks. The approach could reduce dependence on manually designed memory tools and allow agents to discover task-specific representations.

The principal limitations concern reliability, evaluation breadth, and safety. Context editing can delete information, preserve errors, or introduce self-generated instructions that persist across turns. The paper specifically identifies prompt injection and unauthorized instructions in summaries or editable context as new attack surfaces. Suffix Cache Reuse is approximate because reused states were computed under an earlier prefix, and its effectiveness depends on model architecture and serving implementation. Several experiments also rely on selected benchmarks, restricted models, or limited runs. Consequently, broader testing, stronger integrity controls, improved context-length awareness, and more extensive reinforcement-learning studies are needed before CLMs can be treated as a general solution for production agents.
