# Reinforcement Learning for Out-of-Distribution Reasoning in LLMs: An Empirical Study on Diagnosis-Related Group Coding

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Mayo Clinic and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign developed DRG-SAPPHIRE, a reinforcement learning-based model for automated Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) coding that achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on the MIMIC-IV dataset while providing physician-validated, explainable reasoning.

## Problem

- Automated Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) coding is challenging due to the critical need for accuracy in hospital reimbursement, traditionally requiring labor-intensive manual assignment by highly trained specialists.
- Existing LLM methods often treat DRG coding as a black-box classification problem, lacking transparency and explainability, which hinders their adoption in clinical settings where trust and validation are crucial.
- DRG coding is an out-of-distribution (OOD) task for general LLMs because pretraining data typically lacks exposure to private clinical or billing records, and the task involves a high-dimensional search space (over 700 codes) with complex hierarchical rules.

## Method

- Introduced **DRG-SAPPHIRE**, a large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) approach using a variant of ==Group Relative Policy== Optimization (GRPO) to automate MS-DRG code assignment from unstructured clinical notes.
- Implemented a three-step training pipeline consisting of bootstrapping Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on the Qwen2.5-7B base model, and subsequent large-scale RL with verifiable rule-based rewards.
- Proposed and evaluated several algorithmic enhancements to GRPO, including dynamic resampling, targeted interventions for specific CoT reasoning patterns, KL divergence decay, and various reward shaping strategies.

## Results

- The optimal DRG-SAPPHIRE model achieved a state-of-the-art accuracy of **54.8%** on the MIMIC-IV dataset for automated DRG coding, outperforming previous proprietary and open-source models.
- DRG-SAPPHIRE generates interpretable, physician-validated Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, with expert physicians rating its helpfulness and accuracy at a median score of 4 out of 5 across 30 evaluated cases.
- GRPO consistently improved Pass@1 accuracy by approximately 10 percentage points over SFT baselines on a reduced dataset, primarily by sharpening the model's output distribution rather than by expanding its capacity for diverse reasoning exploration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RL performance on knowledge-intensive, out-of-distribution tasks such as DRG coding is fundamentally constrained by the domain-specific knowledge instilled in the base model through Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) prior to RL training.
> - Scaling Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) data to infuse domain knowledge is more computationally efficient and effective for improving model performance in OOD domains compared to solely scaling RL training.
> - For DRG coding, the 'Answer-First' reasoning pattern (predicting the DRG code before the explanation) demonstrated superior performance over explicit CoT-First or Differential Thinking approaches, suggesting implicit knowledge leverage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to automated medical coding, specifically for Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) assignment. This area is significant due to the critical role DRG codes play in hospital reimbursement and operational efficiency, traditionally requiring labor-intensive manual assignment by highly trained specialists.

Existing research in automated medical coding has seen growing interest in LLM applications, but DRG coding presents particular challenges. Prior attempts with LLMs have shown limited success, often framing the task as a multi-class classification problem. These classification-based approaches typically offer limited insight into the rationale behind code assignments, which is a barrier to real-world clinical deployment where transparency and explainability are crucial for trust and validation.

A primary difficulty for off-the-shelf LLMs in DRG coding is the out-of-distribution (OOD) nature of the task. Pretraining corpora for general LLMs rarely contain the private clinical or billing data essential for mastering DRG coding. Additionally, DRG coding is inherently complex, involving a high-dimensional search space (over 700 codes), advanced clinical reasoning to link diagnoses with resource use and severity, and strict hierarchical assignment rules.

Recent advancements in reasoning models, such as DeepSeek-R1, have leveraged large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) with verifiable rewards to achieve state-of-the-art performance on complex mathematical tasks by encouraging extended chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning. However, RL applications in the healthcare domain, particularly for tasks requiring complex reasoning from unstructured clinical notes, are still in early stages. This paper positions itself at the intersection of these fields, exploring how large-scale, reasoning-oriented RL can be applied to address the OOD and explainability challenges of DRG coding. It also contributes to the broader debate on whether RL fosters new capabilities in LLMs or primarily reinforces existing ones, particularly in knowledge-intensive OOD scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and empirically investigate a large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) approach for automated DRG coding from unstructured clinical notes, addressing the inherent challenges of this task for Large Language Models (LLMs).

Specific motivations and objectives include:

1. **Overcoming Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Challenges:** DRG coding is characterized as an OOD task for general-purpose LLMs because their pretraining data typically lacks exposure to private clinical or billing records. This research aims to explore whether RL, particularly Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with rule-based rewards, can effectively adapt LLMs to this specific domain, mitigating the limitations posed by OOD data.

2. **Enhancing Explainability and Trust:** A critical limitation of previous automated DRG coding methods, which often frame the task as multi-class classification, is their inability to provide transparent rationales for code assignments. This lack of explainability hinders their adoption in clinical settings where trust and validation by human experts (e.g., physicians and coding specialists) are essential. The authors aim to develop a model that not only achieves high accuracy but also generates interpretable, physician-validated reasoning through Chain-of-Thought (CoT) processes, aligning with real-world clinical requirements.

3. **Achieving State-of-the-Art Performance:** The research seeks to establish a new benchmark for automated DRG coding accuracy on publicly available datasets like MIMIC-IV, surpassing the performance of existing proprietary and open-source models, including those designed as advanced reasoning models or specialized medical coding LLMs.

4. **Deriving Insights into RL for Knowledge-Intensive OOD Tasks:** Beyond achieving practical performance, a significant objective is to gain a deeper understanding of the prerequisites for successful RL in challenging OOD tasks. This includes systematically investigating:
 - The optimal allocation of data between Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and GRPO under fixed data budgets.
 - The impact of scaling SFT data on subsequent RL performance.
 - The effectiveness of various RL algorithmic enhancements and adaptive learning strategies in domain-specific contexts that differ from common mathematical reasoning tasks.
 The goal is to inform broader strategies for applying RL to similar knowledge-intensive, OOD applications.

5. **Investigating Cognitive Behaviors:** The study explores the role of different cognitive patterns in CoT reasoning (e.g., Answer-First, CoT-First, Differential Thinking) and how these can be incentivized or emerge naturally during RL training for DRG assignment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduced DRG-SAPPHIRE, a model leveraging large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) for automated Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) coding. The methodology encompasses problem formulation, a variant of the GRPO algorithm, several algorithmic enhancements, adaptive learning strategies, and a detailed training and evaluation pipeline.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The objective is to automate the assignment of Medicare Severity Diagnosis-Related Group (MS-DRG) codes from clinical notes. The process involves an extraction function, *h*, to identify the principal diagnosis or procedure, and the presence of Complications or Comorbidities (CC) or Major Complications or Comorbidities (MCC) from a set of clinical documents *D*. A hierarchical mapping function, *f*, then determines the final DRG code *g*. The LLM is tasked with automating this entire process, including generating reasoning.

#### 3.2. Preliminary: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

The core RL algorithm is GRPO, which is a policy-gradient method. It operates by sampling a group of outputs for a given prompt from the current policy and calculating an advantage based on the relative rewards within that group, rather than relying on a separate value function. The objective function maximizes token-level rewards while applying a KL divergence penalty to regularize the divergence from a reference policy. For this work, strict on-policy learning was enforced where the old policy used for sampling is the same as the target policy being optimized.

#### 3.3. Improving GRPO Beyond the Baseline

A series of strategies were proposed to enhance GRPO performance for the DRG coding task:
- **Dynamic Resampling for Advantage Preservation:** To counteract the "gradient-diminishing problem" where uniform rewards across sampled completions lead to zero advantage, the authors implemented a dynamic resampling strategy. If a group of sampled completions for a prompt yields zero reward variance, resampling is performed up to *N_max* times. An optional constraint enforces at least one completion receiving a positive reward to guide gradient updates. This differs from other approaches that discard such prompts, aiming to maximize data utility.
- **Intervening on Cognitive Behaviors:** The authors explored additional reward functions and a specialized SFT dataset to incentivize specific Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning patterns:
 - *Answer-First:* Model outputs the DRG code before the CoT reasoning.
 - *CoT-First:* Model generates reasoning before the DRG code.
 - *Differential Thinking:* Model evaluates multiple potential DRG codes before selecting one.
 This involved adding format penalties for early DRG code output and reconstructing the SFT dataset with prompts designed to elicit differential thinking.
- **KL Divergence Decay:** The KL divergence term in GRPO regularizes the policy. To prevent over-regularization towards the reference policy and potential policy degradation, the authors explored two setups: completely removing the KL term or applying a cosine decay schedule to its coefficient, *β*, gradually reducing it to zero during training.
- **GRPO Variants:** Three variants of GRPO's loss function were systematically evaluated:
 - **Vanilla GRPO:** Original implementation where token-level losses are averaged by completion length before averaging across the group.
 - **DAPO Loss:** Normalizes the sum of token-level losses by the total number of tokens across all completions in the group.
 - **Dr. GRPO Loss:** Normalizes the sum of token-level losses by a constant *max_tokens* rather than actual completion length.
 Additionally, a Dr. GRPO advantage variant removed the standard deviation normalization from the advantage calculation to mitigate question-level difficulty bias.
- **Reward Shaping:** Two rule-based reward components were implemented:
 - *Format Reward:* A binary penalty for incorrect output formatting (<think></think> and <answer></answer> tags).
 - *Accuracy Reward:* Applied only if the format is correct, with three strategies explored:
 - *Dense Reward:* Provides varying positive or negative rewards for partial matches (principal diagnosis, CC/MCC), full matches, or invalid DRG codes.
 - *Balanced Reward:* Similar to Dense but assigns equal positive reward for principal diagnosis or CC/MCC matches.
 - *Strict Reward:* Provides a high positive reward only for a full match, zero for partial/no match but valid DRG, and a penalty for invalid DRG.

#### 3.4. Adaptive Learning Strategy

- **Curriculum Learning:** The authors investigated if ordering training cases by difficulty could improve performance. Four setups were evaluated: excluding easy cases, excluding hard cases, excluding both, and training from easy to hard cases. Difficulty was dynamically defined based on reward variance and accuracy from a baseline run.
- **Staged Learning:** This strategy involved dividing training into three phases. After each phase, hard cases were identified, and either additional Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) was applied to these cases before proceeding to the next stage.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

- **Dataset:** MIMIC-IV dataset, using the "brief hospital course" section of discharge summaries. The full training set comprised 236,192 cases and the test set 26,244 cases (MS-DRG version 34.0). A reduced "DRG-Small" subset (20% of full data) was used for initial ablation studies.
- **Model:** Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct was selected as the base LLM.
- **Training Pipeline:** A three-step process (Figure 3):
 1. Bootstrap CoT reasoning for DRG assignment using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.
 2. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on the Qwen2.5-7B using this "cold-start" dataset.
 3. Large-scale RL with GRPO and verifiable rewards.
- **SFT Dataset Construction:** Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct was prompted with medical records and ground-truth DRG codes to generate reasoning.
- **RL Training:** Conducted using the TRL package for one epoch, leveraging vLLM for inference and DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage 3 for efficiency.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Pass@1 (mean accuracy across 8 runs), Pass@8 (correct DRG in top 8 outputs), and Majority@8 (most frequent output matches ground truth). Physician expert review was conducted on 30 cases for Helpfulness and Accuracy of generated reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings concerning DRG-SAPPHIRE's performance, the dynamics of SFT and GRPO, and the effectiveness of various RL enhancements.

#### 4.1. Results of DRG-SAPPHIRE

The optimal DRG-SAPPHIRE model, trained with a 90% SFT and 10% RL ratio on the full dataset, achieved state-of-the-art accuracy in automated DRG coding. It surpassed proprietary reasoning models, non-reasoning models, DeepSeek-distilled Qwen 32B, and the previous best model, DRG-LLaMA-7B, with an accuracy of 54.8% versus 53.9% (Figure 1A). A key advantage highlighted is DRG-SAPPHIRE's ability to provide interpretable, physician-validated reasoning, which is not offered by classification-based models.

An expert reader study involving four physicians rated DRG-SAPPHIRE’s reasoning with a median score of 4 out of 5 for both Helpfulness and Accuracy across 30 cases (Figure 4). Physicians found the explainability of DRG coding highly valuable for initiatives aimed at improving clinical documentation and understanding DRG assignments, despite occasional factual inaccuracies.

#### 4.2. Optimizing Data Allocation Between SFT and GRPO

Experiments on the DRG-Small subset investigating SFT-GRPO data ratios showed that GRPO consistently improved Pass@1 accuracy over SFT baselines by approximately 10 percentage points across various data splits (Figure 5). This improvement was primarily driven by gains in Maj@8, indicating that RL sharpened the model’s output distribution towards higher-reward pathways. However, Pass@8, which assesses diverse reasoning exploration, declined with GRPO, suggesting that RL, in this context, reinforced existing capabilities rather than introducing new reasoning capacities. The performance ceiling of GRPO was largely determined by the initial SFT model's strength. Computationally, scaling SFT prior to RL was found to be more efficient due to GRPO’s substantial inference-time costs (Figure 5F).

Scaling to the full dataset confirmed these observations. Both GRPO and SFT performance scaled approximately linearly with the logarithm of the number of SFT examples (Figure 1B). Modest benefits from extended GRPO training were observed beyond 500 global steps (Figure 6A). As SFT sample sizes increased, the slope of GRPO performance curves converged with SFT, suggesting a fundamental constraint on RL's effectiveness by the base model's pre-RL knowledge.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies on GRPO Enhancements and Adaptive Learning

- **Dynamic Resampling:** Surprisingly, dynamic resampling strategies, with or without a positive reward constraint, did not consistently improve performance over vanilla GRPO and sometimes resulted in worse outcomes (Table 1). This occurred despite maintaining high reward variance (Figure 7A) but at a substantial increase in computational time (Figure 7C). The authors hypothesize that oversampling zero-variance prompts might introduce instability by skewing batches towards OOD responses.
- **Intervening on Cognitive Behaviors:** While explicit interventions successfully elicited CoT-First and Differential Thinking patterns, their performance lagged behind the naturally emerging Answer-First pattern (Table 1). This suggests that for DRG coding, a direct prediction strategy (Answer-First) may leverage implicit knowledge more effectively than explicit CoT-grounded reasoning.
- **KL Divergence:** Removing the KL penalty frequently led to model collapse, indicating its critical role for cross-domain generalization in this task, contrasting with findings in some mathematical domains (Figure 8A). However, in cases where training completed successfully without the KL penalty, performance surpassed vanilla GRPO (Table 1). A cosine KL decay schedule was found beneficial, particularly in later training stages, preventing over-regularization (Figure 8B) and improving performance in full dataset scaling experiments (Table 2).
- **GRPO Variants:** DAPO loss achieved the highest performance among the GRPO variants, while Dr. GRPO loss performed the lowest (Table 1). All variants exhibited "completion length contraction," where output lengths decreased as accuracy improved, a contrasting trend to mathematical tasks where longer completions often correlate with better performance (Figure 9).
- **Reward Shaping:** The strict accuracy reward, despite providing the sparsest signal, outperformed dense and balanced reward variants (Table 1). Denser reward schemes did not improve principal diagnosis or CC/MCC accuracy, suggesting they might lead to premature convergence to local optima.
- **Adaptive Learning:** Removing easy and hard cases during training showed benefits (Table 1), aligning with observations in other domains that medium-difficulty cases might be most effective for RL. Staged learning with SFT yielded only modest gains despite additional computational cost.

#### 4.4. Prerequisites for Effective GRPO Training

Vanilla Qwen2.5 models (base and instruct) without SFT failed to generate correct DRG codes using GRPO alone, despite adopting the target reasoning format (Figure 10A). Post-SFT, RL performance improved and generally scaled with model size. Higher SFT learning rates (up to 4 × 10⁻⁵) and extended training epochs both boosted GRPO performance (Figure 10C), emphasizing the importance of a strong SFT foundation for reasoning-intensive OOD tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces DRG-SAPPHIRE, a novel model for automated Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) coding that achieves state-of-the-art performance on the MIMIC-IV benchmark. A notable contribution is its ability to generate physician-validated Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning for DRG assignments, significantly enhancing explainability. This interpretability represents a practical advantage over previous methods that primarily function as black-box classifiers, addressing a critical barrier to real-world clinical adoption.

The work's significance extends beyond a specific application, offering empirical insights into the broader challenges of applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) to knowledge-intensive, out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks in Large Language Models (LLMs). The finding that RL performance on OOD tasks like DRG coding is fundamentally constrained by the domain knowledge encoded in the base model prior to RL training is impactful. The observed logarithmic scaling relationship between the number of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) examples and subsequent RL performance suggests that "knowledge infusion" through SFT is a critical prerequisite. This implies that for such tasks, scaling SFT may be more effective and computationally efficient than scaling RL alone. This conclusion offers a "bitter lesson" by re-evaluating the prevailing narrative to "scale RL" and redirects focus to foundational knowledge transfer via SFT for OOD domains.

The potential impact of DRG-SAPPHIRE in healthcare settings is considerable:
- **Operational Efficiency:** Automated DRG coding can reduce the labor-intensive burden on highly trained coding specialists, freeing up resources and potentially accelerating the coding process.
- **Financial Forecasting and Reimbursement:** Early and accurate DRG predictions can inform hospital operations, financial planning, and reimbursement strategies, optimizing resource allocation.
- **Clinical Documentation Improvement:** The physician-validated reasoning generated by DRG-SAPPHIRE can serve as an educational tool for clinicians, helping them understand DRG assignment logic and improve their clinical documentation to better reflect patient severity and resource utilization. This fosters transparent feedback mechanisms for continuous quality improvement initiatives.
- **Bridging Research Gaps:** By demonstrating effective RL application in a complex, high-stakes medical domain, this research contributes to the development of trustworthy AI in healthcare, inspiring further work on verifiable reward systems and interpretable LLMs for clinical tasks.
- **Generalizable Insights for OOD Tasks:** The study's conclusions regarding the importance of SFT for RL in OOD contexts are generalizable, providing guidance for researchers attempting to adapt LLMs to other specialized domains where pretraining data is scarce or proprietary.
