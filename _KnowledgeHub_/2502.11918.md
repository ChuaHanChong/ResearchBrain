# VLP: Vision-Language Preference Learning for Embodied Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Vision-Language Preference learning (VLP) automates preference annotation for embodied manipulation tasks by leveraging vision-language models. It generates accurate preference feedback from natural language instructions, enabling RL agents to perform comparably or better than those trained with scripted labels and outperforming other VLM-based reward methods while generalizing to unseen tasks.

## Problem

- Reward engineering in reinforcement learning (RL) is a labor-intensive process requiring domain expertise, often leading to reward hacking where agents exploit the reward function rather than achieving intended goals.
- Preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) typically relies on expensive and time-consuming human preference annotations, limiting its scalability.
- Existing VLM/LLM-based preference labeling methods often require access to environment information or produce inaccurate labels.

## Method

- ==Introduces Vision-Language Preference== learning (**VLP**), a framework leveraging vision-language models (VLMs) to automate preference annotation for embodied manipulation tasks.
- Constructs a Multi-Task Vision-Language Preference (MTVLP) dataset, combining videos of agent trajectories from Meta-World tasks with GPT-4V-generated natural language instructions and preference labels.
- Develops a VLP model architecture that uses CLIP's visual and text encoders, a cross-modal transformer for feature interaction, and an MLP to predict a trajectory-level preference score based on three types of language-conditioned preferences.

## Results

- VLP-generated preference labels, when used with offline RLHF algorithms, achieve performance comparable to, and in some cases, better than scripted preference labels derived from ground-truth rewards.
- VLP significantly outperforms other VLM-based reward shaping methods (e.g., R3M, VIP, LIV, CLIP, VLM-RM, RoboCLIP) in training RL agents for embodied manipulation tasks.
- The VLP framework demonstrates strong generalization abilities, maintaining high preference prediction accuracy on both unseen tasks and novel language instructions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision-language models (VLMs) can automate preference annotation for embodied tasks by effectively aligning visual observations with natural language instructions.
> - Training a VLM on a multi-task video-language preference dataset enables the model to learn generalizable preference representations for diverse manipulation tasks.
> - Defining explicit language-conditioned preference types (intra-task, inter-language, inter-video) enhances the model's ability to robustly predict accurate preferences, leading to better RL agent performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated impressive capabilities in training agents to perform complex tasks. However, a persistent challenge in RL is the design of effective reward functions, a process known as reward engineering. This process is labor-intensive, requires domain expertise, and often results in reward hacking, where agents exploit the reward function rather than achieving the intended goals.

Preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional reward engineering. Rather than defining explicit reward functions, PbRL learns from preferences between trajectories, typically provided by human annotators. However, collecting human preference annotations is expensive and time-consuming, limiting the scalability of PbRL approaches.

This paper introduces Vision-Language Preference learning (VLP), a novel framework that leverages the capabilities of vision-language models (VLMs) to automate the preference annotation process for embodied manipulation tasks. VLP reduces the need for human annotations while providing accurate preference feedback that aligns with human intentions expressed through natural language instructions.

### 2. Background and Challenges

Several approaches have attempted to address the limitations of reward engineering:

1. **Imitation Learning (IL)**: Learning from expert demonstrations, but limited by the quality and cost of collecting demonstrations.

2. **Reward Shaping with VLMs**: Using VLMs to shape rewards based on visual observations, but often providing noisy and inaccurate reward labels.

3. **Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL)**: Learning from preferences between trajectories, but requiring expensive human annotations.

4. **LLM/VLM-based Preference Labeling**: Automating preference labeling using large language models, but requiring access to environment information or suffering from inaccurate labels.

VLP addresses these challenges by:

1. Automating preference annotation without requiring access to environment information
2. Providing more accurate preference labels by aligning visual and language modalities
3. Enabling learning from human instructions expressed in natural language
4. Generalizing to unseen tasks and language instructions

### 3. The VLP Framework

The VLP framework consists of two main components:

1. A Multi-Task Vision-Language Preference Dataset (MTVLP) containing videos of agent trajectories paired with language instructions
2. A Vision-Language Preference Model that predicts preference scores for video-language pairs

During training, the model learns to align visual and language features to predict preferred trajectories under specific language instructions. During inference, the trained model can automatically generate preference labels for new tasks, which can then be used to train RL agents using standard preference-based RL algorithms.

### 4. Multi-Task Vision-Language Preference Dataset

The authors constructed a new dataset, MTVLP, based on the Meta-World benchmark, consisting of five representative manipulation tasks:

For each task, the dataset includes:

1. **Videos**: Trajectories of varying optimality levels (expert, medium, random) capturing the agent's behavior
2. **Language Instructions**: Natural language descriptions of the tasks generated by GPT-4V
3. **Preference Labels**: Defined based on three types of language-conditioned preferences (explained in a later section)

The diversity of tasks and language instructions enables the model to learn generalizable preference representations that can transfer to new tasks and instructions.

### 5. Vision-Language Preference Model Architecture

The VLP model takes videos and language instructions as input and predicts a preference score. The model architecture consists of:

1. **Video Encoder**: Extracts visual features from video frames using CLIP's visual encoder
2. **Language Encoder**: Encodes language instructions into language tokens using CLIP's text encoder
3. **Cross-modal Transformer**: Facilitates interaction between visual and language features through:
 - Self-attention layers that capture relationships within each modality
 - Cross-attention layers that align features across modalities
4. **Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP)**: Predicts a trajectory-level preference score based on the fused visual and language features

The cross-modal transformer is a key component that enables effective alignment of visual and language features. It allows the model to understand which parts of the video are relevant to specific aspects of the language instruction, improving the accuracy of preference predictions.

### 6. Language-Conditioned Preferences

VLP defines three types of language-conditioned preferences to facilitate effective learning:

1. **Intra-Task Preference (ITP)**: Preferences within the same task, based on trajectory optimality and language instruction. For example, an expert trajectory of "button press" is preferred over a random trajectory when paired with the instruction "press the button."

2. **Inter-Language Preference (ILP)**: Videos from one task are equally preferred when paired with language from another task. This encourages the model to recognize that language instructions and visual content should be aligned.

3. **Inter-Video Preference (IVP)**: The vision-language pair from the same task is preferred over pairs from different tasks. For example, a "button press" video paired with "press the button" instruction is preferred over the same video paired with "close the door" instruction.

The model is trained using a loss function that incorporates these three types of preferences, encouraging it to learn a robust preference representation that captures the alignment between visual and language features.

### 7. Experimental Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the performance of VLP on simulated embodied manipulation tasks. The key findings include:

1. **VLP labels are comparable to or outperform scripted labels**: When used with offline RLHF algorithms (P-IQL, IPL, CPL), VLP labels achieved performance comparable to, and in some cases, better than scripted preference labels based on ground-truth rewards.

2. **VLP outperforms other vision-language reward approaches**: VLP significantly exceeded the performance of other VLM-based reward shaping methods (R3M, VIP, LIV, CLIP, VLM-RM, RoboCLIP) when used to train RL agents.

3. **VLP exhibits strong generalization abilities**: VLP generalized well to unseen tasks and unseen language instructions, maintaining high preference prediction accuracy.

4. **Attention visualization shows accurate focus**: Visualizations of the attention maps show that VLP correctly focuses on task-relevant parts of the scene, such as the button in the button press task or the door handle in the door close task.

These visualizations confirm that VLP learns to attend to task-relevant objects and can correctly interpret the relationship between visual observations and language instructions.

### 8. Broader Impact and Limitations

The VLP framework has significant implications for the field of reinforcement learning and embodied AI:

1. **Reducing the reliance on manual reward engineering**: VLP provides a way to automate the preference annotation process, significantly reducing the need for manually designed reward functions.

2. **Enabling more effective learning from human instructions**: By leveraging VLMs, VLP allows agents to learn directly from human instructions expressed in natural language, improving the user-friendliness of RL systems.

3. **Facilitating the development of embodied AI systems**: VLP contributes to the development of AI systems that can operate in the real world and perform complex tasks by enabling more efficient learning of embodied manipulation tasks.

However, the authors acknowledge several limitations:

1. **Limited complexity of tasks**: The current implementation is limited to relatively simple manipulation tasks. More complex tasks might require more sophisticated model architectures and training procedures.

2. **Specificity of language instructions**: The model's performance depends on the specificity and clarity of language instructions. Ambiguous or complex instructions might lead to less accurate preference predictions.

3. **Simulation-to-reality gap**: The model is trained and evaluated in simulation. Transferring to real-world robotics applications would require addressing the sim-to-real gap.

### 9. Conclusion

VLP represents a significant advancement in preference-based reinforcement learning by automating the preference annotation process using vision-language models. By aligning visual and language modalities, VLP provides accurate and generalizable preference feedback that can be used to train RL agents for embodied manipulation tasks.

The experimental results demonstrate that VLP is effective in providing preference labels that lead to high-performing RL agents, comparable to or better than those trained with scripted labels based on ground-truth rewards. Moreover, VLP generalizes well to unseen tasks and language instructions, highlighting its potential for scalable and flexible reinforcement learning.

Future work could extend VLP to more complex tasks, explore its applicability to real-world robotics applications, and investigate ways to improve the specificity and clarity of language instructions to enhance preference prediction accuracy.
