# m2sv: A Scalable Benchmark for Map-to-Street-View Spatial Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The m2sv benchmark is introduced to evaluate Vision-Language Models' spatial reasoning by requiring them to align a north-up overhead map with a Street View image to infer camera orientation. This reveals a substantial performance gap, with human accuracy at 95.0% compared to the best VLM's 65.2%.

## Problem

- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) demonstrate brittle performance in spatial reasoning tasks that demand alignment between abstract overhead (allocentric) maps and egocentric ground-level views.
- Existing multimodal benchmarks often use synthetic data or simplify real-world complexities, which may overestimate VLMs' true spatial competence.
- Current AI systems struggle with fine-grained spatial alignment and 'directional intelligence,' a crucial capability for applications in autonomous navigation and robotics.

## Method

- A scalable benchmark, ==m2sv==, was developed where models identify which labeled direction on an overhead map corresponds to the orientation of a Street View camera at the same intersection, with 2 to 7 candidate directions.
- A =='blueprint metadata' pipeline== was implemented to create m2sv-20k, a dataset of ==20,000 examples== across 32 cities, ensuring scalability and reproducible dataset generation.
- Various proprietary and open-source VLMs were evaluated in zero-shot settings, and an open-source model was fine-tuned using ==Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL)== on 11,000 reasoning traces.

## Results

- Humans achieved **95.0%** accuracy on m2sv, significantly outperforming the best proprietary VLM (Gemini-3-Pro) at **65.2%** and open models like Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct at **35.5%** (random baseline **31.4%**).
- Supervised Fine-Tuning improved Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct's accuracy from **34.3%** to **39.8%**, and subsequent Reinforcement Learning further boosted it to **43.9%**, yet performance remained substantially below human levels.
- Qualitative failure analysis identified common errors including egocentric–allocentric confusion, over-reliance on unreliable or transient visual cues, landmark misbinding, and internal contradictions in reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A significant performance disparity of over 30 percentage points between human performance (95.0%) and the best VLM (65.2%) highlights that current VLMs lack robust, human-like spatial reasoning for real-world cross-view alignment.
> - Fine-tuning on specific spatial tasks primarily induces benchmark-specific reasoning policies and can lead to a loss of difficulty-adaptive reasoning depth, rather than developing genuinely transferable spatial representations.
> - The benchmark serves as a diagnostic tool, revealing recurring VLM failure modes such as egocentric–allocentric confusion (e.g., left-right inversions), over-reliance on unreliable visual cues, and failures to maintain consistent spatial representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical and recognized limitation of current Vision-Language Models (VLMs): their brittle performance on spatial reasoning tasks, particularly those requiring alignment between abstract overhead representations (like maps) and egocentric, ground-level views (like Street View imagery). The paper positions `m2sv` as a necessary tool to expose and mitigate this gap, which is crucial for applications in navigation, robotics, and embodied AI.

The authors meticulously situate `m2sv` within several related and established research domains, highlighting its unique contributions:

- **Spatial Reasoning and Mental Modeling:** The paper acknowledges that aligning allocentric and egocentric representations is a core problem in spatial AI. It draws parallels with benchmarks like MindCube (Yin et al., 2025), which probes how VLMs form internal "cognitive maps" and perform tasks like perspective taking and mental rotation. `m2sv` extends this by grounding the task in real-world, noisy data, moving beyond synthetic or simplified diagrams. The work also directly relates to observed VLM failures in spatial reasoning, such as "left-right confusion" (Hao et al., 2025), "internal logic contradictions" (Liu et al., 2024), and "cue weighting" issues or "tunnel vision" (Berman & Deng, 2025), all of which `m2sv` is designed to diagnose.

- **Cross-View Geo-Localization (CVGL):** While CVGL is a relevant field focused on matching ground-level images to overhead perspectives, `m2sv` distinguishes itself significantly. Traditional CVGL benchmarks (e.g., CVUSA, CVACT, CV-Cities, VIGOR) primarily focus on image-level retrieval for geographic *location* (latitude/longitude), often simplifying or bypassing orientation by using north-aligned panoramas. `m2sv`, however, *isolates orientation* as the main reasoning bottleneck. By fixing the location at a specific intersection and providing multiple candidate orientations, `m2sv` prevents models from relying on low-level visual correlations for retrieval and instead demands a robust geometric alignment and understanding of the 3D spatial relationship between views.

- **Planetary-Scale Coordinate Prediction:** The paper also contrasts `m2sv` with models like PIGEON (Haas et al., 2024) and PlaNet (Weyand et al., 2016), which predict global coordinates from single images. These models often leverage regional semantic cues and are evaluated on broader geocell classification. `m2sv`, in contrast, focuses on fine-grained spatial alignment (pinpointing orientation at a known intersection), where current AI systems still struggle, emphasizing "directional intelligence" over broad localization.

- **Map Understanding and Navigation:** `m2sv` complements benchmarks like MapQA (Li et al., 2025) and MAPWise (Mukhopadhyay et al., 2025), which evaluate semantic map understanding and topological reasoning, and Touchdown (Chen et al., 2019), which focuses on sequential language grounding for embodied navigation. Instead of broad map interpretation or multi-step navigation, `m2sv` isolates a fundamental, single-step geometric alignment primitive between egocentric and allocentric views in real-world settings.

- **Benchmark Theory and Saturation:** Crucially, the paper frames `m2sv` within the context of "benchmark saturation" (Stanford HAI, 2024; Ott et al., 2022). As frontier VLMs achieve near-human or superhuman performance on many established benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, GSM8K), there's an urgent need for "unsaturated" tests that remain challenging and prevent memorization. `m2sv`, with its large performance gap between humans and state-of-the-art VLMs, serves as a diagnostic tool for true progress in grounded spatial reasoning, resistant to simple pattern-matching or data contamination.

In essence, `m2sv` carves out a unique and critical niche by providing a scalable, real-world benchmark that specifically targets the difficult problem of cross-view geometric orientation inference, a capability that VLMs demonstrably lack and which is vital for real-world intelligent agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce **m2sv (map-to-street-view)**, a scalable benchmark designed to evaluate and advance spatial reasoning capabilities in Vision-Language Models (VLMs). The core task of `m2sv` is to infer the camera's viewing direction by aligning a north-up overhead map with a Street View image captured at the same real-world intersection.

The motivations behind developing `m2sv` are multifaceted and driven by recognized limitations in current AI research:

- **Addressing VLM Weaknesses in Spatial Grounding:** Despite impressive performance on many multimodal benchmarks, VLMs exhibit brittleness in tasks requiring consistent spatial grounding across viewpoints. Specifically, aligning abstract, allocentric (overhead) representations with egocentric (ground-level) views remains a fundamental challenge.
- **Filling a Benchmark Gap:** Existing multimodal reasoning benchmarks often emphasize synthetic diagrams, charts, or simplified visual puzzles, which may inadvertently overestimate VLMs' spatial competence by avoiding the complexities of real-world viewpoint alignment, partial observability, and temporal mismatches. `m2sv` aims to provide a benchmark that truly reflects these real-world challenges.
- **Enabling Critical Applications:** The ability to seamlessly align map and street-level views is essential for practical applications in autonomous navigation, robotics, and embodied AI assistants. Without this capability, real-world deployment of such systems is severely hampered.
- **Systematic Study of Difficulty:** Beyond merely evaluating performance, `m2sv` is designed to facilitate a systematic study of difficulty in spatial reasoning. By leveraging known intersection geometry and candidate directions, the authors can define scalable structural difficulty signals (e.g., number of candidate directions, angular symmetry). They complement this with human response-time measurements to differentiate between structural ambiguity and cognitive effort, allowing for a nuanced understanding of where models succeed and fail.
- **Extensive Failure Analysis:** The benchmark is intended to serve as a diagnostic tool, enabling detailed qualitative failure analysis. This allows researchers to move beyond aggregate accuracy scores to identify recurring breakdowns in VLM reasoning, such as egocentric–allocentric confusion, over-reliance on unreliable cues, and internal inconsistencies.
- **Promoting Robust and Transferable Spatial Intelligence:** Ultimately, the goal is to motivate future work on grounded spatial reasoning across viewpoints, pushing VLMs towards more robust and transferable spatial representations, rather than benchmark-specific adaptations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors developed `m2sv` with a focus on scalability, real-world data, and a systematic approach to evaluating spatial reasoning.

**Benchmark Design:**
Each `m2sv` example presents two key visual inputs:
1. **North-up Overhead Map:** A map centered at a real-world intersection, annotated with labeled directional rays (A, B, C, D, etc.).
2. **Street View Image:** An egocentric image captured near the same intersection, oriented along one of the labeled rays.
The task for the model is to identify which labeled direction on the map corresponds to the orientation of the Street View camera. The number of candidate directions varies from 2 to 7, with a median of 3, leading to a random guess baseline of approximately 31.4%. Accuracy is the primary evaluation metric.

**Dataset and Blueprint Pipeline:**
To ensure scalability and reproducibility, the authors introduced a "blueprint metadata" approach. This decouples the dataset specification (intersection coordinates, candidate azimuths, correct labels, Street View panorama IDs) from image rendering, allowing for deterministic reconstruction of the dataset on demand.
- **Data Sourcing:** Intersections are sampled from a seed list of major metropolitan areas globally to ensure geographic diversity.
- **Filtering Criteria:** Strict criteria are applied to control ambiguity and ensure fair evaluation:
 - Street View panoramas are restricted to within 5 meters of the intersection center (median 2.05m), minimizing positional offset.
 - A minimum angular separation of 15° between candidate azimuths is enforced to exclude visually ambiguous, near-collinear road configurations.
- **`m2sv-20k`:** The final benchmark comprises 20,000 examples across 32 cities, with per-city caps to prevent over-representation.
- **`m2sv-sft-11k` (Reasoning Traces):** A subset of 11,000 examples is annotated with structured reasoning traces generated by Gemini-2.5-Pro. These traces explicitly compare map geometry and Street View imagery. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), only the 4.4k *correct* traces (4.37k for train, 485 for validation) were used.

**Experimental Setup:**
The evaluation involved a range of Vision-Language Models (VLMs):
- **Zero-shot Baselines:** Proprietary models (Gemini-3-Pro, GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Thinking) and an open-source model (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct) were tested without task-specific adaptation using a standardized prompt. A human baseline was established on a 200-example subset.
- **Adaptation Baselines:** The open-source Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct model was fine-tuned using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) under two regimes:
 - **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Using the filtered `m2sv-sft-11k` reasoning traces.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Applied on top of the SFT checkpoint.
- **Cross-Benchmark Transfer:** `m2sv`-trained Qwen3-VL-8B variants were evaluated on other established multimodal benchmarks (MMMU, MME-Cog/Per, MMStar, Omni3D) and specifically on the MindCube suite (another spatial reasoning benchmark) to assess generalization.

**Measuring Difficulty:**
The authors systematically analyzed difficulty using both scalable structural signals and human-perceived effort:
- **Structural Difficulty:**
 - **Number of Candidate Directions (#options):** Analyzed chance-normalized accuracy gain, defined as `(a - c) / (1 - c)` where `a` is accuracy and `c` is chance accuracy.
 - **Road-Azimuth Symmetry:** Measured based on the angular spacing between candidate directions, categorizing intersections into quantiles from most symmetric to most asymmetric.
- **Human-Perceived Difficulty:** Response time (elapsed time to answer) from human annotators on a 200-example subset was used as a proxy for cognitive effort.
- **Model Behavior vs. Difficulty:** The length of model-generated reasoning traces was analyzed as a function of human-perceived difficulty.

**Failure Mode Analysis:**
A qualitative failure analysis was conducted on challenging examples to identify recurring patterns of errors across models, focusing on categories rather than an exhaustive taxonomy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several critical findings, highlighting persistent challenges for Vision-Language Models in real-world spatial reasoning:

#### 4.1. Zero-shot Baselines and the Human Performance Gap

- **Significant Gap:** Humans achieved 95.0% accuracy on `m2sv`, demonstrating that the task is reliably solvable. In stark contrast, the best proprietary VLM, Gemini-3-Pro, scored only 65.2%, leaving a substantial gap of over 30 percentage points.
- **Open Models Struggle:** Most open models performed only marginally above the random baseline (e.g., Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct at 35.5% vs. 31.4% random), indicating a severe lack of robust mechanisms for aligning overhead maps with egocentric views without specific adaptation.

#### 4.2. Adaptation Baselines on `m2sv`

- **Consistent Gains from Fine-tuning:** Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on `m2sv` reasoning traces significantly improved Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct's accuracy from 34.3% to 39.8%. Reinforcement Learning (RL) further boosted performance to 43.9%.
- **Persistent Gap:** Despite these gains, even the adapted models remained over 50 percentage points below human performance, suggesting that adaptation alone does not close the fundamental gap in spatial alignment capabilities.

#### 4.3. Cross-Benchmark Transfer

- **Limited Transferability:** Evaluations on other multimodal benchmarks (MMMU, MME-Cog/Per, MMStar, Omni3D) showed mixed and inconsistent transfer effects. Some metrics improved, others degraded or remained unchanged.
- **Asymmetric Transfer:** Specifically, on the MindCube spatial reasoning suite, `m2sv`-trained models occasionally outperformed the base model, particularly in direct answer formats. However, models trained on MindCube performed near-random on `m2sv`.
- **Conclusion:** These results indicate that current fine-tuning pipelines induce benchmark-specific reasoning policies rather than developing genuinely transferable spatial representations.

#### 4.4. Measuring Difficulty in Map-to-Street-View Reasoning

- **Structural Difficulty (#options):** Chance-normalized accuracy was highest for three-option intersections, not monotonically decreasing with more options. This was attributed to the geometric asymmetry often found in T-junctions, which provides stronger disambiguating cues.
- **Road-Azimuth Symmetry:** Performance peaked at *intermediate* symmetry levels, not monotonically decreasing with increased symmetry. While structural asymmetry helped humans by collapsing the hypothesis space, models did not uniformly benefit or struggle based purely on this measure.
- **Human Effort as a Difficulty Proxy:** Human response times varied widely for symmetric intersections but were uniformly low for highly asymmetric ones. Models degraded sharply with increasing human-perceived difficulty (measured by response time tertiles), approaching chance on the hardest examples, whereas humans maintained near-ceiling performance. This suggests models fail to exploit subtle, stable cues when visual ambiguity is high.

#### 4.5. Model Behavior vs. Difficulty (Reasoning Trace Length)

- **Adaptive Reasoning in Proprietary Models:** Stronger proprietary models (Gemini 3 Pro, Qwen3-VL-235B Thinking) showed an adaptive reasoning strategy, increasing their trace length (number of tokens) as problem difficulty increased.
- **Loss of Adaptivity in Adapted Open Models:** In contrast, the supervised fine-tuned (SFT and SFT+RL) Qwen3-VL-8B models produced nearly constant-length traces across difficulty levels. This indicates that fine-tuning, while improving accuracy, led to a loss of difficulty-adaptive reasoning depth, potentially optimizing for earlier commitment to salient cues rather than flexible deliberation.

#### 4.6. Failure Mode Analysis

Qualitative analysis revealed recurring patterns of VLM failures:
- **Egocentric–Allocentric Confusion:** Frequent left–right inversions when mapping egocentric views to allocentric maps.
- **Over-reliance on Unreliable Cues:** Basing decisions on transient or unreliable visual elements (e.g., roof color, shadows, lighting) not present or stable in the map.
- **Landmark Misbinding:** Correctly identifying landmarks but misattributing them to the wrong candidate direction or parcel, indicating poor spatial precision.
- **False Landmark Projection:** Inventing or misattributing non-existent large-scale structures, then reasoning based on these false cues.
- **Failure to Maintain Consistent Spatial Representation:** Inconsistently describing shared structures (e.g., assigning different widths to opposite headings of the same road).
- **Internal Contradiction and State Inconsistency:** Correctly identifying spatial relationships but then contradicting them during subsequent reasoning steps or verbalization.
- **Symmetry Traps and Insufficient Cue Escalation:** Failing to use fine-grained geometric cues to resolve ambiguity at symmetric intersections, prematurely relying on weak evidence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The `m2sv` benchmark and its accompanying analysis offer several significant contributions to the fields of Vision-Language Models, computer vision, and artificial intelligence research, with considerable potential impact:

- **Diagnostic Tool for VLM Limitations:** `m2sv` fills a critical gap by providing a scalable, real-world benchmark that specifically isolates and measures the challenging task of geometric orientation inference across disparate viewpoints. This task is demonstrably difficult for even state-of-the-art VLMs, making `m2sv` an excellent diagnostic tool to pinpoint fundamental weaknesses that existing benchmarks may overlook.
- **Highlighting the Human-AI Gap in Spatial Reasoning:** The stark performance disparity (95% human vs. ~65% best VLM) unambiguously highlights that current VLMs lack robust, human-like spatial reasoning abilities, particularly in aligning real-world allocentric and egocentric views. This gap underscores the need for foundational algorithmic improvements rather than just scaling existing architectures.
- **Informing Future Model Development:** The detailed failure analysis and insights into model behavior (e.g., the loss of difficulty-adaptive reasoning during fine-tuning) provide clear, actionable directions for future research. Researchers can now target specific issues such as:
 - Developing more robust internal spatial coordinate systems to prevent egocentric–allocentric confusion.
 - Improving cue weighting mechanisms to avoid over-reliance on unreliable visual evidence.
 - Enhancing models' ability to maintain consistent spatial representations across reasoning steps.
 - Designing training regimes that encourage adaptive reasoning depth based on perceived ambiguity, rather than premature commitment to cues.
 - Exploring architectures that facilitate fine-grained geometric alignment to break symmetry traps.
- **Advancing Grounded AI for Real-world Applications:** The capability to accurately align map and street-level views is foundational for critical applications in robotics (e.g., autonomous navigation, self-localization), augmented reality, and embodied AI assistants. By exposing VLM limitations in this area, `m2sv` directly motivates research that will enable more reliable and intelligent agents operating in complex real-world environments.
- **Contributing to Benchmark Theory:** `m2sv` contributes to the ongoing discussion about "benchmark saturation" by offering an "unsaturated" test that remains challenging for frontier models. This is crucial for driving genuine progress in AI, preventing models from simply memorizing patterns, and pushing towards more generalizable intelligence.
- **Scalable and Reproducible Research:** The "blueprint metadata" pipeline ensures that `m2sv` is scalable and deterministically reproducible, fostering transparency and facilitating large-scale experimentation across the research community.

In conclusion, `m2sv` is a timely and valuable contribution that not only provides a rigorous evaluation tool for a critical AI capability but also lays the groundwork for understanding and overcoming persistent challenges in vision-language models' spatial reasoning, ultimately pushing the boundaries of grounded AI.
