# Flow Matching Imitation Learning for Multi-Support Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical control architecture is presented that utilizes Flow Matching imitation learning to enable the Talos humanoid robot to perform complex multi-support manipulation tasks. This system learns multi-modal whole-body behaviors, including dynamic contact placement and switching, demonstrating robust execution autonomously and through shared autonomy on a physical robot.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots typically compartmentalize locomotion and manipulation, preventing them from leveraging their entire body for stability, extended reach, and complex multi-contact tasks.
- Model-based approaches for multi-contact tasks require precise environment models and struggle with non-linear dynamics and the computational demands for real-time, reactive behaviors.
- Traditional imitation learning methods fail to capture multi-modal distributions in expert demonstrations, often producing averaged, unfeasible actions for tasks with multiple valid strategies.

## Method

- A hierarchical control architecture combines a high-level ==Flow Matching== policy for learning multi-modal behaviors with a low-level model-based whole-body controller (SEIKO) for robust execution and contact force management.
- Flow Matching, a generative model, learns continuous flow vector fields to transform a simple distribution into the complex, multi-modal expert trajectory distribution, enabling deterministic and faster inference compared to Diffusion models.
- A shared autonomy mode allows a human operator to command primary manipulation while the learned policy intelligently assists by autonomously managing support contact placements and switches, aiding in out-of-distribution tasks.

## Results

- In simulation, Flow Matching achieved 99-**100%** success in multi-modal contact placement, significantly outperforming supervised learning (**52%** success) and demonstrating slightly better out-of-distribution generalization than Diffusion models.
- The Talos humanoid robot successfully executed autonomous multi-support manipulation tasks, such as closing dishwasher drawers and pushing boxes, by dynamically adding and removing support contacts.
- Assistive shared autonomy enabled the robot to perform out-of-distribution tasks (e.g., pushing a novel box shape) that purely autonomous policies failed, by intelligently automating support contact placement for human operators.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Flow Matching effectively captures and samples from multi-modal distributions in robotic tasks, yielding superior performance over traditional supervised learning and competitive results with Diffusion models.
> - The deterministic nature and faster inference of Flow Matching (35ms) compared to Diffusion models (178ms for DDPM) make it particularly suitable for real-time, reactive robotic control.
> - A robust hierarchical integration of generative imitation learning with a model-based whole-body controller is essential for translating complex learned behaviors into safe, feasible, and stable actions on full-size humanoid robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a significant challenge in humanoid robotics: expanding manipulation capabilities beyond traditional prehensile gripping and fixed-base locomotion. Conventionally, humanoid robots compartmentalize their functions, using feet for support and locomotion, and hands for manipulation. This approach overlooks the nuanced and versatile ways humans leverage their entire bodies—leaning on surfaces, pushing with forearms, or using multiple contact points—to enhance stability, extend reach, and perform complex contact-rich tasks. The authors term these "multi-support manipulation tasks."

The existing research landscape offers several avenues for tackling such challenges, each with limitations:
- **Model-based approaches** (e.g., trajectory optimization, contact planners) excel at generating optimal whole-body motions and regulating interaction forces. However, they demand a precise understanding of the environment, including suitable contact surfaces, and struggle with the non-linear dynamics of non-prehensile tasks involving sliding and friction. Optimizing contact placement and sequencing involves complex continuous and discrete decisions, making it computationally intensive and often unsuitable for real-time reactive behaviors.
- **Traditional Imitation Learning (IL)** methods (e.g., Behavior Cloning (BC), Dynamic Motion Primitives (DMP), Probabilistic Motion Primitives (ProMP)) learn policies directly from human demonstrations. While effective for simpler tasks within the demonstrated state-space, they typically struggle with high-dimensional inputs (like raw images), accumulate prediction errors leading to state divergence, and, critically, fail to capture multi-modal distributions in demonstrations. If multiple valid strategies exist for a task (e.g., reaching for a contact point on the left or right), traditional BC tends to average these strategies, leading to an unsafe or unfeasible action.

Recent advancements in **generative models**, particularly Diffusion Models, have inspired a new generation of behavior cloning algorithms. These models excel at learning and sampling from complex, high-dimensional probability distributions, making them suitable for capturing the multi-modal nature of human demonstrations. However, Diffusion Models, while powerful, can be computationally demanding for inference, which is a concern for real-time robotic control.

This paper positions itself at the forefront of this landscape by proposing a **unified, hierarchical approach** that intelligently combines the strengths of model-based control with advanced imitation learning.
1. It leverages the group's robust **model-based whole-body controller (SEIKO)** to handle the low-level complexities of multi-contact force distribution, contact switching, and maintaining stability and feasibility on a real robot. This foundation is crucial for translating high-level commands into safe and executable motions.
2. It introduces **Flow Matching**—a novel generative method based on optimal transport theory—as the core of its imitation learning policy. Flow Matching is presented as a deterministic counterpart to Diffusion, offering a simpler framework, faster inference, and improved numerical stability without sacrificing quality. This is a key contribution to the field of robotic learning, as it addresses the real-time inference constraints often faced by Diffusion models.
3. The work specifically tackles the challenges of **whole-body multi-contact and contact switching scenarios** through imitation learning, which has been largely unexplored with generative models. This directly addresses the gap in enabling robots to dynamically decide *when* and *how* to establish and release contacts.
4. Beyond full autonomy, the paper also introduces and validates a **shared autonomy mode**, where the learned policy assists a human operator by intelligently placing contacts. This pragmatic approach mitigates the common limitation of imitation learning where policies perform poorly on out-of-distribution tasks, providing a practical pathway for human-robot collaboration in varied environments.

By integrating robust control, advanced generative learning, and practical human-robot interaction paradigms, this research significantly pushes the boundaries of how humanoid robots can physically interact with their environment, making them more versatile, stable, and capable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **enable humanoid robots to perform complex multi-support manipulation tasks by leveraging their entire bodies for additional support and non-prehensile interactions.** This aims to overcome the current limitations where robots predominantly rely on their feet for support and hands for grasping, overlooking the rich repertoire of multi-contact strategies humans employ.

The specific motivations driving this work are multi-fold:

- **Mimicking Human Versatility:** Humans routinely employ multi-contact strategies—leaning on tables, pushing with forearms, using walls for stability—to extend their reach, enhance stability, and perform tasks involving heavy objects or complex interactions. The authors are motivated to imbue humanoid robots with similar versatility, moving beyond the implicit split between locomotion (feet) and manipulation (hands).
- **Expanding Robot Capabilities:** By integrating multi-support strategies, robots can significantly extend their operational workspace, improve stability during forceful interactions (e.g., pushing), and tackle contact-rich or non-prehensile tasks (e.g., sliding, pushing with the side of the body) that are difficult or impossible with traditional methods.
- **Overcoming Model-Based Limitations:** Current model-based approaches for multi-contact tasks are hindered by the need for a detailed environment model (identifying suitable contact surfaces) and struggle with the complex, non-linear dynamics of non-prehensile manipulation (e.g., sliding friction). Imitation learning offers a way to bypass explicit modeling by learning from human "common sense" and achieving real-time performance.
- **Addressing Multi-Modality in Demonstrations:** Many multi-support tasks allow for multiple valid strategies (e.g., contacting a surface from the left or right). Traditional imitation learning methods, like behavior cloning, struggle with these multi-modal distributions, often averaging them to produce an infeasible or unsafe action. A key motivation is to employ a method capable of accurately capturing and generating samples from such multi-modal distributions.
- **Leveraging Flow Matching for Robotics:** The paper specifically investigates Flow Matching, a novel generative method, motivated by the hypothesis that it is "best suited for robotics applications." This is based on its potential to offer a simpler framework, deterministic outputs, and faster inference compared to Diffusion Models, all while maintaining high quality in generating multi-modal trajectory distributions. Real-time performance is critical for reactive robot control.
- **Enabling Contact Switching:** A core challenge in multi-contact tasks is the discrete decision of when and how to establish or release contacts smoothly and safely. The authors aim to design an imitation learning formulation that can effectively learn and reproduce these complex contact switch transitions.
- **Developing Practical Assistance with Shared Autonomy:** Recognizing that purely autonomous policies derived from imitation learning may struggle with out-of-distribution tasks, a key motivation is to develop a shared autonomy mode. This mode allows the learned policy to assist a human operator, particularly by automating contact placement, thereby enhancing the robot's utility in varied and unforeseen scenarios.
- **Demonstrating on Real Humanoid Hardware:** A significant motivation is to validate the proposed unified approach on a full-size, complex humanoid robot (Talos), moving beyond simulation results to demonstrate real-world applicability and robustness.

In essence, the research aims to unlock a new paradigm of human-like whole-body interaction for humanoid robots, combining the power of generative imitation learning for high-level decision-making with robust model-based control for low-level execution, with a strong emphasis on practical, real-time deployment and human-robot collaboration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper combines a hierarchical control architecture with a novel application of Flow Matching for imitation learning, explicitly designed for multi-support manipulation on a full-size humanoid robot.

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

The system utilizes a two-tiered hierarchical control architecture to ensure robustness and manage complexity:
- **Low-Level Controller (500 Hz):** This is a model-based controller, leveraging the authors' prior work on **SEIKO (Sequential Equilibrium Inverse Kinematic Optimization) Retargeting and SEIKO Controller**. It performs whole-body optimization, multi-contact force distribution, and contact switching. The retargeting module computes feasible whole-body configurations (joint positions and contact forces) to track desired effector pose commands, enforcing safety constraints. The controller regulates contact forces on the position-controlled Talos robot, improving robustness to model errors and enabling smooth contact transitions. It is extended with "damping control" for robustness against imprecise contact placements.
- **High-Level Controller (100 Hz):** This is the learning-based component that outputs Cartesian effector commands (pose targets in the world frame) and discrete contact switch commands for each effector. It determines *what* the robot should do at a higher abstraction level, while the low-level controller figures out *how* to do it safely and physically consistently.

#### 3.2. Operational Modes

The high-level controller operates in three distinct modes:
- **Teleoperation Mode:** Used for collecting expert demonstrations. A human operator directly commands the robot's end-effectors (e.g., hands) using 6-DoF input devices. During these demonstrations, the robot records its effector commands and the poses of external fiducial markers (AprilTags) detected by an onboard RGB-D camera (Orbbec Femto Bolt).
- **Autonomous Mode:** After training, the imitation learning policy directly generates the high-level commands, allowing the robot to execute tasks independently.
- **Assistive Shared Autonomy Mode:** This mode combines human and policy control. A human operator commands one specific effector (e.g., the left hand), while the learned policy autonomously controls the remaining effectors (e.g., placing the right hand for support) and manages contact placements. This is designed for tasks that might deviate from the training distribution.

#### 3.3. Behavioral Cloning Policy and Contact Switch

The core of the high-level controller is a behavioral cloning policy defined as:
**Policy $\pi: s_k \rightarrow a_k$**
- **Input State ($s_k$):** Includes current effector pose commands ($X_{eff,i,k}$), boolean contact states ($c_{eff,i,k}$), elapsed time since last contact switch ($\tau_{eff,i,k}$), latest estimated poses of fiducial markers ($X_{tag,j,k}$), and elapsed time since each marker was last detected ($\tau_{tag,j,k}$). Times are clamped to 20s to prevent unbounded states, and marker detection times are randomized for robustness.
- **Output Action ($a_k$):** A trajectory of future effector pose commands ($X_{eff,i}$) and continuous contact state commands ($\gamma_{eff,i}$) for all effectors over a fixed horizon (N=32 steps, representing 6.4 seconds at a 5 Hz sampling rate). The predicted poses are encoded relative to the current input pose, and orientations use a 6D representation for input and 3D axis-angle for relative output.

**Contact Switch Implementation:** The policy outputs a continuous signal $\gamma_{eff,i}$ indicating the desired contact state. This is converted to a discrete boolean command $c_{eff,i}$ using a hysteresis threshold (0.8 to enable, 0.2 to disable) and a crucial time constraint: a contact switch is only triggered if at least 20 seconds have passed since the last switch for that effector. This delay ensures the low-level controller has sufficient time to smoothly transfer weight and redistribute forces during contact transitions, mimicking the waiting behavior observed in human demonstrations.

#### 3.4. Trajectory Generation with Flow Matching

The behavioral cloning policy is implemented as a generative process using **Flow Matching**.
- **Principle:** Flow Matching learns a continuous flow vector field that transforms a simple, easily sampleable source probability distribution (e.g., a multivariate normal distribution $P_{src} = N(0, I)$) into a complex, multi-modal destination distribution (the expert demonstration trajectories $P_{dst}$). Unlike Diffusion, Flow Matching generates samples deterministically by integrating this learned flow.
- **Training:** The flow model $f$ (a neural network) is trained to minimize a loss function $L_{flow} = E ||f(z_t, t, s) - (a_{dst} - a_{src})||^2$. Here, $z_t$ is an interpolation between a trajectory sampled from the source ($a_{src}$) and a demonstrated trajectory ($a_{dst}$), $t$ is the uniform flow transport time (0 to 1), and $s$ is the associated input state.
- **Inference:** A noisy trajectory $z_0 = a_{src}$ is sampled from the source distribution. The learned flow $f$ is then integrated iteratively from $t=0$ to $t=1$ over a number of steps (e.g., 20 steps) to produce the clean, desired action trajectory $z_1 = a_{dst}$.

#### 3.5. Trajectories Stitching and Processing

To ensure smooth and safe robot operation given the non-instantaneous inference, the policy's output trajectories undergo online processing:
- The policy outputs fixed-length trajectories at regular intervals (e.g., every 4 seconds).
- Due to inference computation time, newly generated trajectories are seamlessly **stitched** with the currently executing trajectory using linear interpolation over a short period (e.g., 0.5 seconds).
- The 5 Hz policy output trajectories are **resampled** to 100 Hz for the high-level controller.
- A **zero-phase low-pass filter** (first-order exponential filter) is applied to remove residual noise from inference and interpolation.

#### 3.6. Hardware Implementation

- **Robot:** PAL Robotics Talos humanoid robot (1.75 m height, 99.7 kg, 32 DoF, 22 controlled in position-control mode).
- **Custom End-Effector:** A 3D-printed, ball-shaped hand replaces the robot's gripper for high-force support contacts.
- **Sensing:** Orbbec Femto Bolt RGB-D camera on the robot's head for AprilTag detection and 3D pose estimation.
- **Software/Hardware Stack:** Policy training in Python (PyTorch) with GPU. Online inference in C++ (Intel i9-9880H CPU). SEIKO controller in C++ using Pinocchio (rigid body library) and QuadProg (QP solver), running onboard at 500 Hz. AprilTag detection at 30 Hz.

This meticulously designed methodology ensures that the learning component can effectively generate complex, multi-modal behaviors, while the robust model-based controller guarantees their safe and feasible execution on a challenging real-world platform.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings across both simulation and real-world experiments on the Talos humanoid robot, demonstrating the efficacy of Flow Matching imitation learning for multi-support manipulation.

#### 4.1. Simulated Reaching and Contact Placement Task

This experiment aimed to compare Flow Matching with Diffusion and traditional supervised learning for a task requiring multi-modal decision-making in contact placement.
- **Task:** The robot had to extend its right hand to establish contact with one of two randomized support platforms, creating a bimodal distribution of valid contact points.
- **Methods Compared:** Expert Demonstrations, Flow Matching (20 integration steps), DDPM (100 denoising steps), DDIM (20 steps, faster inference version of DDPM), and Supervised Learning (traditional behavior cloning with MSE loss).
- **Key Results (Figure 6):**
 - **Multi-Modality Handling:** Both Flow Matching and Diffusion-based methods (DDPM, DDIM) significantly outperformed the Supervised Learning baseline. The supervised approach, unable to handle the bimodal distribution, averaged the two contact locations, often resulting in the robot attempting to place its hand in an empty space between the platforms, leading to falls (52% success rate) and high contact placement error (median 4.1 cm).
 - **Performance Comparison (Flow vs. Diffusion):**
 - **In-Distribution:** All generative methods achieved very high success rates (99-100%) and low contact placement errors (median 1.4-1.5 cm), comparable to human demonstrations (100% success, 1.3 cm error).
 - **Out-of-Distribution:** Flow Matching demonstrated superior generalization, achieving a 78% success rate with a median contact error of 3.4 cm, compared to DDPM (69% success, 4.0 cm error) and DDIM (67% success, 3.9 cm error). This suggests Flow's ability to extrapolate better to novel situations.
 - **Inference Time:** Flow Matching (35 ± 4 ms) was significantly faster than DDPM (178 ± 12 ms) and comparable to DDIM (39 ± 4 ms), reinforcing its suitability for real-time robotic control. This speed, combined with slightly better out-of-distribution performance, positions Flow Matching favorably.

#### 4.2. Simulated Non-Prehensile Manipulation Task

This more complex task tested multi-support and whole-body strategies with higher multi-modality, involving both contact addition and removal.
- **Task:** A humanoid robot used both hands to push a concave T-shaped 3D object on a planar table to a target pose. The task required the robot to extend its reach by placing its right hand on the table for additional support, implying complex contact-rich dynamics and multi-modal pushing sequences.
- **Key Results (Figure 8):**
 - **Generative Models Outperform Baseline:** Similar to the contact reaching task, Flow Matching, DDPM, and DDIM all significantly outperformed the Supervised Learning baseline (55% success rate, slower completion). The baseline struggled with the intricate, multi-modal strategies required for effective object manipulation.
 - **Flow and Diffusion Performance:** Flow Matching (73% success) and Diffusion methods (DDPM 66%, DDIM 65%) exhibited comparable end-of-episode performance.
 - **Flow's Marginal Advantage:** Flow Matching showed a trend toward statistically faster task completion and slightly less variance in performance, consistent with its theoretical advantages and findings in other works.
 - **Failure Modes:** Common failures included the robot colliding with the top of the box or pushing it into an unreachable configuration, highlighting the inherent difficulty of the task.

#### 4.3. Hardware Experiments on Talos Humanoid Robot

These experiments are crucial for validating the real-world applicability and robustness of the proposed system.
- **Autonomous Mode (In-Distribution):**
 - **Dishwasher Task (Figure 9):** The Talos robot successfully closed both upper and lower dishwasher drawers. For the lower drawer (requiring significant forward bend and extended reach), the robot autonomously placed its right hand on top of the dishwasher for support, demonstrating precise contact placement and dynamic contact switching. The learned policy also reacted robustly to disturbances, re-closing drawers if reopened. This validated the hierarchical architecture's ability to execute multi-support manipulation on real hardware.
 - **Box Pushing Task (Figure 10):** The robot autonomously manipulated the red T-shaped box using both hands. Crucially, it dynamically added and removed right-hand contacts for support when pushing from behind, compensating for interaction forces and maintaining balance. The system demonstrated effective reaction to disturbances applied to the object.
- **Assistive Shared Autonomy (Out-of-Distribution):**
 - **Blue U-shaped Box Pushing (Figure 10):** When presented with a blue U-shaped box (an out-of-distribution task, as the policy was trained on the red T-shaped box), the purely autonomous policy failed, getting stuck or attempting to push non-existent sides.
 - **Success with Shared Autonomy:** In this mode, the human operator controlled the left hand, while the Flow Matching policy controlled the right hand, primarily for contact placement. This allowed the human operator to successfully solve the blue U-box task. For instance, the policy automatically placed the right hand for support when the operator commanded the left hand to perform far reaches, or removed it to allow further object manipulation. In the dishwasher task, the policy automatically placed the right-hand support when the operator tried to reach the lower drawer. This demonstrates the practical utility of shared autonomy for addressing the generalization limitations of imitation learning.

In summary, the results strongly support the effectiveness of Flow Matching for learning multi-modal, whole-body, multi-contact policies. The method not only outperforms traditional behavior cloning but also shows competitive or superior performance to Diffusion models with faster inference, critically enabling deployment on a real humanoid robot for challenging multi-support tasks, both autonomously and in a shared autonomy context.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research represents a significant advancement in humanoid robotics, particularly in the domain of physical interaction and manipulation, with far-reaching potential impacts:

#### 5.1. Enhanced Humanoid Capabilities and Dexterity

The most immediate impact is enabling humanoid robots to perform a broader range of complex, human-like multi-support and non-prehensile manipulation tasks. By allowing robots to leverage their entire bodies for support contacts, the work significantly expands their effective workspace, increases stability during forceful interactions, and opens up possibilities for tasks that were previously challenging or impossible (e.g., pushing heavy objects, closing drawers that require leaning). This moves humanoids closer to the versatility seen in human physical interaction with the environment.

#### 5.2. Validation and Promotion of Flow Matching in Robotics

The paper provides strong empirical evidence for Flow Matching as a highly suitable generative model for imitation learning in robotics. Its demonstrated advantages—simpler framework, deterministic outputs, faster inference, and competitive or superior performance compared to Diffusion Models, especially in out-of-distribution generalization—are critical for real-time robotic applications. This validation could accelerate the adoption of Flow Matching over other generative models in the robotics community, leading to more efficient and robust learning from demonstrations.

#### 5.3. Robust Hierarchical Control for Real-World Deployment

The successful deployment of the proposed method on a full-size, complex humanoid robot like Talos is a major achievement. This is attributed to the robust hierarchical architecture that skillfully integrates a learning-based high-level policy (Flow Matching) with a proven model-based low-level whole-body controller (SEIKO). This highlights a crucial pathway for translating complex learned behaviors into safe, feasible, and robust physical actions on real hardware, a common bottleneck in robotics research.

#### 5.4. Practical Advancements in Human-Robot Collaboration

The introduction and successful demonstration of the assistive shared autonomy mode is a significant contribution to human-robot interaction. It addresses a key limitation of imitation learning: poor generalization to out-of-distribution tasks. By allowing the human operator to command primary manipulation while the robot's learned policy intelligently manages support contacts, the system provides valuable assistance. This paradigm enhances human teleoperation, reduces cognitive load on the operator, and makes robots more useful in unstructured or novel environments where fully autonomous solutions might fail. This shared control approach has implications for various applications, from industrial assembly to elder care.

#### 5.5. Foundations for Future Research

The work lays a solid foundation for several promising future research directions:
- **Perception beyond Fiducial Markers:** The authors explicitly mention extending the approach to learning from raw images or point clouds, eliminating the need for fiducial markers. This would make the system more autonomous and applicable in less prepared environments.
- **Whole-Body Non-Prehensile Interaction:** The framework can be extended to utilize the robot's entire body geometry (e.g., forearms, torso) for non-prehensile tasks, further broadening the scope of robot interaction.
- **Complex Loco-Manipulation:** The integration of multi-support strategies with locomotion could lead to advanced loco-manipulation tasks, where robots dynamically adjust their support base while moving and interacting with the environment.
- **Natural Language Integration:** The "Words2Contact" reference suggests future work on integrating natural language instructions to identify support contacts, paving the way for more intuitive and flexible human-robot interfaces.

In conclusion, "Flow Matching Imitation Learning for Multi-Support Manipulation" is a impactful paper that not only pushes the technical boundaries of humanoid control and learning but also delivers practical, demonstrable solutions on state-of-the-art robotic hardware. Its significance lies in making humanoids more versatile, robust, and capable partners for complex physical tasks, while also advancing the underlying machine learning methodologies for robotics.
