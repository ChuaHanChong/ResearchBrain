# Learning Reusable Hybrid Motion Priors for Humanoid Locomotion from Motion Imitation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Sapienza University of Rome, University College London, and Inria developed a methodology to distill motor skills from motion imitation into a reusable hybrid motion prior (HMP) for humanoid robots. This HMP enables task-level policies to control a Unitree G1 robot across various locomotion tasks, including robust velocity tracking, point-goal navigation, and fall recovery, with successful zero-shot sim-to-real deployment.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning policies for humanoid robots are typically task-specific, requiring extensive retraining and re-acquisition of fundamental motor skills for each new objective.
- Motion imitation efficiently provides diverse movements but results in policies that are merely reference trackers, unable to solve higher-level tasks or adapt to unscripted situations independently.
- There is a lack of reusable low-level control spaces that allow task-level policies to operate on abstract motor competence rather than directly in the robot's full joint-action space.

## Method

- A ==three-stage training pipeline== is introduced to transform motion imitation skills into a reusable Hybrid Motion Prior (**HMP**).
- The **HMP** architecture comprises a ==proprioceptive encoder== for continuous latent representation, a ==residual vector-quantized (RVQ) codebook== for discrete motion choices, and an ==action decoder==.
- Task-level policies are trained using reinforcement learning to select discrete codes from the frozen **HMP**, which then generate continuous joint-position actions for the robot.

## Results

- The distilled HMP maintained high fidelity to the expert's behavior, with metrics like body-position error (**62.2 mm**) and joint-position error (**0.721 rad**) closely matching the expert.
- The single, frozen HMP was successfully reused across simulated velocity tracking (**0.46 ± 0.15 m/s** linear velocity RMSE, **2.1%** fall rate), point-goal navigation (average speed **~2 m/s**, **4.2%** fall rate), and fall-recovery velocity tracking (**98%** recovery from falls).
- A velocity-tracking policy trained with the HMP achieved zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating balanced and responsive locomotion qualitatively consistent with simulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The HMP exhibits a clear division of roles, with the proprioceptive encoder handling fundamental balance and basic locomotion, while the RVQ codebook provides task-conditioned modulations.
> - Varying the Residual Vector Quantizer (RVQ) depth reveals a hierarchical structure in learned gaits, where increasing depth enables the generation of faster and more dynamic motions.
> - The 'rotation trick' for codebook training, compared to a straight-through estimator, yields a more stable and robust latent representation, significantly improving downstream task policy performance and robustness (e.g., 2.1% vs. 10.2% fall rate).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses challenges in developing versatile and robust controllers for humanoid robots, specifically focusing on the acquisition and reuse of fundamental motor skills. Deep reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated effectiveness in generating robust controllers for legged and humanoid robots, capable of deployment on physical hardware. However, a common limitation is that RL policies are typically task-specific, necessitating a new reward function and extensive retraining for each new objective. This often involves re-acquiring basic motor competence such as balance, stepping, and whole-body coordination, which is computationally inefficient.

Motion imitation offers an alternative paradigm for acquiring motor competence. By training policies to track retargeted human motion-capture data, robots can learn diverse and natural skills without explicit reward engineering for each behavior. Despite this, motion imitation policies primarily function as reference trackers; they execute a provided motion but do not inherently solve downstream tasks like navigating to a point, following a velocity command, or reacting to high-level task observations independently.

The present work integrates these two approaches by leveraging the motor competence gained through motion imitation and making it accessible to task-level policies. It contributes to the field of reusable low-level control spaces, where downstream policies operate on a learned, more abstract representation rather than directly in the robot's full joint-action space. Previous work in this area has explored continuous priors, such as neural motor primitives or latent control spaces, for physics-based characters and legged robots. Complementary to these are discrete representations, which limit task-level decisions to a finite vocabulary of motor choices, often utilizing vector quantization (VQ) or residual vector quantization (RVQ). While such discrete and hybrid continuous-discrete formulations have shown results in simulated character control, their application and demonstration on floating-base humanoid robots for real-world tasks have been less prevalent. This paper aims to bridge this gap by proposing a concrete, experimentally validated method for deploying a codebook-based action interface on a humanoid robot.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a methodology that transforms motor skills acquired through motion imitation into a reusable hybrid motion prior (HMP) for humanoid locomotion. This HMP is intended to serve as a low-level action interface that can be leveraged by diverse task-level policies without requiring retraining of the underlying motor competence.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key challenges in current humanoid control:
- **Inefficiency of Task-Specific RL:** Each new task in RL typically demands a unique reward design and an entirely new training process. During this process, policies must re-learn foundational motor skills (e.g., balance, stepping, contact management, whole-body coordination), which is redundant and resource-intensive.
- **Limitations of Motion Imitation:** While motion imitation efficiently endows robots with a broad range of natural movement skills from human data, the resulting policies are inherently reference trackers. They require a pre-defined motion reference to operate and cannot directly solve higher-level tasks or adapt to unscripted situations without such a reference.
- **Lack of Reusable Motor Competence:** There is a need for control architectures that can decouple the acquisition of basic motor skills from the learning of task-specific behaviors. This would allow for the rapid development of new robotic capabilities by building upon a pre-existing "motor vocabulary."
- **Interpretability and Control:** Discrete representations offer an interface that restricts task-level decisions to a finite, potentially interpretable set of motor choices, which could simplify the design and debugging of higher-level policies.

To address these motivations, the authors set out to:
- Design a continuous-discrete HMP architecture that combines a proprioceptive latent representation with a residual vector-quantized (RVQ) codebook.
- Develop a three-stage training pipeline to learn this HMP: (1) training a motion-imitation expert, (2) distilling its competence into the HMP using residual quantization and a specialized training technique (the rotation trick), and (3) training downstream task policies to act by selecting codes from the frozen HMP.
- Validate the reusability of the learned HMP across multiple distinct locomotion tasks in simulation (velocity tracking, point-goal navigation, fall-recovery velocity tracking).
- Analyze the internal structure and function of the HMP, including the roles of its continuous and discrete components, the effect of RVQ depth on learned gaits, and the impact of the rotation trick on latent organization and downstream robustness.
- Demonstrate the practical applicability of the approach through zero-shot sim-to-real deployment on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology employs a three-stage pipeline to convert motion-imitation skills into a reusable hybrid motion prior (HMP) for humanoid locomotion. The approach combines continuous proprioceptive encoding with discrete residual vector quantization.

#### 3.1. A. Phase 1: Motion-Imitation Expert Training

The first phase involves training a motion-imitation expert policy.
- **Objective:** To acquire a diverse set of motor competencies by tracking retargeted human motion-capture clips.
- **Method:** A Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is utilized within an asymmetric actor-critic framework.
- **Data:** The expert is trained on 27 LaFAN motion clips, including various gaits (walking, running, sprinting), jumping, and fall-and-get-up motions, retargeted to the Unitree G1 morphology.
- **Inputs:** The actor receives instantaneous proprioceptive observations (base angular velocity, projected gravity vector, joint positions/velocities relative to nominal posture, previous action), a proprioceptive history embedding (computed from a temporal convolutional history encoder over a 10-step window), and a motion command extracted from the reference trajectory.
- **Reward:** A composite reward function combines terms for base, body, joint, and velocity tracking, along with regularization for contacts, action smoothness, and joint limits.
- **Robustness:** Domain randomization, external perturbations, observation noise, and adaptive motion/phase sampling are used to enhance robustness.

#### 3.2. B. Phase 2: Hybrid Motion Prior (HMP) Distillation

The second phase distills the behavior of the trained motion-imitation expert into the HMP architecture.
- **Objective:** To create a reusable, deployable HMP composed of a proprioceptive encoder, a codebook, and an action decoder.
- **Process:** The frozen expert generates on-policy rollouts, and a student model (the HMP) is trained via supervised learning on this collected data.
- **Architecture Components:**
 - **Proprioceptive Encoder ($P_\theta$):** Takes instantaneous proprioceptive observation and history embedding as input to produce a continuous latent representation ($z_{prop}$). This encoder is retained at deployment.
 - **Reference Encoder ($E_\phi$):** A training-only component that receives proprioception, history embedding, and the motion command to produce a reference latent ($z_{ref}$).
 - **Residual Vector Quantizer (RVQ) Codebook:** Instead of quantizing the full latent, the RVQ quantizes the residual $y = z_{ref} - sg(z_{prop})$ (where $sg$ is the stop-gradient operator). This design encourages the proprioceptive encoder to capture fundamental, inferable information, while the codebook specializes in encoding the motion intent not directly inferable from proprioception. The RVQ consists of $M=8$ stages, each with $K=1024$ code entries. It selects one code from each active stage and sums them to produce the quantized residual ($\hat{y}$). Quantizer dropout is used during training, where $\text{M}' \le M$ stages are randomly activated, promoting a coarse-to-fine structure in the codebook.
 - **Action Decoder ($D_\psi$):** This decoder receives the proprioceptive latent and the quantized residual ($\hat{z} = sg(z_{prop}) + \hat{y}$), along with proprioceptive observations and history embedding, to reconstruct the expert's continuous action ($\hat{a}$). It is observation-injected, meaning environmental observations are fed into every hidden layer, making it a conditional action generator.
- **Distillation Objective:** A composite loss function ($L$) is minimized:
 - $L_a$: Squared error between reconstructed and expert actions.
 - $L_m$: Penalizes the magnitude of the residual, annealed upwards to prevent premature over-constraining.
 - $L_r$: Temporal regularizer discouraging abrupt changes in latents and residuals across consecutive steps.
 - $L_q$: Commitment loss for updating codebook entries, which are updated separately via exponential moving averages.
- **Codebook Training with Rotation Trick:** To address the non-differentiability of nearest-neighbor quantization, the "rotation trick" is employed for gradient propagation. Unlike the standard straight-through estimator (STE) that copies gradients directly, the rotation trick applies a transformation that scales and rotates the gradient to align with the selected code vector, aiming to improve latent organization and gradient flow. This is applied independently at each active RVQ stage.
- **Resulting HMP:** After distillation, the reference encoder is discarded. The frozen HMP comprises the proprioceptive encoder, the codebook, and the action decoder, providing a reusable action interface.

#### 3.3. C. Phase 3: Downstream Task Learning by Code Selection

The final phase involves training task-level policies using the frozen HMP.
- **Objective:** To enable task policies to solve specific locomotion tasks by selecting discrete codebook entries, leveraging the motor skills embedded in the HMP without modifying it.
- **Task-Conditioned Code Policy ($F_\xi$):** A task-level policy receives the current proprioceptive observation, history embedding, and a task-specific command. It outputs categorical logits for each RVQ stage.
- **Action Generation:** During training, code indices are sampled from the categorical distributions defined by these logits. During evaluation, the highest-logit index is selected. These indices retrieve code vectors from the frozen HMP's codebook, which are summed and passed to the action decoder to produce the continuous joint-position action for the robot.
- **Training:** The task-level policy ($F_\xi$) is trained using PPO, optimizing based on the downstream task reward, while the HMP remains frozen.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings from evaluations in simulation and on a real robot, as well as an analysis of the HMP's internal mechanics.

#### 4.1. A. Distillation Evaluation

- The distillation process successfully preserved the behavior of the motion-imitation expert. When the distilled model (reference encoder + HMP) was evaluated on the same retargeted LaFAN motions, it closely matched the expert's performance. Metrics such as body-position error (62.2 mm vs. 62.4 mm), body-orientation error (0.210 rad vs. 0.209 rad), and joint-position error (0.721 rad vs. 0.712 rad) were nearly identical. Action statistics also remained consistent, confirming high fidelity in behavior preservation.

#### 4.2. B. Locomotion Tasks in Simulation

The same frozen HMP was successfully reused across three distinct locomotion tasks:
- **Velocity Tracking:** The task-level policy reliably tracked commanded planar and yaw velocities. It achieved a linear velocity RMSE of 0.46 ± 0.15 m/s and an angular velocity RMSE of 1.03 ± 0.46 rad/s, with a fall rate of 2.1%. The policy exhibited diverse gaits (standing, walking, running, turning, lateral movements) adapting to command changes.
- **Point-Goal Navigation:** The policy effectively navigated through sequential targets, combining goal-directed walking, heading corrections, and gait adaptation. It reached new goals approximately every 1.4 seconds, maintained an average speed of about 2 m/s, and fell in 4.2% of trials.
- **Fall-Recovery Velocity Tracking:** Under repeated strong external disturbances (5 m/s sagittal or 3 m/s lateral base-velocity perturbations every 5 seconds), the policy recovered from approximately 98% of induced falls. It returned to an upright posture within an average of 1.5 ± 0.6 seconds and resumed velocity tracking.

#### 4.3. C. Velocity Tracking on the Real Robot

- The velocity-tracking policy was deployed on a physical Unitree G1 robot without any real-world fine-tuning (zero-shot sim-to-real transfer). The robot successfully tracked forward and lateral commands, and performed in-place and walking turns. The qualitative behavior on hardware was observed to be consistent with simulation, demonstrating balance and command responsiveness, although visible oscillations were present during motion.

#### 4.4. D. Analysis of the Hybrid Motion Prior (HMP)

- **Role of Proprioceptive Encoder and Codebook:**
 - When the codebook contribution was silenced ($\hat{y} = 0$), the robot maintained static and dynamic balance and spontaneously produced basic locomotor patterns (standing, walking, short running bursts) but without any goal-directed behavior. This indicated that the proprioceptive encoder ($P_\theta$) captures the fundamental balance and locomotion competence.
 - Conversely, when the proprioceptive encoder's contribution was silenced ($z_{prop} = 0$), the robot fell within a few control steps in almost every rollout. This supports the design principle that the codebook encodes *modulations* of the proprioceptive latent rather than complete latent actions.
 - The combined effect suggests a clear division of roles: the proprioceptive encoder acts as a balance- and locomotion-aware backbone, while the codebook provides task-conditioned modulations.
- **RVQ Depth Analysis:** Varying the number of active RVQ stages ($M_{act}$) revealed a hierarchical structure in the learned gaits.
 - With $M_{act} = 1$ or $2$, policies were stable but limited to stationary behavior or slow walking, respectively, and could not achieve high commanded speeds.
 - A significant transition occurred at $M_{act} = 3$, enabling fast walking and almost-running behaviors, reaching speeds up to 3.0 m/s without falls.
 - With $M_{act} = 5$ or $8$, the policy developed faster and more natural running gaits. $M_{act} = 5$ appeared to offer the best trade-off for tracking performance in the tested scenario. This indicated that earlier stages in the RVQ hierarchy are sufficient for balance and slow gaits, while deeper stages enable more dynamic and faster motions.
- **Effect of the Rotation Trick on Codebook Structure:** Comparison between HMPs trained with the rotation trick (RT) and a standard straight-through estimator (STE) showed:
 - Both variants achieved comparable action-level MSE, indicating similar imitation fidelity.
 - The STE variant exhibited significantly larger codebook and residual magnitudes (more than twice those of RT), as well as higher quantization error, suggesting it relied more heavily on the quantized residual for action generation. This was also reflected in its higher training losses.
 - The RT variant showed higher global perplexity across stages, indicating a more uniform and potentially better utilization of the available codes.
 - The benefits of the RT persisted across both coarse (stages 1-3) and fine (stages 4-8) quantization stages.
- **Rotation Trick and Downstream Robustness:** Task-level policies trained on the RT HMP demonstrated significantly greater robustness in velocity tracking. The STE variant experienced a fall rate of 10.2% compared to 2.1% for the RT variant, despite similar tracking errors. This suggests that the rotation trick leads to a more stable and robust latent representation for downstream tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robot control by introducing a systematic method for creating reusable motion priors from imitation learning.

The key contributions are:
- **A Novel HMP Architecture:** The proposed continuous-discrete HMP effectively combines a proprioceptive encoder, a residual vector-quantized codebook, and an action decoder. This architecture allows task-level policies to operate by selecting discrete codes, thereby reusing pre-learned motion-imitation skills without direct access to motion references.
- **A Robust Three-Stage Training Pipeline:** The pipeline clearly separates the acquisition of fundamental motor competence, its distillation into a reusable prior, and the training of high-level task policies. The integration of residual quantization and the "rotation trick" for codebook training enhances the quality and robustness of the learned representation.
- **Demonstrated Reusability and Generalization:** The work extensively validates the HMP's reusability across diverse locomotion tasks (velocity tracking, point-goal navigation, fall recovery) in simulation using a single, frozen HMP.
- **Real-World Applicability:** The successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of the velocity-tracking policy to a Unitree G1 humanoid robot demonstrates the practical utility and robustness of the approach in physical environments.
- **Insights into Latent Structure:** The detailed analysis of the HMP's internal components clarifies the distinct roles of the proprioceptive encoder and the codebook, illustrating how continuous and discrete representations can complement each other. The RVQ depth analysis reveals an interpretable, hierarchical organization of gait patterns, while the comparison with the straight-through estimator highlights the critical importance of the rotation trick for improved latent space organization and downstream policy robustness.

The potential impact of this work is substantial:
- **Accelerated Robot Skill Development:** By decoupling low-level motor competence from high-level task learning, the approach can significantly reduce the training time and computational resources required to develop new robot skills. Researchers and practitioners can leverage a pre-trained, robust motor vocabulary, focusing their efforts on task-specific reward design and policy learning.
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** The ability to execute diverse tasks without relying on explicit motion references enables more autonomous and adaptive robot behavior in complex environments.
- **Increased Interpretability:** The discrete nature of the codebook offers a more interpretable interface for task-level policies, potentially simplifying the design, debugging, and understanding of robot behaviors.
- **Broader Application to Humanoids:** The successful demonstration on a Unitree G1 humanoid, which is a complex, floating-base system, suggests the methodology could be applicable to other humanoid platforms and more complex loco-manipulation tasks.

#### 5.1. Limitations and Future Work

Despite these achievements, the paper acknowledges certain limitations. The real-robot deployment, while successful, exhibited visible oscillations during walking and turning, suggesting room for improvement in motion smoothness. Future work aims to:
- Extend the evaluation to a broader set of downstream tasks, including complex loco-manipulation scenarios.
- Further improve the smoothness and robustness of the frozen HMP, particularly for more dynamic real-world motions, to mitigate observed oscillations and enhance overall performance in physical environments.
