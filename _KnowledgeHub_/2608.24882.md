# Latent Action as Intention Enables Efficient Future Imagination for World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> LAWA is a world action model that replaces test-time future-observation generation with prediction of compact latent actions treated as future intentions. A mask-supervised discrete tokenizer, pre-trained on action-free robot and egocentric video, supplies codebook targets; video, latent-action, and action experts train under structured joint attention, with the video branch dropped at inference. It reaches 65.6% few-shot and 80.8% full-data RoboCasa success at 42.9% lower latency than Joint-WAM.

## Problem

- World action models that iteratively denoise future observations add heavy test-time computation before any action executes, obstructing real-robot control at practical rates.
- Removing test-time future imagination entirely restores speed but generalizes markedly worse, especially with scarce robot demonstrations and under out-of-distribution perturbations.
- Tokenizers learned purely from video reconstruction favor static scene appearance over the small interaction dynamics that actually determine manipulation success.

## Method

- Discrete ==latent action tokenizer==: DINOv2 patch features, factorized spatio-temporal attention, frame-differenced transitions quantized to a learnable ==codebook==, with a causal forward decoder reconstructing the next observation.
- Manipulation-centric auxiliary supervision: a SAM-style ==mask decoder== predicts hand/manipulator masks from the latent action prompt, targets auto-generated by SAM 2, plus speed-aligned and rebalanced ==action-free egocentric pre-training==.
- **LAWA**'s ==multi-model joint attention== with a structured mask lets action tokens read the evolving latent intention but never future-video tokens; ==flow-matching== denoises latent and action branches jointly, video branch omitted at inference.

## Results

- RoboCasa: **65.6%** few-shot and **80.8%** full-data success, beating the matched Fast-WAM baseline by **9.6** and **4.5** points and matching Joint-WAM's **64.1%/78.8%**.
- LIBERO-Plus zero-shot: **74.4%** overall, **14.4** points over Fast-WAM and **4.0** over Joint-WAM, with **44.3-point** and **27.5-point** gains under camera-viewpoint and sensor-noise shifts.
- Latency **338.5 ms** per chunk versus Joint-WAM's **593.1 ms**; real-world xArm7 tasks average **40.0%** with **25%** of demonstrations, exceeding full-data Fast-WAM's **33.8%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Future imagination need not be discarded for speed: a compact latent bottleneck preserves the generalization benefit of predicting futures at a fraction of visual-generation cost.
> - The latent pathway is causally load-bearing, not decorative: injecting noise or shuffling it in time drops success from 80.8% to 52.2% and 56.4%.
> - Compact latent prediction alone underperforms explicit future prediction; the advantage appears only once scalable action-free video pre-training amplifies it, and mask supervision beats optical flow.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation tasks often require policies that can anticipate future interactions and environmental changes rather than merely reacting to current observations. World Action Models (WAMs) address this requirement by integrating action learning with predictive modeling of future visual dynamics. These models generate explicit representations of task progression and environmental dynamics by predicting future observations, which is particularly beneficial in scenarios with limited robot demonstration data.

A common WAM paradigm, referred to as Joint-WAM in this paper, involves iteratively denoising future observations. While this approach enhances generalization and performance, it introduces significant test-time computational overhead, posing a practical challenge for real-robot control due to increased latency before an action can be executed.

In response to this efficiency bottleneck, approaches like Fast-WAM have been proposed. Fast-WAM maintains video prediction during training to leverage the co-training benefits but removes the future-video branch during inference to reduce computational load. However, the authors' controlled comparisons indicate that Fast-WAM exhibits reduced generalization capabilities compared to future-aware alternatives, especially in few-shot learning and out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. This suggests that the advantages of future modeling might not exclusively stem from video co-training but also from the explicit representation of future states available at inference. This observation highlights a trade-off between generalization performance and inference efficiency inherent in current WAM designs.

The concept of latent actions has emerged as a method to compress visual transitions into action-like variables, facilitating pre-training on videos without explicit action labels. Prior works have explored quantizing inter-frame transitions, learning task-centric latent actions, or grounding them through future-video prediction. Some approaches predict continuous visual foresight, while others transfer future information from a training-only posterior. The present work builds upon this landscape by exploring how compact latent actions can provide an efficient, explicit representation of future intentions without incurring the cost of full observation-space video generation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a World Action Model (WAM) architecture that can efficiently leverage future imagination to improve robotic control, particularly in scenarios with scarce demonstration data and under distribution shifts, without incurring the substantial inference latency associated with explicit future observation generation.

The motivation for this work stems from the observed limitations of existing WAM paradigms:

1. **High Inference Latency of Future-Aware WAMs (e.g., Joint-WAM):** Models that explicitly predict and iteratively denoise future observations (referred to as Joint-WAM) provide a strong signal for task progress and environmental dynamics, leading to better generalization and performance. However, this process is computationally intensive at test time, creating a bottleneck for real-time robot control.
2. **Generalization Deficiencies of Latency-Optimized WAMs (e.g., Fast-WAM):** Approaches like Fast-WAM attempt to mitigate latency by removing the future-video prediction branch during inference, retaining it only for training. While this improves efficiency, the authors' matched implementations demonstrate that Fast-WAM exhibits notably worse generalization, especially in few-shot settings and when encountering out-of-distribution scenarios. This indicates that merely co-training with video prediction during training might not fully capture the benefits of an explicit future representation at inference.
3. **The Need for an Efficient Future Representation:** The observed performance gap suggests that an explicit representation of how a task should progress is valuable, but generating high-fidelity visual observations is too costly. Therefore, there is a need for a more compact and efficient form of "future imagination" that can guide action generation without the computational burden of pixel-level video prediction.

To address these challenges, the authors propose LAWA, which aims to:
- **Retain test-time future imagination:** Unlike Fast-WAM, LAWA explicitly uses a future representation during inference.
- **Improve inference efficiency:** This future representation is shifted from the observation space to a compact latent-action space, thereby avoiding the high cost of generating future visual frames.
- **Enhance generalization:** The compact, manipulation-centric latent actions are expected to provide a more focused and robust signal for action generation, particularly when demonstration data is limited or conditions change.
- **Leverage diverse data sources:** Develop a mechanism for scalable pre-training using action-free robot and egocentric videos to improve the learned latent action representations.

The central hypothesis is that treating a temporally structured sequence of compact latent actions as "future intentions" can offer a beneficial trade-off, providing future-aware action generation with improved generalization and performance while maintaining inference efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LAWA's methodology is characterized by its use of compact latent actions as an operational representation of future intentions, enabling efficient future imagination at inference time without generating future observations. The system is structured into a two-stage training process: first, a latent action tokenizer is pre-trained, and then, a multi-modal policy is trained using this tokenizer.

#### 3.1. Latent Action Tokenizer

The latent action tokenizer is designed to compress sequences of observations into discrete latent action tokens that capture dynamic transitions.

- **Architecture:** It processes an observation sequence ($o_{t:t+H}$) to produce discrete latent action tokens ($l_{t+1:t+H}$). A DINOv2 encoder extracts patch tokens from each frame, which are then contextualized by a non-causal transformer using factorized spatial and temporal attention. The resulting features are differenced between consecutive frames to encode transitions and then spatially compressed. These compressed tokens are assigned to the nearest entry in a learnable discrete codebook. A causal forward decoder then reconstructs the next observation ($o_k$) by concatenating the projected latent action ($l_k$) with the patch tokens of the previous observation ($o_{k-1}$), ensuring that the discrete representation captures transition information not inferable from static appearance.
- **Manipulation-centric Auxiliary Supervision:** To prevent video reconstruction from being dominated by static scene appearance, which might cause underrepresentation of small but critical manipulation dynamics, the tokenizer is augmented with a mask decoder. This decoder, akin to SAM, predicts hand or robot-manipulator masks for each transition. The mask targets are automatically generated using SAM 2, enabling scalable and annotation-free training. This objective is intended to bias the latent actions toward interaction regions.
- **Action-free Egocentric Pre-training:** To address the limited data diversity of robot-only datasets, the tokenizer and forward decoder are co-trained on action-free videos from both robot data and diverse egocentric datasets. Per-source frame sampling is employed to align motion speed distributions across heterogeneous data, reducing optimization interference. Additionally, weighted rebalancing is applied during sampling to ensure robot videos constitute a significant portion (approximately 20%) of training samples, thereby preserving robot-domain grounding.

#### 3.2. Latent Action as Intention Policy Training

After the tokenizer is pre-trained and frozen, it is used to encode ground-truth observation sequences into target latent actions ($l_{t+1:t+H}$) for training the main robotic policy.

- **Architecture and Inference:** LAWA jointly trains three experts: video denoising, latent action denoising, and action denoising. During training, the current observation ($o_t$) is kept clean, while future targets for all three modalities (future-video latents, latent actions, and executable action chunks) are independently corrupted with noise. At inference, the future-video branch is discarded. LAWA encodes $o_t$ once and then jointly denoises the latent actions and executable actions from noise. The predicted latent states, trained toward discrete codebook-derived transition targets, are used as continuous "future intentions" to guide the action expert. No nearest-neighbor projection is applied during or after denoising; the action expert conditions on the evolving continuous latent state.
- **Multi-model Joint Attention:** The three experts are coupled through a multi-model joint attention mechanism within a Diffusion Transformer, while retaining modality-specific Transformer blocks and timestep conditioning. A structured attention mask controls information flow: current-observation tokens cannot access future information. Noisy future-video tokens attend to all video tokens. Latent action tokens attend only to the current observation and the latent action sequence. Action tokens attend to the current observation, the latent action sequence, and the action sequence. This mask ensures the action expert can utilize the evolving future intention without leakage from future video information. Language instructions and current proprioception are also conditioned. At inference, with future-video tokens omitted, current-observation features are cached, and only the latent action and action experts perform iterative denoising.

#### 3.3. Training Objective

The training involves two stages:

1. **Tokenizer Pre-training:** The latent action tokenizer is optimized using a combined loss:
 $L_{tok} = L_1 + \lambda_{perc} L_{perc} + \lambda_{mask} L_{mask}$
 where $L_1$ and $L_{perc}$ (LPIPS-based) supervise next-frame reconstruction, and $L_{mask}$ (binary cross-entropy, Dice, and IoU losses) supervises mask prediction. Noise-substitution quantization is used to manage the codebook.

2. **Robot Policy Training:** With the tokenizer frozen, the three experts are optimized using flow matching. For each modality $m \in \{vid, lat, act\}$, clean targets ($y_m$) are corrupted with sampled Gaussian noise ($\epsilon_m$) at a shifted flow time ($\tau_m$) to create $y_m^{\tau_m}$. A velocity field $v_m = \epsilon_m - y_m$ is targeted. The policy minimizes:
 $L_m = E[w_m(\tau_m) \Vert \hat{v}_{m\theta} - v_m \Vert_2^2]$
 where $\hat{v}_{m\theta}$ is the predicted velocity, conditioned on $y_m^{\tau_m}$, $\tau_m$, and context $C_m$ as per the attention mask. The overall robot policy objective is:
 $L_{LAWA} = \lambda_{vid} L_{vid} + \lambda_{lat} L_{lat} + \lambda_{act} L_{act}$
 This objective balances world modeling (video term), matching codebook-derived transitions (latent term), and learning executable control (action term).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LAWA involved extensive experiments across simulation benchmarks (RoboCasa, LIBERO-Plus) and real-world robotic tasks, comparing its performance, generalization, and inference efficiency against Fast-WAM and Joint-WAM, among other baselines.

#### 4.1. Performance on Simulation Benchmarks

- **RoboCasa:** LAWA achieved state-of-the-art average success rates. In the few-shot setting (10% of training data), LAWA obtained 65.6%, surpassing the matched Fast-WAM baseline by 9.6 percentage points and exceeding DIAL by 7.3 points. In the full-data setting, LAWA reached 80.8%, outperforming Fast-WAM by 4.5 points and demonstrating comparable success to the matched Joint-WAM (78.8%). These results indicate LAWA's ability to retain the performance level of explicit future-observation prediction while improving over test-time future-imagination-free alternatives.
- **LIBERO-Plus (Zero-shot Transfer):** LAWA achieved a competitive overall zero-shot success rate of 74.4% on LIBERO-Plus, which tests robustness to environmental perturbations. This performance surpassed OpenVLA-OFT by 4.8 points and the matched Joint-WAM by 4.0 points (70.4%). Against the matched Fast-WAM, LAWA demonstrated a 14.4 point improvement (74.4% vs. 60.0%). The gains were particularly notable under camera-viewpoint and sensor-noise shifts, with improvements of 44.3 and 27.5 points, respectively, over Fast-WAM. This highlights LAWA's enhanced generalization under distribution shifts.

#### 4.2. Comparison Between Paradigms

- **Effectiveness of Latent Action:** Inference-time perturbation studies on RoboCasa demonstrated the functional importance of the predicted latent action sequence. Introducing Gaussian noise to the latent-action state reduced full-data success from 80.8% to 52.2%, while temporal shuffling yielded 56.4%. This indicates that action generation relies on both the content and temporal structure of the latent sequence. Qualitative analysis of attention maps showed LAWA focusing more on manipulated objects and interaction regions, unlike Fast-WAM which distributed attention more broadly.
- **Effectiveness and Scalability of Egocentric Pre-training:** Action-free egocentric pre-training significantly benefited LAWA. In the few-shot and full-data settings, LAWA gained 5.9 and 4.5 points, respectively, from egocentric pre-training. Without it, LAWA trailed Joint-WAM. Fast-WAM and Joint-WAM also benefited but to a lesser degree (1.5/1.7 points and 1.0/0.5 points respectively). Scaling the egocentric video corpus from 10% to 100% led to further gains for LAWA (3.6 points in full-data, 4.0 points in few-shot), indicating its continued benefit as data diversity increases.
- **Performance–Latency Trade-off:** LAWA achieved a favorable balance between performance and inference latency. It required 338.5 ms per action chunk, which is 42.9% lower than Joint-WAM's 593.1 ms while achieving comparable success rates. While Fast-WAM was faster at 196.5 ms, this came with substantially lower success rates. LAWA thus demonstrated improved performance and generalization over Fast-WAM while maintaining Joint-WAM-level performance at reduced latency.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

Component ablation studies on RoboCasa confirmed the contributions of proposed elements:
- Introducing latent actions alone improved Fast-WAM by 5.2 (few-shot) and 1.7 (full-data) percentage points.
- Action-free egocentric pre-training provided additional gains of 5.1 (few-shot) and 3.0 (full-data) points.
- Regarding auxiliary losses for the tokenizer, mask prediction was found to be more effective than optical flow prediction. Mask prediction improved performance by 0.8 and 1.5 points (few-shot/full-data) relative to no auxiliary loss, whereas flow prediction degraded performance. The mask objective is interpreted as a manipulation-oriented inductive bias.

#### 4.4. Performance on Real-World Tasks

Real-world evaluations on a UFACTORY xArm7 across four tasks (fine-grained assembly: Gear, Battery; long-horizon manipulation: Block, Laboratory) demonstrated LAWA's superior performance over Fast-WAM:
- LAWA consistently outperformed Fast-WAM across all tasks and data settings (25%, 50%, 100% of demonstrations).
- With 25% of demonstrations (50 trajectories per task), LAWA achieved an average success rate of 40.0%, surpassing Fast-WAM's performance with the full training set (33.8%).
- On long-horizon tasks, LAWA demonstrated significant advantages, especially in few-shot settings. For instance, with 25% data, Fast-WAM recorded no successful trials on Block or Laboratory, while LAWA achieved 45.0% and 30.0% respectively.
- On fine-grained assembly tasks, LAWA also showed strong performance, outperforming Fast-WAM by 20-25 points on Gear and Battery, suggesting the utility of mask-supervised latent actions for precise operations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work introduces LAWA, a novel World Action Model (WAM) architecture that contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing the trade-off between performance, generalization, and inference efficiency in future-aware control systems.

The key significance and potential impact areas are:

1. **Efficient Future Imagination:** LAWA demonstrates that explicit future imagination is not necessarily detrimental to inference speed. By representing future intentions through compact latent actions rather than full observation-space video generation, LAWA retains the benefits of future awareness for action generation while significantly reducing computational latency (42.9% lower than Joint-WAM). This offers a practical pathway for deploying WAMs in real-time robotic control applications.
2. **Enhanced Generalization and Performance:** The architecture consistently improves performance and generalization over inference-optimized WAMs like Fast-WAM, especially in few-shot learning and out-of-distribution scenarios (e.g., 9.6 points higher in few-shot RoboCasa, 14.4 points higher in zero-shot LIBERO-Plus). This suggests that the compact latent action pathway provides a more robust and task-relevant signal for policy learning, leading to more capable robots.
3. **Scalable Learning from Action-Free Videos:** The developed mask-supervised latent action tokenizer, enhanced by scalable action-free pre-training on egocentric and robot videos, offers an effective method for leveraging vast amounts of unlabeled video data. The pre-training strategy, including motion-speed alignment and weighted rebalancing, enables the model to benefit from diverse data while maintaining robot-domain grounding, demonstrating scalability as the video corpus grows. This capability is crucial for developing generalist robotic policies without relying solely on expensive, labeled robot demonstrations.
4. **Practical Real-World Applicability:** The demonstrated superior performance on fine-grained assembly and long-horizon real-world manipulation tasks, even with limited demonstrations, indicates LAWA's potential for real-world deployment. Its ability to achieve high success rates with less data compared to baselines could accelerate the development and adoption of autonomous robotic systems.

In summary, LAWA establishes a WAM paradigm that effectively balances the need for future-aware control with practical inference efficiency and strong generalization. This approach could influence the design of future robotic control systems, facilitating more capable and deployable robots through efficient future imagination and scalable pre-training techniques.
