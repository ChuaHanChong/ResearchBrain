# To Retain or to Adapt? Generalizing Continual Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Lanzillotta et al. propose a re-evaluation of Continual Learning, shifting focus from mitigating catastrophic forgetting to optimizing real-time adaptation through the Average Lifelong Error (ALE). Their research introduces an analytical framework that decomposes learning performance into Instability and Transient Error, demonstrating that retaining all past knowledge is not always optimal and can hinder adaptation in non-stationary environments.

## Problem

- Continual Learning (CL) algorithms primarily focus on mitigating "catastrophic forgetting" by retaining past knowledge, often implicitly assuming the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) solution is universally ideal.
- This retention-centric approach can be detrimental for real-time adaptation in genuinely non-stationary environments where past data may become less relevant or even misleading for future tasks.
- A lack of a unified analytical framework to quantitatively assess the trade-offs between retaining historical knowledge and adapting to new tasks within CL.

## Method

- The research adopts the ==Average Lifelong Error (ALE)== objective, framing Continual Learning as an ==online optimization== problem aimed at minimizing real-time expected error under computational constraints.
- A novel decomposition of Continual Learning performance is introduced: ==Transfer Efficiency (TE)== is analyzed through ==Instability== (stationary bias from conflicting tasks) and ==Transient Error== (the cost of learning from a cold start).
- A **Predictive Continual Learning** framework is proposed, which generalizes CL objectives by allowing algorithms to explicitly model and optimize for expected future performance based on a dynamically updated model of future tasks.

## Results

- Empirical and theoretical evidence confirms that JTL can be suboptimal, even detrimental, in non-stationary environments (e.g., MD5, MT10, and synthetic CIFAR variants), sometimes significantly underperforming Independent-Task Learning (ITL).
- The analysis validates the existence of a "Critical Task Duration" (Nmax⁡∞N_{\max}^\inftyNmax∞), a threshold beyond which the initial warm-start advantage of JTL is surpassed by ITL's ability to specialize, particularly when average Instability is positive.
- The "Window Algorithm," a proof-of-concept for Predictive CL, consistently outperforms both JTL and ITL in controlled continuous drift environments, demonstrating that an adaptively sized memory window can yield superior real-time performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Joint-Task Learning (JTL) objective is not universally optimal; retaining all past knowledge can lead to suboptimal performance in truly non-stationary environments due to the inherent bias it introduces.
> - The environment's dynamics, particularly the "Growth Rate" of task optima, are critical in determining the asymptotic balance between the benefits of knowledge retention and the costs of adaptation.
> - Optimal Continual Learning strategies should dynamically adapt their memory horizon based on environmental non-stationarity, rather than maintaining a fixed retention policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Continual Learning (CL) has largely focused on developing algorithms that mitigate "catastrophic forgetting," a phenomenon where a neural network forgets previously learned tasks upon learning new ones. This objective implicitly assumes that an ideal lifelong learner should approximate the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) solution, which entails retaining and utilizing all past knowledge. This paper challenges this foundational premise by arguing that such a retention-centric approach can be detrimental to real-time adaptation in non-stationary environments.

The work builds upon the Average Lifelong Error (ALE) objective introduced by Kumar et al. (2025), which frames CL as an online optimization problem aimed at minimizing real-time expected error under computational constraints. This perspective suggests that forgetting can be a functional necessity for adaptation within finite resources. The present paper extends this information-theoretic framing by providing an optimization-time characterization of the trade-offs involved.

The research establishes connections between CL and several adjacent fields:
- **Online Learning and Stochastic Optimization:** The ALE metric is linked to concepts of static and dynamic regret. The paper leverages the framework of "predictable sequences" (Rakhlin & Sridharan, 2013) to propose a generalization of CL objectives, positioning JTL as a specific instance under an ergodicity hypothesis. The transient analysis draws from Lyapunov-type results in SGD literature (Murata, 1999; Bach & Moulines, 2013).
- **Multi-Task Learning (MTL):** The comparison between JTL and Independent-Task Learning (ITL) mirrors the core MTL question of whether joint training is beneficial. This paper interprets JTL as an online, cumulative version of MTL, where the task pool grows dynamically.
- **Learning to Learn and Online Meta-Learning:** The framework has ties to these areas, particularly the "Online-Within-Online" setting, where regret across sequences of tasks is considered. The paper's Instability bounds for JTL are noted to relate to asymptotic interference costs in Meta-Learning algorithms like MAML.
- **Continual Learning Literature:** This work complements prior linear-model CL analyses (Evron et al., 2022; Goldfarb et al., 2024) by focusing on real-time error (ALE) rather than retrospective forgetting. It aligns with the "Big World Hypothesis" (Javed & Sutton, 2024), which posits that CL is fundamentally about tracking rather than exhaustive memorization, a perspective that the ALE objective embodies.

By unifying these diverse perspectives, the paper aims to provide a common analytical toolkit and language for understanding real-time adaptation in dynamic environments, moving beyond the traditional forgetting-centric view of CL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are:

- **Challenging the Retention-Centric Premise of CL:** The paper's fundamental motivation is to scrutinize the pervasive, often unstated, assumption in Continual Learning that a lifelong learner should strive to approximate the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) solution by retaining all previously acquired knowledge. The authors contend that this premise, while suitable for stationary or ergodic environments, becomes problematic when environments are genuinely non-stationary and past data may become less relevant or even misleading for future tasks.
- **Shifting Focus to Real-Time Adaptation:** Instead of prioritizing knowledge retention and mitigating catastrophic forgetting as the sole objective, the research proposes a shift in focus to real-time adaptation, formalized through the Average Lifelong Error (ALE). The motivation here is to align CL objectives with the practical demands of systems operating in dynamic, real-world scenarios where current performance is paramount.
- **Quantifying the Utility of Memory:** A key motivation is to quantitatively assess when retaining historical knowledge transitions from a beneficial warm-start advantage to an optimization liability. This involves understanding the interplay between environmental dynamics (how tasks change) and learning dynamics (how algorithms adapt).
- **Decomposing Continual Learning Performance:** To achieve this quantification, the paper aims to introduce and formalize analytical tools—Transfer Efficiency, Instability, and Transient Error—that decompose the complex trade-offs inherent in continual learning. These measures are designed to separate the "quality of the final solution" from the "speed of learning."
- **Identifying the Critical Task Duration:** A specific objective is to derive a "Critical Task Duration"—a closed-form threshold—beyond which historical knowledge, particularly when it induces a positive stationary bias, becomes detrimental. This aims to provide concrete guidance on when to retain and when to adapt.
- **Generalizing Continual Learning Objectives:** The research is motivated by the observation that both JTL and Independent-Task Learning (ITL) represent limiting assumptions about future task structure. The paper seeks to generalize CL beyond this dichotomy by proposing a "Predictive Continual Learning" framework, where algorithms explicitly model and optimize for expected future performance, thereby opening a principled design space for adaptive strategies.
- **Providing a Unified Analytical Framework:** By connecting CL to online learning, multi-task learning, and stochastic optimization, a broader motivation is to bridge disciplinary gaps and offer a common language and analytical toolkit for studying real-time adaptation under non-stationary conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research integrates theoretical formalism, analytical derivations, and empirical validation across simulated and real-world benchmarks.

#### 3.1. Formalism and Objective Function

The work begins by adopting the Average Lifelong Error (ALE) objective from Kumar et al. (2025), which measures the real-time expected error of a learning agent over its lifetime in a dynamic environment. This objective is specialized to a supervised learning setting, where observations are batches of (input, label) pairs.
The environment is modeled as a task-based sequence, meaning the data-generating process is locally stationary within defined task intervals. The learning algorithm is treated as a discrete-time stochastic process, where parameter updates depend on the current parameters and a batch of data drawn from an algorithm-specific sampling distribution $D_n$.

#### 3.2. Algorithmic Baselines

Two core algorithms are defined as extreme cases for analysis:
- **Joint-Task Learning (A_JTL):** This agent aims to minimize the average loss over all tasks observed up to the current time. In practice, this is conceptualized as an experience replay mechanism with an infinitely large buffer, where the sampling distribution $D_k^{JTL}$ for task $k$ is a weighted mixture of current and all preceding task distributions. The parameters are not reset at task boundaries, allowing for knowledge transfer.
- **Independent-Task Learning (A_ITL):** This agent optimizes solely based on the current task's data stream ($D_k^{ITL} = P_k$). At each task boundary, the parameters are reset to a fixed initialization distribution, effectively discarding prior knowledge to prevent negative transfer.
Both algorithms are constructed to have equivalent computational costs per iteration, ensuring that performance differences are due to their learning strategies rather than compute budget.

#### 3.3. Transfer Efficiency Decomposition

The central analytical tool is **Transfer Efficiency (TE_k)**, defined as the performance gain of the JTL agent over the ITL baseline (TE_k = ALE_k(A_ITL) - ALE_k(A_JTL)). To understand the mechanisms driving TE, the Average Task Error (ALE_k) is decomposed into two components for task-wise convergent algorithms:
- **Instability (I_k):** The stationary performance gap between the JTL and ITL agents at convergence ($I_k = L_k(Q_{JTL}^\infty) - L_k(Q_{ITL}^\infty)$). It quantifies the inherent bias of the JTL solution due to optimizing over conflicting past tasks.
- **Transient Error (δ_k(A)):** The average excess loss accumulated by an algorithm during its mixing phase, as it converges from initialization to its stationary risk over the task duration.
This decomposition yields $TE_k = -I_k + (\delta_k(A_{ITL}) - \delta_k(A_{JTL}))$, separating the stationary bias from the learning speed advantage. Based on this, a **Critical Task Duration ($N_{\max}^\infty$)** is derived, indicating a threshold beyond which the initial advantage of transfer is lost when Instability is positive.

#### 3.4. Quadratic-Loss Analysis

To make the transfer mechanisms analytically transparent, the framework is applied to quadratic loss functions ($L_j(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta_j^*)^T H_j (\theta - \theta_j^*) + c_j$). Key assumptions include Hessian alignment (simultaneously diagonalizable) and bounded curvature.
- **Closed-form Expressions:** Exact derivations for Instability (decomposed into approximation bias and task diversity variance) and Transient Error (expressed as a drop in weighted potential energy defined by a "Relative Curvature Matrix") are provided.
- **Growth Rate:** A **Growth Rate (ψ(k))** is introduced to characterize how the environment expands, defining bounded ($\psi(k)=1$), diffusive ($\psi(k)=k$), and linear drift ($\psi(k)=k^2$) regimes for task optima.
- **Asymptotic Conditions:** Asymptotic conditions for positive transfer are derived, showing that the balance is governed by the Instability and the ITL Transient Error for large numbers of tasks.
- **Continuous Drift Model:** A specific instance using a Wiener-walk model for optimal parameters is analyzed, yielding a concrete closed-form for the Critical Task Duration.
- **Deep Linear Networks:** The quadratic analysis is extended to deep linear teacher-student models under Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) and Maximal Update (μP) parametrizations to investigate how optimization bias (due to parametrization) affects transfer, even for the same function class.

#### 3.5. Predictive Continual Learning

The paper proposes **Predictive Continual Learning**, a generalized framework where algorithms optimize expected future performance under an *explicit, dynamically updated model of future tasks* (Φ). JTL and ITL are shown to be specific instances of this framework.
- **Window Algorithm:** A proof-of-concept algorithm that predicts the future using a finite window of recent tasks (parameter $W$).
- **Mean Squared Displacement (MSD):** This measure characterizes the expected squared Euclidean distance between task solutions within a window, informing the optimal window size.
- **Optimal Window Size:** For the quadratic model, the optimal window size ($W^*$) is derived, showing dependence on the environment's non-stationarity regime (e.g., $W^* \rightarrow \infty$ for bounded, $W^*=1$ for linear drift, finite $W^*$ for diffusive).

#### 3.6. Empirical Validation

The theoretical predictions are validated on:
- **Simulated Environments:** Shallow and deep linear models for controlled analysis of Growth Rate, Critical Task Duration, and parametrization effects.
- **Continual Image Classification Benchmarks:** CLEAR (natural temporal shifts) and MD5 (sharp semantic shifts using diverse datasets), Permuted/Shuffled CIFAR-10 (controlled input/label corruption).
- **Reinforcement Learning Benchmark:** MT10 (a 10-task suite of robotic manipulation environments from Meta-World).
Experiments involve training ResNet-18 for supervised tasks and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) for RL tasks, with careful hyperparameter tuning and matched compute budgets between ITL and JTL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several findings that challenge existing assumptions in Continual Learning and propose new perspectives:

- **Challenging the JTL Premise:** The paper demonstrates, both empirically and theoretically, that the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) objective is not universally optimal for continual learners. While JTL can offer benefits in environments where past knowledge remains predictive (e.g., CLEAR benchmark), it can lead to suboptimal or detrimental performance in non-stationary settings characterized by significant distributional drift (e.g., MD5, MT10, and synthetic CIFAR variants). This indicates that the goal of retaining all knowledge may actively hinder real-time adaptation.

- **Validation of Transfer Efficiency Decomposition:** The proposed decomposition of Transfer Efficiency (TE) into Instability (stationary bias from conflicting tasks) and Transient Error (cost of learning from scratch) is empirically validated across a range of supervised learning and reinforcement learning benchmarks. The results show that TE accurately captures the trade-off, with the sign of TE tracking the inverse of Instability (positive TE when Instability is low/negative, negative TE when Instability is high/positive).

- **Identification of Critical Task Duration:** The theoretical framework predicts, and empirical results corroborate, the existence of a "Critical Task Duration" ($N_{\max}^\infty$). This is a closed-form threshold beyond which, if the average Instability is positive, the warm-start advantage of JTL is overcome by the ITL agent's ability to specialize to the current environment. As task duration $N$ increases, the ITL agent has more time to converge, reducing its transient error and potentially outperforming JTL despite a cold start.

- **Growth Rate Governs Asymptotic Transfer:** For quadratic objectives, the environment's "Growth Rate" (ψ(k)), which quantifies how fast task optima spread over time, is identified as a decisive factor for the asymptotic balance between JTL and ITL. In environments with higher growth rates (e.g., linear drift), JTL's stationary compromise becomes progressively worse on new tasks, leading to greater instability. The theoretical analysis shows that the JTL Instability scales with ψ(k), while its Transient Error is of a lower order.

- **Impact of Optimization Bias and Parametrization:** Experiments with deep linear networks demonstrate that the choice of model parametrization (e.g., NTK vs. μP) significantly influences the Transfer Efficiency landscape, even when the underlying function class is identical. This suggests that the optimizer's inductive bias, and not just the statistical properties of the data, plays a crucial role in shaping the trade-offs between retention and adaptation. The lazy (NTK) regime tends to exhibit higher instability than the rich (μP) regime.

- **Introduction of Predictive Continual Learning:** The paper proposes "Predictive Continual Learning" as a generalized framework that encompasses JTL and ITL as specific instances. This framework allows algorithms to explicitly optimize expected future performance under a dynamically updated model of future tasks, moving beyond fixed objectives.

- **Efficacy of the Window Algorithm:** As a proof of concept for Predictive CL, the "Window Algorithm" (which considers only a finite history of recent tasks) is shown to consistently outperform both JTL and ITL in controlled continuous drift environments. The optimal window size ($W^*$) varies depending on the environment's characteristics: $W^* \rightarrow \infty$ for bounded environments (recovering JTL), $W^* = 1$ for linear drift environments (recovering warm-start ITL), and a finite $W^*$ for diffusive environments. This demonstrates that adapting the memory horizon based on environmental dynamics can yield superior real-time performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Continual Learning (CL) by offering a conceptual re-evaluation and providing a robust analytical framework with potential practical implications.

- **Reconceptualization of Continual Learning:** The work fundamentally re-frames the core problem of CL from mitigating catastrophic forgetting to optimizing real-time adaptation, as measured by the Average Lifelong Error (ALE). This shift challenges the prevailing JTL-centric view, suggesting that forgetting is not always a failure but can be a necessary mechanism for efficient adaptation in truly non-stationary environments. This perspective could lead to a broader range of research questions and algorithmic designs in CL, moving beyond regularization- and replay-based methods primarily aimed at retaining all past knowledge.

- **Unified Analytical Framework and Language:** By drawing explicit connections to Online Learning, Stochastic Optimization, Multi-Task Learning, and Online Meta-Learning, the paper provides a cohesive analytical toolkit and a common language for discussing the trade-offs in CL. The introduction of **Transfer Efficiency, Instability, Transient Error, and Critical Task Duration** offers quantitative metrics and theoretical constructs that can be used to rigorously analyze and compare CL algorithms, moving beyond empirical benchmarks alone. This interdisciplinary approach can foster cross-pollination of ideas and accelerate theoretical progress in CL.

- **Guidance for Algorithm Design:** The insights derived from the decomposition of Transfer Efficiency and the analysis of Growth Rates provide concrete guidance for designing adaptive CL algorithms.
 - It suggests that memory strategies (e.g., replay buffer size, parameter regularization) should be dynamic and conditional on environmental non-stationarity rather than fixed a priori.
 - The concept of **Critical Task Duration** offers a theoretical basis for deciding when to "forget" or de-prioritize older knowledge.
 - The observation that optimization bias (due to model parametrization or optimizer choice) significantly impacts transfer profiles highlights the need for a joint consideration of data dynamics and learning dynamics in algorithm design.

- **Introduction of Predictive Continual Learning:** The **Predictive CL** framework is a significant contribution, transforming the space between JTL and ITL from an empirical gap into a principled design space. By making the "future model" an explicit component that can be learned and adapted, this framework opens up new avenues for developing more intelligent and adaptive CL systems. Algorithms could dynamically infer the most appropriate predictive target based on observed environmental statistics (e.g., using Mean Squared Displacement), rather than relying on static assumptions. The **Window Algorithm** serves as a compelling proof of concept, demonstrating how a simple adaptive memory strategy can outperform fixed-memory approaches.

- **Impact on Practical Applications:** For real-world systems operating in dynamic environments (e.g., autonomous agents, recommendation systems, climate modeling), the findings suggest that strategies focused on optimal real-time performance and adaptive forgetting might be more effective than those striving for perfect retention. This could lead to more robust and efficient lifelong learning agents that can truly adapt to evolving conditions.

While the conclusions are primarily situated within the Average Lifelong Error objective, the analytical framework remains applicable for understanding trade-offs under other objectives, providing a versatile tool for future CL research.
