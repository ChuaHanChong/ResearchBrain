# SceneStreamer: Continuous Scenario Generation as Next Token Group Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> SceneStreamer introduces a unified autoregressive Transformer framework that generates continuous, interactive driving scenarios by modeling entire dynamic scenes as a sequence of discrete tokens. Policies trained within these generated environments demonstrate improved robustness and generalization capabilities for autonomous driving planners.

## Problem

- Existing traffic simulators, like log-replay systems, lack interactivity, preventing background agents from dynamically responding to an ego vehicle's actions, which limits closed-loop AD policy evaluation.
- Many data-driven generative models for traffic scenarios rely on one-shot motion prediction or static initial states, leading to covariate shift and unrealistic outcomes when unrolled over long simulations.
- Prior methods often separate scenario generation into inefficient two-stage processes (initialization then motion), making it difficult to model dynamic agent populations and continuous agent entry/exit in an open-world traffic system.

## Method

- **SceneStreamer** represents the entire dynamic driving scene, including map, traffic lights, and heterogeneous agent states and motions, as a structured sequence of discrete tokens.
- An encoder-decoder Transformer autoregressively predicts the next token group, employing a novel token group attention mechanism with a structured causal mask to enforce semantic and temporal dependencies.
- A 'state-forcing' mechanism allows for continuous simulation with dynamic agent populations, enabling flexible scenario generation, motion prediction, and scene densification (injecting new agents) within a unified framework.

## Results

- SceneStreamer achieves competitive Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) scores for initial state realism, demonstrating its ability to generate realistic agent distributions and generalize across diverse agent types.
- Reinforcement learning policies trained in SceneStreamer-generated scenarios consistently outperform those trained on log-replay data, showing higher route completion rates, lower collision rates, and improved average rewards.
- The framework exhibits strong performance on the Waymo Sim Agents Challenge test set, achieving competitive realism and behavioral likelihood metrics for a general-purpose simulation environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A unified autoregressive tokenization approach, where agent states are generated relative to local map segments, ensures semantic and physical consistency over long generative sequences, preventing implausible scenarios.
> - Structured generation order, including autoregressive prediction of agent attributes (type, map segment ID, relative state dimensions), is critical for maintaining realism and avoiding invalid configurations.
> - Training autonomous driving planners in diverse, adaptively generated interactive scenarios, rather than static log-replay data, significantly enhances their robustness and generalization capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development and evaluation of autonomous driving (AD) systems critically rely on realistic and interactive traffic simulation. Existing approaches to traffic simulation can be broadly categorized.

One common category involves replaying logged trajectories from real-world datasets, as seen in frameworks like CARLA and Waymax. While these methods are faithful to observed driving behaviors, they inherently lack interactivity; background agents do not dynamically respond to the actions of an ego vehicle, thereby limiting their utility for closed-loop evaluation of AD policies. This limitation can lead to unrealistic interactions, such as rear-end collisions, if the ego vehicle deviates from its recorded path.

Another significant line of research focuses on data-driven generative models that learn to synthesize traffic scenarios from real data. These models often frame scenario generation as a motion prediction problem, where an agent's future trajectory is predicted given its history, map context, and signals. However, many such models are designed for one-shot prediction, meaning they predict a full trajectory without intermediate interaction updates. When unrolled in a simulation, small prediction errors can compound, leading to "covariate shift" where the simulator enters out-of-distribution states, resulting in unrealistic outcomes. Autoregressive models have been proposed to mitigate this by predicting motion step-by-step, but they often still rely on provided initial states, limiting the diversity of initial traffic layouts and preventing new agents from entering the scene. Some works decouple scenario generation into two stages: first generating initial conditions (e.g., agent positions and types) and then performing motion prediction. This separation can be inefficient, inflexible, and typically fixes the number of agents from the outset, failing to model the open nature of real traffic where participants continuously enter and leave.

Furthermore, procedural generation methods use hand-coded rules or templates, which can restrict realism and diversity. Diffusion-based generative models are also emerging for scene-level generation, but some of these approaches either separate static and dynamic generation phases or produce entire scenes in a single forward pass, which can limit real-time interactivity and the ability to dynamically modify scenes mid-simulation.

SceneStreamer addresses these identified limitations by proposing a unified, autoregressive framework that treats the entire dynamic driving scene as a sequence of tokens. This approach moves beyond static initialization, log-replay, and rigid two-stage generation, enabling continuous scenario generation with dynamic agent populations and flexible scene editing. It differentiates from other unified generative simulators like UniGen by maintaining temporal causality in its generation order and using map-anchored token groups rather than ego-centric representations or relying on a logged seed, allowing for direct generation from map tokens and more adaptable mid-simulation scene manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-driven simulation framework capable of generating realistic, diverse, and interactive traffic scenarios over unbounded horizons. This overarching goal is motivated by several key limitations in existing traffic simulation methods for autonomous driving (AD) systems:

1. **Modeling Dynamic and Evolving Agent Populations:** Current data-driven simulation methods frequently rely on static scenario initialization or replaying pre-recorded data. This approach inherently restricts the simulation's ability to model dynamic, long-horizon scenarios where agents continuously enter (e.g., vehicles turning from a side street) and exit the scene. A core motivation is to enable a simulation environment that supports the continuous introduction and retirement of agents, reflecting the open-system nature of real-world traffic.

2. **Enhancing Interactivity for Closed-Loop Evaluation:** Log-replay based simulations, while data-faithful, lack interactivity. Background agents do not respond to the actions of the ego vehicle (or self-driving car, SDC). This absence of reactivity limits their effectiveness for closed-loop evaluation, which is critical for thoroughly testing and validating AD policies where the SDC's actions influence the behavior of other traffic participants. The work aims to facilitate reactive traffic behaviors that adapt to the SDC's actions.

3. **Mitigating Covariate Shift in Autoregressive Models:** While autoregressive models have improved motion prediction, many are trained as one-shot predictors or rely on fixed initial states. When unrolled over long simulation horizons, small prediction errors can accumulate, leading the simulator to generate out-of-distribution states and unrealistic outcomes, a phenomenon known as covariate shift. The motivation is to design a model that maintains semantic and physical consistency over long generative sequences.

4. **Achieving Unified and Flexible Scenario Generation:** Prior methods often separate the generation of initial scene conditions from the prediction of agent motions, leading to a two-stage process. This separation can be inefficient, inflexible, and prevents the model from sharing context between the initialization and motion prediction phases. A key objective is to create a unified framework that can jointly generate agent states (position, type, shape) and their subsequent motions within a single autoregressive model, ensuring consistent conditioning and flexibility.

5. **Supporting Versatile Simulation Tasks:** The authors aim for a framework that is versatile enough to support various downstream applications beyond simple motion prediction. This includes full-scenario generation from scratch, scenario densification (injecting new agents into an existing scene), and closed-loop simulation for training AD planners.

6. **Improving Downstream Planner Performance:** Ultimately, the motivation is to demonstrate that scenarios generated by this framework can serve as an effective training environment for reinforcement learning (RL) policies. The goal is to show that policies trained in SceneStreamer-generated scenarios exhibit superior robustness and generalization capabilities compared to those trained solely on log-replay data, thereby validating the framework's utility as a high-fidelity simulation environment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SceneStreamer proposes a unified autoregressive framework that casts continuous scenario generation as a next-token prediction task. The entire dynamic driving scene, including static map information, traffic light signals, agent states, and motion vectors, is represented as a structured sequence of discrete tokens generated step-by-step by a Transformer model.

#### 3.1. Scenario as a Token Sequence

The core idea involves constructing a single autoregressive token sequence `x_1:T` composed of static map tokens followed at each timestep by traffic light tokens, agent state tokens, and agent motion tokens. The Transformer model, an encoder-decoder architecture, predicts the next token or token group `pθ(x_t | x_<t)` based on all previously generated tokens `x_<t`.

##### 3.1.1. Map Tokens (<MAP>)

Static map context (e.g., lane segments, crosswalks) is preprocessed into polylines. A PointNet-like encoder extracts features for each map segment. These features are then passed through the SceneStreamer Encoder, which produces map tokens. Each map token is enriched with a unique discrete Map ID embedding and geometric information (center position, heading) to support relative attention. These map tokens remain fixed throughout the simulation, serving as static cross-attention keys/values for the decoder.

##### 3.1.2. Traffic Light Tokens (<TL>)

Each traffic light is represented by a single token per step. This token encodes its discrete state (green, yellow, red, unknown), a unique identifier, and the Map ID of the segment it resides in. Similar to map tokens, its temporal-geometric context (position, orientation, timestep) is incorporated for relative attention. All traffic light tokens for a given step are generated in a single batch via a dedicated MLP head performing a 4-way classification.

##### 3.1.3. Agent State Tokens (<AS>)

For every active agent, including those newly injected, SceneStreamer uses an ordered sequence of four tokens to encode its dynamic and semantic state:
- **<SOA> (Start-of-Agent):** A flag indicating the beginning of an agent's state description.
- **<TYPE>:** A categorical token identifying the agent's type (vehicle, pedestrian, cyclist).
- **<MS> (Map Segment ID):** The index of the map segment where the agent is located.
- **<RS> (Relative State):** An 8-dimensional vector representing the agent's dimensions (length, width, height), longitudinal and lateral offset from the centerline of its assigned map segment, heading residual relative to the segment's orientation, and velocity components in the map segment's frame. Each dimension of the relative state is discretized into 81 uniform bins.

The generation of agent state tokens is autoregressive. The model first samples the agent type from a distribution predicted by an agent type head. Based on the sampled type, it selects a map segment ID from candidates using a map ID prediction head. Finally, conditioned on the decoder output for the Map ID token, a specialized "Relative State Head" (a small Transformer decoder with AdaLN) autoregressively generates the 8 relative state dimensions sequentially. This structured, causal generation process is designed to ensure semantic and physical consistency (e.g., placing a pedestrian on a sidewalk lane).

For agents that persist from the previous timestep, SceneStreamer employs "state-forcing." Instead of generating new state tokens, it reconstructs their agent state tokens from their observed (or previously predicted) global state, identifying the closest map segment and computing relative states. This mechanism unifies dynamic agent injection (via sampling) and agent motion continuation (via state-forcing), enabling continuous simulation with variable agent sets.

##### 3.1.4. Motion Tokens (<MO>)

To model agent motion, SceneStreamer predicts an instantaneous control input for each agent, parameterized as a pair of acceleration (a) and yaw rate (ω). Both `a` and `ω` are uniformly quantized into 33 bins, resulting in 1,089 discrete motion classes. A first-order bicycle model is used to update the agent's state based on the selected `(a, ω)` pair over a 0.5s timestep.
The ground-truth motion token is determined by enumerating all candidate `(a, ω)` pairs and selecting the one that minimizes the Average Corner Error (ACE) between the predicted and ground-truth 2D bounding boxes at the next timestep.
Each motion token is an embedding composed of the motion label, agent type, agent ID, velocity, shape, and temporal-geometric information. A special `μ_start` label is used for an agent's first appearance. A motion head (linear classifier) predicts the probability distribution over these motion labels, and nucleus sampling is used during inference.

#### 3.2. Model Architecture and Attention

SceneStreamer utilizes an encoder-decoder Transformer. The encoder processes map segments into static map tokens. The decoder autoregressively generates dynamic tokens (TL, AS, MO) layer by layer. Each decoder layer combines:
- **Cross-attention** between dynamic tokens and static map tokens to incorporate geometric context.
- **Self-attention** over dynamic tokens, governed by a custom **token group attention mechanism**. This mechanism uses a structured causal mask (Figure 2B) to enforce semantic and temporal dependencies. Tokens within the same group can attend freely, an object's later tokens attend to its earlier tokens, and any token group can attend to relevant preceding contexts (e.g., motion tokens attend to current traffic light and agent state tokens).
- **Relative Attention:** Attention weights are modulated by biases computed from the spatial and temporal relations (∆x, ∆y, ∆ψ, ∆t) between query and key tokens, providing spatial-temporal awareness. A K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) mask is applied to spatial neighbors for scalability, reducing attention cost.

#### 3.3. Training

The model is trained end-to-end using cross-entropy loss for all prediction heads. Training is conducted in two stages: pretraining (traffic light and motion tokens) and finetuning (all dynamic tokens, including agent states).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates SceneStreamer across multiple tasks to assess the quality of its generated scenarios and its utility for downstream applications, particularly autonomous driving (AD) planner training.

#### 4.1. Initial State Quality

The realism of SceneStreamer-generated initial states is assessed using the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) metric, comparing generated agent distributions to real-world logged data.
- **Competitive Performance:** Under a strict evaluation protocol (vehicles within 50m of the ego vehicle), SceneStreamer achieves competitive MMD scores across position, heading, size, and velocity, particularly when employing its autoregressive (AR) decoding scheme.
- **Generalization to Diverse Agent Types:** Under a relaxed protocol that includes all agent types (vehicles, pedestrians, cyclists), SceneStreamer continues to produce realistic agents, demonstrating its generalization capabilities beyond just vehicles.
- **Importance of AR Decoding:** An ablation study showed that disabling AR decoding (i.e., predicting all agent attributes independently in parallel) leads to a notable drop in performance. This indicates that the ordered, causal token generation within SceneStreamer is crucial for producing semantically and physically consistent agent states and avoids invalid combinations.

#### 4.2. Motion Prediction Quality

SceneStreamer's capability as a motion predictor is evaluated by autoregressively forecasting future trajectories for 8 seconds, given initial traffic states and agent histories. Two versions were compared: SceneStreamer-Motion (trained only on motion/traffic light tokens) and SceneStreamer-Full (trained on all dynamic tokens).
- **Accuracy vs. Diversity Trade-off:** SceneStreamer-Motion shows higher accuracy with lower Average Displacement Error (ADE) and Final Displacement Error (FDE) metrics. SceneStreamer-Full, while slightly less accurate, exhibits higher Average Displacement Diversity (ADD) and Final Displacement Diversity (FDD), suggesting it generates a wider range of behaviors. This difference is hypothesized to be partly due to the increased complexity and attention demands of jointly predicting agent states.
- **Reasonable Performance:** Both versions achieve reasonable motion prediction performance on the Waymo validation set, confirming their ability to generate coherent agent movements.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Visualization

Qualitative examples demonstrate SceneStreamer's ability to generate plausible and diverse scenarios in different modes:
- **Motion Prediction:** Generated trajectories align with map lanes and exhibit coherent movements.
- **Full-Scenario Generation:** Scenes are populated with agents whose initial states and subsequent motions appear realistic and contextually appropriate.
- **Scenario Densification:** New agents are injected into existing scenes, displaying realistic behaviors such as jaywalking, U-turns, and queueing, indicating the model's capacity for adaptive scene editing. While common failure cases like overspeeding, collisions, and signal violations are observed in some generated scenarios, the overall behavior demonstrates realism.

#### 4.4. Planner Learning with SceneStreamer

The utility of SceneStreamer as a simulation environment for training autonomous driving (AD) planners is evaluated by training reinforcement learning (RL) agents. Policies are trained in SceneStreamer-modified scenarios and tested on unaltered log-replay scenes.
- **Improved Planner Performance:** RL policies trained with SceneStreamer-generated scenarios consistently outperform policies trained on unmodified log-replay traffic across all evaluated metrics: Average Episodic Reward, Episode Success Rate, Route Completion Rate, Off-Road Rate, Collision Rate, and Average Cost.
- **Benefits of Adaptive Training:** Adaptive training, where the Self-Driving Car (SDC) policy's own rollout informs SceneStreamer's background agent generation, further enhances robustness and reward for the RL planner.
- **Superior Robustness and Generalization:** The best-performing setup, involving full scenario generation with reject sampling for new agents, achieves the highest route completion and lowest cost. This indicates that SceneStreamer serves as an effective, high-fidelity simulation platform that fosters more robust and generalizable AD policies.

#### 4.5. WOSAC Results

On the 2025 Waymo Sim Agents Challenge test set, SceneStreamer demonstrates competitive realism and behavioral likelihood metrics when compared to strong baselines like UniMM and CAT-K. Although it does not achieve the lowest minADE (minimum Average Displacement Error), its strong performance across other realism metrics validates its efficacy as a general-purpose simulator for evaluating agent behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SceneStreamer represents a notable advancement in data-driven traffic simulation, addressing several fundamental limitations of existing methods and offering significant potential impact for the development and deployment of autonomous driving (AD) systems.

#### 5.1. Technical Significance

- **Unified and Continuous Generation:** The framework introduces a unified autoregressive approach that models an entire driving scene—including static map elements, dynamic traffic lights, heterogeneous agent states, and their motions—as a single sequence of discrete tokens. This unifies tasks previously handled by disparate models, leading to more coherent and contextually aware scenario generation.
- **Dynamic Agent Populations:** Unlike prior methods constrained by static initialization or fixed agent sets, SceneStreamer's token-based design enables the continuous introduction and retirement of agents over unbounded horizons. This capability is critical for simulating realistic, long-duration traffic flows characteristic of real-world open systems.
- **Novel Agent State Tokenization:** The method employs a structured, autoregressive scheme for generating agent states (type, map segment, relative state attributes). By tokenizing states relative to local map segments and generating them in a causally constrained sequence, SceneStreamer ensures semantic and physical consistency, mitigating the generation of implausible agent configurations.
- **Versatility through State-Forcing:** The mechanism of "state-forcing" allows SceneStreamer to adapt dynamically to various tasks, including motion prediction, full scenario generation, and scene densification (injecting new agents), without requiring model modifications. This flexibility enhances its utility across different simulation needs.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact for Autonomous Driving

- **Improved AD Planner Training and Evaluation:** A core contribution is the empirical demonstration that reinforcement learning (RL) policies trained in SceneStreamer-generated scenarios achieve superior robustness and generalization compared to those trained on traditional log-replay traffic. This positions SceneStreamer as a high-fidelity simulation environment capable of generating diverse, reactive traffic that challenges and refines AD policies, leading to safer and more capable self-driving cars.
- **Enhanced Safety-Critical Scenario Generation:** The ability to generate complex, dynamic, and diverse scenarios can accelerate the identification and mitigation of corner cases and safety-critical situations, which are difficult and costly to encounter in real-world testing.
- **Data Augmentation:** SceneStreamer can serve as a powerful tool for data augmentation, providing virtually limitless synthetic data to train AD systems, thereby reducing reliance on expensive and time-consuming real-world data collection.
- **Closed-Loop Simulation:** By enabling background agents to react to the ego vehicle's actions, SceneStreamer facilitates true closed-loop simulation, essential for validating the interactive behavior of AD systems.

#### 5.3. Broader Scientific Impact

- **Advancement in Generative Models for Dynamic Environments:** The work contributes to the broader field of generative modeling by demonstrating an effective token-based autoregressive approach for complex, dynamic, multi-agent systems. The insights into structured tokenization and attention mechanisms could be transferable to other domains requiring the generation of evolving, interactive sequences.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** SceneStreamer's modular design and open-source nature (as indicated by the abstract) can serve as a foundation for future research, enabling the community to explore advancements in agent behavior modeling, reactive simulation, and the integration of generative models with AD development pipelines.
