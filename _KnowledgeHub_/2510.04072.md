# Slow-Fast Policy Optimization: Reposition-Before-Update for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Slow-Fast Policy Optimization (SFPO) framework, developed by researchers from Georgia Institute of Technology, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, and NVIDIA, introduces a three-stage update mechanism for on-policy reinforcement learning that stabilizes gradient estimates and reuses rollout data more effectively in LLM reasoning tasks. It achieves an average accuracy improvement of up to 2.80 points on math reasoning benchmarks and reduces required rollouts by up to 4.93 times, resulting in up to 4.19 times faster training compared to vanilla GRPO.

## Problem

- Existing on-policy RL methods for LLMs, like GRPO, suffer from high-variance gradient estimates due to low-quality initial rollouts, leading to unstable model updates.
- These methods inefficiently use rollout data by performing only a single optimization step per batch, underutilizing valuable gradient information.
- Naive off-policy reuse of rollout data can lead to performance degradation due to distribution mismatch between the policy generating samples and the updated policy.

## Method

- **SFPO** introduces a three-stage update mechanism: a '==Fast Trajectory==' of K inner updates on the same rollout batch to stabilize gradient estimates.
- A 'Reposition' stage then interpolates the fast trajectory's endpoint back towards the starting point using parameter alpha (α) to mitigate off-policy drift.
- A final 'Slow Correction' step applies an additional gradient update at the repositioned point for further refinement, with an adaptive schedule for α based on policy entropy to balance exploitation and stability.

## Results

- SFPO achieved an average accuracy improvement of up to 2.80 points on math reasoning benchmarks, with specific gains like +7.5 on AIME25 for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-**1.5B**.
- The method required significantly fewer rollouts to achieve target performance, needing 3.21× to 4.93× fewer rollouts compared to GRPO.
- SFPO demonstrated substantial wall-clock time reductions, achieving 2.62× to 4.19× faster training, without introducing additional GPU memory overhead.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Performing multiple inner gradient updates on a single rollout batch can effectively damp idiosyncratic fluctuations and produce a more stable update direction.
> - Interpolating the updated parameters back towards the original policy (repositioning) acts as a regularizer, preventing off-policy drift and maintaining training stability, especially when reusing data.
> - An adaptive scheduling of the interpolation parameter α, informed by policy entropy, allows for aggressive exploitation early in training while prioritizing stability near convergence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to notable advancements in complex multi-step reasoning tasks, including mathematical problem-solving and scientific question answering. A significant factor contributing to these advancements is the application of reinforcement learning (RL) techniques for fine-tuning LLMs, utilizing reward signals to enhance semantic correctness and solution quality.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is an example of an on-policy policy gradient method that has been widely adopted in large-scale LLM training pipelines. Works such as DeepSeekMath (Shao et al., 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) have demonstrated that GRPO can substantially improve LLMs in mathematical reasoning and induce emergent reasoning behaviors.

However, existing on-policy RL algorithms like GRPO exhibit structural inefficiencies, particularly during early training stages. When initial rollouts are of low quality or uninformative, stochastic rewards can lead to high-variance gradient estimates, destabilizing model updates. This issue is compounded by the common practice in GRPO implementations of performing only a single optimization step per rollout batch. This "one-shot" update often yields a noisy and less reliable step direction, underutilizing the information available within each batch. While previous work (Shao et al., 2024) explored reusing rollout data, simply applying off-policy updates has been observed to degrade performance as training progresses due to distribution mismatch, leaving the effective use of reused data for sample efficiency an open challenge.

The broader landscape includes research focused on stabilizing and simplifying GRPO, such as DAPO (Yu et al., 2025b), which addresses entropy collapse, and Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025), which removes normalization terms. Other complementary lines of work aim to improve LLM training efficiency through data filtering and selection (Ivison et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025). Some works (Mroueh, 2025; Mroueh et al., 2025) have also analyzed off-policy policy gradient operations under verifiable rewards and variants of GRPO.

This work, Slow-Fast Policy Optimization (SFPO), positions itself as an enhancement to on-policy policy-gradient methods like GRPO. Unlike methods that modify the objective function, add regularization, or select data, SFPO operates by restructuring the update mechanism itself. It is presented as an orthogonal and plug-compatible solution designed to stabilize noisy updates and improve sample efficiency by decomposing each training step into a structured three-stage trajectory, without altering the underlying objective, rollout generation, or regularization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and stable policy optimization framework for enhancing Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning, specifically addressing the limitations observed in existing on-policy policy-gradient methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

The motivation stems from several identified drawbacks of current approaches:
1. **Instability from high-variance gradients:** In the early phases of RL training for LLMs, the quality of generated rollouts is often low. The stochastic rewards derived from these weak rollouts lead to high-variance gradient estimates. These noisy gradients can cause unstable updates, making the training process fragile and difficult to converge effectively.
2. **Inefficient use of rollout data:** Standard GRPO implementations typically perform only a single optimization step per batch of rollouts. This "one-shot" update strategy is considered inefficient as it discards potentially valuable gradient information that could be further exploited through additional updates on the same data. This underutilization of data contributes to lower sample efficiency.
3. **Degradation from naive off-policy updates:** While reusing rollout data for multiple updates could improve sample efficiency, simply applying such off-policy updates to the policy model can lead to performance degradation. This is due to the inherent distribution mismatch between the policy generating the rollouts and the policy being updated multiple times, which can bias gradient estimates and destabilize training.
4. **Need for improved convergence and reduced cost:** The fragility and inefficiency of current methods translate into longer training times and higher computational costs. There is a need for an update mechanism that can accelerate convergence and reduce the number of required rollouts and overall wall-clock time without introducing significant engineering overhead or memory costs.

To address these motivations, the authors propose SFPO, aiming to transform noisy, one-shot updates into structured trajectories. This is intended to yield more stable optimization, higher sample efficiency, and faster convergence in LLM reasoning tasks, while maintaining compatibility with existing policy-gradient pipelines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Slow-Fast Policy Optimization (SFPO) is introduced as a three-stage update mechanism designed to improve the stability and sample efficiency of on-policy policy gradient algorithms like GRPO. The core idea is to reuse rollout data more effectively by structuring each training iteration into a "fast-reposition-slow" decomposition. The methodology ensures plug-compatibility by not altering the base loss, rollout collection, or KL/clip regularization of the underlying on-policy method.

The three stages of SFPO are:

#### 3.1. Stage I: Fast Trajectory

This stage addresses the instability caused by single, high-variance stochastic gradient steps.
- **Problem:** In standard on-policy methods, a single gradient step (θ_{s+1} = θ_s - η∇L(θ_s)) per iteration, estimated from one batch of rollouts, often leads to unstable updates due to noisy gradient estimates, especially early in training.
- **Approach:** SFPO performs K multiple inner updates on the *same* batch of rollouts. Starting from the current parameters θ_{s,0}, a sequence of parameters θ_{s,0} → θ_{s,1} →... → θ_{s,K} is generated, where each step refines the gradient direction: θ_{s,k+1} = θ_{s,k} - η∇L(θ_{s,k}).
- **Intuition:** The cumulative effect of these K sequential corrections helps to damp idiosyncratic fluctuations and align with the underlying gradient direction. This can be viewed as integrating the local gradient field, producing a more stable update direction at the end of Stage I. Mathematically, for a local quadratic approximation of the loss, the displacement (θ_K - θ_0) acts as a curvature-aware low-pass filter, stabilizing the direction compared to a single step.

#### 3.2. Stage II: Reposition

This stage mitigates the off-policy drift introduced by reusing rollouts in Stage I.
- **Problem:** After K inner updates using rollouts generated at θ_{s,0}, the endpoint θ_{s,K} may significantly diverge from the distribution that produced those samples. This distribution mismatch is a known issue in off-policy learning and can lead to biased gradient estimates and training instability.
- **Approach:** Inspired by Lookahead Optimization, SFPO introduces a reposition step that interpolates the fast trajectory's endpoint back toward its starting point: e_θ_{s,K} = θ_{s,0} + α(θ_{s,K} - θ_{s,0}), where α ∈.
- **Intuition:** The parameter α controls the degree of off-policy drift. Smaller α keeps the update closer to the original on-policy iterate (θ_{s,0}), while larger α relies more on the fast trajectory, potentially risking greater mismatch. This interpolation can be interpreted as solving a linearized proximal subproblem, where α acts as an implicit trust-region radius, enforcing stronger contraction toward the on-policy point for smaller α values.

#### 3.3. Stage III: Slow Correction

This final stage refines the update direction with an additional gradient step.
- **Approach:** After the repositioning, one more gradient step (the "slow" correction) is applied at the interpolated point e_θ_{s,K}: θ_{s+1} = e_θ_{s,K} - η∇L(e_θ_{s,K}).
- **Intuition:** This creates a predictor-corrector structure. Stage I provides a stabilized trajectory, Stage II controls off-policy drift, and Stage III applies a final correction aligned with the local curvature at the refined update point. This step is intended to mitigate overshooting and preserve progress along the stabilized trajectory. Theoretical intuition suggests that the descent is bounded, and the factor F(K, α) represents the combined effect of Stage I and II, balancing exploitation of gradient information with control over distributional drift.

#### 3.4. Scheduling α

An adaptive schedule for the interpolation parameter α is employed.
- **Motivation:** A non-zero α is beneficial early in training to exploit the stabilized fast trajectory when gradient signals are strong. However, near convergence, where curvature and stochastic noise dominate, a large α can amplify drift and instability.
- **Approach:** α is scheduled online. Policy entropy (H_s) is computed at each iteration, and a rolling buffer of past entropy values is maintained. A one-sided z-score (Z_s = (H_s - μ_s) / σ_s) is calculated. If |Z_s| exceeds a threshold τ, a "trigger index" s* is set, and α is subsequently set to 0 for all future iterations. Otherwise, α remains at its initial value α_0.
- **Intuition:** Sharp entropy fluctuations are interpreted as the policy being close to a local optimum where noise is dominant. In such cases, interpolation is disabled by setting α=0, effectively reverting to a more standard on-policy update for stability. This focuses exploitation in early, high-signal phases and prioritizes stability near convergence. The rationale for adapting α over K is that α is a soft trust parameter, while K directly impacts computational runtime.

#### 3.5. Unified SFPO Update

The three stages combine into a single update rule: θ_{s+1} = θ_{s,0} - η [α Σ_{k=0}^{K-1} ∇_θ L(θ_{s,k}) + ∇_θ L(e_θ_{s,K})]. This unified update is designed as a plug-compatible replacement for existing on-policy policy-gradient methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Slow-Fast Policy Optimization (SFPO) was evaluated through extensive experiments using various LLMs and mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with comparisons against vanilla GRPO.

#### 4.1. Math Reasoning Benchmarks

SFPO consistently outperformed vanilla GRPO across all tested base models and benchmarks.
- **Overall Performance:** SFPO achieved an average accuracy improvement of up to 2.80 points on math reasoning benchmarks.
- **Small-scale Models:** For Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, SFPO raised average accuracy from 38.35 to 40.19 (a +1.84 gain). For DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, it increased from 47.73 to 50.53 (a +2.80 gain), with a notable +7.5 absolute gain on the challenging AIME25 benchmark.
- **Larger Models:** Qwen2.5-Math-7B saw average accuracy rise from 48.36 to 49.19 (+0.83). DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B improved from 60.47 to 63.04 (+2.57). Qwen3-4B-Base gained +1.60 (from 43.99 to 45.59).
- **Dataset Robustness:** SFPO also demonstrated superior performance on the larger Skywork-OR1 Math RL training dataset, further indicating its robustness across different training data scales and distributions (e.g., for DS-Qwen-1.5B, AIME24/25 improved from 32.92/25.83 to 34.17/27.50).

#### 4.2. Training Dynamics

Comparisons of training behaviors revealed distinct advantages for SFPO.
- **Validation Accuracy:** SFPO consistently achieved faster convergence and sustained higher global accuracy throughout training. For instance, Qwen3-4B-Base with SFPO reached a higher stabilized accuracy within 150 steps, which GRPO could not match even after 400 steps.
- **Response Length:** SFPO exhibited better control over response length, converging to a stable range of approximately 2700 tokens for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, while GRPO sometimes collapsed to overly short responses. This suggests SFPO avoids both "overthinking" and insufficient reasoning.
- **Entropy Loss:** SFPO resulted in lower entropy loss compared to GRPO. This was interpreted as SFPO enabling more efficient exploration by quickly eliminating unproductive search paths, rather than suppressing exploration.
- **Reward Score:** SFPO achieved higher and more stable reward scores throughout training, indicating better alignment with the reward function and more robust convergence.

#### 4.3. Efficiency Analysis

SFPO demonstrated significant efficiency gains over GRPO.
- **Rollout Efficiency:** SFPO required substantially fewer rollouts to achieve the same best accuracy as GRPO. Specifically, it needed 3.21×, 3.50×, and 4.93× fewer rollouts for DS-Qwen-1.5B, Qwen3-4B-Base, and DS-Qwen-7B, respectively.
- **Wall-Clock Time Reduction:** This translated directly into reduced training time, with SFPO achieving speedups of 2.62×, 2.65×, and 4.19× for the same models.
- **GPU Memory:** SFPO introduced no additional GPU memory overhead, as it does not require storing heavy optimizer states. The per-step computational overhead for SFPO (with K=3) was measured to be approximately 1.37× that of GRPO, primarily due to additional backward passes. However, the significantly reduced number of steps required for convergence led to overall wall-clock efficiency improvements of 3–6.6×.

#### 4.4. Analysis and Ablation Study

The study also included ablations to understand the impact of SFPO's components and hyperparameters.
- **Impact of α and K:**
 - For small K (e.g., K=3), SFPO remained stable across various α values and consistently outperformed GRPO, confirming that multiple inner updates with repositioning reduce gradient noise.
 - For large K (e.g., K=7), larger α values amplified off-policy drift, leading to performance degradation. A smaller α was required to mitigate this drift and restore stability, highlighting the interaction between K and α.
- **Interpolation (Stage II) against Off-Policy Overfitting:** Without interpolation (α=1), performance initially increased but then declined as training progressed, indicating overfitting to small batches of rollouts and amplified noise. Interpolation (α < 1) served as a regularizer, preventing distributional drift and stabilizing training. Explicit comparisons showed interpolation offered better performance and stability than a KL-divergence penalty.
- **Importance of Stage III (Slow Correction):** Incorporating the slow correction consistently improved stability and accuracy, providing a curvature-aware adjustment that realigned updates with the correct optimization trajectory.
- **Necessity of Entropy Control:** Removing the adaptive entropy control (EC) led to a noticeable accuracy drop and rapid divergence of entropy loss, indicating instability. This suggests EC is crucial for maintaining SFPO's stability and reliability.
- **Strategies for Decaying α:** Both a direct reset of α to zero and a gradual linear decay after the entropy trigger yielded similar performance, leading to the adoption of the simpler direct reset for efficiency.
- **Generalizability to Coding Tasks:** SFPO demonstrated consistent improvements on the LiveCodeBench coding benchmark, achieving higher peak performance than GRPO, suggesting its efficacy extends beyond mathematical reasoning.
- **Orthogonality to DAPO:** When applied on top of DAPO, SFPO continued to provide benefits in accuracy and convergence speed, indicating its gains are not solely from addressing zero-advantage batches and that it can be integrated with other GRPO-family variants.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Slow-Fast Policy Optimization (SFPO) framework introduces a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for large language models, particularly in the domain of complex reasoning tasks.

**Key Contributions:**
- **Enhanced Training Stability:** SFPO addresses the inherent instability of one-shot policy updates, especially during the early phases of training when rollouts are noisy. By decomposing each update into a structured fast-reposition-slow trajectory, it effectively stabilizes gradient directions, leading to more robust learning.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency:** The method significantly reduces the number of rollouts required to achieve target performance levels. This is a crucial advantage, as rollout generation is often the most computationally expensive part of RL training for LLMs.
- **Accelerated Convergence:** SFPO achieves faster convergence and higher sustained accuracy across various models and reasoning benchmarks, directly translating into reduced wall-clock training time.
- **Plug-Compatible Design:** A notable aspect of SFPO is its ability to integrate seamlessly with existing on-policy policy-gradient pipelines, such as GRPO, without requiring modifications to the underlying objective function, rollout generation process, or regularization schemes.

**Practical Implications:**
- **Reduced Computational Cost:** The substantial reduction in required rollouts and overall training time (up to 4.19× speedup) implies significantly lower computational resources and energy consumption for developing and fine-tuning reasoning LLMs. This can make advanced LLM training more accessible to a wider range of researchers and institutions.
- **More Robust Development Cycles:** The increased stability of training can lead to more predictable and reliable development processes, reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning or restarts due to divergent training runs.
- **Broader Applicability:** As a plug-compatible mechanism, SFPO can be easily adopted by practitioners using GRPO or other GRPO-family variants, potentially accelerating advancements across diverse reasoning domains beyond mathematics, as demonstrated by its performance on coding tasks.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The modular, three-stage structure of SFPO provides a natural foundation for future extensions. The authors suggest avenues such as integrating curriculum learning, meta-learning variants, or developing richer adaptive schedules for its hyperparameters (like α), further advancing data-aware optimization strategies for LLM reasoning.

In summary, SFPO offers a practical and effective solution to critical challenges in LLM RL training, providing a method that is both performant and computationally efficient, with broad applicability within the LLM research and development community.
