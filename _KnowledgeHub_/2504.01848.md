# PaperBench: Evaluating AI's Ability to Replicate AI Research

> [!summary] Summary
> PaperBench is a benchmark that tests whether AI agents can replicate 20 ICML 2024 Spotlight and Oral papers from scratch: read the paper, write a codebase, and run the experiments. Author-co-developed hierarchical rubrics (8,316 gradable tasks) are scored by an LLM judge. The best agent, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, scores 21.0%, below the human baseline.

## Problem

- Existing evaluations of AI research ability rarely require replicating full papers end to end, so they miss the long-horizon skills of understanding, coding from scratch, and running experiments.
- Grading a single paper replication takes human experts tens of hours, so faithful evaluation of agent attempts does not scale without an automated, validated judge.
- Coarse pass/fail evaluation of research replication gives no partial-credit signal, so incremental progress on very hard, multi-day tasks stays invisible and hard to compare across agents.

## Method

- Author-co-developed ==hierarchical rubrics== decompose each paper into a ==weighted tree of binary leaf criteria== (Result Match, Execution, Code Development), with scores propagating up to a final ==Replication Score==.
- Agents write a repository with a ==reproduce.sh entrypoint== that is re-run in a fresh A10 GPU VM. Authors' code is blacklisted, and a ==post-hoc monitor== checks for violations.
- An LLM-based **SimpleJudge**, built on o3-mini-high, grades submissions against rubrics and is validated on **JudgeEval**, a gold-labelled human-graded dataset. A lightweight **Code-Dev** variant grades only code development.

## Results

- With BasicAgent, Claude 3.5 Sonnet scores **21.0%** and o1 **13.2%**. DeepSeek-R1 scores **6.0%**, GPT-4o **4.1%**, Gemini-2.0-Flash **3.2%**, and o3-mini **2.6%**.
- IterativeAgent, which forbids ending early, lifts o1 to **24.4%** (**26.0%** over **36 hours**) and o3-mini to **8.5%**, but lowers Claude 3.5 Sonnet to **16.1%**. On Code-Dev, o1 reaches **43.4%**.
- On a 3-paper subset, ML PhDs reached **41.4%** after **48 hours** versus **26.6%** for o1. o1 led early but plateaued after about an hour. The best judge scored **0.83 F1** on JudgeEval.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Agents write plans and code quickly but fail at long-horizon execution: they quit early, do not strategize around the time budget, and plateau while humans keep improving.
> - Agent scores depend heavily on scaffold and prompting: forcing continued work helps o-series models but hurts Claude, so cross-model comparisons must control for scaffold.
> - Hierarchical, weighted rubrics with partial credit, plus a judge validated against human labels, are a scalable pattern for evaluating open-ended long-horizon tasks that lack a single ground truth.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

PaperBench addresses a crucial gap in the evaluation of AI agents, particularly concerning their ability to perform complex, long-horizon, and open-ended machine learning research and development (ML R&D) tasks. The existing landscape of AI agent benchmarks often falls short in capturing the full spectrum of capabilities required for real-world scientific endeavors.

**Comparison with existing benchmarks:**

- **Code Reproduction vs. Replication:** Prior work like CORE-Bench (Siegel et al., 2024) focuses on computational reproducibility, where agents are given an existing code repository and tasked with reproducing its results. PaperBench significantly elevates this challenge by requiring agents to replicate research *from scratch*, meaning they must understand a paper's contributions, design and implement a codebase, and execute experiments without access to the original authors' code. This shifts the evaluation from code adaptation to fundamental comprehension, software engineering, and experimental design.
- **Kaggle Competitions vs. State-of-the-Art Research:** Benchmarks such as MLE-bench (Chan et al., 2024), MLAgentBench (Huang et al., 2024), and DSBench (Jing et al., 2024) evaluate agents on Kaggle competitions. While valuable, Kaggle tasks are often well-defined, dated, and simpler than cutting-edge ML research. PaperBench explicitly uses 20 recent (ICML 2024 Spotlight and Oral) papers, ensuring relevance to contemporary ML challenges and pushing agents to grapple with the complexities and occasional underspecification inherent in frontier research.
- **Self-Contained Tasks vs. Broad Scope:** RE-Bench (Wijk et al., 2024) proposes challenging ML engineering tasks. However, PaperBench aims to cover a broader range of sub-tasks over a longer horizon of work, where a simple "scoring function" often cannot capture the full scope of achievements. The hierarchical rubric system of PaperBench allows for granular assessment of partial progress across diverse contributions.
- **Idea Generation vs. Empirical Validation:** Recent work has explored LLMs generating research ideas (Si et al., 2024) or solving toy research problems (Jansen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). PaperBench moves beyond conceptual understanding or simplified problem-solving to demand full empirical replication, including code development, execution, and result matching—a comprehensive test of an agent's ability to "do science."
- **Automated Judging:** The paper acknowledges existing work on LLMs acting as judges (Zheng et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2023) but notes that PaperBench applies this to significantly harder tasks with complex, unstructured outputs. The introduction of JudgeEval is a novel contribution to evaluate the reliability of such automated judges.

In essence, PaperBench pushes the boundaries of AI agent evaluation by creating a high-fidelity, end-to-end benchmark that simulates a core activity of ML researchers: replicating and validating published empirical findings. It addresses the critical need for robust methods to evaluate the autonomous capabilities of AI systems in complex scientific domains, particularly as these systems approach human-level performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce **PaperBench**, a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the capability of AI agents to replicate state-of-the-art machine learning research papers. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

- **Accelerating ML Progress:** The authors posit that AI agents capable of autonomously replicating ML research could significantly accelerate progress in the field by automating a labor-intensive aspect of the research cycle.
- **Measuring AI Model Autonomy and R&D Capabilities:** A central motivation is to provide a rigorous, objective measure of AI models' autonomy and their ability to conduct ML R&D. This is directly tied to the "preparedness" and "responsible scaling" frameworks adopted by leading AI organizations (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind) to assess the capabilities and potential risks of advanced AI systems. High performance on PaperBench would indicate a non-trivial capacity for autonomous scientific work.
- **Rigorous and Objective Evaluation:** Human replication of papers is time-consuming and subjective. PaperBench aims to standardize this process through its detailed, author-approved rubrics and automated LLM-based grading. This allows for scalable and consistent evaluation of AI agents.
- **Identifying Current AI Agent Limitations:** By setting a challenging, real-world task, PaperBench serves to expose the current strengths and weaknesses of frontier AI models. The authors specifically sought to understand how agents perform on "long-horizon tasks," including understanding complex contributions, developing code from scratch, and troubleshooting experiments.
- **Facilitating Future Research:** By open-sourcing the benchmark (papers, rubrics, and evaluation code), the authors aim to stimulate further research in the development of more capable AI agents and robust evaluation methodologies for AI engineering capabilities.
- **Addressing Underspecification in Research:** The creation of paper-specific addendums, co-developed with original authors, helps clarify ambiguities often present in published papers, making the replication task more tractable and the evaluation more fair.

In summary, PaperBench is motivated by the dual imperatives of accelerating scientific discovery through AI automation while simultaneously ensuring that such powerful autonomous capabilities are developed and understood within a robust safety and governance framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PaperBench's methodology is meticulously designed to create a comprehensive and objective evaluation of AI agents' ML research replication abilities.

**A. Benchmark Dataset Construction:**
The core of PaperBench consists of 20 "Spotlight and Oral" papers from the International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML) 2024. This selection ensures the papers are representative of contemporary, cutting-edge AI research across diverse topics like deep reinforcement learning, robustness, and probabilistic methods. A rigorous filtering process, initially aided by GPT-4o, was applied to select suitable papers, ensuring they:
- Have substantial empirical experiments requiring non-trivial engineering.
- Are reproducible on a single machine (no distributed training).
- Do not depend on closed-source models or require new human data collection.
- Contain sufficient detail for replication from scratch.
Critically, agents are explicitly disallowed from using or viewing the original authors’ codebases, forcing them to genuinely understand and implement the methods from the paper alone.

**B. Rubric-Based Evaluation System:**
For each paper, a detailed, hierarchical rubric is created. This is a highly labor-intensive process, taking "multiple weeks per paper," and is performed in collaboration with one of the original paper authors to ensure accuracy and realism.
- **Hierarchical Decomposition:** Rubrics are structured as a tree, breaking down the overall replication task into increasingly granular requirements. The root node represents the "core contributions," which are decomposed into specific experimental outcomes, then into finer details like model implementation or specific result matching criteria.
- **Grading Criteria:** There are 8,316 individually gradable "leaf nodes" across the 20 papers. Each leaf node has a binary pass/fail criterion (0 or 1). Parent nodes receive a score equal to the weighted average of their children's scores, propagating up to a final "Replication Score" for the entire paper (out of 100%).
- **Requirement Types:** Leaf nodes fall into three categories:
 - **Code Development:** Assesses the correctness of the implemented source code.
 - **Execution:** Verifies if specific experimental steps were successfully run during the reproduction process.
 - **Result Match:** Checks if the outputs (e.g., tables, figures, metrics) generated by the agent's code align with the paper's reported results. This offers a granular way to score partial progress.
- **Addendums:** Each paper is accompanied by a manual "addendum" providing clarifications from the original authors and defining out-of-scope sections, mitigating inherent underspecification in research papers.

**C. Automated Grading with LLM Judges:**
Given that human grading takes "tens of hours per paper," the authors developed an automated LLM-based judge called "SimpleJudge."
- **SimpleJudge Implementation:** This judge uses a large language model (primarily OpenAI's o3-mini-high) to independently grade each leaf node. It's prompted with the paper, rubric details, the specific leaf node requirement, and relevant files from the agent's submission (filtered for context management).
- **Judge Evaluation (JudgeEval):** To ensure the reliability of the automated judge, a separate benchmark, JudgeEval, was created. This dataset consists of partial replications of selected papers, each manually graded by human experts to serve as ground truth. JudgeEval compares the LLM judge's performance (F1 score, accuracy, etc.) against these human labels. o3-mini-high achieved an F1 score of 0.83 on JudgeEval, deemed "a reasonable stand-in for a human judge" at a cost of approximately $66 USD per paper.

**D. Agent Environment and Rules:**
- **Execution Environment:** Agents operate within an Ubuntu 24.04 Docker container with access to a single A10 GPU.
- **Task Output:** Agents must produce a Git repository containing all code, including a `reproduce.sh` script to execute experiments. This script is run in a fresh, clean VM setup to ensure actual reproduction.
- **Allowed Resources:** Agents can browse the internet but are explicitly forbidden from using paper authors' original code repositories or other blacklisted online replications. API keys for necessary services (e.g., HuggingFace, OpenAI API) are provided.
- **Agent Scaffolding:** The authors used a simple ReAct-based "BasicAgent" scaffolding with tools for bash, Python execution, web browsing, and paginated file reading. They also developed "IterativeAgent," a variant designed to prevent premature termination and encourage piecemeal work, specifically tailored for long-horizon tasks.

**E. Human Baseline:**
To contextualize AI agent performance, 8 ML PhDs were recruited to attempt a subset of 4 papers under similar conditions to the AI agents (single GPU, no blacklisted resources, tracked work time). This provides a crucial human performance comparison over time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments on PaperBench yielded several important insights into the current capabilities and limitations of frontier AI models in replicating ML research.

**A. Overall Agent Performance:**
- **Frontier Models' Scores (BasicAgent):** The best-performing tested agent in the main setup was Anthropic's Claude 3.5 Sonnet (New), achieving an average replication score of 21.0%. OpenAI's o1 model scored 13.2%. Other evaluated models (o3-mini, GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash, DeepSeek-R1) performed significantly weaker, scoring under 10%.
- **Limitations of BasicAgent:** Manual inspection of agent logs revealed that most models using BasicAgent frequently terminated early, either claiming completion or citing insurmountable problems. They struggled with strategic time management and did not effectively utilize the full 12-hour allocation. This points to a general weakness in long-horizon task execution.

**B. Impact of IterativeAgent Scaffolding:**
- **Performance Uplift for OpenAI Models:** The "IterativeAgent" scaffolding, which prevents early termination and encourages piecemeal work, significantly boosted scores for OpenAI's models. o1's average score rose from 13.2% to 24.4%, and o3-mini's from 2.6% to 8.5%.
- **Sensitivity to Prompting:** Interestingly, Claude 3.5 Sonnet's score *decreased* with IterativeAgent (from 21.0% to 16.1%), suggesting model-specific sensitivities to prompting strategies. The authors hypothesize that a modified BasicAgent preventing early termination might allow Claude 3.5 Sonnet to perform better.
- **Extended Runs:** An extended 36-hour run of o1 with IterativeAgent reached a score of 26.0%.

**C. Performance on PaperBench Code-Dev:**
- On the lightweight PaperBench Code-Dev variant (focusing only on code development), o1 (IterativeAgent) achieved a score of 43.4%. This indicates that current models are considerably better at *writing* correct code than at integrating, executing, and validating it to reproduce results.

**D. Human Baseline Comparison:**
- **Human Performance:** On a 3-paper subset, the human baseline (best of 3 ML PhD attempts after 48 hours of effort) achieved an average replication score of 41.4%.
- **Agent vs. Human Trajectory:** A key finding illustrated in Figure 3 (comparing o1 IterativeAgent to human performance over time) is that o1 initially outperforms humans in the very early stages (first hour) but then plateaus. Humans, while slower to start (presumably due to paper digestion), continue to improve their scores over longer durations, eventually outperforming the AI agents after 24 hours. This suggests agents are good at rapidly generating initial code but lack the strategic depth and iterative refinement capabilities of human experts for complex, prolonged tasks.

**E. Performance by Requirement Type:**
- Analysis stratified by requirement type (Table 9) confirms that models perform significantly better on "Code Development" nodes than on "Execution" or "Result Match" nodes. For example, o1 IterativeAgent scored 43.3% on Code Development but only 4.5% on Execution and 0.0% on Results Analysis. This highlights a critical limitation: while agents can write substantial amounts of code, they struggle with the downstream tasks of robust execution, debugging, and ensuring numerical/empirical fidelity to the paper's reported results. Humans, while not perfect, show a much more balanced performance across these categories.
- **High Variance:** The results also show notable variance across different runs on the same paper for AI agents, indicating a degree of instability in their performance.

In summary, current frontier AI agents demonstrate non-trivial capabilities in understanding and developing code for ML research papers, but they are still far from competently performing end-to-end replication. Their primary weaknesses lie in long-horizon planning, robust execution, debugging, and verifying results, areas where human experts currently maintain a significant advantage.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PaperBench represents a significant contribution to the field of AI evaluation, with far-reaching implications for AI research, development, and safety.

**A. Advancing AI Evaluation and Capabilities Measurement:**
- **Novelty and Rigor:** PaperBench stands out as a unique and highly rigorous benchmark for evaluating AI agents' ability to perform complex, real-world ML R&D tasks from scratch. By requiring agents to build entire codebases, conduct experiments, and generate results, it moves beyond simpler code generation or problem-solving tasks.
- **Granular Assessment:** The detailed, hierarchical, and author-approved rubrics allow for exceptionally granular measurement of partial progress, providing richer insights into agent capabilities beyond a simple pass/fail. This is crucial for understanding nuanced performance on multifaceted tasks.
- **Scalability:** The development of the LLM-based judge (SimpleJudge) and the JudgeEval benchmark addresses the critical need for scalable evaluation of such complex tasks, overcoming the prohibitive cost and time of purely human grading. This paves the way for more frequent and comprehensive evaluations of frontier models.

**B. Implications for AI Development and Research:**
- **Identifying Weaknesses:** The findings clearly highlight the current limitations of frontier models in long-horizon planning, robust execution, and result validation. This provides concrete areas for future research and development in agentic AI, focusing on improving debugging, iterative refinement, and strategic reasoning.
- **Accelerating Scientific Discovery (Future Potential):** The long-term vision is that highly capable AI agents could significantly accelerate scientific discovery, including critical fields like AI safety and alignment research, by autonomously replicating and extending frontier work. PaperBench serves as a tool to measure progress towards this goal.
- **Benchmarking Progress:** As AI capabilities evolve, PaperBench will serve as a consistent benchmark to track advancements in AI agents' autonomy and ML expertise, providing a quantitative measure of how close they are to performing independent research.

**C. AI Safety and Responsible Scaling:**
- **Monitoring Emerging Capabilities:** Perhaps the most significant impact lies in the context of AI safety. By measuring "autonomous R&D capabilities," PaperBench directly contributes to frameworks like OpenAI's Preparedness Framework and Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy. This allows leading AI labs to monitor the pace of capability development and anticipate potential risks.
- **Addressing Rapid Innovation Risks:** The "Impact Statement" articulates a crucial concern: if powerful AI models can rapidly replicate, refine, and improve state-of-the-art techniques, they could accelerate development at a pace that outpaces our ability to understand implications, conduct thorough risk assessments, or implement governance measures. PaperBench provides a vital early-warning system for these emerging capabilities.
- **Facilitating Preparedness:** By open-sourcing the benchmark, the authors enable the broader AI safety community to participate in evaluating and forecasting the capabilities of AI systems, fostering collective preparedness for the powerful impacts of increasing AI autonomy.

**D. Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper openly discusses limitations, such as the dataset size (though compensated by rubric granularity), potential data contamination (due to pre-training on existing codebases), the labor-intensive nature of rubric creation, and the inherent non-determinism and imperfect accuracy of LLM judges. These limitations, however, also point to fruitful areas for future research, including automated rubric generation, more robust and cost-effective LLM judges, and the study of adversarial agent behaviors (e.g., "specification gaming"). The introduction of PaperBench Code-Dev also provides a more accessible entry point for researchers due to reduced computational requirements.

In conclusion, PaperBench is a forward-thinking benchmark that not only assesses the current state of AI agents in replicating complex ML research but also serves as a critical tool for monitoring, understanding, and responsibly guiding the development of increasingly autonomous AI systems in scientific discovery.
