# Foundations of Reinforcement Learning and Control:Connections and New Perspectives

> [!summary] Summary
> A tutorial clarifies the foundational differences and commonalities between reinforcement learning and control theory, proposing a hybrid architecture that combines a high-level SAC policy with a low-level adaptive control system. This integrated approach enables autonomous agents to learn complex behaviors while adaptively compensating for non-stationary environment dynamics, demonstrated by maintaining high locomotion performance in a Half-Cheetah despite altered joint friction.

## Problem

- The historical and methodological divergence between Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Control Theory (CT), despite their shared roots in dynamic programming, leads to distinct approaches for optimizing unknown dynamical systems.
- Developing autonomous systems that combine the empirical performance of RL with the formal guarantees and real-time adaptation of CT for uncertain, high-dimensional, and non-stationary environments remains a challenge.
- Fixed RL policies often degrade in performance when deployed in dynamic or changing real-world conditions, lacking inherent robustness to unexpected system variations.

## Method

- A tutorial providing a unified notation and conceptual framework to clarify the distinct methodologies and common principles of RL and CT.
- Detailed exposition of representative methods: ==Model-Reference Adaptive Control (MRAC)== for online adaptation and ==Actor-Critic (Soft Actor-Critic, SAC)== for learning high-performance policies.
- An original **hybrid SAC-MRAC architecture** combining a high-level SAC policy to generate desired reference signals with a low-level MRAC system that adaptively tracks these references, compensating for system dynamics changes.

## Results

- The hybrid SAC-MRAC system successfully maintained high locomotion performance for a Half-Cheetah robot when its joint friction dynamics were suddenly altered.
- The low-level MRAC controller in the hybrid system adaptively tuned its parameters to restore nominal dynamics, allowing the high-level SAC policy to sustain optimal behavior.
- The tutorial provides a comprehensive mapping of concepts across RL and CT, identifying areas for synergy in model usage, exploration strategies, and handling partial observability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RL and CT, though sharing foundational concepts like dynamic programming and stability, diverged due to different historical applications (simulated vs. physical systems) and priorities (empirical performance vs. formal guarantees).
> - Model-Reference Adaptive Control can effectively ensure real-time stability and track desired system behavior even under significant, unmodeled changes in system dynamics.
> - A fixed, high-performing RL policy trained in a static environment will experience considerable performance degradation when environmental dynamics shift, highlighting its lack of inherent robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses the long-standing challenge of optimizing the control of unknown dynamical systems using feedback, a problem central to both Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Control Theory (CT). Historically, these two fields, despite sharing common roots in dynamic programming, have developed distinct methodologies, objectives, and academic cultures. Control theory, emerging from engineering applications in the 1960s with adaptive control, has traditionally prioritized formal stability, safety, and robustness guarantees for physical systems ("in-vivo"). In contrast, reinforcement learning, developed largely within computer science since the 1980s, has focused on algorithmic design, sample efficiency, and empirical performance in simulated environments ("in-silico").

Recent advancements in data availability and computational capabilities have initiated a convergence between these communities. Control researchers are increasingly adopting data-centric, algorithmic, and computational perspectives, while RL researchers are applying their methods to physical systems, integrating domain knowledge, and seeking stronger guarantees. This confluence has enabled the study and deployment of control strategies in complex, high-dimensional environments, such as robotic locomotion and manipulation, which demand controllers capable of handling non-equilibrium motion, non-smooth dynamics, and real-time changing goals.

Despite this growing synergy, a significant conceptual and methodological gap persists. This tutorial aims to bridge this gap by clarifying the distinct approaches of each field, identifying their complementary strengths, and outlining pathways toward more unified and practically effective methodologies for data-driven decision making. It positions itself as a foundational resource for experts in either field to better understand and engage with the tools and perspectives of the other, thereby fostering further interdisciplinary collaboration.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to provide a foundational understanding of the core differences, common principles, and complementary strengths between Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Control Theory (CT). The authors aim to facilitate better mutual understanding and engagement between experts in these two adjacent scientific fields, ultimately outlining a path toward more unified approaches for data-driven decision making.

The motivation behind this objective is multifaceted:

- **Persistent Disciplinary Gap:** Despite sharing a common heritage in dynamic programming and decades of mutual influence, RL and CT have evolved along divergent paths. This divergence is attributed to distinct primary environments (in-silico simulation for RL vs. in-vivo physical systems for CT) and different epistemological priorities (empirical benchmarks and scalability for RL vs. formal certificates and stability for CT). This has led to communities with separate terminologies, notations, and solution methods.
- **Converging Practical Challenges:** Modern control problems, particularly in robotics and autonomous systems, are characterized by high-dimensionality, inherent uncertainties, and non-stationary dynamics. These challenges exceed the scope of classical control methods alone and demand adaptive and learning capabilities. Simultaneously, RL, traditionally focused on optimal performance in static environments, increasingly recognizes the need for robustness, safety, and real-time adaptation for real-world deployment. The convergence of these practical demands highlights an opportunity for integrating the strengths of both fields.
- **Leveraging Complementary Strengths:** RL excels at learning complex, high-dimensional behaviors and optimizing for cumulative rewards from scratch. Control theory provides robust frameworks for stability analysis, constraint satisfaction, and online adaptation in the presence of uncertainty, often with strong theoretical guarantees. The authors argue that combining these strengths could lead to systems that are both high-performing and reliable.
- **Illustrative Demonstration:** To concretize the potential for synergy, the paper motivates and presents an original hybrid approach that combines elements of both fields. By demonstrating this on a classical locomotion control problem (Half-Cheetah), the authors aim to show how high-level learning from RL can effectively integrate with low-level adaptation from adaptive control to achieve enhanced performance and robustness in dynamic environments.
- **Educational and Conceptual Clarity:** The paper serves as a tutorial, systematically introducing key concepts from both adaptive control (e.g., Model-Reference Adaptive Control) and RL (e.g., actor-critic algorithms) using a unified notation. This structured presentation is motivated by the need to clarify methodological differences, emphasize common principles, and foster a shared language, thereby making the interdisciplinary landscape more accessible to researchers.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper adopts a tutorial-style approach to explain and connect concepts from reinforcement learning and control theory. It is structured to provide a general introduction, detail representative methodologies from each field, and then demonstrate a novel combination.

#### 3.1. Unified Problem Definition and Foundations

The paper begins by formalizing the general problem of sequentially selecting actions to achieve desired dynamical system behavior.
- **Unified Notation**: A common language and notation are established for dynamical systems, state space, action space, dynamics functions, and other core concepts, acknowledging and clarifying the distinct terminologies used in RL (e.g., Markov Decision Process, reward, value function) and control (e.g., deterministic dynamics, cost, cost-to-go, Lyapunov function).
- **Running Example**: The Half-Cheetah robotic system in a MuJoCo simulation serves as a consistent illustrative example throughout the tutorial, allowing for concrete demonstrations of both RL and control methods.
- **Common Principles**: The discussion highlights shared foundations such as dynamical systems, the concept of stability (and Lyapunov functions as a tool for analysis), and optimal control/decision-making rooted in dynamic programming (Bellman equations, policy iteration). The relationship between cost-to-go functions and Lyapunov functions is explained, particularly in the context of the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR).
- **Divergence Analysis**: The paper explicitly discusses the historical and cultural divergences that shaped the fields, contrasting RL's focus on "in-silico" environments, algorithmic certification, and empirical performance with control's "in-vivo" applications, formal guarantees (e.g., Lyapunov stability), and robustness concerns.

#### 3.2. Core Technical Methodologies

The core technical section details two representative methodologies: Model-Reference Adaptive Control (MRAC) from control theory and actor-critic algorithms from reinforcement learning.

- **Model-Reference Adaptive Control (MRAC)**:
 - **Architecture**: MRAC is presented as a classical control-theoretic paradigm for online parameter adaptation. It involves a stable reference model that specifies desired system behavior (e.g., target joint angles for the Half-Cheetah). An adaptive controller adjusts its parameters (feedforward gain $K_r$, feedback gain $K_s$, nonlinear compensation $W$) in real time to ensure the plant (physical system) tracks the reference model output.
 - **Mechanism**: The tracking error ($e_k$) between the realized system state and the reference model state is used to drive an adaptation law. This law, often derived from a Lyapunov function (e.g., $U(e, \Theta) = e^T P e + tr(\Theta^T \Gamma^{-1} \Theta \Lambda)$), ensures the decrease of the Lyapunov function over time, thereby guaranteeing boundedness of closed-loop signals and asymptotic tracking error convergence (or contraction in discrete time).
 - **Matching Assumption**: The design relies on a "matching assumption" or "realizability assumption," implying that ideal controller gains exist to make the closed-loop system mimic the reference model.
 - **Illustration**: The method is demonstrated on a second-order system (representing a Half-Cheetah joint) where dynamics (damping) change over time. MRAC automatically tunes controller parameters to maintain accurate reference tracking despite this change.
 - **Extensions**: A brief overview of advanced adaptive control techniques beyond classical MRAC, including adaptive backstepping for nonlinear systems, robust adaptive laws (e.g., deadzone-adapted disturbance suppression), and the importance of persistent excitation for learning.

- **Actor-Critic Reinforcement Learning**:
 - **Concept**: Actor-critic methods are introduced as a canonical class of model-free RL algorithms. They aim to learn a parameterized policy ($\pi_\theta$, the "actor") that maximizes expected discounted rewards, and an approximation of the action-value function ($q_w$, the "critic") to evaluate actions.
 - **Policy Gradient Theorem**: This theorem forms the theoretical basis, allowing the gradient of the objective to be estimated without explicit knowledge of environment dynamics.
 - **Learning Process**: The critic $q_w$ is updated using Temporal Difference (TD) updates from sampled transitions in a replay buffer. The actor $\pi_\theta$ is updated using a policy gradient, often incorporating an "advantage" term ($q_w(s,a) - v(s)$) and reparameterization tricks for continuous action spaces.
 - **Illustration**: The Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm is used as a specific example in the Half-Cheetah environment. Details on replay buffer usage, minibatch updates, entropy regularization, and target networks are provided. The goal is to learn a locomotion policy that maximizes speed without pre-defined reference trajectories.
 - **Limitations**: The discussion acknowledges challenges such as biased gradients, potential for divergence in nonlinear function approximation, lack of explicit stability/safety guarantees during learning, and the heuristic nature of many deep RL implementations.

#### 3.3. Hybrid RL-Adaptive Control Approach

The paper presents an original approach combining the strengths of both fields.
- **Motivation**: Modern RL policies (like SAC) are often trained for static environments and suffer performance degradation when dynamics change. This section addresses how to make such policies adaptive.
- **Architecture**: A high-level SAC policy is trained to output desired joint angles (acting as a reference signal $r(t)$). A low-level MRAC controller then takes these desired angles as its reference and outputs the actual torques (actions $a(t)$) to the robot joints.
- **Implementation (Half-Cheetah)**:
 - An SAC policy is trained on the Half-Cheetah in its original dynamics.
 - A linear reference model for the MRAC is learned from data collected during the SAC policy's successful operation with a fixed low-level controller.
 - When the Half-Cheetah's joint damping coefficients are suddenly reduced (a dynamics change), the low-level MRAC controller adaptively tunes its parameters (specifically, $k_{\dot{\theta}}$) to ensure the physical joints continue to track the SAC-generated desired angles.
 - **Practical Adjustments**: The authors note that for the highly nonlinear Half-Cheetah system, modifications to the standard MRAC (e.g., replacing the error term with a one-step prediction error and clamping parameters) were necessary, acknowledging a departure from linear-setting theoretical guarantees.
- **Outcome**: This hybrid approach demonstrates that the adaptive low-level controller successfully restores the nominal dynamics, allowing the fixed high-level SAC policy to maintain high performance despite environmental changes.

#### 3.4. Additional Considerations

The paper further discusses three key aspects to deepen the comparative analysis:
- **Model-based vs. Model-free Algorithms**: Examines how each field uses or avoids explicit models of system dynamics, from "gray-box" models in control and system identification to model-based and model-free RL. It notes the blurring lines (e.g., replay buffers as nonparametric models).
- **Exploration and Finite-time Performance**: Compares RL's concepts of exploration-exploitation trade-offs, regret minimization, and optimism in the face of uncertainty (OFU) with control's notion of persistent excitation for parameter identification, highlighting the goal of efficient information gathering.
- **Partial Observability**: Contrasts control theory's reliance on belief state estimation (e.g., Kalman filter, Bayesian filtering) with RL's common practice of summarizing historical observations directly (e.g., frame stacking, recurrent neural networks) due to the intractability of belief states in complex environments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several findings that both highlight the distinctions between RL and control theory and demonstrate the benefits of their integration:

- **Common Foundations, Divergent Practices**: It is established that both RL and control theory originate from shared principles like dynamic programming, Lyapunov theory, and optimal control. However, their historical development, driven by differing environments (simulated vs. physical) and priorities (empirical performance vs. formal guarantees), led to distinct methodologies and cultures. This differentiation provides a clear framework for understanding the disciplinary gap.
- **Unified Problem Framing**: The tutorial successfully unifies the notation and problem definitions for dynamical systems, policies, objectives, and key components across both fields. This aids in illustrating that the underlying control problem is fundamentally similar, despite terminological differences.
- **Effectiveness of Model-Reference Adaptive Control (MRAC)**:
 - MRAC is demonstrated as a robust method for online adaptation. In a second-order system representing a Half-Cheetah joint, MRAC effectively tunes controller parameters in real-time.
 - The illustration shows that MRAC can maintain accurate tracking of a reference signal even when the system's inherent dynamics (e.g., damping) undergo a significant and sudden change. This emphasizes MRAC's strength in handling parametric uncertainty and ensuring stability.
- **Effectiveness of Actor-Critic Reinforcement Learning (SAC)**:
 - Deep RL algorithms like Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) are shown to be highly effective at learning complex, high-performance behaviors, such as continuous locomotion in the Half-Cheetah environment, from direct interaction.
 - During initial training, SAC achieves high cumulative rewards, demonstrating its capability to discover optimal policies for a given (static) environment.
- **Limitations of Fixed Policies in Non-stationary Environments**:
 - A significant finding is the performance degradation of a purely RL-trained, fixed policy when the environment's dynamics change. The Half-Cheetah example shows a drastic drop in total reward after a change in joint friction, indicating that such policies are not inherently robust to non-stationarity.
 - Even a fixed low-level controller combined with SAC shows only marginal improvement, reinforcing the need for adaptive mechanisms.
- **Demonstration of Hybrid RL-MRAC Architecture for Enhanced Adaptability**:
 - The core practical result is the successful integration of a high-level SAC policy with a low-level MRAC controller. This hybrid architecture effectively addresses the challenge of non-stationary environments.
 - In the Half-Cheetah experiment, when joint friction is altered, the low-level MRAC component actively adapts its parameters. This adaptation restores the nominal low-level dynamics that the high-level SAC policy was trained on, thereby enabling the robot to maintain high performance.
 - This demonstrates that the strengths of RL (learning complex behaviors) and adaptive control (online compensation for dynamic changes) can be synergistically combined.
- **Identification of Key Differences and Similarities**: The paper provides a clear conceptual mapping for aspects like model usage (model-based vs. model-free, system identification), exploration strategies (regret minimization, optimism, persistent excitation), and handling of partial observability (belief states vs. history summarization).
- **Open Research Problems**: The tutorial concludes by outlining several critical open problems that necessitate interdisciplinary research, including long-horizon planning with stability, hierarchical architectures, experience consolidation, robust RL under distribution shifts, and the integration of hard safety constraints.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper holds significant implications for the future direction of research in autonomous systems and data-driven decision making, primarily by fostering greater interdisciplinary collaboration and offering concrete solutions to current limitations.

- **Bridging Disciplinary Divides**: The most immediate impact is its role as a tutorial designed to bridge the long-standing conceptual and methodological gap between reinforcement learning and control theory. By providing a unified language, comparing fundamental principles, and explicitly addressing cultural differences, the paper is poised to increase mutual understanding and facilitate cross-pollination of ideas and techniques between these two vital fields. This could lead to a more coherent and integrated research community.
- **Enabling Robust and Adaptive Autonomous Systems**: The demonstration of a hybrid RL-MRAC architecture for the Half-Cheetah system offers a practical blueprint for developing autonomous agents that combine the strengths of both paradigms. This approach allows RL to learn complex, high-performance task-level behaviors, while adaptive control provides the crucial real-time stability, robustness, and online adaptation capabilities necessary for deployment in uncertain and non-stationary real-world environments. This is particularly significant for applications in robotics (e.g., legged robots on varied terrain, manipulators handling changing payloads), where dynamic uncertainty is prevalent.
- **Advancing Safe and Reliable AI**: The paper underscores the control community's emphasis on formal guarantees (stability, safety, robustness) and highlights how these can be integrated into learning-based systems. By demonstrating how adaptive control can compensate for dynamic shifts, it contributes to the broader effort of developing "safe RL" solutions. This is critical for moving AI systems from simulations to safety-critical applications in the physical world, where hard constraint satisfaction is non-negotiable.
- **Stimulating New Research Directions**: By clearly articulating open problems at the intersection of RL and control, such as long-horizon planning with short-term stability, hierarchical learning architectures, robust RL under distribution shifts, and effective handling of partial observability, the paper provides a roadmap for future interdisciplinary research. It encourages researchers to explore novel combinations of feedforward and feedback mechanisms, distributed learning, and methods for experience consolidation across different timescales.
- **Educational Resource for Interdisciplinary Training**: As a comprehensive tutorial, this paper serves as an invaluable educational resource. It can accelerate the training of a new generation of researchers who are proficient in both RL and control, thereby fostering the development of truly intelligent and reliable autonomous systems.
- **Broadening the Scope of Data-Driven Control**: The work contributes to the ongoing paradigm shift in control engineering towards data-driven methods. It demonstrates how empirical data can be systematically leveraged not just for initial learning but also for continuous adaptation and robust operation of complex dynamical systems, potentially influencing fields beyond traditional robotics, such as industrial processes and even the design of advanced AI agents.
