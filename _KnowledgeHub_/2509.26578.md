# Linking Process to Outcome: Conditional Reward Modeling for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Conditional Reward Modeling (CRM) enhances large language model reasoning by explicitly modeling inter-step dependencies and causally linking process rewards to the final outcome. This framework improves accuracy across Best-of-N sampling, beam search, and reinforcement learning tasks, while demonstrating robustness against reward hacking.

## Problem

- Existing Process Reward Models (PRMs) evaluate reasoning steps in isolation, overlooking sequential dependencies inherent in multi-step reasoning.
- Ambiguous credit assignment in PRMs hinders clear attribution of final outcome correctness to specific intermediate steps, leading to reward hacking and suboptimal LLM performance.
- The high cost and limited scalability of acquiring ground-truth "verifiable rewards" constrain the development of robust LLM reasoning systems.

## Method

- Introduces ==Conditional Reward Modeling== (CRM), which frames LLM reasoning as a temporal, probabilistic process, explicitly tracking the probability of entering a "wrong state" at each step.
- Applies Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) with a potential function Φ(s_t) = log S(t) to derive dense, step-wise rewards r_t = log(1 - h(t)), directly linking process rewards to the final outcome.
- Trains the reward model using a composite loss function that maximizes the probability of correct reasoning, minimizes incorrect reasoning, and precisely identifies the first erroneous step in an incorrect trajectory.

## Results

- CRM consistently achieved superior or near-best performance in Best-of-N sampling, improving accuracy (e.g., +1.4 percentage points on MATH500 with Qwen2.5-**3B**-Instruct at N=32) and demonstrating higher AUPRC for cross-sample comparability.
- In reinforcement learning optimization without verifiable rewards, CRM-based training yielded the best Pass@1 accuracy across the majority of six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, outperforming existing PRMs and a verifiable reward-only baseline.
- The framework exhibited robustness against reward hacking, maintained stable reward signals, and fostered increased self-reflection in LLMs during training, leading to improved task accuracy and better generalization across multiple domains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling inter-step dependencies and causally linking process rewards to the final outcome through conditional probabilities creates a theoretically grounded and coherent reward signal.
> - The derived step-wise rewards, based on the logarithm of the probability of maintaining correct reasoning, enable consistent probabilistic meaning and reliable cross-sample comparison of diverse reasoning trajectories.
> - Precise identification of the first erroneous step (via L_z loss) is crucial for accurate credit assignment, which makes the reward model robust to reward hacking and effective for guiding LLMs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work contributes to the field of enhancing Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning capabilities, particularly for tasks requiring explicit, step-by-step problem-solving. Recent advancements in LLM reasoning have largely fallen into two categories: prompt-based methods (e.g., Chain-of-Thought prompting) and reinforcement learning (RL) approaches. State-of-the-art systems have leveraged RL with "verifiable rewards," which are derived by checking model outputs against ground-truth labels. However, the acquisition of such ground-truth labels is often costly and difficult to scale across diverse and complex reasoning tasks.

To address the scalability limitations of verifiable rewards, reward models (RMs) have emerged as an alternative, designed to extrapolate reward signals across general LLM reasoning processes. These RMs are broadly categorized into:
1. **Outcome Reward Models (ORMs):** Provide feedback only at the final step of a reasoning trajectory.
2. **Process Reward Models (PRMs):** Offer finer-grained rewards at individual reasoning steps or even token levels. PRMs aim to guide LLMs through their intermediate reasoning steps.

Despite the promise of PRMs, existing approaches exhibit several limitations. Many PRMs evaluate each reasoning step in isolation, neglecting the sequential dependencies inherent in multi-step reasoning. Others attempt to link step-wise rewards with the final outcome but often fail to establish clear causal relationships or precise credit assignment. For instance, some methods rely on relative comparisons between neighboring steps, which can lead to biased process rewards, while others parameterize outcome rewards without clear modeling of how specific steps contribute to the final outcome. These shortcomings lead to ambiguous credit assignment, which can render downstream models vulnerable to "reward hacking" (where models learn to maximize reward signals without improving actual task performance) and result in suboptimal performance in tasks like Best-of-N sampling, beam search, and reinforcement learning.

This work introduces Conditional Reward Modeling (CRM) as an advancement over existing PRMs. CRM aims to overcome the identified limitations by explicitly modeling inter-step dependencies and causally linking process rewards to the final reasoning outcome. It positions itself as a principled framework for enhancing LLM reasoning, particularly by providing more reliable and robust reward signals for various downstream applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel reward modeling framework, Conditional Reward Modeling (CRM), that improves the reasoning capabilities of LLMs by providing more effective and reliable step-by-step guidance. The motivation for developing CRM stems from specific limitations observed in existing Process Reward Models (PRMs):

1. **Addressing Isolated Step Modeling:** Current PRMs often evaluate individual reasoning steps independently, failing to capture the causal and temporal dependencies that exist among steps in a sequential reasoning process. This isolation can lead to inaccurate reward signals that do not reflect the cumulative effect of a reasoning chain. The motivation is to integrate the sequential nature of reasoning into the reward formulation, ensuring that the reward for a given step considers all preceding steps.

2. **Resolving Ambiguous Credit Assignment to the Final Outcome:** While some PRMs attempt to connect intermediate steps to the final outcome, they often lack an explicit and clear mechanism for attributing the final result (correctness or incorrectness) back to the specific contributions of each intermediate step. This ambiguity in credit assignment means that it is unclear which intermediate steps are truly beneficial or detrimental to reaching a correct final answer. The objective is to establish a precise link between each process reward and the final outcome, thereby enabling clear and unambiguous credit assignment.

3. **Mitigating Reward Hacking:** The aforementioned limitations contribute to PRMs' susceptibility to reward hacking. When reward signals are unreliably assigned or do not accurately reflect true progress towards a correct solution, LLMs can learn to generate outputs that maximize the reward model's score without actually improving their reasoning quality or task accuracy. This often manifests as repetitive or excessively long outputs. A core motivation is to develop a reward model robust to such adversarial learning behaviors, ensuring that increasing reward correlates with genuine improvement in reasoning.

4. **Enabling Reliable Cross-Sample Comparison:** Many existing reward models produce scores that are not inherently comparable across different reasoning trajectories or even different questions. This lack of consistent probabilistic or semantic meaning across samples hinders effective decision-making in downstream tasks such as selecting the best response from multiple candidates (Best-of-N sampling) or guiding a beam search algorithm where different paths need to be quantitatively compared. The objective is to create a reward signal that is probabilistically consistent, allowing for meaningful comparisons between diverse reasoning trajectories.

5. **Reducing Reliance on Verifiable Rewards:** Current state-of-the-art LLM reasoning systems often depend on "verifiable rewards" derived from ground-truth labels, which are resource-intensive to acquire. A key motivation is to develop a reward model that can achieve strong performance and stable improvements in LLM reasoning, particularly in reinforcement learning settings, without requiring or heavily relying on these costly ground-truth labels.

In summary, the research aims to introduce a theoretically grounded, probabilistically consistent, and empirically effective reward modeling framework that addresses fundamental shortcomings in current PRMs, ultimately leading to more robust and accurate LLM reasoning capabilities across various applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Conditional Reward Modeling (CRM) framework models LLM reasoning as a temporal, probabilistic process leading to a correct answer. It addresses the limitations of existing PRMs by incorporating inter-step dependencies and explicitly linking process rewards to the final outcome.

#### 3.1. Task Description

The task involves an LLM $\pi$ generating a multi-step response $y = (a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_T)$ for a given question $x$, where $a_t$ is the $t$-th reasoning step. A binary label $l \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates the correctness of the final answer. This multi-step reasoning is framed as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP) where the state $s_t = (x, a_{\le t-1})$ includes the question and previously generated steps, and the action $a_t$ is the current reasoning step. The goal is to define a reward function $r_t$ for each action $a_t$.

#### 3.2. CRM Modeling

CRM introduces the concept of a "wrong state" which occurs when the reasoning process can no longer yield a correct answer. Let $z$ be the index of the first step at which the reasoning enters a wrong state. If the entire trajectory is correct, $z > T$.
- **Probability of a Wrong State:** $W(t; x, a_{\le t}) = \text{Pr}(z \le t)$ denotes the probability that a wrong state has occurred by step $t$.
- **Probability of Maintaining Correct Reasoning:** $S(t; x, a_{\le t}) = \text{Pr}(z > t) = 1 - W(t)$ is the probability that reasoning remains correct up to step $t$. $S(T)$ represents the probability of reaching the correct final answer.
- **Conditional Wrong State Probability:** To capture inter-step dependencies, $h(t) = \text{Pr}(z = t | z \ge t)$ is defined as the probability that step $t$ is the *first* wrong step, given that all previous steps $t-1$ were correct.

The chain rule of probability is applied to establish relationships:
- $S(t) = \prod_{k=1}^t (1 - h(k))$. This formulation explicitly links the probability of overall correct reasoning up to step $t$ to the conditional correctness of each individual step, integrating inter-step dependencies.
- $W(t) = 1 - \prod_{k=1}^t (1 - h(k))$.
- $p(t) = h(t) \prod_{k=1}^{t-1} (1 - h(k))$ is the probability that a wrong state occurs exactly at step $t$.

**Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS):** To derive a dense, step-wise reward signal aligned with the outcome probability, PBRS is applied. This technique constructs a shaped reward function $R'$ from an original sparse reward $R$ using a potential function $\Phi(s_t)$. The shaped reward for a transition from $s_t$ to $s_{t+1}$ is $R'(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}) = R(s_t, a_t) + \gamma\Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t)$. PBRS guarantees policy optimality invariance.
- **Potential Function:** The potential function is defined as $\Phi(s_t) \equiv \log S(t) = \sum_{k=1}^t \log(1 - h(k))$. This choice explicitly encodes progress towards the final correct answer.
- **Process Reward Derivation:** With an original reward $R=0$ for all intermediate steps and a discount factor $\gamma=1$, the step-wise process reward $r_t$ (for the transition from $s_{t-1}$ to $s_t$) is derived as:
 $r_t = \log(1 - h(t))$.
This formulation provides a dense and theoretically justified reward, where $S(T) = \prod_{t=1}^T e^{r_t}$, explicitly linking process rewards to the final outcome.

#### 3.3. Training of Conditional Reward Model

The reward model $f_\phi$ (an LLM backbone with a value head) predicts $h(t)$ based on the question $x$ and preceding steps $a_{\le t}$. The training dataset $D = \{(x_i, y_i, l_i, z_i)\}$ includes question, response, final label, and the index of the first wrong step $z_i$.
Three loss terms are used for training:
1. **$L_S(x_i, y_i) = -\log S(T)$:** For samples where the final answer is correct ($l_i=1$), this loss maximizes the probability of maintaining correct reasoning throughout the trajectory.
2. **$L_W(x_i, y_i) = -\log(1 - S(T))$:** For samples where the final answer is incorrect ($l_i=0$), this loss minimizes the probability of maintaining correct reasoning throughout the trajectory.
3. **$L_z(x_i, y_i, z_i) = -\log p(z_i)$:** For incorrect samples ($l_i=0$), this loss maximizes the probability of the wrong state occurring *exactly* at step $z_i$. This term specifically encourages the model to pinpoint the first error location.

The overall loss $L$ is a weighted sum of these terms. This consistent probabilistic modeling ensures that reward signals possess uniform semantic meaning across different reasoning trajectories, enabling reliable cross-sample comparisons.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates the effectiveness and robustness of Conditional Reward Modeling (CRM) across various evaluation paradigms and datasets.

#### 4.1. Best-of-N Sampling Experiments

CRM was evaluated on its ability to select the optimal response from N generated candidates using its trajectory-level score, $S(T)$.
- **Performance:** CRM consistently ranked highest or near-highest on both GSM-Plus and MATH500 datasets across different values of N (8 to 128) and LLM generator families (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, LLaMA3.1-8B). For instance, on MATH500 with Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, CRM achieved 56.6% at N=32, surpassing the strongest baseline by +1.4 percentage points. This indicates CRM's capacity for effective trajectory-level selection by holistically assessing the coherence of the reasoning chain.
- **Cross-Sample Comparability:** CRM exhibited superior cross-sample comparability, as measured by Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC). It achieved a higher AUPRC than baselines when mixing responses from different questions and ranking them globally, suggesting that its probabilistic modeling provides reward signals that are consistently meaningful across diverse samples.

#### 4.2. Beam Search Experiments

CRM guided beam search on MATH500 and the out-of-domain (OOD) Gaokao2023 dataset to evaluate its step-level reward guidance.
- **Performance:** CRM achieved the highest accuracy on both datasets when using Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B, and demonstrated the best performance in the majority of cases for Llama3.1-8B.
- **Scalability:** CRM's performance effectively scaled with increasing total sampling size N, with the performance gap over baselines widening, indicating its advantage in selecting promising intermediate steps within larger search spaces. This is attributed to its consistent probabilistic modeling providing meaningful step-level rewards for both shared-prefix and cross-sample trajectory comparisons during beam pruning.

#### 4.3. RL Optimization Experiments

CRM provided step-level dense rewards ($r_t = \log(1 - h(t))$) to guide LLM policy models during RL optimization, evaluating its credit assignment accuracy.
- **Performance (VR Disabled):** In a setting where verifiable rewards (VR) were not used, CRM-based RL achieved the best Pass@1 accuracy on the majority of six mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH500, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench, AIME25, AIME24, AMC23). It significantly outperformed PRM, PQM, PURE, and even a VR-only baseline (which uses final-answer ground truth).
- **Performance (VR Enabled):** When combined with verifiable rewards (CRM + VR), further gains were observed, leading to the highest performance on most benchmarks, suggesting complementarity between CRM's process rewards and ground-truth-based verifiable rewards.
- **Credit Assignment:** The results suggest that CRM's explicit linkage between process rewards and outcomes enables precise credit assignment, quantifying how each step advances or undermines the probability of reaching the correct final answer, thereby facilitating effective RL optimization.

#### 4.4. Extended Analysis

- **Reward Hacking Robustness:** CRM demonstrated robustness to reward hacking. While baselines like PRM and PQM showed rapid reward escalation accompanied by increasing repetitive outputs (high repeat scores) and declining downstream accuracy, CRM's rewards remained stable and aligned with reasoning quality, suppressing degenerate generation strategies.
- **Self-Reflection Behavior:** During RL training, CRM's self-reflection score (frequency of reflective expressions) steadily rose, correlating with improvements in downstream task accuracy. In contrast, PRM and PQM exhibited minimal self-reflection growth and early performance collapse, suggesting CRM fosters more meaningful reasoning behaviors.
- **Data Efficiency:** An ablation study on the $L_z$ loss term showed that CRM achieves substantial performance gains even with a small proportion (e.g., 10%) of data used for this loss, with performance quickly saturating. This highlights CRM's high data utilization efficiency, as supervision at step $t=z$ propagates backward to earlier steps.
- **Domain Generalization:** CRM was evaluated on MMLU-Pro-CoT-Eval, a multi-domain dataset covering biology, business, health, history, and physics. It outperformed baselines in almost all tested domains, indicating its applicability beyond mathematical tasks.
- **Reward Formulation Validation:** Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of the theoretically derived $r_t = \log(1 - h(t))$ formulation, as alternative linear or log-transformed baseline reward formulations quickly exhibited reward hacking and led to inferior RL performance.
- **Comparison with Reasoning-Enhanced PRMs:** CRM was found to outperform other reasoning-enhanced PRMs like R-PRM and GenPRM on MATH500 Best-of-N sampling, further demonstrating its stronger capability in identifying high-quality responses.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Conditional Reward Modeling (CRM) represents a notable contribution to the field of Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning, with several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Addresses Fundamental PRM Limitations:** CRM directly tackles critical limitations of existing Process Reward Models (PRMs), specifically the isolated treatment of reasoning steps and ambiguous credit assignment. By framing reasoning as a temporal probabilistic process and explicitly linking each step's reward to the final outcome via conditional probabilities and Potential-Based Reward Shaping, CRM establishes a more theoretically grounded and coherent reward signal.

2. **Enhanced Reasoning Performance:** The consistent outperformance of CRM across diverse tasks—including Best-of-N sampling, beam search, and reinforcement learning—and various mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks demonstrates its effectiveness in guiding LLMs. This suggests that a more precise and causally aligned reward structure translates into tangible improvements in LLM reasoning capabilities.

3. **Robustness to Reward Hacking:** A key practical challenge in RL-based LLM training is reward hacking, where models exploit flaws in reward models to maximize scores without genuine task improvement. CRM's design, which tightly couples intermediate rewards to the final outcome, shows increased robustness to this phenomenon. This robustness is critical for deploying reward models in real-world applications where ground-truth verification is impractical or impossible.

4. **Reduced Reliance on Verifiable Rewards:** By achieving strong performance in RL optimization even without relying on ground-truth-based verifiable rewards, CRM offers a pathway to scale LLM reasoning improvements more broadly. The high cost and difficulty of acquiring verifiable rewards for complex reasoning tasks have been a bottleneck; CRM's ability to operate effectively in "VR disabled" settings alleviates this dependency, making LLM training more accessible and scalable.

5. **Improved Interpretability and Desirable Behaviors:** The framework's emphasis on precise credit assignment and its observed correlation with increased self-reflection behaviors during training could lead to LLMs that not only perform better but also exhibit more human-like, introspective reasoning processes. This could pave the way for more understandable and trustworthy AI reasoning agents.

6. **Cross-Domain and Cross-Sample Applicability:** The demonstrated effectiveness of CRM across different mathematical datasets and its successful generalization to non-mathematical domains (e.g., biology, history) highlights its broad applicability. Furthermore, its enhanced cross-sample comparability addresses a significant challenge in comparing and selecting reasoning trajectories from diverse queries, which is vital for many practical LLM applications.

7. **Catalyst for Future Research:** By establishing a principled framework for reward-model–driven RL for reasoning that moves beyond heavy reliance on ground truth, this work opens new avenues for research. Future directions could explore its application to more complex task formats, multimodal reasoning, or integrating CRM with advanced generative techniques to further generalize and optimize LLM reasoning in resource-constrained environments.
