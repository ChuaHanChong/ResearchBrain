# How Does Critical Batch Size Scale in Pre-training?

> [!summary] Summary
> This study establishes that the critical batch size (CBS) in large language model pre-training scales primarily with the total training data size, not the model's parameter count. It introduces and validates Exponential Weight Averaging (EWA) for efficient, target-loss-driven training and offers theoretical explanations for the observed scaling behaviors.

## Problem

- The precise scaling behavior of the critical batch size (CBS) in large language model pre-training, specifically its relationship with model and data sizes, was not fully understood.
- Previous scaling law studies often scaled model and data sizes jointly, making it difficult to ascertain their independent impact on optimization efficiency.
- Traditional fixed-duration training protocols limit flexibility and efficient exploration of optimal training strategies, especially when aiming for a target performance level.

## Method

- Researchers precisely defined ==critical batch size (CBS)== as the batch size incurring 20% overhead in steps to reach a target validation loss relative to linear scaling from an optimal small batch size.
- Controlled experiments systematically decoupled the effects of model size and data size on CBS, by holding one constant while varying the other.
- ==Exponential Weight Averaging (EWA)== was proposed and evaluated as a training strategy to flexibly reach target validation losses without predefined training durations or learning rate schedules.

## Results

- Empirically, CBS scaled as approximately **D^0.47** with data size (D) when model size was fixed, but only as **N^0.087** with model size (N) when data size was fixed, demonstrating its primary dependence on data.
- The 'Constant+EWA' strategy matched or exceeded the performance of cosine learning rate scheduling, providing comparable or improved training efficiency across various batch sizes.
- Theoretical analysis, leveraging infinite-width neural network limits and mini-batch SGD in linear regression, substantiated the empirical findings that CBS grows with data size but is invariant to model size in certain regimes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The critical batch size primarily scales with the amount of training data, showing a weak dependence on model size when data is held constant.
> - Exponential Weight Averaging (EWA) is an effective and flexible alternative to traditional learning rate schedules, particularly beneficial for large-batch and long-duration training by enabling target-loss-driven convergence.
> - Optimal Adam optimizer hyper-parameters (e.g., beta_2) are highly sensitive to training duration and batch size, requiring careful tuning beyond default values for peak efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of large-scale machine learning, optimization, and the emerging field of scaling laws for neural networks. The broader landscape is dominated by the increasing size and complexity of models, particularly large language models (LMs), and the immense computational resources required to train them.

Previous seminal works, such as those by McCandlish et al. (2018), Kaplan et al. (2020), and Hoffmann et al. (2022a, "Chinchilla"), established "scaling laws" that describe how model performance (e.g., final loss) depends on factors like model size, dataset size, and computational budget. These works have guided practitioners in allocating resources for optimal model training. Specifically, the Chinchilla paper highlighted the phenomenon of "under-training" in large models and proposed a compute-optimal scaling strategy where model size and data size should increase proportionally.

Within this context, the efficiency of optimization algorithms, particularly large-batch training, is crucial. Large batch sizes enable greater data parallelism across hardware accelerators, potentially reducing training time (You et al., 2017; Goyal et al., 2018). However, this benefit is not limitless; beyond a certain "critical batch size" (CBS), increasing the batch size can lead to diminishing returns in computational efficiency (McCandlish et al., 2018; Shallue et al., 2019). Understanding and predicting this CBS is therefore paramount for optimizing pre-training.

This paper addresses several critical gaps in the existing literature:
1. **Precise Formalism of CBS:** While the concept of CBS is recognized, a precise formalism relating it to model and data sizes, especially for pre-training, has been lacking. The authors propose a concrete measure of CBS as the point of 20% overhead relative to linear scaling.
2. **Decoupling Model and Data Size Effects:** Previous scaling law studies often scaled model and data sizes jointly (e.g., Chinchilla). This work systematically decouples these effects to understand their independent impact on CBS. Prior works studying optimal batch size (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024; Besiroglu et al., 2024; Porian et al., 2024) often focused on achieving minimum loss rather than the critical batch size that balances efficiency and performance, and also did not decouple model and data scales.
3. **Training Beyond Fixed Durations:** Many existing training protocols rely on fixed token budgets, which implicitly determine training duration and learning rate schedules. The authors introduce a methodology (Exponential Weight Averaging, EWA) that allows for flexible training durations, enabling exploration of optimization efficiency without being constrained by predefined schedules.
4. **Theoretical Justification:** The paper provides theoretical underpinnings for their empirical observations using advanced concepts like infinite-width neural networks (Yang and Hu, 2021) and analysis of mini-batch SGD in linear regression (Zou et al., 2023).

The work directly contributes to the ongoing discourse on efficient large model training, providing both practical guidance and fundamental theoretical understanding. It also distinguishes itself from concurrent work (e.g., Filatov et al., 2024) by its comprehensive approach to decoupling scaling effects, providing theoretical justifications, and addressing hyper-parameter optimization challenges across a broad range of settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to gain a deeper, more systematic understanding of the scaling behavior of the Critical Batch Size (CBS) in the pre-training of large autoregressive language models. This understanding is crucial for enabling more efficient and resource-optimized training strategies in an era where model sizes and training costs are continuously escalating.

The key motivations driving this study are:

1. **Optimizing Data Parallelism:** In large-scale model training, data parallelism is a primary strategy for distributing computation across multiple hardware accelerators. The efficiency of data parallelism is directly tied to the batch size. Increasing the batch size reduces the number of optimization steps but also increases the computational cost per step. The CBS marks the threshold where further increases in batch size lead to diminishing returns in terms of overall computational efficiency. Identifying and understanding how CBS scales is essential for practitioners to make informed decisions about the optimal degree of data parallelism, balancing training time with computational resources (FLOPs).
2. **Addressing Resource Constraints:** Training large models demands significant computational resources. Resource constraints compel practitioners to make difficult trade-offs between compute, data, and training time. By systematically analyzing CBS, the authors aim to provide insights that allow for more strategic resource allocation, potentially reducing wasted compute and accelerating development cycles.
3. **Decoupling Scaling Factors:** Previous research on scaling laws often scaled model size ($N$) and data size ($D$) proportionally (e.g., Chinchilla). This intertwined scaling makes it difficult to ascertain the independent impact of each factor on optimization metrics like CBS. A core motivation is to decouple these effects to isolate whether the growth of CBS is primarily driven by larger models or by larger datasets/training durations.
4. **Developing a Robust CBS Measurement:** The absence of a precise, universally accepted formalism for CBS in the literature posed a challenge. The authors aim to operationalize CBS by proposing a concrete measure: the batch size that results in a certain overhead (specifically, 20%) compared to ideal linear scaling in terms of steps to reach a target validation loss. This formalization provides a consistent metric for empirical investigation.
5. **Enabling Flexible Training Durations:** Traditional pre-training often relies on fixed token budgets and corresponding learning rate schedules. This limits flexibility when exploring optimal training strategies or trying to reach a specific performance target. A motivation is to develop and evaluate strategies, such as Exponential Weight Averaging (EWA), that allow training beyond fixed durations, making the experimental setup more adaptable and reflective of real-world scenarios where a target loss might be the primary goal.
6. **Providing Theoretical Justification:** Beyond empirical observations, the authors are motivated to provide theoretical explanations for the observed scaling behaviors. By leveraging tools from infinite-width neural network theory and least-squares regression analysis, they aim to solidify the understanding of why CBS scales with data size but remains relatively invariant to model size. This theoretical grounding adds depth and generality to the empirical findings.

In essence, the research seeks to provide a comprehensive guide for navigating the complexities of large-scale language model pre-training by illuminating the fundamental scaling properties of critical batch size, thereby contributing to more efficient and principled deep learning practices.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a rigorous mixed-methods approach, combining extensive empirical experimentation with theoretical analysis to investigate the scaling of critical batch size (CBS).

#### 3.1. Empirical Methodology and Experimental Design

The empirical investigation focused on pre-training a series of autoregressive transformer-based language models on the C4 dataset.

- **Model Architectures:** The authors trained models ranging in size from 85 million (M) to 1.2 billion (B) parameters. These models used a context length of 512 and standard transformer components (e.g., Adam optimizer).
- **Target Loss and CBS Definition:** A novel approach was taken to define and measure CBS.
 - **Target Validation Loss:** Instead of fixed training durations, the goal was to reach a *target validation loss*. For models in "Chinchilla settings" (where model and data size scale jointly), this target loss was determined by training a base model (e.g., 256 batch size) for a Chinchilla-optimal number of steps.
 - **CBS Measurement:** CBS was formally defined as the batch size where the number of steps required to reach the target validation loss is 20% higher than what would be predicted by perfect linear scaling from an optimal small batch size (specifically, 256). This involved fitting power laws to the relationship between the number of steps and batch size.
- **Hyper-parameter Management:**
 - **Extensive Sweeps:** The study involved comprehensive hyper-parameter sweeps for critical optimizer parameters, including learning rate, momentum (β1), and the second-moment decay rate (β2) of Adam.
 - **Training Beyond Fixed Durations:** To overcome the limitations of fixed training durations, the authors proposed and benchmarked **Exponential Weight Averaging (EWA)** (specifically, "Constant+EWA" strategy) as an alternative to common learning rate schedulers (cosine, WSD, schedule-free). EWA maintains a running average of model weights, enabling training to continue until a target loss is achieved without predefining the total steps. This strategy proved competitive, especially for large batch sizes.
 - **Warmup Steps and Weight Decay:** Ablation studies were conducted on warmup step fractions and the effect of weight decay. While weight decay showed minor improvements, it did not significantly impact CBS scaling, so it was disabled for the main experiments.
 - **Optimal Hyper-parameter Tuning:** Optimal values for β2 and EWA decay rate (τ) were identified through sweeps for each model and batch size combination, as these parameters were found to significantly affect training efficiency, especially for long-duration or small-batch training.
- **Controlled Scaling Experiments:** To decouple the effects of model size ($N$) and data size ($D$) on CBS, two key controlled experiments were performed:
 1. **Fixed Data Size (Scaling N):** Models of varying sizes (85M-1.2B) were trained on a *fixed total amount of data* (e.g., 3.07B tokens for 151M models) to reach specific target losses. This allowed for observing the impact of model size alone.
 2. **Fixed Model Size (Scaling D):** A single model size (302M) was trained on *varying amounts of data* (0.28x, 0.5x, 1x, 2x, 4x Chinchilla steps for batch size 256 runs), enabling the study of data size's effect in isolation.
- **Ablation Studies on Model Properties:**
 - **Context Length:** The impact of different context lengths (512 to 4096) on optimization efficiency was assessed using proxy models (151M).
 - **Width vs. Depth:** The study also compared scaling strategies by increasing model width (hidden size) versus increasing model depth (number of layers) to achieve larger model sizes, examining their influence on CBS and overall efficiency.
- **Evaluation:** Models were evaluated on a holdout C4 validation set. A hybrid evaluation protocol was used to ensure frequent evaluations, especially towards the end of training, to accurately capture the steps needed to reach the target loss.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Methodology

The theoretical approach aimed to provide a formal basis for the empirical observations regarding CBS scaling with model and data size.

- **Fixed Data Size, Scaling Model Size (Infinite-Width Limits):** The authors leveraged the concept of infinite-width neural networks (Yang and Hu, 2021; Bordelon and Pehlevan, 2022). They invoked the Maximal Update Parameterization (μP) framework, which suggests that beyond a certain width, the training dynamics and performance of neural networks become effectively independent of further increases in model size. This theory implies that CBS should become invariant to model size in this regime.
- **Fixed Model Size, Scaling Data Size (Least Squares Regression):** To analyze the impact of data size, the authors studied mini-batch SGD in a well-specified Gaussian linear regression problem. This involved:
 - Defining population risk and analyzing the dynamics of error vectors (bias and variance).
 - Characterizing the excess risk achieved by averaging mini-batch SGD iterates.
 - Applying "capacity and source conditions" from nonparametric linear regression literature (Caponnetto and De Vito, 2007) to model the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix and the energy of the optimal weight vector.
 - Deriving a theoretical expression for CBS that minimizes sequential runtime while achieving the optimal rate of excess risk, showing its dependence on data size ($D$).

By combining controlled, large-scale empirical experiments with rigorous theoretical analysis, the authors aimed to provide a comprehensive and robust answer to how critical batch size scales in pre-training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several crucial empirical findings and theoretical justifications regarding the scaling of critical batch size (CBS) in large language model pre-training.

#### 4.1. Empirical Findings

The central empirical finding of the paper is the decoupled scaling behavior of CBS:

- **CBS Scales Primarily with Data Size, Not Model Size:**
 - **Chinchilla Setting (Joint Scaling):** When model size ($N$) and data size ($D$) are scaled up together (as in compute-optimal Chinchilla training), CBS indeed increases. For instance, the fitted scaling law showed CBS $\propto N^{0.47}$.
 - **Fixed Data Size (Scaling N, D fixed):** Crucially, when the total data size ($D$) was held constant, increasing the model size ($N$) resulted in CBS remaining nearly invariant. The fitted scaling law in this regime showed CBS $\propto N^{0.087}$, indicating a very weak dependence.
 - **Fixed Model Size (Scaling D, N fixed):** Conversely, when the model size ($N$) was held constant and only the data size ($D$) (or training duration) was increased, CBS was observed to increase, similarly to the Chinchilla setting. The fitted scaling law in this case showed CBS $\propto D^{0.47}$.
 - **Qualitative Understanding:** Side-by-side comparisons confirmed that models of different sizes trained on the same data quantity exhibited similar CBS values, reinforcing the conclusion that CBS growth is predominantly driven by increased data size rather than model size.

- **Impact of Hyper-parameter Choices and Training Strategies:**
 - **Exponential Weight Averaging (EWA):** The "Constant+EWA" strategy was found to be highly effective for training beyond fixed durations. It consistently improved model training efficiency compared to a constant learning rate without EWA and matched the performance of established decaying schemes like cosine scheduling, especially for large batch sizes. EWA eliminates the need to predefine total training durations, offering greater flexibility.
 - **Momentum (Adam $\beta_1$):** A large momentum value (e.g., $\beta_1 = 0.95$) was found to be critical for efficient language model pre-training, significantly outperforming training without momentum, especially at larger batch sizes.
 - **Second Moment Decay Rate (Adam $\beta_2$):** The optimal choice of $\beta_2$ was context-dependent. A smaller $\beta_2$ (e.g., 0.95) was beneficial for large-batch training over shorter durations, while a larger $\beta_2$ (e.g., 0.9995) improved efficiency for long-duration training and substantially for small batch sizes.
 - **Warmup Steps:** The optimal warmup step ratio (e.g., 0.25 of total steps) was important for achieving efficient training, particularly for larger batch sizes.
 - **Weight Decay:** Weight decay offered only a slight performance improvement and had minimal impact on the critical batch size, leading the authors to disable it in their main experiments to simplify analysis.

- **Model Scaling and Architecture Ablations:**
 - **Context Length:** Different context lengths (from 512 to 4096) exhibited similar relative optimization efficiency across various batch sizes, justifying the use of 512 for most experiments.
 - **Width vs. Depth:** When scaling up models, increasing either width or depth in compute-optimal regimes had similar effects on CBS and overall efficiency. This finding provides practical guidance, suggesting that scaling width, which is often favored for parallelization, does not come with a CBS penalty compared to scaling depth.

#### 4.2. Theoretical Implications

The empirical findings were substantiated by theoretical analyses:

- **Fixed Data Size, Scaling Model Size (Infinite-Width Limits):** The theory of infinite-width neural networks (e.g., Maximal Update Parameterization, μP) suggests that beyond a certain width, the training dynamics and performance become effectively independent of further increases in model size. This supports the empirical observation that CBS remains nearly invariant when model size is scaled up while data size is fixed. The critical batch size does not necessarily increase with larger models beyond a certain point.

- **Fixed Model Size, Scaling Data Size (Least Squares Regression):** Analysis of mini-batch SGD in a Gaussian linear regression problem provided a theoretical basis for CBS scaling with data size.
 - **Optimal Hyper-parameters:** Under certain capacity and source conditions (power-law decay of eigenvalues and optimal weight vector components), the theory showed that when the "variance error" (due to noise) tends to dominate the "bias error" (due to approximation capacity), the CBS $B^*(D)$ scales as $B^*(D) = \Theta(D^c)$, where $c \geq 0$. Specifically, $0 < c < 1/2$ indicates that CBS grows with data size.
 - **Runtime Optimization:** This theoretical result implies that increasing the batch size as a function of data size can reduce sequential runtime without compromising the optimal rate of excess risk minimization, particularly in regimes where variance error is dominant.

In summary, the results demonstrate a clear and robust scaling law for CBS, predominantly linking its growth to the available data rather than the model's parameter count. This has profound implications for how large models should be pre-trained efficiently.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the field of large-scale machine learning, with both immediate practical implications and long-term impact on the theoretical understanding of deep learning optimization.

#### 5.1. Practical Implications

1. **Optimized Resource Allocation for Pre-training:** The most direct and impactful finding is that Critical Batch Size (CBS) scales primarily with **data size**, not model size. This insight fundamentally changes how practitioners should think about resource allocation for data parallelism:
 - **Greater Data Parallelism with More Data:** When increasing the dataset size (or training duration), practitioners can confidently employ significantly larger batch sizes without sacrificing computational efficiency (measured in FLOPs). This enables greater data parallelism, which can substantially reduce wall-clock training time for large models.
 - **No Free Lunch for Model Size:** Conversely, simply increasing model size without commensurately increasing data will not necessitate or benefit from larger batch sizes beyond a certain point, preventing wasted compute on inefficient large-batch training.
 - **Guidance for Cloud Resource Management:** This understanding can directly inform decisions about the number of GPUs/TPUs to allocate for training, optimizing the trade-off between communication overheads (often associated with very small batches) and convergence slowdowns (from excessively large batches).

2. **Flexible Training Protocols with EWA:** The validation of Exponential Weight Averaging (EWA) as an effective strategy for training beyond fixed durations is a valuable practical contribution.
 - **Target-Loss Driven Training:** EWA allows practitioners to focus on achieving a target validation loss rather than being constrained by pre-defined token budgets or learning rate schedules. This flexibility is crucial in research and development cycles where the optimal training duration is often unknown beforehand.
 - **Simplified Hyper-parameter Management:** It simplifies the complex task of designing and tuning learning rate schedules, particularly for exploratory experiments or scenarios with varying data availability.

3. **Refined Optimizer Hyper-parameter Tuning:** The detailed ablation studies on Adam's $\beta_1$ (momentum) and $\beta_2$ (second moment decay rate) provide crucial guidance for optimizing training. The finding that optimal $\beta_2$ values differ significantly for short vs. long training durations and small vs. large batch sizes highlights the need for dynamic or context-aware hyper-parameter tuning, moving beyond universal default values.

4. **Informed Architectural Scaling:** The observation that scaling model width or depth has similar impacts on CBS in compute-optimal regimes offers practical advice for model architects. Since wider models are often easier to parallelize without incurring additional latency, this finding supports prioritizing width scaling without concerns about adverse effects on batch size efficiency.

#### 5.2. Broader Impact and Significance

1. **Advancing Scaling Law Research:** The paper moves beyond merely predicting final loss or optimal hyper-parameters. It provides a formal definition and empirical measurement of a critical **efficiency notion** (CBS) and systematically decouples the effects of model and data scales. This refined understanding contributes significantly to the evolving science of scaling laws, offering a more granular view of how different factors impact training dynamics.

2. **Theoretical Grounding for Empirical Observations:** By providing theoretical justifications rooted in infinite-width neural networks and least-squares regression, the research elevates the empirical findings from mere observations to principled insights. This enhances confidence in the conclusions and provides a framework for generalizing them to other architectures or tasks. The connection between data size and CBS in the least squares problem offers a simplified yet powerful model for understanding why variance-dominated regimes benefit from larger batch sizes.

3. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a strong foundation for future research in several areas:
 - **Dynamic Batch Sizing:** The insights into CBS scaling with data could inspire adaptive batch sizing strategies that dynamically adjust during training based on data progress.
 - **Beyond Transformers:** Investigating if these CBS scaling laws hold for other model architectures (e.g., multimodal models, vision transformers) or different data modalities.
 - **Hardware-Software Co-design:** A deeper understanding of CBS can inform the design of future AI hardware and distributed training frameworks, optimizing them for varying model and data scales.
 - **Fundamental Optimization Theory:** The theoretical derivations contribute to the broader field of optimization, particularly in understanding mini-batch SGD behavior in high-dimensional and over-parameterized settings.

In conclusion, this paper provides a robust, empirically validated, and theoretically supported framework for understanding how critical batch size scales in the demanding environment of large language model pre-training. Its findings empower practitioners to make more intelligent design and resource allocation choices, ultimately accelerating the development of next-generation AI models.
