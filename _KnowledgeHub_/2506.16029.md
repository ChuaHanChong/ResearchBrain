# EvoLM: In Search of Lost Language Model Training Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> A systematic analysis of multi-stage large language model training dynamics investigates how design choices across pre-training, continued pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning impact model capabilities, providing a transparent framework and introducing outcome reward model scores as a reliable proxy for generative task evaluation.

## Problem

- A lack of systematic, transparent, and reproducible understanding of the intricate scaling behaviors and design trade-offs in the multi-stage training lifecycle of large language models (LLMs).
- Existing scaling laws primarily focus on the pre-training phase, neglecting the complex interactions and effects of subsequent continued pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning.
- Difficulty in reliably evaluating downstream generative reasoning tasks post-training, as traditional language modeling metrics like perplexity become less indicative.

## Method

- Developed **EvoLM**, a transparent experimental framework and ==model suite==, by training over 100 LLaMA-2 based models (0.5B, 1B, 4B parameters) from scratch with full control over hyperparameters and data at each stage.
- Systematically investigated the impact of computational budgets and model sizes across four sequential training stages: pre-training, continued pre-training (CPT), supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL).
- Implemented a comprehensive evaluation protocol for upstream cloze tasks and downstream generative reasoning tasks (both in-domain and out-of-domain), utilizing an outcome reward model (ORM) score as a new proxy metric.

## Results

- Pre-training beyond **80x**-**160x** model size for the studied configurations shows diminishing returns, and in some cases, degradation in downstream task performance.
- A **5%** replay budget of general-domain data during continued pre-training effectively mitigates catastrophic forgetting, preserving both upstream and downstream performance.
- Outcome Reward Model (ORM) scores exhibit strong predictive power for downstream task accuracy (Pearson correlation **0.62**-**0.84**), serving as a more reliable unsupervised validation metric than perplexity during post-training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Excessive general-domain pre-training and supervised fine-tuning lead to diminishing returns and can degrade performance on certain downstream tasks, suggesting saturation points.
> - Continued pre-training on domain-specific data induces catastrophic forgetting of general knowledge, which is effectively mitigated by small replay budgets (e.g., 5%) of pre-training data.
> - Reinforcement learning primarily increases the probability of sampling high-quality rollouts by sharpening confidence in already-correct outputs, rather than fundamentally enhancing reasoning capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the contemporary landscape of large language model research, specifically addressing critical challenges related to understanding, optimizing, and reproducing the complex multi-stage training process.

Historically, the scaling laws introduced by works like Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla, 2022) established quantitative relationships between model performance (measured by pre-training log-loss/perplexity) and factors like model size, data quantity, and computational budget. However, these early scaling laws primarily focused on the pre-training phase. Modern LLM development has evolved into a multi-stage pipeline, including pre-training, continued pre-training (CPT), supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL) from human feedback. The interactions between these stages are highly complex, and their combined effects on downstream performance are not well understood.

Existing literature often struggles with several limitations:
1. **Opaqueness of Training Regimes**: Many state-of-the-art LLMs, especially proprietary ones, lack transparent reporting of their full training details across all stages. Even open-weight models often rely on off-the-shelf base models whose training specifics are not fully controlled or disclosed, introducing confounding factors.
2. **Limited Control over Variables**: Studies often analyze models with varying sizes, pre-training data, and post-training data components without systematic control, making it difficult to isolate the impact of specific design choices.
3. **Reliance on Intermediate Checkpoints**: Evaluations sometimes use intermediate checkpoints from longer training runs, which may not represent optimally trained models due to incomplete learning rate decay or other optimization issues, leading to potentially misleading conclusions about model capabilities.
4. **Training-Inference Mismatch**: Predicting downstream problem-solving performance from upstream language modeling metrics (like perplexity) remains challenging due to the inherent differences between these tasks and the non-smooth nature of downstream improvements (Schaeffer et al., 2023).

EvoLM directly tackles these gaps by providing a *systematic, transparent, and reproducible* framework. It extends the scaling law paradigm beyond pre-training to encompass the entire LLM lifecycle. By training over 100 models from scratch with full control over hyperparameters and data at each stage, the authors provide empirical evidence to refine current understandings of scaling, catastrophic forgetting (French, 1999), and the role of various fine-tuning techniques (SFT, RL). It also contributes to the ongoing debate about whether RL truly enhances fundamental reasoning capabilities or primarily amplifies existing knowledge and confidence (Yue et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). The introduction of ORM scores as reliable proxy metrics further addresses the challenge of evaluating generative reasoning tasks in a scalable manner, complementing traditional perplexity measures that often fail post-training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:

1. **Systematically Analyze LLM Training Dynamics**: Provide a comprehensive and systematic investigation into how design choices at each stage—pre-training, continued pre-training (CPT), supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL)—impact language models' capabilities.
2. **Enhance Transparency and Reproducibility**: Establish an end-to-end development pipeline using open toolkits and data sources to ensure all experiments are transparent and fully reproducible by the broader research community. This includes releasing all trained models, datasets, and the entire training and evaluation pipeline.
3. **Quantify Scaling Behaviors**: Characterize how varying computational budgets (tokens, examples, epochs) and model sizes (1B, 4B parameters) influence both upstream language modeling performance (perplexity) and downstream problem-solving capabilities (generative rollout performance) across in-domain (ID) and out-of-domain (OOD) generalization.
4. **Identify Key Insights and Trade-offs**: Discover critical insights, such as diminishing returns from excessive training, effective strategies for mitigating catastrophic forgetting during CPT, the role of CPT in bridging training phases, and the intricate trade-offs involved in configuring SFT and RL.
5. **Address Gaps in Current Understanding**: Fill existing knowledge gaps regarding the precise impact of multi-stage training on model behaviors, especially concerning reasoning abilities and generalization, which often remain obscured in less transparent studies.

The motivation stems from the recognition that while scaling LLMs has enabled remarkable applications, the complex, multi-stage training process makes it difficult for developers to understand the impact of their choices. This opacity hinders efficient resource allocation, reliable performance forecasting, and the responsible development of these powerful models. By illuminating these dynamics, the authors aim to provide actionable guidance for practitioners and a robust foundation for future research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **EvoLM**, a transparent model suite and experimental framework designed for systematic analysis.

**Model Architecture and Size:**
- All models are decoder-only autoregressive LMs, initialized using the **LLaMA-2 architecture**.
- Two main model sizes are studied: **1 Billion (1B) and 4 Billion (4B) parameters**. A 0.5B model is also used for some pre-training studies.
- Over **100 LMs** were trained from scratch.
- All models were trained with **complete learning rate decay**, and only final checkpoints were used for evaluation to ensure optimal performance for each configuration.

**Training Pipeline (Four Sequential Stages):**
1. **Pre-training**:
 - **Data**: FineWeb-Edu (publicly available corpus).
 - **Token Budgets**: Ranged from 20x model size (compute-optimal based on Chinchilla scaling law) up to 320 Billion (320B) tokens, to investigate effects of mild to excessive over-training.
2. **Continued Pre-training (CPT)**:
 - **Data**: FineMath (domain-specific mathematical text).
 - **Token Budgets**: 2 Billion (2B) to 42 Billion (42B) tokens.
 - **Forgetting Mitigation**: Incorporated pre-training data replay strategies (e.g., interleaving FineWeb-Edu during FineMath CPT) to prevent catastrophic forgetting.
3. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**:
 - **Data**: QA pairs augmented from GSM8K and MATH datasets, collected from MetaMathQA, OpenMathInstruct2, and NuminaMath. Low-quality prompts were filtered using model correctness consistency.
 - **Compute**: Varied by number of epochs (1 to 32) and dataset size (50K to 400K examples).
4. **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**:
 - **Algorithm**: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
 - **Reward**: Binary verifiable reward.
 - **Data**: Same data sources as SFT but with no overlap with the SFT dataset.
 - **Compute**: Varied by number of epochs (1 to 32) and dataset size (50K to 400K examples).

**Evaluation Protocols:**
Models were evaluated on both upstream and downstream tasks, considering in-domain (ID) and out-of-domain (OOD) generalization.

- **Upstream Cloze Tasks**: Assess general language modeling capabilities via next-token prediction (0-shot accuracy on HellaSwag, Winogrande, PIQA, OBQA, ARC-Easy/Challenge). Used for pre-trained and CPT models.
- **Downstream Generative Tasks**: Assess problem-solving abilities in a conversational setting. Used for SFT and RL-finetuned models.
 - **In-Domain (ID) Math Reasoning**: GSM8K-Platinum, MATH.
 - **Out-of-Domain (OOD) General Reasoning**: CRUXEval (code), BGQA (logic), TabMWP (table), StrategyQA (commonsense).
 - **Evaluation Metrics**:
 - **Accuracy**: Pass@1 (greedy), Maj@16 (majority vote from 16 samples), RM@16 (highest ORM score from 16 samples), Pass@16 (any correct answer among 16 samples), Correct Ratio@16 (ratio of correct solutions in groups with at least one correct solution).
 - **ORM Score**: An outcome reward model (Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2) assigned scalar scores to generated solutions as a proxy for quality.

**Reproducibility**:
The authors commit to releasing all pre-trained and post-trained models, training datasets for all stages, and the entire training and evaluation pipeline to facilitate open research and reproducibility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yields twelve key takeaways across the different training stages:

**Scaling Up Pre-training Compute:**
- **Takeaway 1**: Excessive general-domain pre-training leads to diminishing returns and can even degrade performance on some downstream tasks (saturation around 80x to 160x model size in this study).
- **Takeaway 2**: Under limited pre-training budgets, smaller post-trained models can outperform larger ones. Conversely, once pre-training tokens reach saturation, increasing model size translates into substantial gains in post-training performance.

**Scaling Up Continued Pre-training (CPT) Compute:**
- **Takeaway 3**: CPT on domain-specific data induces catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained general knowledge, harming both upstream and downstream performance. A small replay budget (e.g., 5% of general domain data) effectively mitigates this degradation.
- **Takeaway 4**: Domain-specific post-training requires adequate domain-specific CPT data; without it, SFT performance is suboptimal, and RL can even degrade performance.
- **Takeaway 5**: As domain-specific CPT data increases, in-domain downstream performance steadily improves, and SFT models benefit more from subsequent RL fine-tuning.
- **Takeaway 6**: With sufficient domain-specific CPT data, post-training on in-domain tasks not only improves in-domain performance but also generalizes effectively to out-of-domain tasks.

**Scaling Up Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Compute:**
- **Takeaway 7**: Excessive SFT improves in-domain (ID) performance with diminishing returns but does not necessarily improve and can even degrade out-of-domain (OOD) performance, indicating over-specialization.
- **Takeaway 8**: Excessive SFT, especially with overly many epochs, can limit the effectiveness of further RL improvements.

**Scaling Up Reinforcement Learning (RL) Compute:**
- **Takeaway 9**: RL with excessive epochs or examples improves downstream performance on both ID and OOD tasks, but with diminishing returns (saturation at 4-8 epochs or 50-100K examples in this study).
- **Takeaway 10**: Beyond the saturation regime, RL primarily increases the probability of sampling high-quality rollouts by sharpening confidence in already-correct outputs, rather than enhancing models’ fundamental reasoning capabilities (Pass@16 degrades while Correct Ratio@16 increases).
- **Takeaway 11**: Under a constrained downstream data budget (e.g., 100K examples), allocating more examples to SFT maximizes in-domain gains but weakens OOD generalization. Conversely, allocating more to RL improves OOD performance. Additionally, for a fixed compute budget, deeper policy optimization per sample (more epochs on a moderate dataset) is generally more cost-effective than broader data coverage (larger dataset, fewer epochs) for RL.

**Additional Studies:**
- **Takeaway 12**: Intermediate checkpoints from longer pre-training runs are not reliable surrogates for fully trained smaller models. Standalone models trained completely for a specific token count perform better than intermediate checkpoints extracted at the same token count from a longer run.
- **Observation**: Outcome Reward Model (ORM) scores exhibit consistently strong predictive power for downstream task accuracy across various datasets (Pearson correlation coefficients from 0.62 to 0.84), serving as a more reliable unsupervised validation metric than perplexity during post-training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The EvoLM study makes several significant contributions to the field of language model research and development:

1. **Empirical Foundation for Multi-Stage Training**: By providing the first systematic and transparent analysis of LLM training across all four major stages with over 100 models trained from scratch, the paper offers invaluable empirical evidence. This foundational work moves beyond anecdotal observations to establish quantifiable insights into the complex interplay of various training components.
2. **Guidance for Resource Allocation**: The findings offer practical guidance for practitioners on optimizing computational resources. For instance, the identification of diminishing returns in pre-training and post-training helps avoid wasteful "over-training." The study provides concrete recommendations for balancing general-domain knowledge retention with domain-specific adaptation during CPT and for allocating data between SFT and RL based on desired ID versus OOD performance.
3. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Open Science**: The commitment to open-sourcing all models, datasets, and the entire training/evaluation pipeline is a critical step towards fostering reproducibility and collaborative research in a field often characterized by opaque methodologies and inaccessible models. This enables other researchers to build upon, verify, and extend these findings.
4. **Refined Understanding of LLM Capabilities**: The detailed analysis clarifies the nature of improvements gained at each stage. It underscores that while RL can boost confidence and generation quality, its ability to fundamentally enhance reasoning beyond what's learned in pre-training or SFT is limited, primarily amplifying existing capabilities. This nuanced understanding is crucial for designing more effective and targeted training strategies.
5. **Validation Metrics for Generative Tasks**: The discovery of ORM scores as reliable unsupervised proxy metrics for downstream generative task accuracy is highly impactful. It addresses a long-standing challenge of evaluating complex generative outputs without extensive human annotation or relying on potentially miscalibrated perplexity metrics, particularly valuable in data-constrained scenarios.
6. **Challenges to Common Practices**: The finding that intermediate checkpoints are not reliable surrogates for fully trained models serves as a strong caution against a common shortcut in research, advocating for rigorous, complete training schedules when comparing models of different "sizes" in terms of tokens seen.

**Limitations and Future Directions**:
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Model Scale**: The study focuses on smaller models (up to 4B parameters). Future work needs to investigate if these trends generalize to much larger, frontier models.
- **Scope of Objectives**: The focus is on reasoning-centric post-training. Exploring other objectives like safety alignment, instruction-following, tool-calling, and coding tasks would provide a broader picture.
- **RL Methodology**: Only PPO with verifiable rewards was used. Investigating alternative RL algorithms (e.g., DPO, RLAIF) and reward mechanisms could yield further insights.

Despite these limitations, EvoLM provides a robust framework and a wealth of empirical insights that will undoubtedly influence how researchers and developers approach the complex, multi-stage training of future language models, making the process more transparent, efficient, and understandable.
