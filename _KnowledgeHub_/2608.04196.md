# SiMDex: Mining Similar Egocentric Videos for Cross-Embodiment Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A similarity-based data mining framework, SiMDex, leverages human egocentric videos to improve Vision-Language-Action models for dexterous manipulation. This approach enhances overall task success rates by 13.4 percentage points and reduces the need for robot-specific data by approximately four-fold.

## Problem

- High-Degrees-of-Freedom (DoF) dexterous manipulation requires vast amounts of robot-specific training data, which is costly and difficult to collect, leading to data scarcity.
- Large-scale human egocentric video datasets are often heterogeneous, meaning only a small fraction of the data is relevant to specific robotic tasks, and indiscriminate use introduces noise.
- Existing methods lack the ability for scalable, fine-grained, task-aware retrieval from massive cross-embodiment human video pools without requiring modifications to robot policy architectures.

## Method

- **SiMDex** develops a ==unified morphology-agnostic representation== for human and robot hands, focusing on wrist-local fingertip positions to enable meaningful cross-embodiment comparisons in a shared 42-dimensional action space.
- Implements a three-stage ==similarity-based data mining== pipeline (Recall, Ranking, Re-ranking) to efficiently retrieve high-quality, task-relevant human demonstrations from tens of millions of samples.
- Integrates the mined human data with robot demonstrations using a ==masked supervision== strategy, allowing a standard VLA model (e.g., GR-Dexter) to be trained without architectural modifications.

## Results

- SiMDex increased the overall success rate for dexterous manipulation tasks from **47.7%** to **61.1%** (**+13.4 percentage points**) compared to a baseline trained with randomly sampled human data.
- The framework enabled comparable performance using approximately **6 hours** of robot demonstrations, a nearly **four-fold** reduction from the **~25 hours** required by the baseline model.
- SiMDex consistently outperformed the baseline across various robot data scales, showing its largest gains (**+17.2%**) at 0.5x robot data and maintaining a stable performance floor even when robot data was scarce.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A multi-stage retrieval cascade, combining broad cues (pose, language) with fine-grained motion dynamics and optical flow-based visual verification, progressively refines the quality of mined human demonstrations.
> - Representing manipulation actions and states using wrist-local fingertip positions provides an effective morphology-agnostic common ground for transferring skills between human and robot embodiments.
> - Selectively mining a small, high-quality subset of human data (less than 5% of the total pool) for specific tasks yields substantially better results and data efficiency than random sampling, particularly in low-robot-data regimes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in SiMDex is situated within the rapidly evolving field of robotic manipulation, particularly concerning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and their training data requirements. VLA models have demonstrated significant advancements in robotic control, with performance typically correlating with the volume of training data. However, the collection of robot-specific data is expensive, time-consuming, and requires specialized teleoperation expertise, creating a bottleneck for scaling these models.

To address this, researchers have explored alternative data sources, with egocentric human manipulation videos emerging as a promising avenue. Large-scale egocentric datasets, such as Ego4D, Ego-Exo4D, and EgoDex, are continually expanding, offering thousands of hours of human activity. While these datasets provide a vast pool of potential knowledge for generalist robot policies, their heterogeneous nature—spanning a multitude of daily activities—means that only a fraction of the data is directly relevant to specific robotic manipulation tasks. Naively incorporating all available human data can introduce noise, dilute task-relevant supervision, and hinder learning efficiency. Conversely, ignoring this data entirely foregoes the potential for cross-embodiment knowledge transfer and generalization that VLA models can leverage from human demonstrations.

Prior work in data curation for robot learning has largely focused on filtering or reweighting demonstrations within existing robot datasets, or retrieving behaviors solely from offline robot data. Some recent studies have utilized retrieved human videos to assist policy learning, typically by providing auxiliary inputs or in-context exemplars, which often necessitates modifications to the policy architecture. What remains a challenge is the ability to perform task-aware, per-demonstration retrieval from massive cross-embodiment human video pools (on the order of tens of millions of samples) based on fine-grained action-level similarity, and to directly integrate this curated data into VLA training without altering the model architecture or training procedure.

SiMDex addresses this gap by presenting a framework for efficient, targeted data mining from large-scale egocentric human video corpora. It positions itself as a complementary, model-agnostic approach that aims to enhance the data-efficiency of any VLA pipeline, particularly for dexterous manipulation tasks that are especially challenging due to expanded action spaces and contact-rich dynamics, where real robot data is exceptionally scarce. By reframing human data selection as a recommendation problem, akin to industrial recommendation systems, SiMDex seeks to leverage the benefits of large-scale human data collection twice: first for broad pre-training, and then for precise, task-specific post-training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and scalable method for efficiently utilizing large-scale egocentric human video data to enhance robot dexterous manipulation, particularly in the context of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model post-training. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Dexterous Manipulation:** Dexterous manipulation with high-Degrees-of-Freedom (DoF) robotic hands presents significant challenges compared to simpler gripper operations. The complexity of action spaces and contact-rich dynamics makes collecting sufficient real-world robot data exceedingly difficult and costly. Existing robot datasets for dexterous hands are often orders of magnitude smaller than those for grippers, creating a bottleneck for advancing VLA models in this domain. SiMDex aims to alleviate this bottleneck by providing a complementary data source.

2. **Improving Efficiency of Large-Scale Human Egocentric Data:** While there has been an explosive growth in large-scale egocentric human video datasets (e.g., EgoDex, Ego4D), the question of how to efficiently and effectively use this data for robot learning remains. These datasets are inherently heterogeneous, containing a vast array of activities only a small fraction of which are directly relevant to a specific manipulation task. Indiscriminate use of such data can introduce noise, dilute useful signals, and lead to inefficient learning. The motivation is to move beyond naive data mixing and towards a more targeted, task-aware selection process.

3. **Leveraging Cross-Embodiment Knowledge More Effectively:** Human egocentric videos contain a wealth of knowledge regarding manipulation skills. Pre-training VLA models on this broad data allows for the acquisition of cross-embodiment knowledge and generalization capabilities. The motivation is to capitalize on this pre-trained knowledge more precisely during the post-training phase for specific target tasks, ensuring that the most relevant human demonstrations are revisited and utilized.

4. **Enabling Scalable, Fine-Grained, and Architecture-Agnostic Data Curation:** Existing data curation methods in robot learning often operate at a different scope, either within robot datasets or by providing retrieved human videos as auxiliary inputs that require changes to the policy architecture. A key motivation for SiMDex is to perform task-aware, per-demonstration retrieval from massive, cross-embodiment human video pools based on fine-grained action-level similarity. Furthermore, this process should directly augment VLA training without requiring modifications to the underlying VLA architecture or training procedure, thereby increasing its applicability and ease of integration.

5. **Reframing Data Selection as a Recommendation Problem:** Drawing inspiration from industrial recommendation systems that efficiently query billion-scale catalogs, SiMDex is motivated to cast human data selection for VLA post-training as a recommendation problem. This approach aims to progressively distill task-relevant subsets from a vast human video pool, making the process scalable and practical for tens of millions of samples.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to enable robots to learn dexterous manipulation skills more effectively and efficiently by intelligently curating and leveraging the abundant, but often noisy, reservoir of human egocentric video data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SiMDex is conceptualized as a similarity-based data mining framework designed to retrieve high-quality, task-relevant human data from large-scale egocentric video pools to enhance the post-training of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for dexterous manipulation. The methodology is structured around a lightweight, scalable similarity cascade that operates on a morphology-agnostic action space, aiming for practicality in mining tens of millions of samples.

#### 3.1. Unified Morphology-Agnostic Representation

A foundational component of SiMDex is the creation of a unified representation that abstracts away the kinematic and morphological differences between robot and human hands. This ensures that similarity comparisons are meaningful across different embodiments.

- **Fingertip-centric Geometric Representation:** The framework adopts a representation focused on fingertip positions and wrist poses, which captures task-relevant contact structure while remaining independent of specific joint-level kinematics.
- **Wrist-Local Retargeting:** For both human and robot demonstrations, per-frame wrist poses ($T_t \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}$) and fingertip positions in world coordinates ($q_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$) are recovered. Each fingertip position is then re-expressed relative to its corresponding wrist frame as $q_{loc_t} = T_t^{-1} q_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$. This transformation isolates the intrinsic grasp geometry from the absolute workspace location.
- **Per-Hand State:** The five wrist-local fingertip positions for each hand are concatenated to form a 15-dimensional per-hand state ($p_t \in \mathbb{R}^{15}$).
- **Wrist Action:** The wrist action ($d_t \in \mathbb{R}^6$) is defined as a local delta in the current wrist frame, comprising a translational part and a rotational part derived from relative rotation matrices.
- **Data Sources and Preprocessing:**
 - **Robot Demonstrations:** Collected via bimanual teleoperation, with wrist and fingertip states derived from forward kinematics.
 - **Human Demonstrations:** Sourced from EgoDex, which provides tracked wrist and fingertip transforms from egocentric videos. These trajectories undergo preprocessing, including transformation to a body-centered frame (to remove ego-motion), filtering for hand visibility and velocity, and temporal smoothing, before the same retargeting procedure is applied.
- **Shared Action Space:** For bimanual manipulation, the shared per-timestep action ($a_t$) concatenates the left and right wrist deltas and fingertip states, resulting in a 42-dimensional vector ($a_t = (d_{L_t}, d_{R_t}, p_{L_t}, p_{R_t}) \in \mathbb{R}^{42}$). This Cartesian, wrist-relative representation is invariant to workspace location and serves as the basis for both data mining and cross-embodiment supervision.
- **Robot-Specific Actions:** Robot samples also include additional 46 dimensions representing arm and hand joint actions. These are used exclusively for robot-specific supervision during VLA training and are not part of the morphology-agnostic representation for human-robot similarity computation.

#### 3.2. Similarity-Based Data Mining Pipeline

Given a set of robot demonstrations (anchors, $D_r$) and a large pool of human demonstrations (targets, $D_h$), the goal is to retrieve a subset ($D_h^* \subset D_h$) that exhibits high similarity to the robot anchors in hand configuration, language semantics, and motion dynamics. Due to the massive scale of $D_h$ (tens of millions of samples), an exhaustive comparison is infeasible. SiMDex employs a three-stage cascade of increasing computational cost and granularity:

- **Stage I: Recall (Broad Candidate Retrieval):** This initial stage aims to efficiently retrieve a broad set of candidate human samples using lightweight signals.
 - **Pose Similarity:** The initial fingertip state is normalized into a 15-dimensional descriptor ($\hat{p} \in \mathbb{R}^{15}$), and nearest neighbors are identified using Euclidean distance, capturing similar initial grasp postures.
 - **Language Similarity:** Each language instruction is encoded into a 384-dimensional sentence embedding ($e \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$), and candidates are retrieved based on cosine similarity, reflecting task semantics.
 - **Combination:** The rankings from pose and language similarity are combined using rank fusion to generate a preliminary candidate set for each robot anchor.

- **Stage II: Ranking (Fine-grained Motion Ranking):** This stage refines the candidates by assessing fine-grained motion similarity over a 30-step future action sequence. Four complementary components are used:
 - **Wrist Translation Waveform and Rotation Waveform:** These compact speed profiles capture the rhythm and dynamics of wrist movements.
 - **Finger Trajectory ($F_{fg} \in \mathbb{R}^{30 \times 15}$):** Preserves the spatial structure of finger movements.
 - **Wrist Trajectory ($F_{ee} \in \mathbb{R}^{31 \times 3}$):** Preserves the spatial structure of wrist end-effector movements.
 - **Scoring and Deduplication:** Each component yields a per-component rank. These ranks are summed to form a fused score ($r = r_{tr} + r_{rot} + r_{fg} + r_{ee}$). The top-ranked samples are then deduplicated by their source trajectory, retaining only the highest-ranked entry from each unique source. This deduplicated output constitutes the final mined subset ($D_h^*$) used for VLA training.

- **Stage III: Re-ranking (Embodiment-Agnostic Visual Verification):** This optional stage provides a visual verification layer using optical flow, which captures pixel-level motion patterns independently of hand morphology and retargeting accuracy.
 - **Method:** For each anchor-target pair, dense optical flow is aggregated into a clip-level descriptor. These descriptors are then used to re-score candidates based on their similarity, offering a visually grounded validation of motion alignment.

#### 3.3. VLA Training

The mined human data ($D_h^*$) is integrated with the robot demonstrations ($D_r$) to train a VLA model.

- **Data Streams:** The two data sources are combined and loaded as independently weighted data streams.
- **Architecture:** The base VLA model used is GR-Dexter, a $\pi^0$-like flow-matching VLA for dexterous manipulation. This model comprises a vision-language backbone that encodes visual observations and language instructions, and an action decoder that predicts a future action chunk over a horizon ($H=30$). Crucially, SiMDex does not modify this VLA architecture or its training procedure; it only changes the source of human data.
- **Cross-Embodiment Supervision:**
 - **Unified Action Space:** Both robot and human data are mapped to an 88-dimensional action space. This space includes the 42 shared dimensions (wrist deltas and fingertip states) and 46 robot-specific dimensions (arm and hand joint actions).
 - **Masked Supervision:** Robot samples are supervised across all 88 dimensions. Human samples are supervised only on the 42 shared dimensions; the robot-specific dimensions are filled with placeholders and excluded from loss calculation via a binary mask ($m \in \{0,1\}^{88}$). This masking strategy enables unified training of a single model without architectural modifications.
- **Training Objective:** A flow-matching objective is employed. Given a ground-truth action chunk, noise is sampled, and an interpolated state is formed with a flow target. The model regresses the predicted velocity using a masked mean squared error (MSE) loss. The mask ensures that human samples only contribute gradients through the shared dimensions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to assess SiMDex's performance against a baseline using randomly sampled human data, its scalability with varying robot data budgets, and the individual contributions of its retrieval stages.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance (RQ1)

SiMDex demonstrated an improvement in overall success rate for dexterous manipulation tasks. When compared to an identical VLA baseline (GR-Dexter) trained with an equivalent amount of randomly sampled human data, SiMDex increased the overall success rate from 47.7% to 61.1%, representing a gain of 13.4 percentage points. This achievement was accomplished using approximately 1.49 million mined human samples, which constitutes less than 5% of the total 32 million samples available in the human pool. This finding indicates that targeted data retrieval significantly outperforms indiscriminate data mixing, even when the volume of human data is substantially reduced.

#### 4.2. Per-Task Performance

The benefits of SiMDex varied across the three real-world dexterous manipulation tasks:

- **Flick Wheel Task:** SiMDex showed the most substantial improvement on this task, nearly doubling the success rate from 24.5% to 45.5% (+21.0%). The baseline model almost entirely failed the "Twist" (0.13 score) and "Flick" (0.00 score) sub-stages, which require fine-grained finger dexterity. This suggests that the mined human data effectively conveyed complex finger skills that were largely absent in the randomly sampled human data.
- **Pick & Place Task:** Performance on the Pick & Place task improved from 54.0% to 83.4% (+29.4%). Consistent gains were observed across the manipulation of four objects with diverse geometries, indicating that SiMDex contributes to stronger generalization capabilities across different object shapes.
- **Drill Task:** In the standard setting, SiMDex's performance on the Drill task (54.5%) was slightly lower than the baseline (64.5%). However, the task exhibited high variance. Further analysis in the data scaling ablation clarified this outcome.

#### 4.3. Data Scaling Ablation (RQ2)

To understand how SiMDex's benefits scale with robot data availability, experiments were conducted by varying the robot data budget from 0.25x (~3.1 hours) to 2x (~24.9 hours), while keeping the amount of mined human data constant.

- **Stable Performance Floor:** SiMDex consistently outperformed the baseline at every robot data scale. Crucially, SiMDex maintained a relatively stable performance floor, with success rates around 57-58% across the 0.5x to 2x robot data regimes. In contrast, the baseline's performance degraded sharply as robot data decreased, dropping from 50.9% to 40.9% at 0.5x.
- **Maximum Gains in Low-Data Regimes:** The largest performance gain for SiMDex (+17.2%) was observed at 0.5x robot data. Even at the extreme low-data point (0.25x), SiMDex still provided a noticeable improvement of +6.8%. This finding indicates that the mined human data acts as a reliable anchor, effectively compensating for scarce robot demonstrations.
- **Drill Task Reversal:** The per-task breakdown of the scaling ablation provided insight into the Drill task's initial result. While Pick & Place and Flick Wheel benefited across almost all scales, the Drill task exhibited a sign reversal: SiMDex showed positive gains under scarce robot data (+8.9% at 0.25x, +13.4% at 0.5x) but trailed the baseline when robot data was ample (1x, 2x). This suggests that high-quality human demonstrations for drilling might be rare in the human pool. When robot data is limited, these few relevant samples are fully exploited; but once sufficient robot demonstrations are available, the sparse mined drilling samples may introduce variance rather than significant new signal. This aligns with the hypothesis that selective mining is most impactful when robot data is the limiting factor.
- **Practical Implication:** A significant practical implication is the potential for reducing robot data collection efforts. With SiMDex, approximately 6 hours (0.5x) of robot demonstrations achieved performance comparable to a baseline trained on roughly 25 hours (2x) of robot data, representing a nearly four-fold reduction in the required robot data collection.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Visualization of Retrieval Stages (RQ3)

Qualitative visualization of the retrieval pipeline demonstrated the progressive refinement of sample quality through each stage:

- **Recall Stage:** Initially retrieved samples often matched coarse cues such as similar initial hand poses or language-relevant objects (e.g., "wheel," "basket"). However, the actions in these samples were frequently unrelated to the fine-grained manipulation required (e.g., simple planar grasping instead of tool use or twisting).
- **Ranking Stage:** This stage improved the alignment of fine-grained motion, bringing aspects like pinching and twisting patterns, finger curvature, and rotation direction closer to the anchor. However, without visual grounding, samples with kinematically similar, but spatially distant or contextually inappropriate, hand positions could still rank highly.
- **Re-ranking Stage:** The Re-ranking stage, utilizing optical flow, resolved many of these visual mismatches by comparing actual pixel-level motion patterns. This led to the selection of samples whose motions closely mirrored the robot's bimanual behavior, such as a left hand stabilizing while the right aligns a tool (Drill), a coordinated stabilize-and-twist action (Flick Wheel), or sequential precision grasping of small items (Pick & Place). This confirmed that each stage contributes a complementary signal to the overall mining quality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SiMDex represents a contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the challenges associated with scaling robotic manipulation capabilities using large, heterogeneous datasets. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Mitigating the Robot Data Bottleneck:** The primary impact of SiMDex is its ability to reduce the reliance on costly and difficult-to-collect robot demonstrations. By providing a method to effectively utilize existing human egocentric videos, it offers a practical pathway to achieve higher robot manipulation performance with significantly less robot-specific data. The demonstrated 4x reduction in robot data collection required to achieve comparable performance is a substantial practical benefit for researchers and practitioners.

2. **Enhancing Data Efficiency for VLA Models:** SiMDex validates the hypothesis that selective data curation is more effective than indiscriminate data mixing when training VLA models from diverse human video pools. By mining a small, high-quality subset (<5% of the total pool) of task-relevant human demonstrations, the framework substantially improves overall success rates. This highlights an approach for maximizing the utility of large-scale egocentric datasets, ensuring that the initial investment in collecting such data yields benefits not just for broad pre-training but also for precise, task-specific post-training.

3. **Improving Dexterous Manipulation Skills:** The framework specifically addresses dexterous manipulation, a domain that is particularly challenging due to its complex action spaces and contact-rich dynamics. The marked improvements observed in tasks requiring fine-grained finger dexterity (e.g., Flick Wheel) and generalization across object shapes (e.g., Pick & Place) demonstrate its ability to imbue robots with more nuanced manipulation capabilities.

4. **Robustness in Low-Robot-Data Regimes:** SiMDex provides a stable performance floor, meaning it maintains a high level of performance even when robot demonstrations are scarce. This makes the approach particularly valuable in real-world scenarios where collecting extensive robot data is often impractical, thus democratizing access to advanced robotic manipulation for a broader range of applications.

5. **Architecture-Agnostic Integration:** The methodology's design allows it to augment VLA training without requiring any modifications to the VLA architecture or its training procedure. This model-agnostic nature increases its versatility and ease of adoption for various existing and future VLA pipelines.

6. **Novel Data Selection Paradigm:** By reframing human data selection as a recommendation problem and employing a multi-stage cascade, SiMDex introduces a scalable and effective paradigm for curating training data. This conceptual shift may inspire future research into intelligent data management for foundation models in robotics.

7. **Contribution to a Vision of Learned Robotics:** SiMDex aligns with a broader vision where robots do not need to learn every skill from scratch. Instead, they can intelligently query and adapt relevant human experiences from a vast shared knowledge reservoir. This represents a step towards robots being able to efficiently acquire new skills by leveraging humanity's collective manipulation knowledge embedded in everyday videos, transforming the task of robot skill acquisition from a data-collection burden into an intelligent data retrieval and adaptation problem.
