# Collaborative Loco-Manipulation for Pick-and-Place Tasks with Dynamic Reward Curriculum

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich developed a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework enabling single and collaborative legged robots with arms to perform end-to-end pick-and-place tasks. This system, utilizing a dynamic reward curriculum, achieved high success rates in real-world experiments and improved training efficiency by 55% with 18.6% faster execution times in simulation for long-horizon mobile manipulation compared to existing multi-stage RL approaches.

## Problem

- Industries like logistics and construction rely on human labor for physically demanding and repetitive pick-and-place (P&P) tasks, which carries risks of injury.
- Developing autonomous, end-to-end mobile manipulation for complex, long-horizon P&P tasks with single-armed legged robots presents challenges for whole-body control and task sequencing.
- Coordinating multiple mobile manipulators for collaborative P&P of larger payloads introduces exploratory bottlenecks, difficulties in attributing individual robot actions in shared environments, and managing high-dimensional control spaces.

## Method

- A hierarchical ==reinforcement learning== pipeline was developed, featuring a High-Level (HL) policy for strategic task sequencing (approach, pick up, transport, release) and a Low-Level (LL) policy for joint-level execution, controlling the robot's legs, arm, and gripper.
- A dynamic reward curriculum was introduced, comprising a stage transition graph with seven predefined stages and an adaptive reward scaling rule, to guide a single HL policy through the multi-stage P&P task and prevent undesirable shortcuts.
- A teacher-student distillation pipeline was implemented to bridge the sim-to-real gap, allowing the student policy to operate with noisy real-world measurements and infer its current task stage from external observations, enabling autonomous recovery from unmodeled disturbances.

## Results

- The single-agent policy achieved an 8 out of 9 trial success rate in real-world P&P tasks, while the dual-agent policy successfully completed all 3 initial runs with a **2.0** kg box.
- The dynamic reward curriculum demonstrated improved training efficiency by **55%** and reduced execution time by **18.6%** in simulation compared to state-of-the-art multi-stage RL approaches.
- The system exhibited robustness to perturbations in hardware experiments, with a single robot successfully adapting to a displaced box by re-grasping and completing the task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The dynamic reward curriculum effectively mitigates learning bottlenecks, prevents catastrophic forgetting, and promotes the acquisition of recovery behaviors by adaptively scaling stage-wise rewards based on training progress.
> - Hierarchical control enables seamless whole-body coordination, allowing a single policy to simultaneously manage complex locomotion and manipulation tasks for both individual and collaborative robots.
> - Robots dynamically shift their attention to different environmental elements (e.g., own gripper, object handles, neighbor's base) during various task phases, demonstrating emergent coordination strategies like inferring a neighbor's motion through physical coupling.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses the challenges of achieving autonomous, end-to-end mobile manipulation for complex, long-horizon tasks, particularly pick-and-place (P&P) operations, in both single and collaborative multi-robot settings. Current industrial applications in sectors like logistics and construction often rely on human labor for physically demanding and repetitive tasks, which carry risks of injury. While advancements in robotic hardware have enabled robots to traverse complex environments and perform dynamic object manipulation, the required levels of autonomy and coordination for extensive mobile manipulation have not been fully demonstrated in real-world scenarios.

The paper focuses on legged machines equipped with a single arm, chosen for their adaptability and all-terrain capabilities in practical applications. A critical aspect addressed is collaborative manipulation, which becomes necessary for handling larger payloads beyond the capabilities of a single robot. Coordinating such systems introduces specific challenges, including exploratory bottlenecks (e.g., grasping is a prerequisite for lifting), the difficulty of attributing individual robot actions in collectively updated environments for learning algorithms, and the high dimensionality and long-horizon nature of P&P tasks that demand varied control resolutions.

**Relation to Existing Work:**

- **Whole-Body Control:** Prior research in whole-body control for four-legged mobile manipulators has explored optimization-based and differential dynamic programming approaches. However, these methods are sensitive to model inaccuracies. Learning-based approaches offer a potential solution by jointly optimizing locomotion and manipulation. This research builds upon previous work by Ma et al. by implementing a low-level controller with separate policies for leg and arm joints, but distinctively leverages fully learned policies for all actuators within a unified framework.
- **Collaborative Loco-Manipulation:** Previous studies on collaborative payload transport with quadruped robots have included methods like pushing objects, carrying on backs, or towing. Other works involve robots carrying payloads rigidly attached to their bases, focusing primarily on coordinated locomotion rather than direct object manipulation. Approaches using passive or actuated arms typically assume the robots are already connected to the payload. This paper distinguishes itself by addressing the *entire scope* of collaborative P&P, from initial handle identification and assignment to grasping, transporting, and finally releasing the object.
- **Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Complex, long-horizon tasks are often decomposed into multiple stages, with separate controllers trained for each. A limitation of these methods is a performance decline during stage transitions due to a lack of holistic understanding. Some approaches attempt to improve cross-stage consistency through mechanisms like optimizing a convex sum of critics or transferring replay buffers. Liu et al. proposed a single-policy approach with a stage allocator. This work adopts a similar stage allocator but introduces a *dynamic reward curriculum* to promote smoother critic transitions, simplify reward shaping, and significantly enhance sample efficiency while mitigating catastrophic forgetting of earlier stages.
- **Curriculum Learning:** Traditional curriculum learning (CL) typically focuses on progressively increasing difficulty for a single skill or on unguided training of multiple sub-skills. In contrast, this research designs its dynamic reward curriculum specifically for tasks involving *ordered sequences of sub-skills* over extended time horizons.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the current reliance on human workers for physically demanding and repetitive pick-and-place (P&P) tasks in industries such as logistics and construction. These tasks often pose significant risks of physical injury to human laborers. The goal is to develop autonomous robotic solutions to mitigate these risks and improve efficiency.

The central objective is to develop a computationally efficient control architecture capable of reliably executing P&P tasks from start to finish. This encompasses the entire process: approaching a payload, grasping it, transporting it to a designated area, and releasing it at the target location.

Specific motivations and objectives include:

- **Adaptability and All-Terrain Capability:** The research specifically focuses on legged machines equipped with a single arm. This choice is motivated by the need for highly adaptable, all-terrain mobile systems that can operate effectively in varied and complex environments, which is crucial for practical industrial applications.
- **Enabling Collaborative Manipulation:** For larger and heavier payloads, single robots may be insufficient. A key objective is to enable collaborative manipulation, where multiple legged robots work together to transport objects, thereby unlocking functionalities beyond the reach of individual units.
- **Addressing Challenges in Complex Robot Control:** The work aims to overcome several inherent challenges associated with long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks:
 - **Exploratory Bottlenecks:** Designing a control scheme that efficiently guides robots through critical prerequisites, such as successfully grasping an object before it can be lifted or moved.
 - **Coordination in Multi-Robot Systems:** Developing algorithms that can manage the collective updates to the environment in dual-robot systems, allowing learning algorithms to account for the interactive effects of individual robot actions.
 - **High Dimensionality and Long-Horizon Tasks:** Creating an effective control scheme that can manage the system's inherent high dimensionality and the long-horizon nature of P&P tasks. This requires seamless whole-body coordination and the ability to operate at different control resolutions, from fine-grained manipulation to high-level decision-making, while achieving overall task objectives.
- **Advancing Reinforcement Learning Techniques:**
 - To develop a hierarchical RL controller that can coordinate quadruped robots with arms and grippers to perform P&P tasks. This policy should simultaneously control the robot's legs, arm, and gripper, functioning in both individual and collaborative scenarios.
 - To introduce a novel dynamic reward curriculum designed to enable effective and sample-efficient learning of complex sequential behaviors in long-horizon, multi-stage problems. This curriculum aims to progressively guide agents through payload-centered sub-objectives.
 - To validate the proposed method through extensive simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating its superior sample efficiency and robustness compared to existing state-of-the-art methods. The authors state a specific objective of being the first to demonstrate an RL pipeline tackling the full scope of collaborative P&P with two fully actuated single-armed legged manipulators.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors frame the P&P problem by considering box-shaped objects equipped with two custom-designed handles. The objective is for either a single robot or a team of two to autonomously transport the object to a desired location by manipulating these handles. The methodology employs a hierarchical reinforcement learning (RL) pipeline.

**A. Hierarchical Policy**
A bi-level control system is utilized, comprising a High-Level (HL) policy for strategic planning and a Low-Level (LL) whole-body policy for executing commands at the joint level. Both policies are trained using RL to maximize a discounted expected return.

1. **Low-Level Policy:**
 - The LL controller uses distinct policies for managing leg joints ($q_{leg}$) and arm joints ($q_{arm}$), consistent with previous work.
 - For locomotion, an existing learned policy by Ma et al. is adopted.
 - A separate manipulation policy is trained to generate joint position targets for the 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (DOF) arm. This policy tracks desired End Effector (EE) pose and base velocity commands.
2. **High-Level Policy:**
 - The HL policy is responsible for managing the sequence of tasks: approaching, picking up, and transporting the box to a designated goal.
 - Its inputs include proprioceptive observations, object states, the goal position, and the states of any collaborating robot.
 - The policy outputs an 8-dimensional action vector that serves as commands for the pre-trained LL policy. This vector includes the robot's base velocity and EE pose commands (defined by the position and yaw of the downward-oriented EE).
 - The HL policy also directly controls the opening and closing of the gripper via a binary command.

**B. Dynamic Reward Curriculum**
To enable a single HL policy to manage the multi-stage nature of P&P tasks and prevent undesirable shortcuts, a dynamic reward curriculum is introduced. This curriculum consists of a stage transition graph and an adaptive reward scaling rule.

1. **Stage Transition Graph:**
 - Seven distinct stages (S0-S6) are defined: Idle, Gripper ready, Object grabbed, Object height, Object on goal, Object placed, and Object released in goal.
 - Agents begin at S0 at the start of each episode and progress sequentially by satisfying predefined transition conditions until task completion (S6).
 - Forward transitions are strictly enforced to ensure mastery of intermediate skills.
 - Backward transitions are selectively enabled during the object pickup (S2) and release (S4) stages. This is intended to facilitate the learning of recovery behaviors and refine object interaction strategies.
2. **Adaptive Reward Scaling:**
 - Each stage `i` is associated with a set of reward functions `J_i`. These stage-wise rewards ($r_j$) are designed to incentivize progression towards subsequent stages.
 - The complete HL reward function at episode `e` is defined as $R_e^{HL}(s) = R_{fixed}(s) + \sum_{j: \exists r_j \in J} \rho_e^j r_j(s)$. Here, $R_{fixed}$ promotes general desirable objectives like action regularization and collision avoidance, while $\rho_e^j$ are dynamic coefficients.
 - The coefficients $\rho_e^j$ adapt dynamically across episodes. If the previous episode ended by reaching stage `i`, all rewards corresponding to stage `i` are reinforced (scaled up) to help agents reach stage `i+1`. Conversely, if `r_j` was not part of the final stage reached in the previous episode, its impact is reduced (scaled down).
 - A reward scale multiplier `K` dictates the reactivity of the curriculum, and `μ_j` and `μ_min` are used to bound the reward weights. The weights remain constant within an episode.
 - This dynamic adjustment allows the HL policy to begin learning later stages before fully mastering earlier ones, fostering efficient forward-looking behaviors and enabling recovery strategies.
 - After a robot completes stage 6 for five consecutive training episodes, a final stage with fixed reward scales is entered to stabilize the training process.

**C. Teacher-Student Distillation**
To address the sim-to-real gap, a teacher-student training pipeline is implemented.
- The teacher policy is trained in simulation using the dynamic reward curriculum and accurate measurements.
- The student policy is then trained using fixed rewards and a policy distillation approach that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between its action distribution and the teacher's.
- The student policy operates with noisy real-world measurements, lacks explicit stage-related information, and must infer the EE pose from a history of the arm's DOFs.
- A key advantage of the student policy is its ability to determine its current stage based solely on external observations. This enables robots to recognize new configurations (e.g., an object falling far from the target) as an earlier stage (like S0) and autonomously restart the P&P task, unlike the teacher which is constrained by a fixed stage graph.

**D. Implementation Details**
- **Success/Failure Criteria:** Task success is defined as the box being delivered within 0.5 meters of the goal. Failure occurs if any robot falls or if the task is not completed within 45 seconds.
- **Initialization:** Robots and objects are randomly initialized around the goal position, with distances ranging from 0.6 to 5 meters.
- **Object Variation:** Boxes with different weights and shapes are used during training.
 - Single-robot P&P: Three box assets (1.0 kg to 1.3 kg, 0.22 m to 0.45 m edge dimensions).
 - Dual-robot P&P: Two box assets (1.9 kg and 2.2 kg, 0.3 m to 1.5 m edge dimensions). All box dimensions are randomly scaled by a factor between 0.8 and 1.2.
- **Algorithm:** The multi-agent (MA) adaptation of the PPO algorithm as described in is used for training.
- **Simulation Environment:** All simulation experiments are conducted in Isaac Gym.

**E. Hardware Experiments**
- **Platform:** ANYmal D platforms equipped with a 6-DOF robotic arm (DynaArm) and a Robotiq 2F-140 gripper.
- **Tasks:** Robots are tasked with picking up and carrying a 1.5 kg box (single-robot) or a 2.0 kg box (dual-robot) to a target location.
- **Localization:** The pose of the box relative to the robot bases is estimated using a motion capture system.
- **Communication:** Multi-robot communication is implemented using the gRPC framework.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings, demonstrated through both simulation and real-world experiments.

**A. Hardware Experiments Performance**
- The **single-agent policy** achieved a success rate of 8 out of 9 trials in real-world experiments.
- The **dual-agent policy** successfully completed all 3 initial runs. In 3 additional trials, the robots delivered the box but experienced stalls before drop-off, attributed to motion capture (mocap) dropouts.
- **Robustness to Perturbations:** In a single-agent setup, the robot successfully adapted to a displaced box during P&P execution by pursuing the object, re-establishing its grasp, and completing the operation. These results suggest the system's reliability and robustness in realistic settings.

**B. Analysis of Stage Progression During Training (Dual-Robot P&P)**
Analysis of stage progression during training (Figure 5) illustrated the benefits of the dynamic reward curriculum:
- **Motion Refinement:** Backward transitions from "Gripper ready" (S1) to "Idle" (S0) occurred when learning to grasp (S2). This iterative process emphasized S0 rewards, leading to smoother grasping motions and improved gripper alignment, thus avoiding collisions and deadlocks.
- **Recovery Behaviors:** During object lifting (S2), unstable grasp configurations sometimes caused the object to slip, leading to a fallback to S0. The increasing percentages in S1-S2 indicated that robots learned to recover by re-attempting grasps. The comparable proportions of episodes across S0-S3 suggested the combined rewards encouraged stable grasping strategies.
- **Overcoming Bottlenecks:** A specific challenge was learning "Object placed" (S5) after reaching "Object on goal" (S4), as it required lowering, which conflicted with previously learned lifting. The dynamic reward curriculum mitigated this bottleneck by adaptively scaling up S3 rewards, pushing the policy past this difficult transition, evidenced by shifts around 2800 and 3800 training iterations.
- **Mitigating Forgetting:** Policies sometimes forgot lifting motions (S2) after learning "Object placed" (S5). By re-emphasizing lifting rewards, the curriculum enabled robots to consistently perform both lifting and placing, which are opposing motions for a single RL policy.

**C. Sensitivity Analysis**
Saliency maps (Figure 6) revealed that robots dynamically shifted their attention to different environmental elements depending on the task phase:
- **Approaching (A):** The ego robot focused on both object handles and the neighboring robot's base to determine handle assignment.
- **Grasping and Lifting (B):** The ego robot concentrated on its own gripper, the handle it was grasping, and the neighboring robot's gripper, facilitating coordinated lifting.
- **Transporting (C):** Attention primarily shifted to the gripper and the goal position.
- **Nearing Release (D):** The ego robot paid less direct attention to its neighbor, instead focusing on the partner's held handle (Handle 1), suggesting it learned to infer the neighbor's motion through physical coupling. This dynamic attention shift reportedly enhanced coordination and efficiency.

**D. Ablation Study on Dynamic Reward Curriculum**
An ablation study on a 4-stage EE position tracking problem and the single-agent P&P task demonstrated the efficacy of the dynamic reward curriculum (Figure 7, Figure 8, Table II):
- **"Liu et al. (max)"** (rewards deactivated and set to maximum after stage completion) became stuck at S3, as large earlier-stage rewards dominated gradient optimization, hindering the learning of new, initially small rewards for S4.
- **"Liu et al. (min)"** (rewards set to minimum after stage completion) failed to pass S1, as the policy exploited mastered rewards rather than transitioning to new stages without immediate return.
- These ablated models exhibited difficult reward tuning, local minima, and poor sample efficiency. In contrast, the authors' dynamic reward curriculum adaptively adjusted stage rewards, mitigating these issues and leading to higher task success and faster completion times. For the single-agent P&P problem, Liu et al. stalled after S4.

**E. Benchmark Comparison**
The complete pipeline was benchmarked against two state-of-the-art methods that train separate policies for multi-stage RL: Bendikas et al. and Erskine and Lehnert (Table II, Figure 8):
- **Bendikas et al.:** Achieved a competitive average success rate (91.2% vs. 93.6% for ours) but longer completion times (13.25s vs. 10.78s for ours). This method's policies, trained without knowledge of later stages, exhibited uncoordinated actions (e.g., extending the arm sideways during transport, risking imbalance), which were difficult to adjust. It was also less sample-efficient, requiring sequential policy convergence.
- **Erskine and Lehnert:** Showed a lower success rate (52.8%) and longer completion times (16.00s). This approach struggled with inconsistent performance, particularly when earlier-stage policies ended in challenging initial states, leading to high variance in end-of-episode stages.
- The authors' method demonstrated improved training efficiency by 55% and reduced execution time by 18.6% in simulation experiments compared to these state-of-the-art approaches for long-horizon RL tasks.

**F. Limitations**
The authors identified several limitations:
- **Reward Design Complexity:** Reward design for collaborative multi-robot P&P, while intuitive, is time-consuming and involves tedious tuning of numerous terms.
- **Simulation Failures:** Failures in simulation often occurred when grippers became stuck by object handles due to poor grasping motions or object perturbations, indicating the difficulty of collaborative manipulation.
- **Dependency on Accurate Sensing:** The method relies on accurate object pose and goal measurements. Real-world sensor noise or mocap dropouts can cause failures.
- **Limited Compliance:** Hardware tests revealed limited compliance, where robots resisted each other under communication delays instead of adapting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a hierarchical reinforcement learning (RL) pipeline that contributes to the field of mobile manipulation, particularly for complex, long-horizon tasks involving legged robots.

**Advancement in Mobile Manipulation:**
The work introduces a computationally efficient control architecture that enables legged robots equipped with a single arm to perform end-to-end pick-and-place (P&P) tasks. This includes the full sequence from approaching and grasping objects to transporting and releasing them. The focus on adaptable, all-terrain legged machines addresses a critical need for robust robotic systems in varied environments, potentially expanding the range of practical applications.

**Novelty in Collaborative Robotics:**
To the authors' knowledge, this is the first RL pipeline to tackle the entire scope of collaborative P&P with two legged manipulators. This includes the initial stages of identifying and assigning handles on a payload, through grasping and transporting, to the final release at a target location. This capability represents a step towards enabling robotic teams to handle bulky and heavy objects beyond the capacity of individual units, thus unlocking new levels of functionality.

**Innovation in Reinforcement Learning:**
- **Hierarchical Control:** The bi-level control architecture, decoupling high-level planning from low-level execution, provides a structured approach for managing the complexity of simultaneous locomotion and manipulation. This allows for unified control of the robot's legs, arm, and gripper.
- **Dynamic Reward Curriculum:** The introduction of a dynamic reward curriculum is a notable methodological contribution for long-horizon, multi-stage RL problems. This curriculum systematically guides agents through sequential sub-tasks, promoting efficient learning by allowing later stages to be explored before earlier ones are fully mastered. It fosters forward-looking behaviors, supports the acquisition of recovery strategies (e.g., re-grasping a dropped object), mitigates catastrophic forgetting of previously learned skills, and helps overcome learning bottlenecks between conflicting behaviors (e.g., lifting and placing). The reported improvements in training efficiency (55%) and reduced execution time (18.6%) in simulation compared to state-of-the-art methods highlight its practical impact on RL training.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The teacher-student distillation pipeline provides an effective mechanism for bridging the simulation-to-real-world gap. The student policy's ability to deduce its current stage from noisy external observations, without explicit stage information, enhances robustness in real-world deployments, allowing autonomous recovery from unmodeled disturbances.

**Potential Societal and Industrial Impact:**
By enabling autonomous collaborative loco-manipulation for P&P tasks, this research offers a pathway to automating physically demanding and repetitive operations currently performed by human workers in industries such as logistics, construction, and manufacturing. This could lead to improved worker safety by reducing exposure to strenuous activities and hazardous environments, while also potentially increasing operational efficiency and productivity.

**Insights into Multi-Robot Coordination:**
The sensitivity analysis, particularly through saliency maps, provides insights into how coordinated robot policies dynamically allocate attention across different components of their observation space (own state, object, neighbor's state). This demonstrates the emergence of sophisticated coordination strategies, such as inferring a neighbor's motion through physical coupling with the object, contributing to the understanding of emergent behaviors in multi-agent RL systems.

The identified limitations, such as the complexity of reward design, susceptibility to sensing noise, and limited compliance in hardware, also point towards important future research directions for enhancing the robustness and adaptability of collaborative mobile manipulation systems.
