# Self-Supervised Contrastive Learning is Approximately Supervised Contrastive Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Texas A&M University establishes a theoretical duality between self-supervised Decoupled Contrastive Learning (DCL) and a supervised counterpart, demonstrating that DCL implicitly approximates a supervised objective whose global minimizers exhibit neural collapse properties. The work explains how self-supervised models learn class-aware representations effective for few-shot learning, showing that semantic structure emerges without explicit labels.

## Problem

- A formal understanding of why self-supervised contrastive learning (CL) effectively learns semantic, class-aware representations without explicit label supervision was missing.
- The theoretical connection between the empirical success of CL in producing high-quality features and well-understood phenomena in supervised learning, such as neural collapse, was not fully established.
- Identifying and quantifying the specific properties of learned representations that enable effective few-shot transfer learning in self-supervised settings needed further theoretical grounding.

## Method

- A theoretical duality was established by proving that the ==self-supervised Decoupled Contrastive Loss (DCL)== approximates a ==Negatives-Only Supervised Contrastive Loss (NSCL)==, with the approximation gap diminishing as the number of semantic classes increases.
- The global minimizers of the NSCL were analyzed under an unconstrained features model, demonstrating that they achieve ==augmentation collapse, within-class collapse, and a Simplex Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF) structure==.
- A novel metric called =='directional CDNV'== was introduced to measure feature variability along class-discriminative directions, and a refined bound on m-shot classification error was derived.

## Results

- Empirical evaluations confirmed that the gap between DCL and NSCL losses decreases at an **O(1/C)** rate as the number of classes (C) increases, validating the theoretical bound.
- DCL-trained models achieved low few-shot classification errors on diverse datasets (e.g., CIFAR10, ImageNet-1K), which were accurately predicted by the newly derived error bound based on directional CDNV.
- Experiments showed DCL-trained representations exhibit properties consistent with neural collapse, including high cosine similarity for positive pairs (**0.90**) and low similarity for negative pairs, along with clear semantic clustering in UMAP visualizations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-supervised contrastive learning, specifically DCL, implicitly minimizes a supervised loss (NSCL) that explicitly excludes same-class negatives, thus explaining its capability to learn class-aware features without direct label supervision.
> - The global minimizers of the approximated supervised loss (NSCL) inherently exhibit neural collapse phenomena, including augmentation collapse, within-class feature collapse, and class-center features forming an optimal Simplex ETF structure.
> - Effective few-shot transfer learning performance in self-supervised models is primarily driven by low feature variability along the directions separating class centers (directional CDNV), rather than uniform isotropic clustering.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of representation learning has seen a significant shift towards unsupervised and self-supervised methods in recent years. Traditional supervised learning (SL) relies heavily on labeled data, which can be expensive and time-consuming to acquire. Unsupervised learning, particularly self-supervised learning (SSL), offers a compelling alternative by learning meaningful representations from unlabeled data. Contrastive Learning (CL) is a leading paradigm within SSL, where models learn by distinguishing augmented views of the same sample (positives) from views of different samples (negatives). Approaches like SimCLR, MoCo, and CPC have demonstrated remarkable empirical success, often achieving performance comparable to or even surpassing fully supervised counterparts on various downstream tasks, particularly in computer vision and natural language processing.

Despite this empirical triumph, the theoretical foundations explaining *why* CL works so effectively, especially in organizing representations according to semantic classes without explicit label supervision, have remained less developed. The broader research landscape includes several lines of theoretical inquiry into CL:
- **Mutual Information Maximization:** Early theories connected CL to maximizing mutual information between different augmented views of data, though subsequent work suggested strict bounds on MI might not always be beneficial.
- **Alignment and Uniformity:** Other studies characterized learned representations by their alignment (positives are close) and uniformity (negatives are spread out) on a hypersphere.
- **Cluster Recovery:** A significant body of work has explored CL's ability to recover meaningful data clusters, often relying on strong assumptions about data distribution, augmentations, or model capacity.
- **Neural Collapse (NC):** In supervised learning, neural collapse describes a phenomenon where, in the final stages of training overparameterized networks, within-class feature vectors collapse to their class means, and these class means arrange themselves into a Simplex Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF) structure. This phenomenon is well-understood for various supervised loss functions (e.g., cross-entropy, MSE, supervised contrastive loss).

This paper uniquely positions itself at the intersection of these theoretical gaps by directly linking self-supervised contrastive learning to a supervised variant, and then demonstrating that this supervised variant naturally leads to neural collapse properties. By establishing this formal duality, the authors provide a missing piece in understanding how label-agnostic CL can achieve class-aware features and facilitate robust few-shot transfer learning, drawing parallels to the well-understood mechanisms of neural collapse in supervised settings. This provides a fundamental theoretical justification for a widely adopted practical paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central question driving this research is: **"How does self-supervised contrastive learning learn representations similar to those learned through supervised learning, despite the lack of explicit supervision?"**

The paper is motivated by the observed phenomenon that SSL-trained models, despite not explicitly using labels during training, often produce high-quality representations that group samples by their semantic classes, resembling those learned through supervised training. This striking similarity necessitates a theoretical explanation.

To address this, the authors set out three key objectives:

1. **Establish a Duality between Self-Supervised and Supervised CL:** The primary objective is to formally demonstrate that standard self-supervised contrastive learning (specifically, the Decoupled Contrastive Learning, DCL) implicitly approximates a supervised counterpart. This supervised variant, termed "negatives-only supervised contrastive loss" (NSCL), explicitly excludes same-class samples from the negative pool.
2. **Characterize Good Representations for Few-Shot Learning:** The paper aims to define and quantify the properties of learned representations that enable effective adaptation to downstream tasks, particularly under few-shot learning scenarios. This involves introducing refined metrics for feature variability.
3. **Characterize the Global Minima of the NSCL Loss:** Building on the established duality, the third objective is to analyze the geometric structure of representations that globally minimize the NSCL loss, and by extension, approximate the behavior of DCL-trained models.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to provide a comprehensive theoretical framework that explains the effectiveness of CL in learning semantic structure and supporting efficient transfer learning, thus bridging the gap between empirical success and theoretical understanding.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper employs a rigorous theoretical and empirical methodology to achieve its objectives:

**Theoretical Approach:**

1. **Connecting DCL to NSCL (Theorem 1):**
 - The authors begin by defining the global Decoupled Contrastive Loss (DCL), a variant of the InfoNCE loss that decouples positive and negative contributions.
 - They introduce the Negatives-Only Supervised Contrastive Loss (NSCL), which is identical to DCL but explicitly restricts negative pairs to *only* come from different semantic classes than the anchor.
 - The core theoretical contribution here is Theorem 1, which proves that the gap between the DCL and NSCL losses diminishes as the number of semantic classes (C) increases. Crucially, this bound is label-agnostic and holds without assumptions on model architecture, data distribution, or augmentation strategy, making it very general. The intuition is that as C grows, the probability of encountering a "same-class negative" in the self-supervised setting (which DCL treats as a negative but NSCL excludes) becomes negligible.

2. **Characterizing Good Representations for Few-Shot Learning (Proposition 1):**
 - The paper formalizes the notion of a "good" representation as one that supports accurate downstream classification with few labeled samples per class (m-shot classification error).
 - It leverages existing concepts like Class-Distance-Normalized Variance (CDNV), which measures how tightly samples within a class cluster relative to the distance between class centers.
 - A novel metric, **directional CDNV** ($\tilde{V}_f$), is introduced. This measures feature variability *only along the line connecting two class centers*, focusing on the component most relevant for distinguishing those classes.
 - Proposition 1 establishes a new bound on the m-shot classification error (for linear probing and nearest-class-center classification). This bound demonstrates that lower directional CDNV is the primary driver of low m-shot error, and the impact of the regular (isotropic) CDNV diminishes as the number of labeled samples (m) increases. This suggests that CL models might not need perfectly collapsed clusters to be effective, as long as their variability in decision-critical directions is low.

3. **Characterizing NSCL Global Minima (Theorem 2):**
 - To analyze the geometric structure of representations, the authors adopt the "unconstrained features model," a common theoretical relaxation in neural collapse literature. In this model, each sample's embedding is treated as a free learnable vector, abstracting away the complexities of neural network architectures.
 - Theorem 2 proves that any global minimizer of the NSCL loss (under this unconstrained features model) satisfies key properties associated with Neural Collapse:
 - **Augmentation Collapse:** All augmented views of the same sample map to the identical point.
 - **Within-Class Collapse:** All samples from the same semantic class collapse to a single, unique class embedding.
 - **Simplex Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF):** These class-center embeddings form a maximally separated, symmetric configuration (a Simplex ETF), which are optimal solutions for various supervised classification problems.
 - This theorem directly links the proposed supervised loss (NSCL) to the known desirable properties of supervised learning representations, providing a theoretical anchor for CL's semantic clustering ability.

**Empirical Approach:**

The theoretical findings are rigorously validated through a series of experiments on diverse computer vision datasets (CIFAR10, CIFAR100, mini-ImageNet, Tiny-ImageNet, SVHN, ImageNet-1K) using popular CL frameworks (SimCLR with ResNet-50 and ViT, and MoCo v2).

1. **Validating Theorem 1:**
 - Models are trained with DCL, and both DCL and NSCL losses are monitored throughout training on training and test sets. The gap between these losses is compared against the theoretical upper bound derived in Theorem 1.
 - Experiments also investigate how this loss gap scales with the number of classes (C), empirically confirming the O(1/C) decay.
 - A critical experiment compares the final NSCL loss achieved by a model trained with DCL versus a model trained directly with NSCL, demonstrating that DCL implicitly drives the NSCL loss close to its direct minimization value.

2. **Validating Proposition 1:**
 - The few-shot downstream performance (NCCC and linear probing errors) of DCL-trained models is measured across varying numbers of shots (m).
 - The empirical few-shot errors are compared against the novel error bound from Proposition 1 (and its optimized corollary), demonstrating its tightness in practice.
 - The evolution of CDNV and directional CDNV during DCL training is tracked, showing how directional CDNV dramatically decreases, explaining the observed low few-shot error despite moderate overall CDNV.

3. **Indirectly Validating Theorem 2:**
 - **Augmentation Collapse:** The cosine similarity between embeddings of different augmentations of the *same* sample (positives) versus *different* samples (negatives) is measured for DCL-trained models, showing strong positive alignment for positives and separability for negatives.
 - **Within-Class Collapse and Simplex ETF:** UMAP visualizations of learned representations confirm that DCL-trained models form clear, separable semantic clusters, with NSCL-trained models showing even tighter clustering, consistent with the neural collapse properties.
 - **Representation Alignment:** Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) and Representation Similarity Analysis (RSA) are used to quantify the similarity between representations learned by DCL and NSCL models, confirming high alignment.

The comprehensive combination of theoretical derivations, new analytical tools, and extensive empirical validation across multiple architectures and datasets strengthens the paper's claims and insights.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key theoretical and empirical findings that collectively deepen the understanding of self-supervised contrastive learning:

**Theoretical Findings:**

1. **Duality between DCL and NSCL (Theorem 1):** The paper formally proves that the self-supervised Decoupled Contrastive Loss (DCL) upper bounds and approximates the Negatives-Only Supervised Contrastive Loss (NSCL). The theoretical gap between these two losses is shown to vanish at a rate of $O(1/\text{C})$ as the number of semantic classes (C) increases. This bound is notably architecture-independent and label-agnostic, making it a general result.
2. **Characterization of NSCL Global Minima (Theorem 2):** Under the unconstrained features model, any global minimizer of the NSCL loss is proven to exhibit several crucial properties:
 - **Augmentation Collapse:** All augmented views of a given sample converge to the same point in the embedding space.
 - **Within-Class Collapse:** All samples belonging to the same semantic class map to an identical representation vector.
 - **Simplex Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF):** The resulting class-center embeddings form a Simplex ETF structure, a maximally separated and symmetric configuration that is known to be optimal in various supervised classification settings (e.g., cross-entropy, MSE, supervised contrastive loss). This finding directly links NSCL to the desirable geometric properties of neural collapse.
3. **Improved Few-Shot Error Bound (Proposition 1):** A new bound for the m-shot classification error (for linear probing and Nearest Class-Center Classification) is introduced. This bound highlights that the few-shot error is primarily driven by the **directional CDNV** (variation along the line between class centers) rather than the overall isotropic CDNV. The impact of overall CDNV diminishes as the number of labeled samples (m) increases, suggesting that tight clustering in all directions is not strictly necessary for good few-shot performance, as long as discriminative directions are well-separated.

**Empirical Results:**

1. **Validation of DCL-NSCL Duality (Figures 3 & 4):**
 - Experiments confirm that DCL consistently upper bounds NSCL, and this gap decays with an increasing number of classes (C), as predicted by Theorem 1. For C=100 (e.g., CIFAR100), the bound becomes very tight.
 - The DCL and NSCL losses exhibit high correlation during training.
 - Crucially, minimizing the DCL loss implicitly brings the NSCL loss close to the value achieved by directly minimizing NSCL. This suggests that DCL effectively learns representations that align with a supervised objective.
2. **Downstream Performance and Few-Shot Error Bound (Figures 5 & 6, Table 1):**
 - While NSCL-trained models achieve lower few-shot errors (as expected due to explicit label supervision), DCL-trained models still exhibit remarkably low few-shot errors for C-way classification, indicating strong implicit clustering.
 - The proposed few-shot error bound (from Proposition 1 and Corollary 1) provides a tight estimate of practical probing performance, especially as the number of shots (m) increases. It effectively predicts the low m-shot error observed in DCL models.
3. **Role of Directional CDNV (Figure 7):**
 - DCL training is shown to cause a significant reduction (by about an order of magnitude) in directional CDNV, while only modestly lowering the overall CDNV. This supports Proposition 1's claim that directional variability is the dominant factor for few-shot performance, explaining how DCL achieves label recovery with limited supervision. In contrast, NSCL training substantially reduces both measures.
4. **Representation Geometry (Table 2, Figures 1 & 8, Table 3):**
 - **Augmentation Collapse:** DCL-trained models show high cosine similarity (approx. 0.90) for same-sample augmentations ("positives") and low similarity (below 0.15) for different-sample pairs ("negatives"), confirming strong augmentation invariance and inter-sample separability as a prerequisite for within-class collapse.
 - **Semantic Clustering:** UMAP visualizations illustrate that DCL-trained models form clear semantic clusters, with NSCL-trained models producing even tighter and more separable clusters, aligning with the predictions of Theorem 2 regarding within-class collapse and ETF structure.
 - **Representation Alignment:** High Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) and Representation Similarity Analysis (RSA) values (e.g., >0.80) between DCL- and NSCL-trained models demonstrate a strong alignment at the representation level, further supporting the theoretical duality.

These results were consistently observed across various datasets and architectures (ResNet-50, ViT) and contrastive methods (SimCLR, MoCo v2), underscoring the generality of the findings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes significant contributions to the theoretical understanding of self-supervised contrastive learning, offering a compelling explanation for its empirical success and connecting it to well-established phenomena in supervised learning.

**Key Significance:**

- **Bridging Theory and Practice in SSL:** The central finding that self-supervised CL (DCL) implicitly approximates a supervised variant (NSCL) provides a foundational theoretical link. This helps to explain *why* label-agnostic CL yields class-aware representations, a question that has largely relied on empirical observation. It clarifies how semantic structure emerges without explicit supervision signals.
- **Connecting SSL to Neural Collapse:** By demonstrating that global minimizers of NSCL exhibit neural collapse properties (augmentation collapse, within-class collapse, Simplex ETF), the paper establishes a direct theoretical connection between SSL and the well-understood dynamics of supervised learning. This integration of concepts from different learning paradigms enriches our overall understanding of deep representation learning.
- **Insights into Few-Shot Learning:** The introduction of directional CDNV and the refined few-shot error bound offer new perspectives on what constitutes a "good" representation for transfer learning. It shows that low variability in decision-critical directions is more crucial than isotropic clustering, especially with more labeled examples. This insight can guide the design of future SSL objectives that explicitly promote such directional separation.
- **Generalizability of Findings:** The architecture-independent and label-agnostic nature of Theorem 1's bound, along with the broad empirical validation across diverse datasets and models, suggests the findings are highly generalizable and not restricted to specific implementations or data types.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Improved SSL Algorithm Design:** The theoretical understanding provided could inspire the development of new self-supervised objectives or regularization techniques that explicitly encourage the properties shown to be beneficial (e.g., tighter directional clustering, faster convergence to NSCL-like minima).
- **Better Understanding of Transfer Learning:** The insights into few-shot error and the role of directional variability can lead to more principled methods for evaluating and optimizing representations for downstream tasks, particularly in low-data regimes.
- **Broader Theoretical Frameworks:** This work lays the groundwork for developing more comprehensive theoretical frameworks that can unify different self-supervised and supervised learning approaches, fostering a more holistic understanding of representation learning in deep neural networks.
- **Application to Other Domains:** The authors explicitly mention extending these ideas to multimodal models like CLIP or large language models (LLMs). Given the widespread use of contrastive or contrastive-like objectives in these domains, the insights could prove invaluable for explaining their emergent capabilities and guiding future research.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations:
1. **Minimizer Proximity:** While the DCL and NSCL losses are shown to be numerically close, the paper does not theoretically prove that their respective *minimizers* (i.e., the learned model parameters or representations) are also close in parameter space. This remains an interesting direction for future theoretical and empirical investigation.
2. **Constants in Error Bound:** The constants in Proposition 1's few-shot error bound can be large and scale linearly with the number of classes, potentially limiting its practical utility for extremely high-dimensional classification tasks. Tightening this bound would enhance its predictive power.

Despite these limitations, this paper represents a significant step forward in formalizing the mechanisms behind self-supervised learning's empirical success. Its rigorous theoretical development, coupled with extensive empirical validation, solidifies the understanding of how contrastive learning effectively organizes data into semantically meaningful, class-aware structures, even without explicit labels.
