# Translation as a Bridging Action: Transferring Manipulation Skills from Humans to Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from HKU-MMLab and ByteDance Seed developed a robotic manipulation framework that transfers skills from human demonstrations using a relative wrist translation as a "bridging action". This method enabled robots to learn novel manipulation tasks with a 0.57 success rate and improved data efficiency for few-shot robot post-training, outperforming approaches based on 6DoF human wrist actions.

## Problem

- Noisy 6DoF human hand pose estimates, particularly in rotational components, make direct transfer of manipulation skills to robots unreliable.
- A fundamental embodiment mismatch between human hands and parallel robot grippers renders human wrist rotations poorly aligned with robot manipulation behaviors.
- Existing human-to-robot skill transfer methods struggle with sub-optimal and distorted robot actions when attempting to learn rotation-inclusive signals from noisy human data.

## Method

- Introduced a ==translation-only relative wrist action== (a_3D-wrist) as a robust, embodiment-agnostic ==bridging representation== to circumvent issues with noisy 6DoF human pose estimation and hand-gripper mismatch.
- Developed a ==VLA model== featuring ==interleaved action token sequencing== and ==attention masking==, enabling unified training across diverse data sources with varying action component availability. Action generation is trained with a ==flow-matching== objective.
- Employed a ==three-stage training pipeline== encompassing large-scale human-only pre-training, human-robot co-training with a ==binding strategy==, and few-shot robot post-training for efficient skill acquisition.

## Results

- The framework successfully transferred novel manipulation skills from human demonstrations, achieving a **0.45** success rate on diverse tasks when co-trained with robot pick-and-place data.
- Pre-training on **600 hours** of human-only data using the bridging action increased overall success to **0.57** and improved data efficiency for few-shot robot learning, yielding a **55.00%** success rate during post-training.
- Translation-only bridging actions led to more stable and effective robot movements, resulting in higher average progress (**0.44**) and success rates (**0.22**) compared to using 6DoF human wrist actions (**0.35** average progress, **0.12** success rate).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Relative wrist translation serves as a more robust and transferable bridging action for robots with parallel grippers compared to noisy 6DoF human wrist actions.
> - Large-scale pre-training on human-only translation data significantly improves data efficiency for subsequent robot learning, demonstrating the scalability of the bridging action.
> - Supervising the shared bridging action on robot-specific data is essential to effectively bind abstract human movements to executable robot end-effector and gripper actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot learning, specifically addressing the challenge of transferring manipulation skills from human demonstrations to robotic platforms. The integration of human data into robot learning is a growing area, motivated by the potential for cost-effective and diverse skill acquisition without the limitations and expense of collecting large-scale robot-specific data.

Prior work in this domain has explored various approaches, including the use of wrist poses, fingertip positions, parameterized hand models, or finger joints as human action representations. Many methods adopt a strategy of treating humans as another form of a bi-manual 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (6DoF) embodiment. However, the paper identifies two primary limitations in these conventional approaches:
1. **Noise in Human Pose Estimation:** Hand-pose estimators used to derive human actions often produce noisy data, particularly in the rotational components.
2. **Embodiment Mismatch:** The contact patterns of human fingers differ fundamentally from those of parallel robot grippers. The additional degrees of freedom in human fingers render wrist rotations less semantically aligned with the manipulation behaviors required for parallel grippers.

The paper aims to overcome these specific challenges by proposing an alternative approach: a bridging action representation based on relative wrist translation. This positions the work as an advancement in cross-embodiment learning, where knowledge is transferred between disparate physical systems (humans and robots). Furthermore, it builds upon recent developments in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, such as $\pi^0$-like architectures, and leverages large-scale pre-training methodologies, including those involving diverse vision-language corpora and data pyramid strategies. By focusing on a shared, robust action space, the research seeks to enhance the scalability and effectiveness of robot skill learning from human data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to ascertain the feasibility of learning novel manipulation skills from human actions and subsequently transferring these skills to a bi-manual robot equipped with parallel grippers. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and addresses specific challenges identified in the existing literature:

**Primary Motivation: Leveraging Human Action Data for Robot Learning**
- **Cost-effectiveness and Scalability:** Human action data is inherently more affordable, abundant, and diverse to collect compared to robot-specific data. The "robot-free setting" significantly reduces collection costs and logistical complexities, allowing for data acquisition in various environments. This makes human data a promising resource for scaling up robot learning capabilities in terms of environmental and skill diversity.
- **Enhancing Embodied Policies:** With the increasing availability of ego-centric human data, there is a strong interest in understanding how this rich data source can improve robot policies, especially concerning generalization, reducing reliance on robot data, and enabling robots to manipulate novel objects or follow new instructions.

**Motivation for a Novel Approach: Addressing Limitations of Prior Human-to-Robot Transfer Methods**
- **Noise in 6DoF Hand-Pose Estimates:** Many existing methods rely on estimating human hand poses, which often yield noisy 6DoF (position and orientation) data. The rotational components, in particular, are susceptible to prediction errors, leading to unreliable action signals when directly mapped to robot end-effectors.
- **Fundamental Mismatch in Contact Patterns:** Human hands possess complex articulation with multiple fingers, allowing for dexterous manipulation and varied contact patterns. In contrast, parallel robot grippers operate with a much simpler, often binary (open/close) contact mechanism. This fundamental difference means that human wrist rotations, while integral to human manipulation, may not translate meaningfully or optimally to control parallel grippers, potentially leading to distorted or suboptimal robot behaviors.
- **Sub-optimality of Rotation-Inclusive Signals:** The authors argue that attempting to learn rotation-inclusive robotic manipulation signals directly from noisy human data and across disparate contact mechanics is challenging and sub-optimal.

**Specific Objectives Derived from Motivations:**
1. **Develop a Robust Bridging Action Representation:** To overcome the noise and embodiment mismatch, the primary objective is to define an action space that is shared between humans and robots and is inherently robust to the aforementioned issues. This led to the proposal of relative wrist translation.
2. **Design a Multi-Embodiment Training Framework:** To effectively utilize diverse data sources (in-the-wild human, in-lab human, robot) with potentially missing action components, an objective is to design a unified training objective and model architecture. This includes developing an interleaved action token representation and attention masking mechanism within a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model.
3. **Validate Effectiveness and Scalability:** A key objective is to empirically demonstrate that the proposed bridging action enables more effective skill transfer from human to robot compared to traditional 6DoF human actions. Furthermore, the research aims to show that this representation can benefit from large-scale human-only pre-training and improve data efficiency for subsequent robot learning.
4. **Characterize Performance Limits:** The study also seeks to understand the upper bound of the bridging representation's capability in robotic skill transfer by minimizing visual and action noise gaps.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on a novel bridging action representation and a multi-stage training framework within a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model.

#### 3.1. Motion Bridging Action Representation

The paper argues against the direct use of 6DoF wrist actions from human demonstrations due to inherent noise in hand-pose estimators and the fundamental difference in contact patterns between human fingers and parallel grippers. Instead, a "translation-only" bridging action is proposed:

- **`a_3D-wrist` (Bridging Action):** This action is defined as the relative wrist translation observed within the initial head-camera frame.
 - To compute this, the wrist pose ($W_w$) at time $t+i$ is first transformed into the head-camera frame at time $t$, resulting in $W_{t+i}^{c_t} = (T_t^{w \leftarrow c})^{-1} W_{t+i}^w$.
 - The relative translation is then calculated as the difference in the translation components: $\Delta W_{3D} = t(W_{t+i}^{c_t}) - t(W_t^{c_t})$.
 - For bi-manual robots and humans, these translations are concatenated, yielding $a_{3D-wrist} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times 6}$ for a $k$-step future window.
 - This representation is considered robust to noisy rotation estimates, physically meaningful under a shared observational perspective, and embodiment-agnostic.

- **`a_6D-eef` (Robot End-Effector Action):** For robot control, the 6DoF end-effector action is defined as the relative wrist motion with respect to the initial end-effector pose: $\Delta W_{6D} = (W_t^w)^{-1} W_{t+i}^w$. This SE(3) element is converted into Cartesian coordinates and Euler angles, resulting in $a_{6D-eef} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times 12}$ for both arms.

- **`a_gripper` (Gripper Action):** This is a binary signal (0 for open, 1 for close) for each gripper, resulting in $a_{gripper} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times 2}$. For in-lab human data, hand closure is annotated as the gripper signal.

- **Unified Action Space and Supervision:** A unified action space $a_t = (a_{3D-wrist_t}, a_{6D-eef_t}, a_{gripper_t})$ is defined. Crucially, different data sources supervise only the reliably available components:
 - In-the-wild human action data: supervises only $a_{3D-wrist}$.
 - In-lab human action data: supervises $a_{3D-wrist}$ and $a_{gripper}$.
 - Robot tele-operation data: supervises $a_{3D-wrist}$, $a_{6D-eef}$, and $a_{gripper}$. This design allows for grounding the shared $a_{3D-wrist}$ into executable $a_{6D-eef}$ for robots.

#### 3.2. VLA Model with Interleaved Action Sequence

- **Architecture:** The model is a $\pi^0$-like end-to-end VLA model, denoted $\pi_{\theta}(l, o_t)$, which generates an action chunk $a_t$ (for $k$ future steps) conditioned on language instruction ($l$) and observations ($o_t$) from multiple cameras (head and two wrists). Missing wrist views for human data are handled by padding blank images.
 - It processes vision and language tokens through a pre-trained VLM. The resulting vision-language KV-cache acts as a context for an Action Transformer, which generates the action chunk.
 - The Action Transformer uses two separate sets of parameters to balance different training objectives and incorporates self-attention layers.

- **Interleaved Action Tokens:** To manage heterogeneous action components across data sources, the action tokens are organized in a specific order: $a_{3D-wrist} \rightarrow a_{6D-eef} \rightarrow a_{gripper}$. This leverages the Transformer's ability to handle variable-length inputs via attention masks and position IDs.
 - The ordering prioritizes the shared bridging signal ($a_{3D-wrist}$) to be attended by the 6DoF action tokens, facilitating explicit knowledge transfer. The gripper signal follows as it typically occurs after end-effector motion.
 - Missing action components are masked in attention layers, and their corresponding loss calculations are omitted.

- **Flow-Matching Action Objective:** Action generation is trained using a flow matching objective. The model predicts a velocity $\hat{v}$ to denoise a noisy action chunk $a_{\tau_t}$ (interpolated between noise $\epsilon$ and ground-truth $a_t$) towards the ground-truth action. The loss function is $L_{FM} = \|\hat{v}(a_{\tau_t}, o_t, l, \tau) - v^*\|_2^2$, computed only on available action components. During inference, only $a_{6D-eef}$ and $a_{gripper}$ are generated for robot control by integrating the predicted velocity.

- **Vision-Language Co-training:** To prevent overfitting on action data and leverage external knowledge, the VLA model is simultaneously co-trained with a collection of vision-language data using a standard next-token prediction (NTP) objective ($L_{NTP}$). The total loss is a weighted sum over batches, with each sample contributing either to $L_{FM}$ or $L_{NTP}$.

#### 3.3. Training Strategies

A three-stage training strategy is adopted:

1. **Stage I: Pre-training on Human Actions:**
 - **Data:** Approximately 600 hours of diverse human actions, including EgoDex tasks (70h), out-sourced free-form household manipulation (500h), and in-lab human actions (45h).
 - **Supervision:** Only the bridging signal $L_{3D-wrist}^{FM}$ is used for supervision due to the scale and variability of human data making reliable rotation estimation and contact pattern standardization difficult.

2. **Stage II: Human-Robot Co-training:**
 - **Data:** Generalized robot pick-and-place data (72h, 100 objects, with instruction "put {object} into {container}") combined with task-specific in-lab human actions (3h per task across 15 tasks, e.g., "open the microwave door"). In-lab human operators mimic gripper postures, and hand closure is annotated as gripper control.
 - **Supervision:** All three losses ($L_{3D-wrist}^{FM}$, $L_{6D-eef}^{FM}$, $L_{gripper}^{FM}$) are active on robot data.
 - **Critical Binding Strategy:** To explicitly link the bridging representation to executable robot actions, $a_{3D-wrist_t}$ is randomly added to or substituted for $a_{6D-eef_t}$ as a prediction target for robot action data.

3. **Stage III: Few-shot Robot Post-training:**
 - **Data:** An additional set of 100 tele-operated robot actions per task from the in-lab human data. Only 10 trajectories per task are used to assess data efficiency.
 - **Purpose:** Fine-tuning the model with limited real-robot demonstrations to further improve performance.

**Implementation Details:** The VLA model uses a Mixture-of-Transformer architecture with approximately 4 billion parameters. It is initialized from a pre-trained VLM. Different global batch sizes are used across stages (1024 for Stage I, 256 for Stages II and III). The action transformer's effective batch size is increased by repeating the VLM's KV-cache.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted on 15 bi-manual manipulation tasks, investigated the effectiveness, scalability, and properties of the proposed bridging action representation.

- **Bridging Action Enables Manipulation Skill Transfer Beyond Generic Pick-and-Place:**
 - Training solely on generalized robot pick-and-place data resulted in low performance (0.21 average progress, 0.12 success rate) on the novel evaluation tasks, indicating its insufficiency for complex manipulation.
 - Co-training the model with both robot pick-and-place data and task-specific human actions using the bridging representation (Stage II) led to substantial improvements (0.49 average progress, 0.45 success rate), demonstrating that the bridging action effectively transfers manipulation knowledge beyond simple pick-and-place tasks. This indicates the robot learned skills like opening microwaves or stacking cups directly from human demonstrations.

- **Scalability with Large-Scale Human-Only Pre-training:**
 - Initiating co-training (Stage II) with a model pre-trained on a large scale of human-only data (Stage I, 600 hours, supervising only $a_{3D-wrist}$) further significantly improved performance (0.65 average progress, 0.57 success rate). This finding suggests that the bridging action representation is scalable and benefits from large-scale human data, even when that data only provides translation-based supervision.

- **Superiority of Translation-Only Bridging Actions over 6DoF Human Actions:**
 - A direct comparison showed that co-training with the proposed translation-only (`a_3D-wrist`) bridging action achieved quantitatively better results (0.44 average progress, 0.22 success rate) compared to co-training with traditional 6DoF human wrist actions (0.35 average progress, 0.12 success rate).
 - Qualitative analysis indicated that 6DoF human actions often led to "noisy and twisted behaviors" in the robot, while the translation-only bridging actions produced more stable and natural robot movements, aligning the robot's end-effector with target objects more effectively.

- **Improved Data Efficiency in Few-Shot Robot Post-training:**
 - Human-only pre-training (Stage I) significantly enhanced the data efficiency for subsequent few-shot robot post-training (Stage III, using only 10 robot trajectories per task). Models benefiting from Stage I pre-training achieved an overall success rate of 55.00% during post-training, compared to 35.83% for models without this pre-training. This suggests that pre-training with human actions, even if non-executable, provides valuable foundational knowledge that accelerates learning from limited robot data.

- **Essential Role of Bridging Objective on Robot Data:**
 - An ablation study confirmed the critical importance of supervising the bridging action (`a_3D-wrist`) on robot data during co-training (Stage II). Removing this objective drastically degraded performance, with the overall success rate dropping from 38.33% to 12.50%. This demonstrates that explicitly binding the bridging representation to executable robot actions is necessary for effective skill transfer.

- **Alignment of Human-only Pre-training with Executable Robot Actions:**
 - Analysis of training losses during co-training showed that models initialized with human-only pre-training (Stage I) exhibited lower training losses and faster convergence for both the 6DoF end-effector action (`a_6D-eef`) and the gripper action (`a_gripper`) compared to models trained from scratch. This implies that optimizing the non-executable wrist-translation action (`a_3D-wrist`) shares a similar objective landscape with executable robot actions, facilitating knowledge transfer.
 - Visualization of predicted actions (Fig. 10) confirmed that the generated bridging actions (`a_3D-wrist`) and 6DoF end-effector actions (`a_6D-eef`) aligned closely across diverse tasks, further suggesting `a_3D-wrist` as a reliable proxy for learning transferable manipulation knowledge.

- **Upper Bound of Bridging Representation:**
 - An experiment treating task-specific robot demonstrations (100 trajectories/task) as if they were human action data (i.e., transforming them into translation-only `a_3D-wrist` and removing observation/action noise gaps) achieved substantially higher performance (73.54% average progress, 55.83% success rate) than the default co-training with human data (59.75% average progress, 38.33% success rate). This indicates that the bridging representation itself is an effective medium for skill transfer, and its potential is maximized when visual and action noise disparities between embodiments are reduced.

- **Failure Case Analysis:**
 - The primary failure cases occurred in tasks demanding precise end-effector configuration during contact-rich manipulation (e.g., "insert straw into cup," "open drawer"). Robots often demonstrated correct task intent but failed at critical steps requiring fine rotational adjustments or secure grasping. This limitation is attributed to the intentional discarding of rotational supervision from human data for robustness against noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenge of effectively transferring manipulation skills from human demonstrations to robotic platforms. The proposed methodology and findings carry several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Scalability of Robot Learning:** By demonstrating that robots can acquire novel, task-specific manipulation skills from large-scale human action data, the work offers a more scalable and economical alternative to traditional robot-centric data collection. Human data is abundant, diverse, and cheaper to collect, which could significantly accelerate the development and deployment of advanced robotic capabilities.
- **Robust Human-to-Robot Skill Transfer:** The introduction of the relative wrist translation as a "bridging action" provides a robust mechanism to overcome inherent limitations of human demonstration data, specifically the noise in 6DoF hand pose estimates and the morphological mismatch between human hands and robot grippers. This robust transfer is critical for reliable real-world robot operation.
- **Advancement in Cross-Embodiment Learning:** The concept of an embodiment-agnostic bridging action and the interleaved action token design contribute to a more generalized framework for learning across different physical systems. This approach could be extended to other forms of cross-embodiment learning, enabling robots to learn from a wider variety of sources beyond human demonstrations.
- **Improved Data Efficiency for Robot Training:** The finding that human-only pre-training significantly boosts data efficiency during subsequent few-shot robot post-training is impactful. It suggests that large foundational models can be pre-trained on broadly available human data, then quickly adapted to specific robot hardware and tasks with minimal, costly robot-specific demonstrations. This could lead to faster iteration cycles and broader applicability of robot learning techniques.
- **Foundational for Generalist Robot Policies:** The VLA model architecture and multi-stage training strategy contribute to the development of more generalist robot policies. By integrating vision, language, and action into a unified framework and leveraging diverse data sources, the research moves towards robots that can understand and execute a wider range of instructions and tasks in varied environments.
- **Guidance for Future Research Directions:** The paper identifies limitations, such as the robot's struggles with tasks requiring fine rotational adjustments, as a consequence of discarding rotational supervision from human data. This directly informs future work by highlighting the need to selectively incorporate limited, reliable rotation cues, perhaps from more controlled human data or fused with robot self-supervision, to tackle highly contact-rich or precise manipulation tasks. It also emphasizes the potential benefits of reducing the observation and action noise gaps between human and robot embodiments for further improvements.
- **Accelerated Automation and Real-world Applications:** The ability to teach robots complex skills more easily from human examples has direct implications for various industries and domestic applications, including manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and personal assistance. Rapid transfer of skills could lead to quicker deployment of automated solutions for intricate tasks.

In summary, this work provides a technically sound approach to a significant problem in robot learning, demonstrating improved performance and scalability. Its implications extend to advancing the theoretical understanding of skill transfer, enhancing practical robot capabilities, and guiding the development of future generalist robotic systems.
