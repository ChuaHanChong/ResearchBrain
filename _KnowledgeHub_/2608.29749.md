# DriftingVLA: Native One-Step Vision-Language-Action Generation via Per-Dimension Temporal Drifting

> [!summary] Summary
> DriftingVLA is a native one-step vision-language-action policy that replaces flow matching's iterative integration with a training-time distribution-drifting objective, learning a direct noise-to-action-chunk generator. Per-Dimension Temporal Drifting gives each action channel its own drifting geometry over its full temporal trajectory. It reaches 98.32% on LIBERO, 81.09% on RoboTwin 2.0, 77.67% real-world, and 3.36x faster chunk generation.

## Problem

- Flow-based VLAs such as pi-0.5 recover each action chunk by numerically integrating a velocity field, putting repeated action-expert evaluations directly on the robot-time control path.
- Existing one-step VLAs derive their generator from the inherited flow transport process via distillation or interval-averaged velocity, rather than training a native direct conditional generator.
- Prior drifting-based policies group action chunks chunk-wise or step-wise: the former mixes heterogeneous channel geometry, the latter fragments each channel's temporal trajectory.

## Method

- Reconfigures ==pi-0.5=='s action branch as a direct noise-to-action-chunk generator, freshly initializing the action expert while retaining and jointly post-training the pretrained ==VLM== backbone.
- **Per-Dimension Temporal Drifting** treats each action channel's full H-step trajectory as one drifting group, giving channel-specific distance rulers, affinities, ==attraction-repulsion== coefficients and force normalization.
- **Shared-context sibling generation** encodes the multimodal condition once and reuses the prefix across G latent samples, whose attraction-repulsion drifting field forms ==stop-gradient regression targets==.

## Results

- LIBERO overall **98.32%** versus **97.10%** for 10-NFE pi-0.5, **97.48%** SnapFlow, **92.85%** MeanFlowVLA; RoboTwin 2.0 **81.09%** versus **79.58%** and **63.76%** for one-step-truncated pi-0.5.
- Real-world six-task suite reaches **77.67%** versus **74.22%** for pi-0.5, **69.44%** SnapFlow, with gains on both single-arm (**89.67%**) and dual-arm (**71.67%**) subsets.
- Ablations: PDTD beats chunk-wise by **3.04** (LIBERO) and **6.92** (RoboTwin) points; warm-starting the flow expert drops to **93.33%**, freezing the VLM to **94.57%**; latency **227.61** to **67.67 ms**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - One-step action generation is best obtained by changing the learned function, not truncating the solver: identical inference budget yields 94.02% versus 98.32%.
> - Grouping geometry for distributional training signals can be factorized per channel without factorizing the generator, preserving cross-dimensional coupling needed for coordinated bimanual control.
> - Moving refinement from inference to optimization is cheap: eight training siblings add only 3.78% GPU-hours and 9.54% peak memory, with zero deployment overhead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an area of development in general-purpose robotic manipulation, leveraging pre-trained multimodal representations for end-to-end continuous control. Recent advancements in this field, such as π₀ and π₀.₅, employ flow matching for generating continuous action chunks. This approach allows policies to model complex and multimodal robot behaviors while benefiting from the semantic and perceptual priors of pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbones.

However, a characteristic of flow-based VLAs is their reliance on multi-step numerical integration of a learned vector field to produce each action chunk during inference. This iterative process introduces latency, which can be a critical constraint in online robot control scenarios. The research landscape has seen efforts to address this latency. Some approaches aim to reduce execution latency while retaining iterative generators, for example, by overlapping action generation with execution or prioritizing near-term actions during flow sampling. Other methods directly pursue one-step action generation by modifying the training or action-generation formulation, such as distilling multi-step flow trajectories into shortcut predictors or adapting flow principles to predict interval-averaged velocities for one-step generation.

DriftingVLA contributes to this landscape by proposing a "native one-step" VLA generation mechanism. Unlike methods that distill or approximate existing iterative processes, DriftingVLA fundamentally reformulates the generative process to learn a direct noise-to-action-chunk mapping. It builds upon the principle of distribution drifting, where distribution refinement occurs during the optimization phase of training rather than through iterative sampling at inference time. This positions DriftingVLA as an alternative to iterative flow-based VLAs and prior one-step acceleration techniques, by directly designing for one-step deployment from its training objective.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for DriftingVLA is to mitigate the deployment-time latency associated with conventional flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models, exemplified by π₀ and π₀.₅, are capable of generating expressive continuous action chunks. However, their reliance on numerically integrating a learned vector field for each action chunk necessitates multiple sequential evaluations of an action expert. This iterative refinement process occurs on the robot-time critical path, thereby increasing latency and potentially hindering responsiveness in real-time robotic control applications.

To address this challenge, DriftingVLA sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Achieve Native One-Step Action Generation**: The central objective is to develop a VLA policy that can generate a complete action chunk in a single forward pass of its action expert. This requires shifting the distribution refinement from an inference-time iterative process to an optimization-time learning process. The model aims to learn a direct mapping from action-shaped Gaussian noise to a complete action chunk, rather than parameterizing a local velocity field that requires subsequent integration.

2. **Preserve and Adapt Pre-trained Multimodal Representations**: DriftingVLA seeks to leverage the representational power of pre-trained VLM backbones, which provide valuable vision-language priors for conditioning robot behavior. The objective is to retain this pre-trained backbone while adapting its action generation interface. Specifically, the original action expert, specialized for flow-velocity prediction, needs to be reconfigured as a direct noise-to-action generator without discarding the learned multimodal context.

3. **Address the Structured Nature of Robot Action Chunks**: Robot action chunks are typically represented as tensors with distinct temporal (H) and action dimension (D) axes. This structure implies heterogeneous control semantics and distributional characteristics across dimensions. Existing distribution drifting frameworks, such as those that treat the entire chunk as a single unit (Chunk-wise) or each temporal slice independently (Step-wise), present limitations:
 - **Chunk-wise drifting**: While preserving the complete temporal horizon, it forces all heterogeneous action channels (e.g., translation, rotation, gripper commands) to share the same distance scale, neighborhood statistics, and drifting geometry. This can lead to "cross-channel geometric interference."
 - **Step-wise drifting**: This approach treats each temporal slice independently, reducing group scope but fragmenting the complete temporal evolution of individual action channels. It also continues to mix heterogeneous channels within each timestep.

4. **Introduce Per-Dimension Temporal Drifting (PDTD)**: To overcome the limitations of prior grouping strategies, DriftingVLA aims to develop a new organization for action chunks. PDTD is designed to treat the complete temporal trajectory of each action dimension (e.g., the full `H`-step trajectory for a specific x-translation dimension) as an independent drifting unit. This objective allows for finer-grained modeling and shaping of dimension-specific action distributions, while ensuring that the complete temporal evolution of each channel is considered as a cohesive unit. A crucial aspect is that this per-dimension decomposition applies only to the training objective's geometry, ensuring that the shared VLA model can still jointly generate the complete action chunk and thus preserve cross-dimensional dependencies essential for coordinated control.

In summary, the motivation is to achieve real-time responsive robotic control by eliminating iterative action refinement at deployment, while simultaneously improving or maintaining control performance through a refined and adapted generative policy learning approach that is sensitive to the inherent structure of robotic actions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DriftingVLA's methodology involves adapting a pre-trained flow-based VLA architecture, specifically π₀.₅, to enable native one-step action generation through distribution drifting. This is achieved by reconfiguring the generative interface and introducing a novel action-chunk organization strategy.

**A. From Flow-Based VLA Models to Direct Action Generation**

1. **Direct Conditional Generation**: DriftingVLA directly parameterizes the deployed policy `f_θ` to map an action-shaped Gaussian latent sample `Z_t ∈ R^(H×D)` and a multimodal context `c_t` to a complete action chunk `b_A_t ∈ R^(H×D)`. This approach contrasts with flow-based methods that learn a velocity field for numerical integration. The function learned during training is the one directly executed at deployment, without approximation or truncation.

2. **Preserving Pre-trained VLA Representation**: The architecture maintains the two-component structure of π₀.₅, comprising a pre-trained multimodal backbone (`θ_VLM`) and an action-generation branch (`θ_AE`). `θ_VLM` is initialized from the pre-trained π₀.₅ model, leveraging its learned vision-language representations. However, `θ_AE` is freshly initialized, as its role shifts from estimating a flow vector field to directly generating an action chunk from noise. During post-training, both `θ_VLM` and `θ_AE` are jointly optimized, allowing the VLM's representations to adapt to the new direct generative objective.

3. **Shared-Context Sibling Generation**: Distribution drifting requires multiple "sibling" samples from the conditional generator during training. For each training example `(c_i, A_i)`, `G` independent latent chunks `Z^(g)_i` are sampled. To optimize computational efficiency, the expensive multimodal context encoding is performed only once for `c_i` to obtain `P_i = E_θ_VLM(c_i)`. This `P_i` is then reused across all `G` siblings for action generation: `b_A^(g)_i = F_θ_AE(Z^(g)_i; P_i)`. This design reduces repeated VLM computation while ensuring that all siblings are generated from the same conditional distribution. The gradients from all sibling-based losses contribute to updating both the VLM and action expert parameters.

**B. Per-Dimension Temporal Drifting (PDTD)**

The paper identifies the organization of the `H × D` action chunk as a critical design choice for distribution drifting, given the distinct semantics of temporal and action-dimension axes. PDTD is introduced to address the limitations of prior grouping strategies:

- **Chunk-wise Drifting**: Treats the entire `H × D` chunk as a single `HD`-dimensional unit. This preserves the full temporal horizon but forces all heterogeneous action channels (e.g., translation, rotation, gripper states) to share a single distance metric, scale, and neighborhood statistics, leading to potential "cross-channel geometric interference."
- **Step-wise Drifting**: Processes each temporal slice `A_(h,:) ∈ R^D` independently. While this allows for distinct statistics at different timesteps, it fragments the complete temporal evolution `A_(:,d) ∈ R^H` of each action channel across `H` separate problems and still mixes heterogeneous channels at each timestep.

**PDTD Approach**: PDTD treats the complete temporal trajectory of each action dimension `A_(:,d) ∈ R^H` as an individual drifting unit. This forms `D` distinct groups, each of `H` dimensions. This organization allows each action channel to have its own temporal drifting geometry, thereby accommodating differences in numerical range, variability, and control semantics.

**Factorized Geometry, Joint Action Generation**: A key aspect is that PDTD factorizes only the *geometry* used to construct the training signal, not the *action generator itself*. The complete action chunk `b_A^(g)_i` is generated jointly by `f_θ(Z^(g)_i, c_i)` before any grouping. PDTD is applied afterward to construct the training signal. This ensures that the model can still learn and represent cross-dimensional dependencies critical for coordinated multi-axis and multi-joint control, even while the supervision signal is dimension-specific.

**C. Per-Dimension Drifting Objective**

The drifting objective in PDTD builds on the attraction-repulsion mechanism. For each condition `c_i` and channel `d`, a reference set `Y^(d)_i` is formed, comprising `G` generated sibling trajectories (`x^(d)_i,g`) and the demonstrated trajectory (`x^(d)_(i,+)`).

1. **Channel-Specific Distance Ruler**: A unique distance ruler `s_d` is estimated for each action channel `d` based on the squared differences between generated and reference trajectories. This normalization allows `s_d` to reflect the specific trajectory variation within that channel, decoupling it from other action dimensions.

2. **Multi-Scale Attraction and Repulsion**: Normalized distances are used to construct affinities `W^(ρ,d)_i,g,u` for multiple bandwidths `ρ`. These affinities balance attraction to demonstrated trajectories (positive references) and repulsion from generated sibling trajectories (negative references). Bidirectionally normalized affinities ensure that weight is assigned only when both the generated trajectory and the reference mutually favor the interaction.

3. **Force Construction**: Trajectory coordinates are normalized by `η_d = max(s_d/√H, ε)` before constructing scale-specific forces `F^(ρ,d)_i,g`. These forces are combined to form the multi-scale drifting field `V^(d)_i,g`, with per-(ρ,d) normalization to prevent any single scale or channel from dominating due to raw numerical magnitude. This ensures that the channel-specific factorization is maintained throughout the entire drifting-field calculation.

4. **Stop-Gradient Regression Target**: The calculated drifting field `V^(d)_i,g` is converted into a frozen regression target `e_x^(d)_i,g = sg(x^(d)_i,g + V^(d)_i,g)`.

5. **PDTD Objective**: The training objective `L_PDTD` is the mean squared error between the generated trajectory coordinates `x^(d)_i,g,h` and their corresponding stop-gradient targets `e_x^(d)_i,g,h` across all valid dimensions, siblings, and timesteps. This target is recomputed at each optimization iteration, allowing the generator parameters to progressively absorb the distribution-level corrections.

**D. Training and Native One-Step Inference**

- **Padding-aware Action Space**: PDTD computations are restricted to valid physical action dimensions and temporal coordinates, excluding padded entries used to accommodate varying embodiments or horizons.
- **Post-training**: The described PDTD objective jointly updates the pre-trained VLM and the freshly initialized action-generation branch. The shared-context sampling contributes to efficient training.
- **Inference**: At deployment, all distribution-level drifting mechanisms are bypassed. A single Gaussian latent chunk `Z_t` is sampled, and the policy `f_θ(Z_t, c_t)` is evaluated once to directly produce the action chunk `b_A_t`.

This methodology allows DriftingVLA to achieve efficient, one-step action generation by shifting the computational burden of distribution refinement from inference to training, while carefully considering the geometric properties of robot actions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DriftingVLA involved comprehensive testing across simulation benchmarks and real-world manipulation tasks, along with ablation studies on its core design choices and computational efficiency.

**Simulation Benchmark Results:**

- **LIBERO**: DriftingVLA achieved an overall success rate of 98.32%, which was 1.22 percentage points higher than the 10-NFE π₀.₅ policy (97.10%) and 0.84 points higher than SnapFlow (97.48%), a representative one-step baseline. On the LIBERO-Long suite, where tasks are less saturated, DriftingVLA reached 94.33% success, surpassing π₀.₅ (92.40%) and SnapFlow (93.07%). A direct one-step evaluation of the conventionally trained π₀.₅ policy (π₀.₅-1step) yielded only 94.02% overall, indicating that merely truncating the solver steps of a flow policy is less effective than DriftingVLA's native one-step generation.
- **RoboTwin 2.0**: DriftingVLA attained 81.09% overall success, exceeding the 10-NFE π₀.₅ (79.58%) by 1.51 points and SnapFlow (78.53%) by 2.56 points. On the harder domain-randomized split, DriftingVLA achieved 77.56%, compared to 76.23% for π₀.₅ and 75.25% for SnapFlow. The π₀.₅-1step variant performed substantially worse at 63.76% overall, including 53.29% on the Hard split. MeanFlowVLA was also consistently outperformed by DriftingVLA on both benchmarks. These results indicate that DriftingVLA's native one-step generation can match or exceed the performance of iterative flow policies and other one-step alternatives.

**Real-World Evaluation:**

- **Overall Performance**: Across six real-world manipulation tasks (two single-arm, four dual-arm), DriftingVLA achieved an overall success rate of 77.67%. This represented a 3.45 percentage point improvement over 10-NFE π₀.₅ (74.22%), and significantly outperformed SnapFlow (69.44%) and MeanFlowVLA (65.11%). DriftingVLA recorded the best mean success rate on five out of the six tasks.
- **Single-arm and Dual-arm Tasks**: DriftingVLA demonstrated improvements in both task categories. For single-arm tasks, average success increased from 86.00% (π₀.₅) to 89.67% (DriftingVLA). For dual-arm tasks, which require coordinated control, average success rose from 68.34% (π₀.₅) to 71.67% (DriftingVLA). This suggests that PDTD's per-dimension geometric supervision does not hinder the VLA model's capacity for coordinated multi-axis action generation.

**Ablation Studies:**

- **Action-Chunk Drifting Geometry**: The Per-Dimension Temporal Drifting (PDTD) consistently outperformed other proposed geometries. On LIBERO, PDTD (98.32%) improved over Chunk-wise (95.28%) by 3.04 points and Step-wise (93.10%) by 5.22 points. On RoboTwin 2.0, the gains were more pronounced: PDTD (81.09%) surpassed Chunk-wise (74.17%) by 6.92 points and Step-wise (70.43%) by 10.66 points. This suggests that allowing channel-specific geometric statistics while preserving full temporal trajectories is beneficial. The Step-wise < Chunk-wise < PDTD ordering was consistent in real-world results.
- **Pre-trained VLA Adaptation**: The study highlighted the importance of specific adaptation strategies. Warm-starting DriftingVLA with a flow-pretrained action expert resulted in lower performance (93.33% on LIBERO) compared to a freshly initialized action expert (98.32%), indicating the benefit of relearning the generative interface. Furthermore, jointly post-training the VLM and the new action expert (98.32%) yielded a 3.75-point improvement over freezing the VLM and training only the action expert (94.57%), emphasizing the value of adapting the multimodal representation to the direct drifting objective.

**Computational Efficiency:**

- **Inference Latency**: DriftingVLA reduced the mean action-chunk latency from 227.61 ms (for 10-NFE π₀.₅) to 67.67 ms, representing a 3.36× speedup and a 70.3% reduction in latency. This directly confirms the effectiveness of its one-step generation in reducing deployment-time overhead.
- **Training Cost**: While DriftingVLA's training involves multiple sibling samples, the overhead was moderate. Increasing the number of drifting siblings (G) from 2 to 8 resulted in a 3.78% increase in GPU-hours (from 87.92 to 91.24) and a 9.54% increase in peak memory (from 45.99 GiB to 50.38 GiB). Importantly, DriftingVLA with G=8 still required fewer GPU-hours (91.24) than the π₀.₅ reference (108.49), due to the shared-context sampling design.

In summary, DriftingVLA demonstrates that native one-step VLA generation can achieve higher or comparable control performance to iterative flow policies, with significant reductions in deployment latency. Its specific design choices, particularly PDTD and strategic VLA adaptation, contribute to its performance and efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of DriftingVLA represents an advancement in the field of robot learning, specifically in addressing the trade-off between expressive action generation and real-time operational efficiency. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several points:

1. **Bridging the Performance-Latency Gap**: The work demonstrates that native one-step action generation can achieve or surpass the control performance of iterative, multi-step flow-based VLA models, while concurrently delivering a substantial reduction in inference latency (a 3.36× speedup). This outcome is significant because it challenges the implicit assumption that complex action generation necessarily requires iterative refinement at deployment. This can facilitate the broader adoption of VLA models in time-critical robotic applications.

2. **Novel Generative Policy Paradigm**: DriftingVLA introduces a generative policy learning framework where distribution refinement is explicitly shifted from inference-time sample evolution to optimization-time generator learning. This contrasts with methods that distill existing iterative processes or adapt them with approximations. This native one-step approach provides an alternative for designing efficient generative models in robotics.

3. **Refined Action-Chunk Geometry for Drifting Models**: The introduction of Per-Dimension Temporal Drifting (PDTD) offers a principled method for structuring distribution drifting when applied to complex, multi-dimensional robot action chunks. By allowing each action channel (e.g., x-translation, gripper state) to have its own temporal trajectory and corresponding geometric statistics, while preserving the VLA's capacity for joint, cross-dimensional control, PDTD contributes to more effective learning of action distributions in heterogeneous action spaces. The empirical validation of PDTD over other grouping strategies underscores its importance as a design choice.

4. **Guidance for Adapting Pre-trained VLAs**: The ablation studies provide insights into effectively leveraging pre-trained VLM backbones for new generative objectives. The findings suggest that a fresh initialization of the action expert, combined with joint post-training of both the VLM and the action expert, is more effective than attempting to warm-start from a flow-pretrained action expert or freezing the VLM. This provides practical guidance for researchers working with large pre-trained models in robotics.

5. **Practical Implications for Robotics**: The measured 70.3% reduction in action-chunk latency directly translates to more responsive and potentially safer robotic systems. This increased efficiency can enable real-time control for complex manipulation tasks in dynamic environments, facilitating deployment in industrial automation, assistive robotics, and human-robot interaction scenarios where low latency is paramount.

6. **Future Research Directions**: DriftingVLA opens avenues for further exploration. Its success suggests the potential for applying native one-step generation to even larger and more diverse multi-embodiment VLA architectures. Future work could investigate adaptive or learned drifting geometries, and explore the approach in broader contexts such as long-horizon planning or highly interactive robotic tasks, further advancing the capabilities of efficient robot learning.
