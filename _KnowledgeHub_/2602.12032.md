# When would Vision-Proprioception Policies Fail in Robotic Manipulation?

> [!summary] Summary
> This research investigates the reasons behind the inconsistent performance of vision-proprioception policies in robotic manipulation, identifying that the visual modality is often suppressed during motion-transition phases. The Gradient Adjustment with Phase-guidance (GAP) algorithm is introduced to adaptively modulate optimization, enhancing collaboration between modalities and leading to more robust and generalizable policies.

## Problem

- Inconsistent empirical observations regarding the efficacy of vision-proprioception policies in robotic manipulation, with some studies reporting improvements and others showing detrimental effects compared to vision-only approaches.
- A lack of clear understanding about *when* and *why* vision-proprioception policies succeed or fail, despite the intuitive benefit of combining modalities.
- Existing explanations like overfitting or causal confusion do not fully account for the observed discrepancies in multimodal policy performance.

## Method

- The authors formally analyze vision-proprioception policies under the ==Behavior Cloning== paradigm, focusing on gradient dynamics during training.
- The Gradient Adjustment with Phase-guidance (GAP) algorithm is proposed, which estimates 'motion-transition phases' using Change Point Detection and a temporal network.
- GAP adaptively modulates the learning rate for proprioceptive feature parameters, reducing their gradient magnitude during motion-transition phases to prevent visual modality suppression and encourage better collaboration.

## Results

- GAP-equipped policies consistently outperformed vision-only and standard fusion baselines, achieving superior success rates (e.g., **94.2%** in Meta-World pick-place) across various simulated and real-world tasks.
- Intervention experiments and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) generalization tests demonstrated that GAP enhances the utilization of the vision modality, reducing performance degradation during transition phases and improving robustness to visual variations.
- The GAP algorithm proved versatile, improving performance across different policy architectures (MLP, diffusion, transformer) and with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models like Octo, showing an average **17%** improvement on Octo-VP models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision-proprioception policies frequently fail during specific 'motion-transition phases' of a task, where accurate target localization is crucial, due to underutilization of the visual modality.
> - The vision modality is suppressed during training because policies 'gravitate toward' concise proprioceptive signals, which offer faster loss reduction, thereby dominating optimization and hindering the learning of subtle visual cues.
> - Dynamically adjusting the optimization process by reducing the influence of proprioceptive gradients during critical motion-transition phases can effectively prevent visual suppression and foster robust multimodal learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of vision and proprioception is a critical area in robotic manipulation. Vision, as the most commonly used modality [16–18], provides rich information for object detection and scene understanding. Proprioception, on the other hand, offers immediate, precise feedback on the robot's internal state (e.g., joint angles, gripper position), crucial for low-level control and fine motor behaviors [1–4]. The expectation has been that combining these modalities would lead to more robust and precise manipulation policies [6, 8, 19–23].

However, the existing literature presents a puzzling inconsistency. Some studies, like HPT, have reported clear performance improvements with joint vision-proprioception policies. In contrast, others, such as Octo, observed that policies trained with additional proprioception sometimes performed worse than vision-only policies. This discrepancy highlights a fundamental gap in understanding: *when and why* do vision-proprioception policies succeed or fail in robotic manipulation? Prior explanations for these failures include overfitting or causal confusion.

This paper directly addresses this critical inconsistency by delving into the concept of "Modality Temporality" [10–12, 24, 25]. This concept posits that the optimal contribution of each sensory modality can vary significantly across different phases of a manipulation task. For instance, vision might be paramount for initial object localization, while proprioception becomes more crucial for executing precise, consistent movements. Existing works have explored dynamic fusion and modality selection to leverage this temporal nature.

The present work extends this understanding by investigating modality temporality from an *optimization perspective*. It identifies that during specific "motion-transition phases" – periods requiring shifts in robot motion and accurate target localization – the vision modality in joint policies often becomes suppressed. This suppression arises because the policy "gravitates toward" more concise proprioceptive signals for faster loss reduction during training, thereby overshadowing the learning of subtle visual cues. By pinpointing this optimization-level issue, the authors offer a novel, fine-grained explanation for the observed inconsistencies and propose a targeted solution. Their work thus significantly contributes to building more reliable and generalizable multimodal robot learning systems, bridging the gap between theoretical expectations and empirical observations in the field.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the inconsistent empirical observations regarding the efficacy of vision-proprioception policies in robotic manipulation. While intuitively, combining visual and proprioceptive information should enhance precision and robustness, recent studies have shown conflicting results, with some reporting improvements and others observing limited gains or even detrimental effects. This raises a fundamental question: "When would vision-proprioception policies fail in robotic manipulation?"

To address this, the paper sets out the following key objectives:

1. **Investigate the failure points:** To identify specific task sub-phases where vision-proprioception policies underperform, particularly pinpointing the role (or lack thereof) of the vision modality during these critical moments. The authors hypothesize that these failures occur during "motion-transition phases" that require accurate target localization.
2. **Uncover the underlying cause:** To analyze the failure mechanism from an optimization perspective, explaining why the vision modality's learning might be suppressed during these phases. The hypothesis is that the policy "naturally gravitates toward concise proprioceptive signals that offer faster loss reduction when training, thereby dominating the optimization and suppressing the learning of the visual modality during motion-transition phases."
3. **Propose an alleviation strategy:** To develop a novel algorithm, Gradient Adjustment with Phase-guidance (GAP), that adaptively modulates the optimization process to prevent the suppression of the visual modality and enable dynamic, effective collaboration between vision and proprioception.
4. **Validate the proposed solution comprehensively:** To rigorously test the GAP algorithm's versatility and effectiveness across a wide range of scenarios, including:
 - Simulated and real-world environments.
 - One-arm and dual-arm robotic setups.
 - Various policy architectures (MLP-based, diffusion-based, transformer-based).
 - Compatibility with both conventional and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.
 - Diverse manipulation tasks, including articulated object manipulation, contact-rich interactions, and rotation-sensitive tasks.

Ultimately, the research aims to provide valuable insights for the development of more robust, generalizable, and high-performing vision-proprioception policies in robotic manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors approach the problem by first formalizing the robot learning task within the Behavior Cloning (BC) paradigm under the Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework. The policy `π` takes an observation `o_t` (comprising RGB sensor readings `v_t` and robot proprioceptive information `s_t`, including gripper 6D pose and opening degree) and predicts actions `a_t`. The training objective is to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between predicted and expert actions.

The vision-proprioception policy employs a standard joint-learning architecture: separate feature extractors (`ϕ_v`, `ϕ_s`) process visual and proprioceptive inputs, respectively. These features are then concatenated and fed into a policy head (`ψ`). The core of their analysis lies in understanding the gradient dynamics during this process.

**Optimization Analysis of Vision-Proprioception Policies:**
The authors argue that during motion-transition phases, visual cues tend to be subtle (e.g., pixel-level changes when a gripper closes), whereas proprioceptive signals are concise and directly indicate changes in robot state (e.g., gripper opening degree). This disparity leads the policy to "naturally gravitate toward" proprioceptive signals during training, as they offer a faster path to reducing the training loss. This proprioception dominance effectively suppresses the learning of the visual modality's parameters (`ω_v`), rendering vision under-utilized during critical motion-transition phases where accurate object localization is crucial for generalization.

**Proposed Solution: Gradient Adjustment with Phase-guidance (GAP) Algorithm:**
To mitigate this suppression, the authors propose GAP, which adaptively modulates the optimization of the proprioceptive modality. The algorithm consists of three main components:

1. **Motion Representation of Robot (Section 4.1):** The robot's motion between timesteps `i` and `j` (`m_i:j`) is concisely represented by changes in the gripper's 3D position (`p_j - p_i`), orientation (`θ_j - θ_i`), and opening degree (`g_j - g_i`). This captures the essential aspects of robot movement.

2. **Motion-Transition Phase Estimation (Section 4.2):**
 - **Change Point Detection (CPD):** To identify discrete "motion-consistent phases," the CPD algorithm is applied to the proprioceptive signals. It minimizes a total cost based on a distance metric (`d`, Equation 4) that quantifies motion inconsistency between a phase and adjacent motions. This metric considers the cosine similarity of position and orientation changes, and the sign consistency of gripper opening changes, weighted by hyperparameters `α` and `β`.
 - **Temporal Network (LSTM):** Recognizing that motion transitions are continuous processes, the discrete outputs from CPD are refined. A temporal network (LSTM) is trained on temporal differences of proprioceptive information (`Δs_i`) to predict "motion-transition phase indicators" (`ρ_i`). These indicators represent the probability of each timestep belonging to a motion-transition phase, providing a continuous measure and mitigating errors from discrete segmentation.

3. **Gradient Adjustment for Modality Collaboration (Section 4.3):**
 - During the gradient descent (GD)-based policy learning, the parameters of the proprioceptive feature chunk (`ω_s`) are updated with an adjusted learning rate. The update rule is modified as: `ω_{s}^{j+1} = ω_{s}^{j} - λ ⋅ (1 - ρ) ⋅ η∇ω_{s}^{j} L_{BC}(ω_{s}^{j})`.
 - Here, `η` is the learning rate, and `λ` is a hyperparameter controlling the degree of adjustment. Crucially, `(1 - ρ)` acts as a scaling factor: when `ρ` (probability of being in a motion-transition phase) is high, `(1 - ρ)` is low, significantly reducing the magnitude of the proprioception's backward gradient. This effectively forces the policy to rely less on proprioception and more on the visual modality during motion-transition phases, preventing vision suppression.
 - Gradient adjustment is typically applied during the early stages of training (e.g., first 50 epochs) to avoid instability.

The proposed GAP algorithm is implemented and evaluated with various policy architectures, including MLP-based, diffusion-based, and transformer-based policies, demonstrating its broad compatibility. Visual features are extracted using a ResNet-18 backbone, temporal features via a 4-layer transformer, and proprioceptive features via a 3-layer MLP. Real-world experiments utilize a 6-DoF xArm 6 with a Robotiq gripper and Intel RealSense D435i cameras, alongside a dual-arm Cobot Magic platform.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across diverse simulated (Meta-World, RoboSuite) and real-world environments (one-arm, dual-arm setups) unequivocally support the authors' hypotheses and demonstrate the efficacy of the GAP algorithm.

### 5. Initial Problem Confirmation (Figure 1)

The study initially confirms the inconsistent performance, showing that standard vision-proprioception (VP) policies can perform 15.8% worse than vision-only (V-only) policies. An intervention experiment revealed that switching to a VP policy during "motion-transition phases" (e.g., "locate base", "assemble them") led to noticeable degradation, while having minimal impact during "motion-consistent phases" (e.g., "move forward"). This directly supports the claim that the vision modality of VP policies fails to take effect during critical transition periods.

### 6. GAP Leads to More Robust Vision-Proprioception Policies (Table 1)

Comparative analyses against V-only policies and other baselines (MS-Bot, Auxiliary Loss, Masking) consistently showed that VP policies equipped with GAP significantly outperform all competitors. For instance, in Meta-World's "pick-place" task, GAP achieved a 94.2% success rate compared to 91.8% for V-only and 78.4% for standard concatenation. In real-world "press button," GAP achieved 20/20 successes, surpassing 18/20 for V-only. This indicates that GAP effectively enables vision-proprioception policies to achieve superior precision and robustness.

### 7. GAP Enhances the Utilization of Vision Modality (Figure 4, Table 2, Table 12)

- **Intervention Experiment (Figure 4):** Repeating the intervention experiment with GAP-equipped policies showed a drastic reduction in performance degradation during motion-transition phases, with positive success rate changes (e.g., +11% for "locate base"), directly demonstrating that GAP enhances the utilization of the vision modality.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization (Table 2, Table 14, Table 15):** GAP policies consistently exhibited superior generalization in OOD scenarios where object initial positions or visual features (table/object color, lighting) varied. For example, in Meta-World "assembly (OOD)," GAP achieved 88% success compared to 78% for V-only and 62% for concatenation, highlighting its ability to foster more robust visual learning.
- **Linear-Probing Experiments (Table 12):** Directly evaluating the quality of learned visual features confirmed that the visual branch trained with GAP significantly outperformed its counterpart without GAP in predicting actions, particularly in OOD settings (e.g., 41% vs 26% for "threading (OOD)").

### 8. Compatibility with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models (Table 3)

Applying GAP to fine-tuned Octo-VP models (Octo-VP†) resulted in an average 17% performance improvement over the original Octo-VP and even surpassed the Octo-V (vision-only) baseline, confirming its effectiveness in enhancing dynamic collaboration within VLA architectures.

### 9. Versatility Across Modality Fusion Approaches (Table 4)

GAP successfully improved performance across various typical fusion approaches (Concatenation, Summation, FiLM), often enabling them to outperform V-only policies. This demonstrates the algorithm's broad applicability beyond a single fusion strategy.

### 10. Visualization of Motion-Transition Phase Estimation (Figure 5)

The estimated transition probabilities (`ρ`) sharply increased during key task moments (e.g., grasping, rotational movement), coinciding with decreases in scaled visual uncertainty. This visual evidence supports the notion that motion transitions are periods where visual cues are subtle, justifying the need for targeted vision enhancement.

### 11. Visualization of Loss Curves (Figure 6)

While GAP resulted in a slower initial loss reduction, it ultimately converged to a lower final loss, indicating a more effective and thorough learning process by preventing premature convergence dominated by proprioception.

### 12. Robustness and Ablations (Appendix B, C)

Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of each GAP module, showing that the refined motion representation for CPD and the LSTM for continuous phase estimation are crucial. The method was also found to be robust to noise injection and variations in hyperparameters, further solidifying its reliability. GAP also extends to on-policy updates, proving its applicability in online learning settings (Table 16).

In summary, the main findings establish that the failure of vision-proprioception policies is rooted in the suppression of the visual modality during motion-transition phases, and that the proposed GAP algorithm provides a robust, versatile, and effective solution to alleviate this, leading to significantly improved and more generalizable robotic manipulation policies.

### 13. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers profound significance and potential impact across several dimensions within the field of robotic manipulation and multimodal machine learning:

1. **Resolving a Critical Inconsistency:** The most immediate impact of this work is its ability to explain and address the long-standing inconsistency in the literature regarding the performance of vision-proprioception policies. By precisely identifying *when* (motion-transition phases) and *why* (optimization dominance by proprioception leading to visual suppression) these policies underperform, the authors provide a clear diagnostic and a targeted solution. This moves the field beyond empirical observation towards a more principled understanding of multimodal sensing in robotics.

2. **Deeper Understanding of Modality Collaboration:** The paper significantly advances the understanding of "modality temporality" – the idea that the importance of different sensory inputs changes over time during a task. By translating this conceptual understanding into a concrete optimization problem (gradient adjustment) guided by phase estimation, it offers a powerful framework for dynamically managing sensory contributions, which is crucial for complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks.

3. **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The comprehensive experimental results, particularly those demonstrating superior performance in Out-of-Distribution (OOD) scenarios and challenging real-world tasks, highlight GAP's ability to foster more generalizable and robust robotic policies. This is vital for deploying robots in real-world environments where variations in object placement, lighting, or object properties are common. The ability to effectively leverage both modalities without one suppressing the other leads to more resilient systems.

4. **Broad Applicability and Versatility:** GAP's compatibility with various policy architectures (MLP, diffusion, transformer), different modality fusion approaches (concatenation, summation, FiLM), and even cutting-edge Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (like Octo) is a major strength. This versatility means that the insights and the algorithm can be readily integrated into a wide array of existing and future robotic learning frameworks, increasing its potential reach and impact. The successful application in both simulated and real-world, one-arm and dual-arm setups further underscores its practical utility.

5. **Guiding Future Research:** By pinpointing an optimization-level problem in multimodal learning, this work opens new avenues for research into more sophisticated training methodologies that actively manage modality interactions. The limitations section, suggesting exploration in cross-embodiment datasets, points to future directions for even more generalized and transferable robotic skills. Moreover, the insights gained can inform the design of future multimodal perception systems, ensuring that subtle but critical information from all sensors is effectively utilized.

In conclusion, "When would Vision-Proprioception Policies Fail in Robotic Manipulation?" provides not just an answer to a critical question but also a robust, adaptable algorithm that promises to unlock the full potential of vision-proprioception integration, paving the way for more capable and intelligent robotic systems.
