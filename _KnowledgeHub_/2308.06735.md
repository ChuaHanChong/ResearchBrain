# AerialVLN: Vision-and-Language Navigation for UAVs

> [!summary] Summary
> AerialVLN introduces a new vision-and-language navigation task for Unmanned Aerial Vehicles in complex, continuous outdoor city environments. The associated dataset and simulator enable training agents to follow natural language instructions, revealing a substantial performance gap between current AI models (1.0-1.6% success rate) and human pilots (80.8% success rate).

## Problem

- Existing Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) tasks are primarily designed for ground-based agents, overlooking the critical and growing domain of autonomous Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs).
- Developing intelligent UAVs for real-world applications (e.g., delivery, inspection) requires agents capable of understanding and executing natural language instructions in complex 3D outdoor environments.
- Aerial navigation presents unique challenges such as larger 3D action spaces, complex spatial reasoning from an aerial perspective, and significantly longer paths compared to typical ground-based VLN.

## Method

- The paper introduces **AerialVLN**, a novel ==vision-and-language navigation== task for UAVs in continuous, city-level outdoor environments.
- A large-scale dataset is developed with 8,446 human-piloted flying paths and 25,338 corresponding natural language instructions, collected by AOPA-certified pilots and annotated by AMT workers.
- A realistic 3D simulator is built using ==Unreal Engine 4== and ==AirSim==, supporting ==4-DOF continuous movement==, dynamic environmental conditions, and diverse city-level scenes with over 870 object types.

## Results

- State-of-the-art VLN baseline models achieve success rates of only **1.0-1.6%** on the full AerialVLN dataset's unseen splits, while human pilots achieve **80.8%** success.
- Success rates drop dramatically with longer paths (**1.8%** for paths >800m vs. **7.4%** for paths ~300m), highlighting challenges in long-horizon planning and maintaining context.
- The significant difference between success rate (SR) and oracle success rate (OSR) indicates that agents often reach the destination vicinity but fail to execute the 'Stop' action precisely.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - AerialVLN is a highly challenging task for current AI models, with state-of-the-art baselines achieving 1.0-1.6% success rates on unseen environments, significantly lower than human performance of 80.8%.
> - Major failure modes for AI agents include navigating long paths and precisely stopping at the target destination, indicated by the large disparity between Oracle Success Rate and Success Rate.
> - Both dataset aggregation and a proposed 'Look-ahead Guidance' strategy incrementally improve performance, but a substantial gap remains, suggesting the need for new architectural or training approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical gap in the rapidly evolving field of Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN). VLN tasks involve training AI agents to navigate through complex environments based on natural language instructions, integrating capabilities from computer vision, natural language processing, and robotics.

**Evolution of VLN Tasks:** The VLN landscape began with indoor, discrete navigation tasks, famously exemplified by Room-to-Room (R2R). Subsequent works expanded on this by introducing:
- **Longer/More Complex Instructions:** RxR introduced multilingual instructions and more complex scenarios.
- **Dialogue-based Navigation:** CVDN explored interactive dialogue instead of static instructions.
- **Object Grounding:** REVERIE and SOON focused on agents identifying and interacting with specific objects.
- **Outdoor Environments:** TouchDown extended VLN to static street-level outdoor environments.
- **Continuous Environments:** VLN-CE shifted from graph-based discrete navigation to continuous action spaces, allowing for more flexible movement.

**The Identified Gap: Lack of Aerial VLN:** Despite these advancements, the authors highlight that almost all existing VLN tasks, whether indoor or outdoor, are designed for "ground-based" agents. This neglects an increasingly important domain: aerial navigation by Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) or drones. UAVs are becoming ubiquitous in applications like goods delivery, traffic/security patrol, infrastructure inspection, and even scenery tours, necessitating autonomous capabilities.

**Comparison with Existing Aerial Work:** While a few works have touched upon aerial navigation with language instructions (e.g.,), AerialVLN distinguishes itself significantly:
- **Scale and Complexity:** Previous aerial tasks (like LANI) were limited to small, confined virtual fields (e.g., 50x50m) with static environments and fewer objects. AerialVLN, in contrast, offers 25 diverse city-level environments, over 870 object types, and significantly longer paths (average 661.8m compared to LANI's 17.3m and ANDH's 144.7m).
- **Action Space:** Earlier aerial tasks often restricted movement to 2-DOF (horizontal), or 3-DOF. AerialVLN supports 4-DOF movement (Forward, Turn Left/Right, Ascend/Descend, Move Left/Right), mirroring the capabilities of real multirotors and introducing vertical navigation challenges.
- **Realism and Dynamics:** AerialVLN utilizes Unreal Engine 4 and AirSim for near-realistic rendering and supports dynamic environmental conditions (weather, illumination, moving objects), making it closer to real-world deployment challenges compared to static environments in previous works like TouchDown.
- **First-Person View vs. Bird's Eye:** Unlike ANDH, which uses a bird's-eye view, AerialVLN requires navigation from a first-person perspective, which is crucial for embodied AI and more akin to how a human pilot would perceive the environment.

By proposing AerialVLN, the authors aim to pioneer research in a new dimension of embodied AI, pushing the boundaries of multi-modal understanding and robotic autonomy into the three-dimensional aerial space. This work provides a challenging benchmark and a robust simulation environment to facilitate the development of intelligent UAVs that can understand and execute complex human instructions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **introduce AerialVLN, a novel vision-and-language navigation task specifically designed for Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) operating in continuous, complex outdoor city-level environments.** Concurrently, the paper aims to **develop a corresponding large-scale dataset and a sophisticated 3D simulator** to facilitate research in this new domain.

The key motivations driving this work are multi-faceted:

- **Addressing a Critical Research Gap:** As highlighted in the broader research landscape section, existing VLN tasks overwhelmingly focus on ground-based agents (indoors or outdoors). This overlooks the burgeoning field of UAV applications, which necessitate intelligent autonomous navigation capabilities. AerialVLN seeks to fill this void by providing a dedicated benchmark for sky-based navigation.
- **Enabling Real-World UAV Applications:** The development of autonomous UAVs is crucial for numerous practical applications, including:
 - **Goods Delivery:** Automated drone delivery requires precise navigation based on spatial instructions.
 - **Traffic/Security Patrol:** UAVs need to follow complex routes and identify specific points of interest.
 - **Scenery Tours/Inspection:** Automating flights for visual documentation or inspection requires understanding high-level commands.
 - **Search and Rescue:** Navigating through complex, often GPS-denied, disaster zones demands robust visual and language understanding.
 Manually operating UAVs for these tasks is resource-intensive and prone to human error; thus, intelligent autonomy is highly desirable.
- **Tackling Increased Navigation Complexity in 3D Space:** Navigating in the sky presents unique challenges significantly more complex than ground-based navigation:
 - **Larger Action Space:** UAVs operate in 3D, requiring agents to consider not only horizontal movement and turning but also vertical actions like "rise up" and "pan down." This expands the action space significantly (4-DOF compared to 2-DOF or 3-DOF in previous works).
 - **More Complex Spatial Relationship Reasoning:** From a bird's-eye perspective, identifying and distinguishing referred objects or buildings requires sophisticated 3D spatial reasoning, including understanding relative heights and positions.
 - **Larger and More Diverse Environments:** City-level outdoor environments are vastly larger and more varied than typical indoor scenes or static street views. This demands enhanced generalization capabilities from agents.
 - **Longer Paths and Horizons:** UAV paths are typically much longer than ground-based paths, requiring agents to maintain context and plan over extended temporal horizons.
 - **3D Obstacle Avoidance:** Agents must learn to perceive and avoid obstacles in full 3D space, which is more challenging than simply avoiding obstacles on a 2D plane.
- **Providing a Challenging Benchmark for AI Research:** The significant gap between current AI performance and human capabilities in this domain (as revealed by baseline evaluations) highlights AerialVLN as a "new challenging task." It aims to serve as a catalyst for new research in areas like long-horizon planning, robust cross-modal alignment, 3D perception, and continuous control for embodied AI.
- **Mimicking Real-World Dynamics:** The simulator is designed to support dynamic environmental conditions (e.g., weather, illumination changes) and interactive elements, aiming to reduce the "sim-to-real" gap and make trained agents more robust for real-world deployment.

In essence, the project is driven by a desire to push the boundaries of embodied AI into the critical and challenging domain of aerial robotics, laying the foundational dataset and simulation tools for future breakthroughs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for AerialVLN involves a comprehensive approach encompassing task definition, simulator development, a unique data collection strategy, and evaluation with baseline models.

#### 3.1. Task Definition

The AerialVLN task requires an intelligent agent (a multirotor UAV) to navigate to a specified destination by following a natural language instruction and utilizing its first-person visual perceptions (RGB and depth images). Key characteristics include:
- **Continuous Navigation:** Unlike many earlier VLN tasks, there are no pre-built navigation graphs; the agent can move freely in the 3D space, mimicking real-world flight.
- **Pose-based State:** The agent's state is defined by its 6-DoF pose (position and orientation).
- **Visual Input:** The agent receives front-view RGB and depth images at each step. Panoramic observations are possible but baselines are restricted to front-view.
- **Action Space:** An 8-action discrete set: Move Forward (5 units), Turn Left (15 degrees), Turn Right (15 degrees), Ascend (2 units), Descend (2 units), Move Left (5 units), Move Right (5 units), and Stop. This provides a balance between fine-grained control and practical action granularity for UAVs.
- **Success Criterion:** Navigation is successful if the agent stops within 20 meters of the target location, reflecting the size of a typical helipad and posing a challenging precision requirement given the large environments.

#### 3.2. Simulator Development

The simulator is built upon **Unreal Engine 4** for realistic rendering and **Microsoft AirSim** plugins for physics-based simulation of UAVs.
- **Realistic Rendering:** Outputs near-realistic RGB and depth images. Semantic segmentation is also available.
- **Environmental Dynamics:** Supports dynamic elements like blowing leaves, running cars, varying illumination (morning, noon, night), and different climate patterns (sun, rain, snow, fog) to enhance realism and improve sim-to-real transferability.
- **Continuous Environment:** Enables free movement in 3D space.
- **Scalability:** Supports 25 different city-level environments (downtown, factories, parks, villages) containing over 870 kinds of objects, demonstrating vast scale and diversity.

#### 3.3. Data Collection

The data collection process for AerialVLN comprises two crucial steps:
- **Path Generation:** Unlike datasets relying on automatic path generation, AerialVLN employs **experienced human UAV pilots holding AOPA (Aircraft Owners and Pilots Association) certificates**. These pilots manually fly the multirotor in the simulator, passing through several pre-defined landmarks to ensure high-quality, realistic trajectories. The continuous pose traces are then cleaned by removing redundant motions (e.g., looking around) and discretized into meta-actions for training. This human-centric approach aims to capture realistic human navigation behaviors.
- **Instruction Collection:** Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) workers are shown videos of the generated drone flights. They are then tasked with providing natural language commands that would guide a pilot to complete the flight. To ensure linguistic diversity and reduce bias, **each video is annotated three times by different workers**. Annotators are specifically instructed to use **referring expressions** (e.g., "building near fountain") to avoid ambiguity. All collected instructions undergo a rigorous manual validation process to ensure quality.
- **Fine-grained Alignment:** Crucially, the dataset also includes alignment of sub-paths to sub-instructions, enabling more fine-grained cross-modality matching learning, which is vital for long and complex instructions.

#### 3.4. Dataset Characteristics and Splits

- **Scale:** 8,446 flying paths, 25,338 instructions, a vocabulary of 4,470 words.
- **Complexity:** Average instruction length is 83 words (compared to 29 for R2R), average path length is 661.8 units (meters), and average actions per path is 204.
- **Linguistic Richness:** Detailed linguistic analysis (Table 2) reveals a high rate of references, coreferences, sequencing, spatial relationships, and directions, indicating a complex language understanding requirement.
- **Splits:** The dataset is divided into standard train, val seen, val unseen, and test splits. Critically, the `val unseen` and `test` splits use entirely new, unseen environments, posing a significant generalization challenge.
- **AerialVLN-S Variant:** A smaller-scale variant, AerialVLN-S, is introduced with 17 smaller scenes and shorter path/instruction lengths (average path 321.3m, 115 actions) to serve as a benchmark for general 3D aerial VLN in first-person view, potentially allowing for easier initial experimentation.

#### 3.5. Baseline Models and Evaluation

The paper evaluates five baseline models, including standard VLN architectures and a proposed extension:
- **Random:** Agent selects actions randomly.
- **Action Sampling:** Agent samples actions based on training set distribution.
- **LingUNet:** An adapted model from a previous aerial VLN task (LANI), adjusted for step-wise paradigm.
- **Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq):** A recurrent policy that concatenates visual features (RGB, depth) and instruction embeddings to predict actions.
- **Cross-Modal Attention (CMA):** A classical VLN model using bi-directional LSTMs for visual tracking and instruction encoding, employing attention mechanisms between visual and language modalities for decision making.
- **Look-ahead Guidance (LAG):** The authors' proposed extension to CMA. It addresses the "shortest path" problem in student-forcing by generating "look-ahead" ground-truth actions. Instead of always forcing the agent back to the shortest path from the current location to the final destination, LAG identifies a point on the original ground-truth path a few steps ahead and uses the shortest path to *that* intermediate point as the ground truth. This encourages the agent to learn the *intended* human-generated path segments rather than just the shortest route.

#### 3.6. Evaluation Metrics

Four widely used VLN metrics are adopted:
- **Success Rate (SR):** Percentage of navigations where the agent stops within 20m of the destination.
- **Oracle Success Rate (OSR):** Percentage of navigations where *any* point on the agent's trajectory is within 20m of the destination (measures if the agent ever reached the vicinity of the goal).
- **Navigation Error (NE):** Distance between the agent's stop location and the destination.
- **Success rate weighted by Normalised Dynamic Time Warping (SDTW):** Considers both navigation success and similarity to the ground truth path.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results consistently demonstrate the significant challenge posed by the AerialVLN task and the large gap between current AI capabilities and human performance.

- **Baselines Perform Poorly:**
 - **Random and Action Sampling** models achieve virtually 0% success rates, indicating that mere chance or simple statistical action distribution is ineffective in this complex environment. Their Oracle Success Rates are also extremely low (<3%), meaning they rarely even pass by the destination.
 - **LingUNet** shows only marginal improvement over statistical baselines, struggling particularly with unseen environments, likely due to its limited capacity to model historical information with its episode-wise paradigm.
 - The more sophisticated **Seq2Seq and Cross-Modal Attention (CMA)** models achieve success rates of only 1.0% to 1.6% on the full AerialVLN dataset's unseen splits. Even on the smaller AerialVLN-S dataset, their success rates are modest (2.2% to 3.9%). While their Oracle Success Rates (OSR) are higher (up to 16% on AerialVLN-S), indicating they sometimes reach the goal vicinity, their failure to stop precisely suggests a major challenge in goal recognition and termination.

- **Significant Gap to Human Performance:** A critical finding is the vast disparity between baseline models and human performance. Human pilots achieve an 80.8% success rate and an average navigation error of 73.5m, starkly contrasting with the AI models' sub-5% success rates and hundreds of meters of navigation error. This large gap strongly validates AerialVLN as a challenging and open research problem.

- **Impact of Dataset Aggregation (DA):** Applying Dataset Aggregation, an offline student-forcing strategy, significantly improves performance, particularly on the AerialVLN-S dataset (e.g., CMA-DA achieves ~6% improvement on seen split and ~1% on unseen splits compared to CMA). This suggests that exposing the agent to the consequences of its own (non-ground-truth) actions during training helps mitigate the training-test discrepancy and improves generalization. However, even with DA, the performance remains much lower than on ground-based VLN tasks (e.g., 4.5% SR on AerialVLN-S vs. 27% on continuous R2R with similar techniques), further emphasizing the difficulty of AerialVLN.

- **Effectiveness of Look-ahead Guidance (LAG):** The proposed Look-ahead Guidance (LAG) method, when combined with CMA, consistently enhances performance on unseen splits (e.g., SR and SDTW improve on AerialVLN-S). This confirms that a more intelligent generation of ground-truth actions during student-forcing training, which accounts for the human-intended path rather than just shortest-path shortcuts, is beneficial for guiding the agent to follow complex instructions.

- **Reasons for Failures:**
 - **Path Length:** Longer paths dramatically decrease success rates. The authors found that success rate on long-path sets (813.2m avg.) was only 1.8%, while short-path sets (326.9m avg.) could reach up to 7.4%. This highlights the challenge of long-horizon planning and maintaining instruction context over extended trajectories.
 - **Stopping Criteria:** The significant difference between SR and OSR indicates a common failure mode: agents often pass by the destination but fail to execute the 'Stop' action precisely within the 20m radius. This points to difficulties in accurately recognizing the final destination and determining when to terminate the mission.

- **Modality Ablation Study:** The ablation study confirms the crucial role of both vision and language inputs. Removing either modality (vision or language) leads to a substantial performance drop, indicating that neither visual nor textual bias dominates the dataset, and successful navigation requires true cross-modal understanding. Within the visual modality, RGB information contributes more to success than depth information, though both are important, suggesting the visual appearance of objects and landmarks is key to interpreting instructions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of AerialVLN marks a significant advancement in the field of embodied AI, particularly in Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) and robotics. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Pioneering a New Frontier in VLN:** AerialVLN is the first large-scale, city-level, 3D continuous VLN task specifically designed for UAVs. This opens up an entirely new dimension for research in multi-modal embodied AI, extending beyond the traditionally ground-based agents. It forces the research community to grapple with complexities unique to aerial environments.

- **Challenging Benchmark for Future Research:** The extremely low performance of current state-of-the-art VLN baselines on AerialVLN, coupled with the vast gap to human performance, firmly establishes it as a highly challenging benchmark. This challenge is driven by several factors:
 - **3D Spatial Reasoning:** Agents must navigate and reason in a full 3D space, considering height, depth, and complex spatial relationships from a bird's-eye view, which is more intricate than 2D ground navigation.
 - **Continuous Control and Action Space:** Operating in a continuous environment with an expanded 4-DoF action space requires more sophisticated control policies than discrete graph-based navigation.
 - **Long-Horizon Planning and Instruction Following:** The remarkably long paths and detailed instructions necessitate robust long-term memory, contextual understanding, and the ability to follow multi-step commands over extended periods.
 - **Dynamic and Unseen Environments:** The simulator's dynamic features (weather, lighting, moving objects) and the inclusion of unseen test environments demand highly generalizable and robust navigation policies.

- **Catalyst for Novel Model Development:** The identified challenges (especially the struggle with long paths and precise stopping) will spur the development of new AI architectures and training paradigms. Future research will likely focus on:
 - **Improved Long-Term Memory and Planning:** Models capable of retaining crucial instruction context and visual information over hundreds of steps.
 - **Robust Cross-Modal Alignment:** Enhanced techniques for grounding language cues (e.g., "turn left at the five-floor building with a yellow shop sign") to dynamic visual perceptions in 3D.
 - **Better Obstacle Avoidance in 3D:** Sophisticated perception and planning for navigating around and through complex 3D structures.
 - **Effective Stop Prediction:** More accurate mechanisms for identifying the final destination and deciding when to terminate the mission.
 - **Sim-to-Real Transferability:** The use of Unreal Engine 4 and AirSim with dynamic environments lays a foundation for future sim-to-real transfer learning research, crucial for deploying autonomous UAVs in the real world.

- **Enabling Real-World UAV Autonomy:** The ultimate impact of AerialVLN lies in its potential to accelerate the development of truly autonomous UAVs. As AI agents improve on this benchmark, they will gain the capabilities needed for practical applications such as:
 - **Automated Drone Delivery Systems:** Reducing the need for human pilots in logistics.
 - **Intelligent Surveillance and Inspection:** Allowing drones to autonomously patrol areas or inspect infrastructure based on natural language commands.
 - **Advanced Search and Rescue Operations:** Enabling UAVs to navigate complex, GPS-denied disaster zones to locate targets.
 - **Autonomous Aerial Cinematography/Tourism:** Automated drone flights for capturing specific visual narratives or touring defined areas.

- **High-Quality Dataset and Simulator:** The meticulous data collection involving AOPA-certified human pilots, multiple AMT annotators, and fine-grained sub-path/sub-instruction alignment ensures a high-quality, linguistically rich, and diverse dataset. The robust, extensible, and near-realistic simulator provides a stable platform for rigorous experimentation and comparison.

In conclusion, AerialVLN is not just another dataset; it's a foundational contribution that defines a new and highly relevant problem space for embodied AI. By highlighting the current limitations of AI in aerial vision-and-language navigation, it sets a clear direction for future research, promising significant advancements in autonomous UAV capabilities and their real-world applications.
