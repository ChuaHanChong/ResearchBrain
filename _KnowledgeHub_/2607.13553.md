# Flow-aware Optimal Navigation in Unsteady Flows through Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Politecnico di Milano developed a reinforcement learning framework using bio-inspired observation strategies to enable autonomous agents to navigate optimally in complex, unsteady double-gyre fluid flows. The velocity history agent demonstrated the highest performance with a 71% success rate and faster learning, while agents provided with explicit global flow parameters generally experienced reduced navigation efficacy.

## Problem

- Autonomous navigation in dynamic, nonstationary, and time-varying fluid flows poses a significant challenge for robotics.
- Traditional optimal control frameworks require complete a priori global knowledge of the flow field, an assumption rarely met in real-world environments.
- Real-world flow environments are characterized by partial observability, computational intractability for full fidelity modeling, and chaotic dynamics, necessitating reliance on local sensory input.

## Method

- Utilized a ==Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3)== reinforcement learning framework to train autonomous agents in a ==continuous control== setting.
- Implemented a custom-built ==gymnasium environment== featuring a parametric, chaotic ==double-gyre flow==, randomizing starting points, target locations, and flow parameters to encourage generalized learning.
- Developed five ==bio-inspired observation strategies== for the agents, including local flow ==velocity==, ==vorticity==, and their historical measurements, to understand the most effective sensory inputs.

## Results

- The velocity history agent achieved the highest success rate of approximately **71%** and demonstrated the **fastest learning convergence** among all tested observation strategies.
- Velocity-aware agents (Velocity and Velocity History) exhibited greater **energy efficiency** with **lower cumulative control penalties**, indicating effective exploitation of background flow.
- Explicitly providing global flow parameters to agents generally led to a deterioration in navigation performance, rather than an improvement, for most agent configurations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating historical velocity measurements enables agents to reconstruct fluid structures more accurately, leading to superior navigation performance.
> - Providing agents with explicit global flow parameters can paradoxically reduce navigation performance, suggesting that implicit learning from local sensory input yields more robust policies.
> - Different local flow observations lead to distinct optimization behaviors, with velocity-aware agents prioritizing energy efficiency and vorticity-aware agents achieving better target proximity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robotic navigation in dynamic, nonstationary, and time-varying fluid flows represents a significant challenge across various applications, including ocean surveying, environmental monitoring, drone operations in windy conditions, and weather balloon station keeping. Traditional optimal control frameworks, such as those used for the Zermelo navigation problem, typically rely on the unrealistic assumption of complete a priori global knowledge of the flow field. In practice, real-world flow environments are characterized by time-variance, partial observability through onboard sensors, computational intractability at full fidelity, and often chaotic dynamics, making accurate prediction difficult. Consequently, autonomous systems must primarily depend on local sensory input to interpret and respond to prevailing flow conditions.

Biological systems offer an alternative paradigm, demonstrating that effective navigation can emerge from intelligent exploitation of localized flow information, rather than requiring full-field knowledge. For instance, fish utilize their lateral line system to detect velocity gradients and vorticity variations, enabling them to navigate and identify obstacles. This bio-inspired approach suggests that flow-aware navigation based on structured local sensing could be a viable strategy in complex, uncertain environments.

Recent advancements in machine learning, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), have provided new tools for addressing complex control problems in high-dimensional and uncertain settings. Previous studies have explored RL for navigation in flow-driven environments, demonstrating its potential. However, these earlier works often encountered limitations related to data efficiency, high training costs, sensitivity to reward function design, and challenges in transferring policies from simulation to real-world deployment, especially in turbulent and partially observed flow conditions.

This research contributes to the broader landscape by addressing these limitations through a systematic investigation of bio-inspired observation strategies within an RL framework. Specifically, it extends prior work by considering navigation to *arbitrary targets* within a *parametric, chaotic double-gyre flow*, a more complex and generalizable problem than single-target navigation. The paper aims to bridge the gap between proof-of-concept RL studies and reliable, flow-aware navigation in real-world environments by identifying critical sensory information and its optimal representation for autonomous agents operating under partial observability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a reinforcement learning (RL) approach for training autonomous agents to navigate optimally in complex, unsteady, and chaotic fluid flow environments. This objective is driven by the practical limitations of classical control methods and inspired by the capabilities of biological navigation systems.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and observations:

- **Limitations of Classical Optimal Control:** Traditional optimal control techniques for navigation require complete, global knowledge of the flow field in advance. This prerequisite is rarely met in realistic scenarios, where fluid flows are inherently dynamic, unpredictable, and only partially observable through onboard sensors. The computational burden of full-fidelity flow modeling further complicates these approaches.
- **Biological Inspiration for Local Sensing:** Natural swimmers, such as fish, successfully navigate complex aquatic environments by relying on localized sensory cues, such as velocity gradients and vorticity, without needing a global map of the flow. This biological paradigm suggests that effective navigation policies can be developed using only local flow information, presenting a promising alternative to global-knowledge-dependent methods.
- **Advancing Reinforcement Learning for Flow Navigation:** While RL has shown promise in controlling agents in flow environments, challenges persist. These include optimizing data efficiency, managing high training costs, designing effective reward functions, and improving the robustness and generalizability of learned policies to facilitate simulation-to-real-world transfer. This paper seeks to refine RL application by investigating key factors that enhance learning and performance.
- **Generalizable Navigation in Complex Flows:** Previous RL studies in flow environments often focused on specific, fixed targets. This work aims to address a more complex and practically relevant problem: training agents to reach *arbitrary targets* within a *parametric, chaotic double-gyre flow*. This increased complexity requires the agent to learn a robust and general navigation strategy rather than a specific trajectory.

To achieve these goals, the research specifically aims to:

- **Compare and Analyze Flow Observation Strategies:** Investigate the efficacy of different bio-inspired sensory inputs, including local flow velocity and local flow vorticity, in informing navigation decisions. This aims to determine which types of localized flow information are most beneficial.
- **Evaluate the Importance of Memory:** Assess the impact of providing agents with short-term memory of past sensory measurements (e.g., historical velocity or vorticity data) on their ability to interpret flow structures and improve navigation performance.
- **Assess the Impact of Global Flow Parameters:** Determine whether explicit knowledge of the global parameters characterizing the flow environment (e.g., gyre amplitude and frequency) enhances or detracts from the agent's ability to learn robust navigation policies. This explores the trade-off between implicit learning from local cues and explicit environmental knowledge.
- **Develop Energy-Efficient Navigation Policies:** Design reward functions that encourage agents to reach and maintain proximity to targets while minimizing control effort, thereby promoting energy-efficient movement that exploits the background flow dynamics.

By addressing these objectives, the study intends to provide insights into designing more effective, bio-inspired robotic navigation systems that can operate autonomously and efficiently in uncertain, real-world fluid environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers employed a reinforcement learning (RL) framework using the Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) algorithm to train autonomous agents. This section details the experimental setup, agent configurations, and evaluation metrics.

#### 3.1. Reinforcement Learning Algorithm

The **TD3 algorithm** was selected due to its suitability for continuous control problems and its robust performance in high-dimensional and uncertain environments. TD3 is an off-policy, actor-critic algorithm that learns a deterministic policy (actor) and two action-value functions (critics). Key features of TD3 utilized include:
- **Twin Critics:** Uses two Q-functions and takes the minimum of the two to estimate target values, which helps mitigate overestimation bias common in other actor-critic methods.
- **Delayed Policy Updates:** The actor and target networks are updated less frequently than the critics, stabilizing the learning process.
- **Target Networks:** Slowly updated copies of the actor and critic networks are used to compute target values, ensuring greater stability during training.
- **Action Exploration Noise:** Gaussian noise is added to the actor's output during training to encourage exploration in the continuous action space.
- **Huber Loss:** Used for training the critics, a robust loss function suitable for RL.

#### 3.2. Simulation Environment

The navigation problem was set within a **custom-built double-gyre gymnasium environment**.
- **Flow Field:** The environment implements a **parametric, chaotic double-gyre flow** defined by a stream function $\phi(x, y, t)$. The velocity field $v = (v_x, v_y)$ is derived from this stream function. The flow is confined to a 2D spatial domain $D = (0, 2) \times (0, 1)$.
- **Agent Dynamics:** The agent is modeled as an **active particle** whose movement is governed by an ODE system. Its velocity is the sum of the local flow velocity and the agent's control input $(u_x, u_y)$.
- **Control Authority:** The agent's control inputs are constrained to $u_x, u_y \in [-0.2, 0.2]$. This limited control authority relative to the maximum flow velocity (up to $A\pi$ where $A$ is a parameter) forces the agent to exploit the background flow dynamics for efficient navigation.
- **Task Randomization:** To encourage the learning of general navigation policies rather than specific paths, the task parameters are randomized for each training and testing episode:
 - **Starting Point ($x_0$):** Sampled uniformly within $D' = [0.1, 1.9] \times [0.1, 0.9]$.
 - **Target Location ($x_g$):** Sampled uniformly within $D'$.
 - **Gyre Parameters ($\mu = (A, \omega)^T$):** The amplitude $A$ is sampled from $[0, 0.5]$ and the angular frequency $\omega$ from $[0.5, 2\pi/3]$. The parameter $\epsilon$ is fixed at 0.1.
- **Episode Duration:** Each episode has a fixed time horizon of $T = 80s$ with a time step of $\Delta t = 0.1s$.

#### 3.3. Agent Observation Strategies

Five distinct bio-inspired observation strategies were developed and compared:
1. **Naive Agent:** Observes absolute time ($t$) and its current relative position to the target ($\Delta x_t, \Delta y_t$). State vector: $s_t = (t, \Delta x_t, \Delta y_t)^T$.
2. **Velocity Agent:** Observes its relative position ($\Delta x_t, \Delta y_t$) and the local flow velocity components ($v_{x,t}, v_{y,t}$) beneath it. State vector: $s_t = (\Delta x_t, \Delta y_t, v_{x,t}, v_{y,t})^T$.
3. **Vorticity Agent:** Observes its relative position ($\Delta x_t, \Delta y_t$) and the local flow vorticity ($\zeta_t = \nabla \times v$) beneath it. State vector: $s_t = (\Delta x_t, \Delta y_t, \zeta_t)^T$.
4. **Velocity History Agent:** Observes its relative position, current local velocity, and the previous $M-1$ local velocity measurements. State vector: $s_t = (\Delta x_t, \Delta y_t, v_{x,t}, v_{y,t}, \dots, v_{x,t-M+1}, v_{y,t-M+1})^T$.
5. **Vorticity History Agent:** Observes its relative position, current local vorticity, and the previous $M-1$ local vorticity measurements. State vector: $s_t = (\Delta x_t, \Delta y_t, \zeta_t, \dots, \zeta_{t-M+1})^T$.
 - For history agents, a memory parameter $M=10$ was chosen based on a preliminary sensitivity analysis (Appendix A), balancing information richness and observation vector complexity.

#### 3.4. Reward Function

The reward function at each timestep $t$ was designed to encourage target acquisition, station-keeping, and energy efficiency:
$r_t = -c_{d,t} - c_{c,t} + b_t$
- **Distance Penalty ($c_{d,t}$):** Squared Euclidean distance to the target: $c_{d,t} = \Delta x_t^2 + \Delta y_t^2$.
- **Control Penalty ($c_{c,t}$):** Rescaled squared norm of the control action: $c_{c,t} = \alpha \|a\|^2$, where $\alpha=10$ is a scaling factor. This penalizes excessive control effort, promoting energy efficiency.
- **Target Bonus ($b_t$):** A bonus of $b_t = 1$ is awarded if the agent is within a specified threshold $\delta=0.055$ of the target.
Maximizing the cumulative reward over an episode thus incentivizes agents to reach the target quickly, remain close to it, and minimize control input.

#### 3.5. Success Criterion

An episode is deemed **successful** if the agent manages to stay within the target proximity threshold ($\delta$) for more than 100 non-consecutive time steps (i.e., over 1/8 of the total episode duration).

#### 3.6. Parameter-aware Navigation Experiment

To investigate the impact of explicit global environmental knowledge, a second set of experiments was conducted. In these "parameter-aware" scenarios, the observation vector for each agent type was augmented with the current gyre parameters $\mu = (A, \omega)^T$.

#### 3.7. Training and Evaluation

Each agent configuration was trained over 8,000 episodes. Results are reported as the mean of three independent training runs using different random seeds (1, 5, 10). Success rate was calculated as a moving average over a window of 200 training episodes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The numerical experiments yielded several observations regarding the performance of different agent configurations, their sensor utility, and the impact of providing explicit global environmental parameters.

#### 4.1. Performance of Different Observation Strategies

- **Velocity History Agent Superiority:** The velocity history agent consistently demonstrated the highest performance, achieving both the highest cumulative rewards and the greatest success rate (approximately 71%). It also exhibited faster learning convergence, reaching peak performance after roughly $3 \times 10^3$ training episodes. This is attributed to its ability to reconstruct fluid structures more accurately by integrating historical velocity measurements.
- **Naive Agent Limitations:** While the naive agent, which observed absolute time and relative position, learned quickly due to time serving as a proxy for gyre dynamics, its asymptotic performance was the lowest (approximately 49% success rate). This indicates a limited capacity to recognize and effectively exploit complex flow features.
- **Vorticity vs. Velocity Agents:** The vorticity agent, despite using a single scalar measure, learned faster than the velocity agent (which uses two velocity components) and achieved comparable asymptotic performance (approximately 58% success rate for vorticity vs. 60% for velocity). This observation suggests that vorticity, being a spatial derivative of velocity, provides highly informative cues about flow structures, particularly in a vorticose environment like the double-gyre.
- **Vorticity History Agent Performance:** The vorticity history agent learned slowly but smoothly, eventually reaching a success rate similar to the single-measurement vorticity and velocity agents (approximately 60%). For this agent, incorporating memory did not provide a distinct advantage in success rate compared to the instantaneous vorticity agent, possibly due to the increased complexity of interpreting a larger observation vector.
- **Overall Success Rates:** No agent achieved a 100% success rate, indicating the inherent difficulty of continuous station-keeping in some of the more challenging randomizations of flow parameters and target locations.

#### 4.2. Sensor Utility Trade-off

Analysis of the cumulative distance and control penalties revealed distinct optimization behaviors linked to the type of sensory information:
- **Energy Efficiency (Control Penalty):** Agents that directly measured the velocity field (velocity agent and velocity history agent) demonstrated greater energy efficiency, achieving notably lower cumulative control penalties. This suggests these agents learned to exploit flow structures to navigate with minimal self-propulsion effort. Visual inspection of trajectories (e.g., Velocity History agent in Fig. 3) supported this, showing movement that leveraged flow currents.
- **Target Proximity (Distance Penalty):** Vorticity-aware agents (vorticity agent and vorticity history agent) appeared to develop a better understanding of flow structures, enabling them to approach the target more accurately. This was indicated by their relatively lower cumulative distance penalties compared to velocity-aware agents, particularly evident during early training phases.

#### 4.3. Impact of Global Flow Parameters (Parameter-aware Navigation)

A critical finding emerged when agents were provided with explicit global flow parameters ($\mu = [A, \omega]$) in their observation vector:
- **Performance Deterioration:** Contrary to expectation, augmenting observation vectors with global flow parameters generally *decreased* navigation performance across almost all agent types, as indicated by lower cumulative rewards and success rates.
- **Inability to Correlate:** The agents appeared unable to infer meaningful correlations between the local flow information they sensed and these explicit global parameters.
- **Differential Impact:** The velocity-aware agents were particularly negatively affected, with their success rate dropping significantly (e.g., velocity agent to approximately 49%). The naive agent remained largely unaffected (around 49% success rate). Vorticity agents experienced a slight decrease (to approximately 54%).
- **Memory Agents' Robustness:** History-aware agents (velocity history and vorticity history) showed less sensitivity to the presence of global parameters. Although their performance was still lower than their "blind" counterparts, they maintained smoother learning curves, which the authors interpret as supporting their deeper understanding of the environment and potentially greater robustness to extraneous information.
- **Implication:** This outcome suggests that RL-based autonomous systems may develop more robust and generalizable policies when compelled to rely on implicit representations derived from local sensory inputs, rather than being provided with explicit, human-readable global parameters.

#### 4.4. Memory Sensitivity Analysis (Appendix A)

An investigation into different memory lengths (M=5, 10, 15) for history-aware agents indicated that an intermediate value of M=10 provided the optimal balance between capturing sufficient historical flow features and avoiding an overly complex observation vector. This led to the best success rates for both velocity and vorticity history agents.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous robotics and artificial intelligence by addressing the fundamental challenge of navigation in complex fluid environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Advancing Flow-Aware Navigation:** The study directly tackles the limitations of classical optimal control methods, which are impractical in real-world, dynamic, and partially observable fluid flows. By successfully demonstrating the application of reinforcement learning for flow-aware navigation to arbitrary targets in a parametric, chaotic double-gyre flow, the work advances the capability of autonomous systems to operate in such challenging conditions.
- **Bio-inspired Robotic Design:** The investigation into different bio-inspired observation strategies offers insights for the design of sensory systems in future autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) and aerial robots. Understanding whether local velocity, vorticity, or their historical records are most effective can guide engineers in selecting optimal sensor payloads and processing architectures to mimic biological navigation capabilities.
- **Elucidating Sensor Utility Trade-offs:** The identified trade-off between velocity-aware agents prioritizing energy efficiency (lower control effort) and vorticity-aware agents achieving better target proximity (structural mapping) is significant. This knowledge is crucial for designing robots where specific operational priorities (e.g., endurance vs. precision) dictate sensor choices and policy objectives. This could lead to specialized robotic platforms optimized for particular tasks.
- **Implications for Reinforcement Learning System Design:** The counter-intuitive finding that providing explicit global flow parameters can *decrease* navigation performance is a substantial insight for the broader RL community. It suggests that agents may learn more resilient and generalizable policies when forced to infer environmental characteristics implicitly from local observations, rather than relying on explicit, human-designed global representations. This finding could influence how state representations are formulated in complex RL tasks, potentially favoring more raw, local sensor data processing over pre-processed global information.
- **Improved Simulation-to-Real Transfer:** By focusing on agents learning from local sensory cues, the methodology inherently moves towards greater relevance for real-world deployment. In real environments, global knowledge is often unavailable or subject to significant uncertainty and noise. Learning robust policies based on local, observable information is a critical step in bridging the gap between simulation-based training and practical application in dynamic, unpredictable settings.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future exploration into more complex scenarios, such as navigation in highly chaotic velocity fields. Furthermore, the insights gained are intended to be leveraged for improving coordinated behaviors in multi-agent systems, where swarms of robots could collaboratively exploit flow features for tasks like distributed sensing or environmental mapping.

In conclusion, this research provides both theoretical understanding and practical guidance for developing more robust, energy-efficient, and biologically plausible autonomous navigation systems capable of operating in complex fluid environments.
