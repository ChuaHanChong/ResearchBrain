# MMSI-Bench: A Benchmark for Multi-Image Spatial Intelligence

> [!summary] Summary
> MMSI-Bench introduces a novel human-curated benchmark to evaluate multi-image spatial intelligence in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) across diverse real-world scenarios. The benchmark reveals a substantial gap, with top MLLMs achieving only 41.9% accuracy compared to humans' 97.2%, particularly struggling with scene reconstruction and multi-step reasoning tasks.

## Problem

- Existing MLLM evaluation benchmarks predominantly focus on single-image spatial understanding or simple relations, failing to capture the complexity of real-world multi-image spatial reasoning.
- Current multi-image VQA benchmarks are often general, not specifically tailored for comprehensive spatial intelligence assessment, or rely on automated question generation, limiting diversity and difficulty.
- A lack of benchmarks capable of providing diagnostic insights into MLLM failure modes for spatial reasoning hinders targeted research and development.

## Method

- Developed MMSI-Bench, a ==multiple-choice VQA benchmark== with 1,000 human-curated questions (1,990 images) designed to require information integration across multiple real-world images.
- Created a ==detailed taxonomy of 11 atomic spatial reasoning tasks==, including camera, object, and region relationships, attributes, and motion, to guide free-form question generation by expert 3D vision researchers.
- Integrated a novel ==automated error analysis pipeline==, leveraging human-annotated step-by-step reasoning processes for each question, to diagnose specific failure categories in MLLMs.

## Results

- Human evaluators achieved **97.2%** accuracy on MMSI-Bench, while the best proprietary MLLM (GPT-5) scored **41.9%**, and the top open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) achieved **30.7%**, indicating a considerable performance disparity.
- Models showed particular weakness in Multi-Step Reasoning and camera motion tasks, performing near random chance on some of these challenging categories.
- The automated error analysis confirmed that 'overlap-matching and scene-reconstruction errors' constitute the largest proportion of MLLM mistakes, providing specific targets for future research.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - There is a vast and persistent gap between MLLM (highest 41.9% accuracy) and human (97.2% accuracy) performance in multi-image spatial intelligence, indicating significant foundational limitations in current models.
> - MLLMs primarily fail due to 'overlap-matching and scene-reconstruction errors,' struggling to identify corresponding elements across images and build coherent 3D scene representations.
> - Both model scaling and common prompting techniques (e.g., Chain-of-Thought, visual cues) yield only marginal or inconsistent improvements, suggesting the need for architectural innovations or richer spatial reasoning data rather than just scale.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are progressing towards embodied artificial general intelligence (AGI), with spatial intelligence identified as a critical capability for their deployment in physical environments. Spatial intelligence encompasses understanding object locations, movements, and their relationships within a scene.

Existing benchmarks for MLLMs primarily focus on single-image spatial understanding and often involve straightforward spatial relations. This approach limits their ability to assess the complex multi-image spatial reasoning required in real-world scenarios, where models must integrate information across multiple frames to track object and ego motion or to relate entities that do not co-occur in a single view. Furthermore, many prior benchmarks rely on automated or template-based question generation from annotated metadata, which can restrict the diversity and difficulty of the posed challenges. Some multi-image benchmarks exist but often lack comprehensive coverage of spatial reasoning, incorporating it only as a minor component within broader visual question answering (VQA) suites, or are set in synthetic environments.

MMSI-Bench addresses these limitations by providing a benchmark specifically designed for multi-image spatial intelligence. It aims to offer a rigorous, diverse, and challenging evaluation tool that reflects the demands of real-world deployments. By emphasizing human-curated content and comprehensive spatial task coverage across real-world scenes, the work seeks to provide a more accurate measure of MLLMs' spatial competence and guide future research efforts in this domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce MMSI-Bench, a multiple-choice Visual Question Answering (VQA) benchmark tailored for evaluating multi-image spatial intelligence in MLLMs. This objective is motivated by several identified gaps and requirements within the field:

1. **Addressing Deficiencies in Existing Benchmarks:** Current MLLM evaluation largely neglects complex multi-image spatial reasoning. MLLMs operating in the physical world need to reason across multiple images to understand dynamic scenes, track motion, and infer relationships between objects not simultaneously visible. The paper highlights that most existing benchmarks focus on single-image understanding or use templated questions, limiting their complexity and diversity. MMSI-Bench aims to fill this gap with a dedicated, challenging benchmark.

2. **Establishing a Comprehensive Spatial Taxonomy:** To systematically evaluate spatial intelligence, the authors sought to define a comprehensive taxonomy. This taxonomy categorizes spatial reasoning tasks based on three fundamental elements—camera (observer), object, and region—and encompasses positional relationships, attributes, and motion for these elements. This structured approach aims to provide a thorough assessment of an MLLM's spatial understanding across various sub-tasks.

3. **Ensuring Data Quality and Diversity through Human Curation:** Recognizing the limitations of automated data generation pipelines (which can lead to predictable and less challenging questions), a key motivation was to adopt a fully human-centric design. This involves expert researchers manually selecting image sets, crafting novel and unambiguous questions, designing plausible distractors, and providing detailed step-by-step reasoning processes. This approach is intended to produce a benchmark that is both diverse and genuinely challenging for advanced MLLMs.

4. **Diagnosing MLLM Failure Modes:** A significant motivation was to move beyond simple accuracy metrics to understand *why* MLLMs fail at spatial reasoning. By providing human-annotated reasoning processes alongside each question, the benchmark enables an automated error analysis pipeline. This pipeline is designed to systematically diagnose specific failure types, such as grounding errors, scene reconstruction errors, or spatial logic errors, offering targeted insights for model improvement.

5. **Benchmarking Current Capabilities and Identifying Gaps:** The research aims to provide a clear assessment of the current state of MLLMs in multi-image spatial intelligence. By evaluating a wide array of existing models against human performance, the authors sought to quantify the performance gap and highlight the remaining headroom for future research. This includes understanding how different model architectures and scales perform across diverse spatial tasks.

6. **Advancing Embodied AI:** Ultimately, the development of robust spatial intelligence is considered foundational for embodied AI systems, such as autonomous vehicles and robotics. By providing a rigorous evaluation framework, MMSI-Bench is intended to accelerate progress towards more capable and reliable MLLMs for real-world interactive applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MMSI-Bench is a multiple-choice Visual Question Answering (VQA) benchmark designed to evaluate multi-image spatial intelligence. The construction and evaluation process involved several stages.

**Benchmark Construction Process:**
The benchmark was developed using a fully human-centric design approach over more than 300 researcher-hours, by six 3D-vision researchers.
1. **Data Collection:** Images were curated from eight diverse real-world datasets to ensure broad scenario coverage and authenticity. These sources include indoor 3D scene datasets (Matterport3D, ScanNet), a local scene reconstruction dataset (DTU), autonomous driving datasets (nuScenes, Waymo), a robotics dataset (AgiBot-World), a video object segmentation dataset (DAVIS 2017), and an egocentric video dataset (Ego4D). The dataset includes approximately 12% outdoor samples, consistent with common VLM pre-training data distributions.
2. **Question-Answer Design and Reasoning Process Annotation:** Researchers visually inspected over 120,000 candidate images to select relevant sets. They then crafted 1,000 novel and challenging multiple-choice questions. Each question is designed to necessitate information integration from multiple images and cannot be answered using a single image or common sense alone. Questions are free-form, avoiding templates, to maximize diversity. Four plausible distractors are provided for each question. Crucially, each question is paired with a detailed, step-by-step human-annotated reasoning process that explicitly leads to the correct answer. For the Multi-Step Reasoning category, questions involve 3 to 11 reasoning steps, with an average of 5.7 steps.
3. **Taxonomy:** The benchmark questions are categorized based on a taxonomy of ten fundamental spatial reasoning tasks and one multi-step reasoning category. These tasks involve three core spatial elements: camera, object, and region. The categories include six types of Positional Relationships (Camera-Camera, Camera-Object, Camera-Region, Object-Object, Object-Region, Region-Region), two types of Attributes (Measurement, Appearance), and two types of Motion (Camera, Object).
4. **Quality Control:** Three independent reviewers, not involved in the initial annotation, systematically audited all 1,000 Q&A pairs. This process ensured clarity, correctness, non-triviality (questions not answerable by single images or common sense), and the absence of linguistic or visual ambiguities. Items failing review were either revised or removed.
5. **Difficulty Annotation:** Human evaluators, separate from the annotators, answered the questions. Their completion times were recorded and normalized to assign difficulty levels (Easy, Medium, Hard), with longer times or incorrect answers indicating higher difficulty.

**Evaluation Setup:**
1. **Models Evaluated:** Thirty-seven MLLMs, encompassing both proprietary (e.g., GPT-5, GPT-4.5, GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-3.7-Sonnet) and open-source models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL3, LLaVA-OneVision), were evaluated.
2. **Baselines:** Random Guessing (25% for four options), Human Performance (average accuracy of five independent evaluators), and Blind GPT-4o (GPT-4o without image input) were used for comparison.
3. **Metrics:** Accuracy (%) was reported based on an exact match between extracted model answers and ground-truth answers. An LLM-based fallback strategy was used to extract responses if models did not adhere to the required output format.
4. **Prompting Techniques:** The study investigated the effect of two prompting strategies:
- **Linguistic Prompting:** Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT) by prepending "Let’s think step by step" to the prompt.
- **Visual Prompting:** Employing the PATS image matching method to establish sparse correspondences between image pairs, drawing lines between corresponding points, and concatenating images as visual input. This was compared against simple image concatenation.
5. **Automated Error Analysis:** Leveraging the human-annotated reasoning processes, an automated pipeline was developed. This pipeline contrasts a model's generated reasoning with the reference reasoning to efficiently diagnose four dominant error types:
- **Grounding errors:** Failure to correctly identify or localize visual elements.
- **Overlap-matching and scene-reconstruction errors:** Failure to identify corresponding points across images or reconstruct the underlying scene.
- **Situation-transformation reasoning errors:** Mistakes in reasoning about spatial directions relative to different reference objects or converting between relative and absolute directions.
- **Spatial-logic errors:** Flaws in spatial logical inference, such as hallucinating relationships or misapplying transitivity.
 The accuracy of this automated analysis was 78% when provided with human-annotated reasoning processes.
6. **Correlation with Downstream Embodied Tasks:** The study analyzed the correlation between MMSI-Bench performance and three embodied and active perception tasks from EMBODIEDBENCH (EB-Navigation, EB-Manipulation, EB-Habitat) to assess transferability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 37 MLLMs on MMSI-Bench yielded several findings regarding the current capabilities of these models in multi-image spatial intelligence:

1. **Substantial Gap Between MLLMs and Human Performance:**
- The highest-performing open-source model, Qwen2.5-VL-72B, achieved approximately 30.7% accuracy.
- The best proprietary model, OpenAI’s GPT-5 reasoning model, attained approximately 41.9% accuracy.
- In contrast, human performance reached 97.2% accuracy.
- This performance differential (over 55% between the best model and humans) is noted as the largest reported gap for existing spatial intelligence benchmarks, highlighting the benchmark's difficulty and the undeveloped state of MLLM spatial intelligence.
- The Blind GPT-4o baseline, lacking image input, scored around 22.7%, close to random guessing (25%), which indicates that visual-spatial reasoning, not language priors, is required to solve the benchmark questions.

2. **Open-Source Models Trail Proprietary Counterparts:** Leading open-source models generally exhibited lower performance compared to proprietary models. For instance, the top open-source model's accuracy was roughly 11% lower than the top proprietary model.

3. **Challenges in Multi-Step Reasoning and Camera Motion:**
- Performance on multi-step reasoning (MSR) tasks was consistently lower than on single-step tasks for most models, indicating difficulty in integrating information across multiple reasoning stages.
- Models, particularly open-source ones, performed poorly on camera motion tasks, suggesting limitations in understanding self-movement, possibly due to a lack of diverse first-person motion data during training.

4. **Limited Gains from Model Scaling:** Increasing parameter count within the same model series (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL3) resulted in only marginal performance improvements (e.g., a 3% increase from 32B to 72B for Qwen2.5-VL). This suggests that model size alone may not be the primary bottleneck for advancing complex spatial reasoning capabilities; rather, data quality and diversity, or architectural innovations, may be more significant factors.

5. **Marginal Effectiveness of Prompting Techniques:**
- **Linguistic Prompting (Zero-Shot CoT):** This approach led to a modest improvement only for GPT-4o, while causing performance degradation in other evaluated models. This indicates that simply prompting models to "think step by step" does not fundamentally enhance their spatial reasoning capabilities if those capabilities are not already present.
- **Visual Prompting (PATS):** This method, intended to provide explicit overlap cues, yielded only slight improvements for two models and performance degradation for two others. This suggests that models may lack the basic spatial intelligence to effectively leverage such visual cues for scene reconstruction or spatial relationship inference.

6. **Dominant Error Types Identified by Automated Analysis:**
- Automated error analysis, facilitated by human-annotated reasoning processes, revealed that even models producing correct answers often exhibited flawed reasoning paths. Reasoning accuracy was consistently lower than multiple-choice accuracy (e.g., GPT-4o: 29.9% reasoning accuracy vs. 30.3% MC accuracy).
- **Overlap-matching and scene-reconstruction errors** were identified as the largest proportion of mistakes across all models, indicating a primary bottleneck in MLLMs' ability to integrate visual information across views and build a coherent 3D understanding of a scene. Other significant error types included grounding errors, situation-transformation reasoning errors, and spatial-logic errors.

7. **Limited Benefit from Existing Spatial Fine-tuning:** Models fine-tuned on other spatial reasoning datasets showed only marginal gains on MMSI-Bench, suggesting that their training data, often template-based, may not generalize to the diverse and challenging scenarios presented in MMSI-Bench.

8. **Correlation with Embodied and Active Perception Tasks:** Performance on MMSI-Bench exhibited strong positive correlations with several downstream embodied and active perception tasks, including EB-Navigation (Pearson=0.8147, Spearman=0.7333), EB-Manipulation (Pearson=0.7402, Spearman=0.7500), and EB-Habitat (Pearson=0.7299, Spearman=0.6167). This indicates that the spatial understanding capabilities measured by MMSI-Bench are relevant and transferable to practical embodied AI applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MMSI-Bench offers several contributions and potential impacts on the field of multimodal AI and embodied intelligence:

1. **Addressing a Critical Evaluation Gap:** The benchmark specifically targets multi-image spatial intelligence, a capability deemed essential for MLLMs to operate effectively in the complex physical world but inadequately assessed by previous benchmarks. By focusing on real-world multi-view scenarios, MMSI-Bench provides a more ecologically valid evaluation environment than single-image or synthetic datasets.

2. **Driving Research in Spatial Intelligence:** The pronounced gap between current MLLM performance and human-level spatial intelligence on MMSI-Bench highlights significant headroom for research. This benchmark can serve as a challenging target, stimulating the development of new model architectures, training paradigms, and data augmentation techniques specifically designed to enhance multi-image spatial reasoning.

3. **Providing Actionable Diagnostic Insights:** The inclusion of human-annotated reasoning processes and the automated error analysis pipeline are significant. This allows researchers to move beyond aggregate accuracy scores to diagnose specific failure modes (e.g., grounding, scene reconstruction, spatial logic). Such targeted insights can guide the development of more robust and reliable MLLMs by focusing efforts on addressing identified weaknesses. The finding that overlap-matching and scene-reconstruction errors are particularly prevalent offers a clear direction for future work.

4. **Advancing Embodied AI:** Spatial intelligence is a foundational component for embodied agents, including autonomous vehicles, robots, and augmented/virtual reality systems. By providing a rigorous benchmark that correlates with performance on downstream embodied tasks (like navigation and manipulation), MMSI-Bench directly contributes to the development of MLLMs that can better perceive, understand, and interact with 3D environments. This can accelerate progress towards more capable and autonomous AI systems.

5. **High-Quality, Diverse, and Challenging Resource:** The human-curated nature of MMSI-Bench, drawing from diverse real-world data sources and employing free-form question design, ensures a high-quality, unambiguous, and linguistically diverse dataset. This level of curation minimizes the biases and limitations often found in automatically generated benchmarks, making it a robust resource for evaluating generalization capabilities.

6. **Ethical Considerations and Reproducibility:** The authors acknowledge the broader societal implications of advanced AI capabilities, including potential misuse, and advocate for ethical deployment. The commitment to releasing all data and code promotes reproducibility, enabling the research community to verify findings and build upon this work efficiently, while also considering environmental impacts of computational resources.
