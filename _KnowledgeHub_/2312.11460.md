# Hybrid Internal Model: Learning Agile Legged Locomotion with Simulated Robot Response

> [!summary] Summary
> A novel Hybrid Internal Model (HIM) framework enables agile and robust legged locomotion by implicitly estimating environmental disturbances through a simulated robot response, leveraging contrastive learning on proprioceptive data. This method achieves 100% success rates on complex real-world stairs and demonstrates superior generalization to unseen terrains and disturbances on quadruped robots, with high training efficiency of just 1 hour on an RTX 4090.

## Problem

- Legged robots face challenges in robustly navigating diverse real-world environments due to partial and noisy observations of crucial external states like terrain properties.
- Existing learning-based locomotion methods often rely on complex two-stage training strategies (oracle policy then knowledge transfer), which can introduce information loss or require additional exteroceptive sensors.
- The inherent sim-to-real gap limits the direct transferability of policies trained in simulation to physical robots, hindering real-world deployment.

## Method

- Proposes a Hybrid Internal Model (HIM) framework that applies Internal Model Control principles to deep reinforcement learning, inferring environmental disturbances implicitly from the robot's simulated response.
- Extracts a "hybrid internal embedding" from historical proprioceptive observations, comprising an explicit velocity estimate and an implicit stability representation.
- Optimizes this embedding via contrastive learning, comparing current observations with future robot states to learn robust, separable representations of environmental properties without direct regression.

## Results

- Achieved a 100% success rate on complex real-world stairs and an average of 176.5 steps on long-range stairs for the Aliengo robot, substantially surpassing baselines like RMA (75.35 steps).
- Demonstrated strong generalization to unseen, compositional, and deformable terrains (e.g., 85% success on gravel and stairs vs. 45% for RMA) and high resilience to various disturbances like payload (8 kg) and missing steps.
- Converged with high sample efficiency, requiring only 200 million samples and 1 hour of training on an NVIDIA RTX 4090, while showing consistently lower velocity tracking errors in diverse simulated environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicitly inferring environmental disturbances from the robot's internal response, rather than explicitly regressing them, offers a robust and sensor-minimal approach to state estimation.
> - Contrastive learning effectively guides the learning of discriminative, noise-resilient environmental representations from proprioceptive data, superior to direct regression methods.
> - Combining explicit velocity estimation with an implicit latent stability representation within the internal model is essential for achieving agile and adaptable locomotion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robot locomotion has seen significant advancements, transitioning from traditional model-based control to increasingly sophisticated learning-based methods, particularly those leveraging Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). Historically, traditional control methods, while stable, struggled to adapt to diverse and unpredictable physical properties (e.g., ice, rough terrain, deformable materials). The advent of powerful physics simulators like Isaac Gym, enabling massively parallel simulations, has propelled DRL into the forefront, allowing robots to learn agile behaviors in simulation and transfer them to the real world (sim2real).

However, the sim2real paradigm presents two major challenges:
1. **Partial and noisy observations:** Real-world robots often lack complete access to external states (like elevation maps, ground friction, contact forces) that are readily available in simulators. Their onboard sensors provide only partial and noisy proprioceptive information.
2. **Sim-to-real gap:** Despite domain randomization techniques used during training to enhance generalizability, discrepancies between simulated and real-world physics and sensor data persist.

Existing learning-based methods address these challenges primarily through a two-stage training strategy:
- **Oracle Policy Training:** An "oracle" policy is first trained in simulation with access to all external states, often with domain randomization to improve robustness.
- **Knowledge Transfer (Mimicking/Adaptation):** The learned knowledge is then transferred to a deployable policy. This often involves:
 - **Mimic Learning (Teacher-Student):** A "student" policy learns to mimic the "teacher" (oracle) policy, but without direct access to external states. This can lead to information loss.
 - **Adaptation Methods:** The policy infers external states from other information (e.g., proprioception) or learns to adapt online. Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) is a prominent example.
 - **Exteroceptive Sensors:** Some methods augment proprioception with exteroceptive sensors like cameras or LiDAR to infer environmental information, but this adds complexity and limits universality across robot platforms.

This paper, "Hybrid Internal Model (HIM)," positions itself as a novel solution that circumvents the limitations of these two-stage approaches. It directly addresses the problem of estimating external states (disturbances) without requiring external sensors or suffering from information loss during mimicking. Instead of trying to explicitly regress environmental parameters (which can be noisy and lead to representation collapse), HIM draws inspiration from the classical Internal Model Control (IMC) principle. IMC involves using an internal model to simulate system response and estimate disturbances, thus enhancing closed-loop stability without directly modeling the disturbance itself.

By applying IMC principles within a DRL framework and employing contrastive learning to optimize a "hybrid internal embedding" that captures robot response, HIM aims to achieve robust, agile, and generalizable locomotion with minimal sensors and high sample efficiency. It contrasts with model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) by avoiding computationally expensive world models and differs from other representation learning methods that use regression objectives, arguing that contrastive learning is more robust to noise and better leverages batch-level information.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and agile locomotion control method for legged robots that can operate effectively in diverse and challenging real-world environments, overcoming the limitations imposed by partial and noisy sensor observations.

The core motivations are:
1. **Accurate State Estimation:** Robust locomotion critically depends on accurate state estimations, especially for external states like terrain friction and elevation maps, which are often unavailable or difficult to measure directly from robot sensors.
2. **Overcoming Two-Stage Training Limitations:** Existing learning-based methods rely on a two-stage training strategy (oracle policy then mimic learning/adaptation), which inherently introduces complexities, potential information loss, and limitations due to reliance on noisy domain randomization for absolute environmental parameter regression.
3. **Minimal Sensor Requirements:** Many solutions require additional exteroceptive sensors (cameras, LiDAR), which increase hardware complexity and limit the universality of policies across different robot platforms. The authors aim for a solution that primarily uses basic proprioceptive information (joint encoders, IMU).
4. **Efficiency and Generalizability:** The goal is to achieve high sample efficiency during training and demonstrate remarkable open-world generalizability to unseen and high-difficulty terrains and disturbances, without specific "mimicking" phases.
5. **Leveraging Classical Control Principles:** The work is explicitly motivated by the classical Internal Model Control (IMC) principle, seeking to translate its insights into a modern learning-based framework for estimating "disturbances" (external states) via simulated robot response rather than direct modeling.

In essence, the paper seeks to enable legged robots to infer and adapt to their environment's dynamics implicitly, based purely on their internal response, leading to more agile, robust, and universally deployable locomotion policies with efficient training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Hybrid Internal Model (HIM)**, draws inspiration from classical Internal Model Control (IMC) principles and integrates them into a learning-based framework for robust legged locomotion.

#### 3.1. Framework Overview

The HIM framework employs an alternating optimization process involving two main phases:
1. **Hybrid Internal Optimization (HIO):** This phase trains the Hybrid Internal Model, which extracts a "hybrid internal embedding."
2. **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** This phase optimizes the policy network using standard reinforcement learning.
These phases are updated iteratively: HIM parameters are updated, then frozen while actor and critic modules are optimized.

#### 3.2. State and Action Space

- **Policy Network Input ($o^a_t$):** The policy receives partial observations, including:
 - **Desired velocity ($c_t$):** Linear velocities (longitudinal, lateral) and angular velocity (yaw).
 - **Proprioception:** Joint positions ($\theta_t$) and velocities ($\dot{\theta}_t$) from encoders, and base angular velocity ($\omega_t$) and gravity direction ($g_t$) from IMU.
 - **Last action ($a_{t-1}$):** Previous control output.
 - **Hybrid Internal Embedding:** The implicitly inferred response from HIM.
- **Value Network Input ($o^c_t$):** During training, the value network has access to privileged information, adding current external force ($f_t$) and surrounding ground height ($h_t$) to $o^a_t$. This aids in a more accurate estimation of state values.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs an action $a_t$, which is a scaled bias for target joint positions ($\theta_{target} = \theta_0 + ka_t$), where $\theta_0$ is the nominal joint position and $k \le 1$ is a scale factor.

#### 3.3. Hybrid Internal Model (HIM) Core

- **IMC Inspiration:** External environmental dynamics (elevation, friction) are treated as system disturbances. Instead of directly modeling these disturbances, HIM estimates the robot's *response* to them, as suggested by IMC.
- **Hybrid Internal Embedding:** This embedding captures the robot's response, characterized by two components:
 - **Explicit Velocity ($\hat{v}_t$):** Directly tracks the desired velocity.
 - **Implicit Stability Representation ($\hat{l}_t$):** Represents the robot's stability in a latent space.
- **Observation Sequence:** HIM uses a sequence of historical observations ($o^a_{t-H:t}$, with H=5 by default) from proprioceptive sensors to extract this embedding via a 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
- **Closed-Loop Control:** The extracted embedding, along with current partial observations, is fed to the policy network, forming a fully closed-loop control system that implicitly infers and distinguishes environmental disturbances.

#### 3.4. Hybrid Internal Optimization (HIO) with Contrastive Learning

The crucial aspect of HIM is how the internal embedding ($\hat{v}_t$, $\hat{l}_t$) is optimized to simulate the robot's response, which is naturally embedded in the robot's successor state ($o^a_{t+1}$).
- **Explicit Velocity Regression:** For the explicit velocity ($\hat{v}_t$), a direct regression objective is used, leveraging its ground truth available in simulation.
- **Implicit Stability (Latent Space) Optimization:** For the implicit response ($\hat{l}_t$), a latent space $Z \subset \mathbb{R}^{16}$ is employed, optimized using **contrastive learning**.
 - **Contrastive Learning Mechanism:** Similar to SwAV (Caron et al., 2020), this method optimizes the embedding to be "close" to the robot's successor state.
 - **Positive/Negative Pairs:** In each training batch, if a pair of ($o^a_{t+1}$, $o^a_{t-H:t}$) belongs to the same trajectory, it's a positive pair; otherwise, it's a negative pair.
 - **Source and Target Encoders:** Historical observations ($o^a_{t-H:t}$) are fed to a "source encoder" and the successor state ($o^a_{t+1}$) to a "target encoder." Both produce latent features ($l^{source}_t$, $l^{target}_t$) which are L2-normalized onto a unit sphere.
 - **Prototypes and Cluster Assignment:** These latent features are then compared against a set of learnable "prototypes" ($E$) to obtain cluster assignment probabilities ($p^{source}_t$, $p^{target}_t$) via a softmax function with a temperature parameter ($\tau$).
 - **Sinkhorn-Knopp Algorithm:** To avoid trivial solutions (where all samples map to one prototype), the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm (Cuturi, 2013) is applied to generate target cluster assignments ($q^{source}_t$, $q^{target}_t$).
 - **Objective Function (J_SwAV):** The representation learning objective maximizes prediction accuracy by making the source feature's assignment probability close to the target feature's assignment, and vice-versa, effectively pulling positive pairs closer in the latent space while pushing negative pairs apart implicitly.
- **Benefits of Contrastive Learning:** This approach makes the policy more robust to noise and improves sample efficiency by utilizing batch-level information to discern different environmental properties. The authors' t-SNE visualizations show a more separable encoding of environments compared to regression-based methods, indicating richer environmental information.

#### 3.5. Training Details

- **Simulation Environment:** Isaac Gym with 4096 parallel environments, 100-step rollouts.
- **Efficiency:** 200 million samples, 1 hour training on NVIDIA RTX 4090.
- **Dynamics Randomization:** Extensive randomization of robot mass, CoM, payload, ground friction, restitution, motor strength, joint PD gains, system delay, external force, and initial joint positions to facilitate sim2real transfer and robustness.
- **Training Curriculum:** A terrain curriculum (20x10 grid of varying difficulty terrains like slopes, rough slopes, stairs, discrete obstacles) and a command curriculum (gradually increasing velocity ranges) are used to progressively challenge the robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates that the Hybrid Internal Model (HIM) significantly enhances legged robot locomotion, achieving high agility, robustness, efficiency, and generalizability with minimal sensor requirements.

#### 4.1. Real-World Performance

Evaluations on Unitree A1, Go1, and Aliengo robots across challenging real-world benchmarks show HIM's superior performance:
- **Stairs:** Achieved 100% success rate on short-range stairs (A1) and an average of 176.5 steps on long-range stairs (Aliengo), vastly outperforming RMA (60% / 75.35 steps), MoB (0% / 0 steps), and Built-in MPC (0% / 0 steps).
- **Unseen Terrains:** Demonstrated strong generalization on compositional terrains (gravel and stairs, 85% success rate for Aliengo vs. 45% for RMA) and deformable slopes (two soft beds, 55% success rate for A1 vs. 10% for RMA), showcasing robustness to environments not explicitly encountered during training.
- **Anti-Disturbance:** Exhibited high resilience to various disturbances:
 - Dragging Obstacle: Withstood up to 10 kg of drag (A1), matching RMA and surpassing MoB/MPC.
 - Vertical Hit: Endured up to 8 kg load during lateral hits (A1), outperforming all baselines.
 - Payload: Carried up to 8 kg payload (A1), superior to RMA, MoB, and MPC.
 - Missing Steps: Achieved 100% success rate in recovering from falling off a 42 cm high platform (Aliengo), while RMA, MoB, and MPC all failed (0%).
Overall, HIM policies maintain more natural gaits and generalize exceptionally well to open-world conditions.

#### 4.2. Simulation Performance

Comparative analysis in Isaac Gym across various terrain types (slopes, rough slopes, stairs, discrete obstacles) and velocity ranges (linear, angular, combined) confirmed HIM's efficiency and robustness:
- **Lower Tracking Errors:** HIM consistently achieved lower linear and angular velocity tracking errors compared to Baseline, Regression, RMA, and MoB. For instance, on stairs (linear velocity), HIM had an error of 0.147, significantly lower than Regression (0.194), RMA (0.306), and others.
- **Efficiency:** The policy converged with only 200 million samples, requiring just 1 hour of training on an RTX 4090 GPU, making it highly sample-efficient.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies, both in simulation and real-world, elucidated the contributions of different HIM components:
- **Importance of Hybrid Internal Model:** Both the velocity estimation and the internal latent embedding within HIM are crucial. Removing either component (e.g., "Ours w/o velocity input," "Ours w/o latent input") consistently led to performance degradation in tracking scores and reachable terrain levels in simulation, and reduced success rates in real-world tasks.
- **Superiority of Contrastive Learning:** Optimizing the hybrid internal model with contrastive learning ("Ours") significantly outperformed using a direct regression objective ("Regression") in both simulation (lower tracking errors) and real-world (higher success rates). This supports the claim that contrastive learning is more robust to noise and better at capturing environmental properties.
- **Separable Latent Space:** t-SNE visualizations showed that HIM's hybrid internal model creates a more separable encoding of different environments compared to regression-based methods, implying that it learns richer and more discriminative environmental information.
- **Prototype Number:** An ablation on the number of prototypes in contrastive learning showed that an appropriate number (e.g., 16 or 32) is critical for optimal performance, balancing initial training speed and final performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Hybrid Internal Model (HIM) represents a significant advancement in learning-based locomotion control for legged robots, with several far-reaching implications and potential impacts:

1. **Paradigm Shift in State Estimation:** By drawing inspiration from classical Internal Model Control, HIM offers a novel, implicit approach to estimating external environmental dynamics. Instead of requiring direct regression of noisy external parameters or relying on exteroceptive sensors, it infers these "disturbances" from the robot's own response, a more robust and efficient strategy. This could inspire new directions in how robots perceive and adapt to their surroundings without explicit world models.

2. **Enhanced Generalizability and Robustness:** The demonstrated ability of HIM-trained robots to traverse unseen terrains, handle compositional environments, and withstand significant disturbances (payloads, hits, missing steps) highlights exceptional open-world generalizability. This robustness is critical for deploying legged robots in unpredictable real-world scenarios, from disaster relief to logistics.

3. **Minimal Sensor Requirements:** HIM's reliance solely on proprioceptive sensors (joint encoders and IMU) makes it highly practical and universally applicable across diverse legged robot platforms. This significantly reduces hardware complexity and cost, facilitating broader adoption of agile locomotion capabilities. It democratizes advanced robot control by making it accessible to platforms without expensive or specialized exteroceptive sensors.

4. **High Sample Efficiency and Fast Training:** Achieving high performance with only 200 million samples and 1 hour of training on a single GPU (RTX 4090) is a remarkable feat. This efficiency drastically reduces the computational resources and time required for developing and iterating on locomotion policies, accelerating research and development cycles.

5. **Effective Use of Contrastive Learning:** The work effectively demonstrates the power of contrastive learning for learning robust representations of environmental dynamics in reinforcement learning, especially for real-world robotics. Its superiority over regression-based methods in handling noise and leveraging batch-level information provides a compelling argument for its wider application in similar domains where implicit state estimation is beneficial.

6. **Bridging Classical and Modern Control:** HIM successfully bridges the gap between classical control theory (Internal Model Control) and modern machine learning (Deep Reinforcement Learning, Contrastive Learning). This cross-pollination of ideas can lead to more principled and robust learning algorithms for robotics.

**Future Impact:**
While HIM currently lacks external perceptions like visual information, the authors acknowledge this as a limitation for higher-level, more complex tasks. Integrating HIM with external perception is a natural next step, potentially allowing for even more sophisticated interaction with the environment, such as object avoidance, detailed navigation, and complex manipulation, while retaining the core benefits of robust internal state estimation. Overall, HIM presents a highly promising and practical framework that pushes the boundaries of agile legged locomotion, paving the way for more autonomous and adaptable robots in real-world applications.
