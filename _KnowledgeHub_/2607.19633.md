# LENS: LLM-guided Environment Simplification for Planning and Control in Clutter

> [!summary] Summary
> The LENS framework, developed by the University of Pennsylvania, employs a Multimodal Large Language Model to create task-focused scene abstractions by pruning irrelevant objects and merging coupled entities. This approach consistently enhanced task success rates and reduced computation time for diverse robotic control systems operating in highly cluttered environments.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation systems struggle with the computational and operational complexity of multi-object clutter, leading to performance degradation and failures.
- Current planning and control methods process all objects in a scene, causing scalability issues and timeouts as clutter increases, even for irrelevant items.
- Manual scene abstraction is costly, does not scale, and is difficult to adapt, highlighting a need for automated, adaptive solutions.

## Method

- **LENS** uses a ==Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM)== to generate ==task-focused scene abstractions== by identifying and pruning irrelevant objects based on a natural language task description.
- It merges functionally or spatially coupled objects into single composite entities to further simplify the scene representation for downstream planning and control systems.
- The framework incorporates a ==closed-loop refinement mechanism==, re-querying the MLLM with failure feedback to adaptively adjust the scene abstraction if initial execution fails.

## Results

- For TAMP, LENS outperformed baselines in heavy clutter and scenes with object stacks by effectively pruning distractors and merging entities.
- In model-based control, LENS-C3+ maintained stable execution times (**40-135 seconds**) even as object count increased, while baseline performance degraded by orders of magnitude (e.g., over **4000 seconds** at 7 objects).
- LENS improved VLA success rates in cluttered simulations (from **0.5** to **0.69**) and on a real robot (e.g., Pineapple pick-and-place from **0.2** to **0.5** success rate).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multimodal Large Language Models can effectively interpret natural language tasks and scene context to semantically determine task-relevant objects and their relationships for abstraction.
> - Simplifying the world model *before* planning or control significantly reduces computational complexity and enhances tractability in cluttered environments for various robotic systems.
> - A closed-loop feedback mechanism allows the system to adaptively refine scene abstractions in response to execution failures, increasing robustness in dynamic scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation faces challenges when confronted with real-world environments characterized by multi-object clutter. Current robotic systems, despite advancements in general-purpose manipulation, exhibit limitations in handling such complexity. The difficulties arise from the scaling of problems due to an increased number of objects, the intricacies of contact physics, the presence of distractors, and potential task ambiguity.

Existing approaches to robotic control and planning often struggle with these factors. Optimization- and planning-based methods, such as Task and Motion Planning (TAMP), typically process every object in a scene, leading to computational costs and failure modes that escalate with clutter, even if many objects are irrelevant to the task. Similarly, learned controllers, including modern Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, are frequently trained on curated, tidy environments. When deployed in dense, unstructured settings, they encounter distribution shifts that can degrade affordance estimates and lead to incorrect contact predictions.

To mitigate these issues, researchers have explored various strategies:
- **Manual Scene Abstraction:** This involves extensive, task-specific manual engineering to simplify scene representations. However, this approach is costly, does not scale effectively, and is difficult to adapt or fine-tune for diverse scenarios.
- **VLM for Action and Plan Generation:** Some works utilize Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for high-level planning, program generation, or skill sequencing. These methods generally operate on a pre-defined world representation, making them complementary to scene abstraction rather than a direct solution for generating it.
- **VLM for Guiding Planning:** Another line of research uses VLMs or Large Language Models (LLMs) to guide classical planners by generating subgoals, predicting constraints, scoring plans, or inferring feasibility. These approaches typically leave the underlying planner and the world model unchanged, focusing on informing the planning process rather than altering the scene representation itself.
- **VLM-Generated Scene Representations:** Approaches in this category use VLMs to build richer semantic representations, such as object-centric scene graphs or relational graphs. While these enhance understanding, they often maintain a complete scene representation, leaving downstream planners or controllers to manage the full combinatorial complexity.
- **Model-Based Control Simplifications:** Complementary work in model-based control addresses complexity through contact-implicit model reduction, shape approximation, or dynamic-resolution models for object piles. These methods, while promising, are often tied to specific tasks or control paradigms.
- **Observation Filtering:** In visuomotor policies, methods like language-conditioned perception or observation filtering reduce visual inputs. However, these often require manually specified labels or fine-tuned models and may not extend to symbolic planning or model-based control.

This research addresses a gap by proposing an automated and adaptive method for scene abstraction that actively *reduces the world model* *before* planning or control. Unlike previous approaches that guide planners or enrich existing representations, this work focuses on simplifying the environment itself, offering a plug-and-play front-end to various robotic stacks. This aims to shift the burden of abstraction from manual engineering to an automated semantic reasoning loop, thereby enhancing the tractability of robotic operations in cluttered, unstructured real-world scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the fundamental challenge that real-world multi-object clutter poses to modern robotic manipulation systems. As environments become increasingly unstructured and dense, the computational burden and operational complexity for robots grow significantly, leading to performance degradation or outright failure.

The key objectives of the LENS framework are:

1. **Address the Scalability Barrier of Clutter:** Recognize that the number of objects, contacts, decision branches, and interaction modes in cluttered scenes fundamentally limits the solvability and real-time performance of robotic planning and control methods. For both optimization-based and learned approaches, performance collapses in complex scenes due to the inability to focus on task-relevant elements.
2. **Automate Scene Abstraction:** Develop a method to automatically generate scene-specific, task-specific, and adaptively updating abstractions. This contrasts with existing practices that rely on extensive, costly, and brittle manual engineering of task-specific heuristics for scene simplification.
3. **Create a General, Plug-and-Play Solution:** Design a framework that can integrate seamlessly as a front-end into various existing planning and control stacks without requiring substantial modifications to the core algorithms. This aims for versatility and ease of deployment across different robotic paradigms.
4. **Leverage Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) for Task Relevance:** Utilize the semantic understanding capabilities of MLLMs to determine and update task relevancy dynamically. This involves identifying what is semantically, geometrically, and dynamically important for a given task, thereby enabling selective focus.
5. **Implement Closed-Loop Adaptive Refinement:** Incorporate a feedback mechanism where execution failures (e.g., infeasible plans, control errors) trigger re-prompting of the MLLM. This allows LENS to iteratively correct and refine its scene abstraction in response to changes in the environment or initial reasoning errors.
6. **Improve Performance Metrics:** Demonstrate through empirical evaluation that this automated abstraction leads to improved scalability, increased success rates, and reduced computation time in highly cluttered manipulation scenes across diverse robotic control paradigms (TAMP, model-based control, and VLA models).

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a flexible and principled mechanism to manage the combinatorial and distributional complexities imposed by clutter, thereby bridging the gap between robots functioning in structured laboratory settings and their deployment in the unbounded complexity of the real world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, **LLM-guided Environment Simplification (LENS)**, operates as a closed-loop system for constructing and refining task-focused scene abstractions. It integrates with existing robotic planning and control stacks as a pre-processing and feedback layer.

**Core Framework (Algorithm 1 and Figure 2):**
1. **Input:** LENS receives a natural language task description (τ) and a complete scene description (O). The format of O varies depending on the downstream system (e.g., object features for TAMP, segmentation masks for VLA).
2. **VLM Query:** A Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM), specifically GPT-4o in this implementation, is prompted. The prompt includes τ, O, and information about the downstream robotic system (S). The VLM is instructed to output a list of task-relevant objects and sublists of objects that can be functionally or spatially coupled and treated as a single entity.
3. **Scene Abstraction (˜O) Generation:** Based on the VLM's output, LENS performs two primary operations on the original scene O to construct the abstracted scene ˜O:
 - **Pruning:** Objects that are not identified as task-relevant by the VLM are omitted from ˜O. The specific implementation of pruning depends on the downstream system:
 - **TAMP:** Pruned objects are removed from the active decision variables. They still participate in collision checking but their poses are considered fixed.
 - **Contact-Implicit Model Predictive Control (C3+):** Pruning reduces the number of rigid bodies, potential contact pairs, and the dimensionality of the linear complementarity problem (LCP), including the surface sampling space.
 - **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models:** Excluded objects are "inpainted" out of the image observation using generative modeling techniques, reducing visual clutter and suppressing distractors.
 - **Merging:** Sublists of objects identified by the VLM are grouped into a single composite entity within ˜O. The definition of merging is adapted for each system:
 - **TAMP:** Merged objects (e.g., a stable stack) adopt a unified ID, with the lowest supporting object as the parent. Their geometries are fused, and they cannot be separated during planning.
 - **C3+:** Objects are clustered if their contact interactions can be reasoned about collectively (e.g., a pile to be pushed). The merged body's geometry is updated in real-time. This reduces the number of bodies and contact pairs but does not necessarily imply physical stability of the group.
 - **VLA Models:** Merging is not applied to the VLA abstraction; ˜O for VLA is a flat list of relevant objects.
4. **Controller Execution:** The abstracted scene ˜O is then passed to the downstream planner or controller.
5. **Closed-Loop Refinement:**
 - **Feedback Trigger:** If the controller execution fails (e.g., due to a timeout, an error code like workspace limits, or runtime errors), feedback is triggered.
 - **VLM Re-query:** A framework-specific feedback prompt, along with the previous ˜O and the current scene, is appended to the original prompt. LENS then re-queries the VLM for a revised scene reduction. This iterative process allows for dynamic adjustment of the abstraction based on task progress and execution outcomes.

**Implementation Details for Various Control Stacks:**

- **Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) Stack (PDDLStream):**
 - **Scene (O):** A graph of objects with poses, masses, and relations (e.g., "stacked on").
 - **Abstracted Scene (˜O):** Defines which objects are active decision variables. Pruned objects have fixed poses. Merged groups adopt a unified ID and cannot be separated. Collision checking still occurs over the full ˜O.
 - **Feedback:** Triggered after a 120-second timeout. The VLM is prompted to expand the object selection if the previous run failed.

- **Contact-Implicit Model Predictive Control (C3+ Stack):**
 - **Scene (O):** A set of objects with poses and names.
 - **Abstracted Scene (˜O):** A reduced set of objects. Pruning reduces bodies, contact pairs, and LCP dimensionality. Merged groups are for collective reasoning about contact interactions (e.g., pushing a pile) and their geometry updates in real-time, even if not physically stable.
 - **Feedback:** Triggered after 250 control iterations or an error code. The VLM is re-queried with failure feedback to revise the scene reduction.

- **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Model Stack (π0.5):**
 - **Scene (O):** An overhead image with object bounding boxes.
 - **Abstracted Scene (˜O):** A flat list of relevant objects; no merging operation is applied.
 - **Pruning Implementation:** Generative modeling-based image inpainting is used to remove excluded objects from the image, producing a filtered input for the VLA policy. This reduces visual clutter and suppresses distractor affordances.
 - **Feedback:** No VLM re-queries are performed for VLA tasks, given their typically shorter task horizons where the initial filtered scene remains valid.

The approach leverages VLMs to inject high-level task semantics into a dynamic scene-abstraction process, aiming to provide a principled and automated way to manage scene complexity for various robotic tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LENS demonstrated improved performance across different robotic control paradigms in cluttered environments.

**General Performance:**
- LENS consistently improved success rates for Task and Motion Planning (TAMP), model-based control (C3+), and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.
- The VLM query time averaged 1.76 seconds, which was considered negligible relative to the overall execution time for robotic tasks.

**Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) Results (Section 5.1):**
- **Environments:** LENS was evaluated in three progressively complex tabletop environments: lightly cluttered, heavily cluttered, and a scenario with clutter and a stack of objects. The task involved moving blue goal objects to a green target region, allowing red objects to be moved if necessary.
- **Performance:**
 - In **light clutter**, LENS-TAMP and the baseline (unfiltered observations) achieved comparable success rates, indicating limited benefit from abstraction when the scene is inherently simple.
 - In **heavy clutter**, LENS-TAMP significantly outperformed the baseline, which frequently timed out due to the large number of distractors. VLM-guided filtering effectively suppressed irrelevant objects, enabling successful plan generation.
 - In the **Clutter & Stack** scenario, LENS-TAMP again surpassed the baseline. The baseline failed due to distractor interference, whereas LENS's ability to merge the stack into a single composite entity simplified the scene and facilitated more reliable plan search.
- **Iterative Refinement:** Success rates were observed across two allowed feedback iterations, indicating that the closed-loop re-prompting mechanism contributed to overall task completion.
- **Qualitative Advantage (Appendix B):** An anecdotal example showed LENS identifying critical blocking objects even when they were spatially distant from the immediate goal, a capability that simple distance-based heuristics lacked.

**Model-Based Control (C3+) Results (Section 5.2):**
- **Task:** Planar pushing, requiring the robot's end-effector to push a goal object ('G') to a target pose and orientation in cluttered scenes. This involved complex object-object, object-robot, and object-ground contact interactions.
- **Simulation Experiments (Object Count Ablation):**
 - **Scalability:** For scenes with 2-4 objects, LENS-C3+ and the baseline (C3+ without abstraction) showed comparable performance. However, as the number of objects increased, the baseline's execution time severely degraded, becoming prohibitively slow (e.g., an order of magnitude slower at 6 objects, and over 4000 seconds at 7 objects). In contrast, LENS-C3+ maintained stable average execution times (40-135 seconds) across all tested clutter levels, demonstrating that task relevance, rather than raw object count, dictates the effective problem size.
 - **Success Rates:** LENS-C3+ achieved a higher success rate (39/45 trials) compared to the baseline-C3+ (17/30 trials).
- **Comparison to Geometric Pruning (Appendix C):** LENS outperformed distance-based geometric pruning baselines, which often failed to identify distant but task-critical objects, thus validating the VLM's semantic reasoning advantage over simple spatial heuristics.
- **Real Robot Experiments:**
 - **Variations:** Experiments included "pruning only" (precise pushing) and "pruning+merging" (gross pushing).
 - **Adaptive Refinement:** The cumulative success rate reached 80% over three feedback loops, with successes distributed across iterations. This suggested that initial aggressive pruning could sometimes omit relevant contacts, and subsequent VLM re-queries, informed by failure feedback, corrected these abstractions, enabling task completion.
 - **Failure Modes:** Observed failures included timeouts and workspace limits.

**Vision-Language-Action Models (VLA) Results (Section 5.3):**
- **Task:** Tabletop manipulation tasks from the Spatial LIBERO benchmark, augmented with additional instances of the goal object to increase visual ambiguity and distractors for the π0.5 VLA policy.
- **Simulation Experiments:**
 - Clutter significantly degraded VLA performance; the baseline success rate dropped from 0.85 (uncluttered) to 0.5 when clutter was introduced.
 - LENS substantially recovered this performance loss, improving the success rate to 0.69, indicating that task-focused scene abstraction can be an effective mechanism for VLAs in cluttered environments.
- **Real Robot Experiments (Hardware VLA):**
 - **Setup:** The hardware experiments involved picking up fruit plushies and placing them in a target bowl, using an object detection and inpainting pipeline for LENS.
 - **Performance:** LENS consistently improved task success rates over the baseline across different fruit types (e.g., Pineapple: LENS 0.5 vs. Baseline 0.2; Apple: LENS 0.7 vs. Baseline 0.0), confirming that the performance gains translate to real-world deployment.

In summary, the results indicate that LENS, by intelligently pruning and merging objects based on VLM guidance and adaptive feedback, significantly enhances the scalability, robustness, and efficiency of diverse robotic control systems in complex, cluttered manipulation scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LENS framework presents a significant contribution to robotic manipulation by directly addressing the pervasive challenge of operating in cluttered, unstructured real-world environments. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

1. **Enhanced Tractability and Scalability:** LENS effectively reduces the combinatorial and distributional complexity that arises from scene clutter. By generating minimal, task-conditioned world models, it enables existing planning and control algorithms—which traditionally struggle with many objects—to operate efficiently and reliably in far more complex settings. This is crucial for scaling robotic applications beyond curated laboratory environments.

2. **Improved Performance and Robustness:** The experimental results consistently demonstrate increased success rates and reduced computation times across TAMP, model-based control, and VLA paradigms. This suggests that LENS makes robotic systems more robust to visual and physical clutter, leading to more dependable task execution in real-world scenarios.

3. **Automation of Scene Abstraction:** A primary impact of LENS is the automation of scene abstraction. Historically, generating effective scene abstractions required extensive, manual, and task-specific engineering. LENS shifts this burden to an automated, VLM-driven semantic reasoning loop, making abstraction generation more accessible, adaptable, and less prone to human engineering overhead.

4. **Versatility and Generalizability:** The framework's ability to integrate as a plug-and-play front-end with three distinct and prominent robotic control stacks (symbolic planning, optimization-based control, and learned policies) highlights its versatility. This suggests that LENS is a generalizable solution that can benefit a wide range of robotic systems without requiring fundamental architectural changes to their core control logic.

5. **Bridging the Lab-to-Real Gap:** By enabling robots to effectively handle clutter, LENS contributes to bridging the gap between robot capabilities demonstrated in controlled laboratory settings and the demands of deployment in the unpredictable complexity of everyday environments. This is a critical step towards broader adoption of robots in homes, warehouses, and other unstructured spaces.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, which also suggest avenues for future research:
- **Computational Cost of Validation:** While abstract representations could theoretically be validated in simulation before execution, this is currently computationally prohibitive for some controllers.
- **Feedback Granularity:** The current feedback mechanism relies on high-level failure signals (e.g., timeouts, error codes) rather than detailed, explicit causes of failure. Future work could explore more nuanced diagnostic feedback to refine abstractions more precisely.
- **Learning from Experience:** LENS does not currently learn from experience across multiple runs or tasks. Incorporating mechanisms for lifelong learning or fine-tuning the abstraction process could further enhance its capabilities.
- **Richer Abstraction Operations:** Future work could explore more sophisticated abstraction operations, potentially through reinforcement learning or further fine-tuning of VLMs, to capture more complex functional relationships between objects.
- **Tighter Perceptual Integration:** Improving the integration between VLM reasoning and perceptual systems could lead to more accurate and robust scene understanding and abstraction.
- **Long-Horizon Multi-Stage Tasks:** Extending LENS to effectively handle long-horizon, multi-stage tasks where the definition of "relevance" dynamically evolves over time presents another area for development.

Overall, LENS represents a step towards developing robots that can autonomously adapt their world understanding to task demands, a capability that is crucial for pervasive robotic deployment in complex and dynamic real-world environments.
