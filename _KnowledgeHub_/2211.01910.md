# Large Language Models Are Human-Level Prompt Engineers

> [!summary] Summary
> The Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE) method automates the generation and selection of effective natural language prompts for large language models. Developed by researchers at the University of Toronto and the Vector Institute, APE achieved human-level or better performance on 24 instruction induction tasks and improved zero-shot Chain-of-Thought reasoning by discovering novel prompts.

## Problem

- The performance of large language models (LLMs) on specific tasks is highly sensitive to the exact wording and structure of the prompt.
- Discovering effective prompts often relies on a time-consuming, expensive, and non-intuitive manual process by human prompt engineers.
- Prior automated prompt engineering methods, such as soft prompts or discrete search, often have limitations like requiring gradient access or not fully leveraging LLM's own generative capabilities.

## Method

- The **Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE)** algorithm automates the process of generating and selecting effective ==natural language instructions== (prompts) for large language models.
- It frames natural language instruction generation as a ==black-box optimization problem==, leveraging LLMs to propose candidate instructions, score them against input-output demonstrations, and iteratively refine them via ==iterative Monte Carlo search==.
- APE employs efficient scoring mechanisms, including ==multi-stage filtering==, to reduce computational costs by prioritizing evaluation for higher-quality prompt candidates.

## Results

- The Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE) method achieved human-level or better performance on all **24** Instruction Induction tasks, with InstructGPT's Interquartile Mean (**0.810**) surpassing that of human-engineered prompts (**0.749**).
- APE discovered an improved Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought prompt, "Let’s work this out in a step by step way to be sure we have the right answer," which boosted InstructGPT's performance on MultiArith from **78.7 to 82.0** and GSM8K from **40.7 to 43.0**.
- The analysis found that larger, human-aligned LLMs are more cost-efficient for prompt generation despite higher per-token costs, due to producing more concise and higher-quality instructions that reduce subsequent scoring costs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large language models can serve as "human-level prompt engineers" by generating and evaluating their own natural language instructions.
> - Framing prompt engineering as "natural language program synthesis" allows for systematic optimization using LLM capabilities.
> - The Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE) method can discover non-obvious, high-performing prompts that surpass human-engineered ones, such as an improved zero-shot Chain-of-Thought prefix.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of large language models, prompt engineering, and program synthesis, addressing a critical challenge in the practical application of LLMs.

**Large Language Models (LLMs) and Their Capabilities:** The paper acknowledges the "unprecedented level of generality" and "remarkable, often superhuman, capabilities" of modern LLMs, citing seminal works like Kaplan et al. (2020) on scaling laws and Brown et al. (2020) on few-shot learning with GPT-3. It recognizes emergent abilities of LLMs, such as zero-shot problem-solving, chain-of-thought reasoning, instruction following, and instruction induction (Wei et al., 2022a; Honovich et al., 2022). The core premise of the paper is to view LLMs as "black-box computers that execute programs specified by natural language instructions," a common conceptualization that highlights the control challenge.

**Prompt Engineering:** This area has rapidly evolved as the primary interface for interacting with and steering LLMs.
- **Manual Prompt Engineering:** The paper highlights that historically, effective prompts have been "handcrafted by humans" (Reynolds & McDonell, 2021). This manual process is often iterative, requires significant human intuition, and is model-specific, making it a bottleneck for widespread LLM adoption and optimal performance. The paper implicitly critiques this manual, often inefficient, approach.
- **Automatic Prompting:** Prior work has explored automating prompt generation.
 - **Soft Prompts / Prompt Tuning:** These methods optimize continuous, differentiable "soft prompts" in the embedding space (Lester et al., 2021; Qin & Eisner, 2021). While effective, they require gradient access and can be computationally expensive at scale, making them less practical for black-box API access to LLMs.
 - **Discrete Prompt Search:** This category involves searching directly in the natural language space, often using methods like prompt generation (Gao et al., 2021), scoring (Davison et al., 2019), and paraphrasing (Jiang et al., 2020). This paper falls squarely into this category but distinguishes itself by performing the entire search using the LLM itself, rather than specialized models or human-designed templates.

**Program Synthesis:** The paper innovatively frames "instruction generation as natural language program synthesis." It draws parallels to classical program synthesis, which aims to automatically generate programs from specifications, often input-output examples (Gulwani et al., 2017; Ellis et al., 2021). The key distinction is that APE searches over the "space of natural language programs" by leveraging the LLMs' own generative and reasoning capabilities, rather than requiring a predefined structured hypothesis space or component library. This approach positions LLMs not just as program *executors* but also as program *synthesizers* of natural language instructions.

In summary, this work directly addresses the inefficiency of human prompt engineering by providing a principled, automated solution that leverages the very capabilities of LLMs themselves. It contributes to the growing body of research on making LLMs more controllable and accessible, bridging the gap between their immense potential and the practical challenges of guiding their behavior.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **automate the process of generating and selecting effective natural language instructions (prompts) for large language models**. The authors propose an algorithm called Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE) to achieve this.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several key observations and challenges in the current LLM landscape:
- **Dependence on Prompt Quality:** Despite their advanced capabilities, the performance of LLMs on specific tasks is highly sensitive to the exact wording and structure of the prompt. A small change can lead to significant performance differences.
- **Human Effort and Intuition Deficit:** Currently, discovering effective prompts often relies on human "prompt engineers" who iteratively experiment and refine prompts. This process is time-consuming, expensive, and non-intuitive, as the internal workings and "understanding" of LLMs are opaque to humans. The paper views LLMs as "black-box computers" where the optimal "programs" (prompts) are not obvious to human operators.
- **Leveraging LLM Generality:** The authors are motivated to harness the inherent generative and reasoning capabilities of LLMs to solve this self-referential problem. If LLMs can generate coherent text and follow instructions, perhaps they can also generate *instructions for themselves* and evaluate their efficacy.
- **Reducing Bottlenecks:** Automating prompt engineering would significantly reduce the human effort involved, making LLMs more accessible and efficient for a wider range of applications and users.
- **Formulating as Program Synthesis:** By framing prompt engineering as "natural language program synthesis," the authors aim to apply principled optimization techniques from classical program synthesis to the domain of natural language, opening new theoretical and practical avenues.

In essence, the paper seeks to enable LLMs to become their own "human-level prompt engineers," abstracting away the manual trial-and-error that currently defines much of LLM deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach (Automatic Prompt Engineer - APE)

The Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE) algorithm frames natural language instruction generation as a **black-box optimization problem**. The goal is to find an instruction (ρ) that maximizes a chosen score function, given a set of input-output demonstrations. APE leverages LLMs in three key roles: as inference models for generating candidates, as scoring models for evaluating candidates, and as resampling models for refining candidates.

The APE workflow (Algorithm 1) consists of the following steps:

1. **Initial Proposal Distribution:**
 - **Concept:** Given a set of training input-output demonstrations (D_train), an LLM is used to generate a pool (U) of candidate instructions. The idea is to make the LLM "infer" the most likely instructions that could produce the given demonstrations.
 - **Forward Mode Generation:** The LLM is prompted with the demonstrations *followed by a blank for the instruction*. This requires the instruction to be predicted at the end of the prompt (e.g., "Here are the input-output pairs:... The instruction was <COMPLETE>"). This is straightforward for autoregressive LLMs.
 - **Reverse Mode Generation:** To offer more flexibility (e.g., when the instruction is naturally at the beginning of a passage), LLMs with infilling capabilities (like T5, GLM, InsertGPT) are used to "fill in the blank" for the instruction (e.g., "I instructed my friend to <INSERT>. The friend read the instruction...").
 - **Customized Prompts:** Task-specific prompts can also be designed for instruction generation, as demonstrated in TruthfulQA experiments.

2. **Scoring Functions:**
 - **Concept:** Each proposed instruction (ρ) is evaluated by executing it with the target LLM (M) on the training demonstrations, and a score function f(ρ, Q, A) measures its alignment with the desired outputs.
 - **Execution Accuracy (f_exec):** This is the primary metric, typically a 0-1 loss (i.e., 1 if M([ρ;Q]) produces A, 0 otherwise). For some tasks, it incorporates invariants (e.g., order-invariant set matching).
 - **Log Probability:** A "softer" probabilistic score, log P(A|[ρ;Q]), which might provide more fine-grained signal during optimization, especially for low-quality candidates.
 - **Efficient Score Estimation:** To reduce computational cost, APE employs a multi-stage filtering scheme. Instructions are first evaluated on a small subset of the training data. Only promising candidates proceed to evaluation on larger, non-overlapping subsets, eventually evaluating the top few on the entire training set. This prioritizes computation for higher-quality candidates.

3. **Iterative Proposal Distributions (Iterative Monte Carlo Search):**
 - **Concept:** If the initial proposal set lacks diversity or sufficiently high-scoring candidates, an iterative refinement process can be applied.
 - **Process:** In each iteration, the top k% of instructions based on their scores are retained. An LLM is then prompted to "generate a variation of the following instruction while keeping the semantic meaning" (Figure 3), using the high-scoring instructions as input. This explores the local search space around promising candidates.
 - **Note:** While designed to improve overall proposal quality, the authors found that iterative search often provides only marginal improvement over the initial, simpler generative process for finding the *best* instruction, hence it's not the default.

4. **Selection:**
 - The algorithm returns the instruction with the highest score over the entire training dataset after the (initial or iterative) search process.

In essence, APE performs a search over the natural language prompt space, using one LLM to generate candidate prompts and another (or the same) LLM to execute and score these prompts against given input-output examples. This closed-loop optimization system allows LLMs to self-discover effective instructions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that APE can effectively automate prompt engineering, often achieving or surpassing human performance across a diverse range of tasks.

- **Instruction Induction:**
 - APE significantly outperforms the prior "Greedy" LLM baseline, which lacked a search and selection process.
 - Crucially, APE achieved **human-level or better performance on all 24** Instruction Induction tasks (Honovich et al., 2022). When using InstructGPT (text-davinci-002), APE's Interquartile Mean (IQM) across all tasks (0.810) surpassed that of human-engineered prompts (0.749).
 - **Few-shot Learning Improvement:** APE-generated instructions, when prepended to standard in-context learning prompts, improved or maintained performance on 21 of 24 tasks. This highlights that zero-shot optimized instructions can also enhance few-shot performance, though some cases (e.g., Rhymes) showed overfitting where zero-shot selected prompts could hurt few-shot performance, suggesting the need for few-shot metrics in such cases.

- **BIG-Bench Instruction Induction (BBII):**
 - On a curated subset of 21 challenging BIG-Bench tasks, APE achieved **comparable or better performance than default human prompts on 17 out of 21 tasks**. This demonstrates APE's applicability and effectiveness on more complex and diverse reasoning and understanding tasks.

- **Zero-Shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning:**
 - APE was used to automatically search for a better CoT prefix than the widely adopted "Let's think step by step." (Kojima et al., 2022).
 - APE discovered the prompt **"Let’s work this out in a step by step way to be sure we have the right answer."** This APE-generated prompt further improved zero-shot CoT performance of InstructGPT on MultiArith from 78.7 to 82.0 and on GSM8K from 40.7 to 43.0, demonstrating its ability to find subtle yet powerful prompting tricks.

- **TruthfulQA:**
 - APE was successfully applied to steer LLMs towards truthfulness and/or informativeness on the TruthfulQA dataset.
 - APE-generated instructions outperformed a human-engineered "help" prompt, achieving over **40% accuracy in providing both true and informative answers** (compared to 30% for the human prompt).
 - The discovered instructions tended to target the extreme ends of the truthfulness-informativeness Pareto frontier (e.g., highly truthful but uninformative "No comment").

- **Quantitative Analyses of APE Components:**
 - **Proposal Quality and Model Size:** Larger, human-aligned LLMs (like InstructGPT 175B) produce higher-quality and more concise initial instruction proposals.
 - **Sample Size:** Sampling more instruction candidates (up to around 64) monotonically increases the likelihood of finding better instructions, with diminishing returns.
 - **Scoring Function:** Execution accuracy proved to be a more reliable metric for instruction selection, correlating better with test performance than log probability.
 - **Iterative Search:** While it generally improves the quality of the candidate set, iterative search yielded only marginal improvements in finding the *absolute best* instruction compared to a single round of generation and selection.
 - **Cost Analysis:** Counter-intuitively, larger, human-aligned LLMs are **more cost-efficient** for instruction generation despite higher per-token costs. This is because they generate more concise and higher-quality instructions, which significantly reduces the cost of the subsequent (and dominant) scoring phase. APE-generated instructions are also much more token-efficient than in-context examples for conveying task information.
 - **Transferability:** APE-generated instructions tend to be **model-specific**. Instructions optimized for InstructGPT often perform poorly on GPT-3, and vice-versa. This highlights the unique internal "understanding" of different LLM architectures and training objectives. However, GPT-3 was shown to generate effective prompts for itself, even if those prompts were unintuitive to humans.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Large Language Models Are Human-Level Prompt Engineers" carries significant implications for the development, deployment, and understanding of large language models.

- **Automation of a Critical Task:** The most immediate impact is the successful automation of prompt engineering. This process, previously reliant on costly and often non-intuitive human effort, can now be systematically and efficiently executed by LLMs themselves. This removes a major bottleneck in deploying LLMs for various applications and democratizes access to high-performance prompting.
- **Enhanced LLM Control and Steerability:** APE provides a powerful new paradigm for controlling the behavior of LLMs. By automatically discovering optimal instructions, it allows users to elicit desired behaviors more reliably and precisely across a wide array of tasks, from simple language understanding to complex reasoning and even ethical considerations (e.g., truthfulness).
- **Advancement in Natural Language Program Synthesis:** The paper's innovative framing of instruction generation as natural language program synthesis is a key conceptual contribution. It demonstrates that LLMs can not only execute "natural language programs" but also synthesize them, opening new avenues for AI to self-optimize and adapt in human-interpretable ways. This could extend to other modalities where natural language serves as an interface (e.g., image generation, robotics).
- **Uncovering Prompting Insights:** The APE algorithm can discover non-obvious or "insightful tricks" for prompting, as evidenced by the improved Zero-Shot-CoT prompt ("Let’s work this out in a step by step way to be sure we have the right answer."). This suggests that automated methods can find optimal solutions that human intuition might miss, potentially leading to new breakthroughs in LLM capabilities.
- **Cost Efficiency in LLM Usage:** The counter-intuitive finding that larger, more capable LLMs are more cost-efficient for prompt generation (due to producing shorter, higher-quality prompts that reduce scoring costs) provides practical guidance for researchers and practitioners optimizing LLM workflows. Furthermore, APE-generated prompts are "context condensers," offering a token-efficient alternative to explicit in-context examples.
- **Future Research Directions:** The work opens several promising avenues for future research, including:
 - Exploring more sophisticated meta-prompt engineering to further improve initial proposal distributions.
 - Investigating methods to improve the transferability of APE-generated instructions across different LLM architectures and training objectives.
 - Applying the APE framework to other generative AI models beyond text (e.g., image, audio, or even formal code synthesis where natural language is the interface).
 - Deepening the understanding of why certain prompts work for specific models and the underlying mechanisms of LLM "understanding."

In conclusion, "Large Language Models Are Human-Level Prompt Engineers" presents a significant step towards autonomous and optimized interaction with advanced AI systems. By empowering LLMs to "engineer" their own prompts, this research contributes to making these powerful models more accessible, controllable, and effective across diverse real-world applications.
