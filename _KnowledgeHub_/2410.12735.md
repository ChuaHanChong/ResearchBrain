# CREAM: Consistency Regularized Self-Rewarding Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> CREAM, a consistency-regularized self-rewarding language model, introduces a mechanism that mitigates bias and overconfident preference labeling in iterative LLM alignment by adapting the DPO loss based on ranking consistency between current and previous model iterations. This approach enables sustained self-improvement and achieves performance competitive with oracle models, particularly for 7B Llama-3, without external human feedback or reward models.

## Problem

- Existing Self-Rewarding Language Models (SRLMs) suffer from unreliable and inaccurate self-generated rewards, especially for smaller LLMs.
- The same LLM acting as both generator and judge can accumulate biases and generate noisy, overconfident preference labels, particularly for responses of similar quality.
- This accumulated bias often leads to diminished returns or performance degradation after a few self-training iterations, limiting SRLMs' scalability and effectiveness.

## Method

- ==CREAM== introduces a ==consistency regularization term== to the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) loss, effectively transforming it into a ==soft-labeled DPO objective==.
- It estimates the confidence in a self-generated preference label by measuring the consistency of reward rankings between the current model iteration and the previous iteration using ==Kendall's Tau==.
- This consistency score adaptively weights the DPO loss, enabling the model to down-weight unreliable or inconsistent preference pairs and mitigate overconfidence and bias accumulation.

## Results

- CREAM significantly outperforms standard SRLM for Llama-2 and Llama-3 models, achieving substantial performance gains and demonstrating consistent iterative improvement across benchmarks.
- For Llama-3, CREAM M3 achieved an average accuracy of **83.37%**, competitively matching or slightly surpassing Oracle M3 (**82.12%**) without relying on external reward models.
- CREAM maintains significantly higher rewarding consistency and achieves higher pairwise ranking accuracy on both self-generated and external datasets compared to standard SRLM.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Blindly trusting self-generated preferences in SRLMs leads to overconfident mistakes and the accumulation of biases, particularly when distinguishing between responses of similar quality.
> - A generalized iterative preference fine-tuning framework can formally represent SRLMs, helping to identify the source of rewarding bias as overconfident preference labeling.
> - The consistency of reward rankings across successive model iterations provides an intrinsic, self-contained signal for assessing the reliability of self-generated preferences without external supervision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to impressive capabilities in various natural language understanding and generation tasks. However, a persistent challenge remains their "alignment" with human values and intentions, often manifesting as issues like hallucinations, biases, or the generation of harmful content. The broader research landscape is heavily invested in addressing these alignment challenges.

Historically, alignment has relied heavily on **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**, where human annotators provide preference feedback to train a reward model, which then guides the LLM's behavior. More recently, **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** emerged as a more efficient alternative to RLHF, directly optimizing the LLM using preference pairs without the explicit need for a separate reward model. Both RLHF and DPO, however, critically depend on large quantities of high-quality human-annotated preference data, which is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and expensive to collect, thereby limiting their scalability.

This bottleneck has spurred the development of **Self-Rewarding Language Models (SRLMs)**. Pioneered by works like Yuan et al. (2024), SRLMs offer a promising solution by leveraging the LLM itself to generate and curate its own preference data. In this paradigm, a single LLM acts in a dual role: as a "policy model" to generate candidate responses and as a "reward model" (often using LLM-as-a-Judge prompting) to score and rank these responses. The ranked responses then form preference pairs for DPO-style training, creating an iterative self-improvement loop.

While innovative, existing SRLMs face significant limitations. The core challenge lies in the *reliability and accuracy* of the self-generated rewards. When the same LLM is both generator and judge, there's a risk of accumulating biases and generating noisy, overconfident preference labels, especially for responses of similar quality. Empirical observations, particularly with smaller LLMs (e.g., 7B parameters), show that improvements from self-rewarding can diminish or even reverse after a few iterations due to this accumulated bias.

CREAM directly addresses this critical flaw. It recognizes that blindly trusting self-generated preferences can be detrimental. Instead, it proposes a **consistency regularization** mechanism that leverages the iterative nature of SRLM training. By comparing the reward rankings from the current model iteration with those from the previous iteration, CREAM estimates the confidence/consistency of preference labels. This allows it to *down-weight* unreliable preference pairs (those with low consistency) during training, effectively mitigating the overconfidence and bias accumulation problem. This work distinguishes itself from other self-improvement methods that often rely on ground truth responses or introduce external reward models, by offering a purely self-contained, consistency-driven regularization approach applicable even for smaller, broadly accessible LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are driven by the inherent challenges of existing Self-Rewarding Language Models (SRLMs):

1. **Formulate a Generalized Iterative Preference Fine-tuning Framework:** The authors aim to provide a unified theoretical understanding of the iterative preference fine-tuning process, which encompasses not only SRLMs but also other methods like RL with Human Feedback (RLHF) and RL with AI Feedback. This framework helps in precisely identifying the source of issues in SRLMs.
2. **Identify and Analyze Rewarding Bias:** A core motivation is to diagnose why SRLMs, particularly smaller ones (e.g., 7B parameters), struggle with sustained improvement and often exhibit diminished returns or degradation. The hypothesis is that the problem stems from "overconfident preference labeling." When an LLM acts as its own judge, it is forced to make binary preference judgments even between responses of very similar quality (which humans might find difficult to distinguish). This leads to noisy, unreliable preference data, and crucially, this noise and bias can accumulate across iterations.
3. **Propose a Mechanism to Mitigate Overconfident Preference Labeling:** Based on the theoretical analysis, the central objective is to develop a method that prevents the model from being overly confident when distinguishing between responses of similar quality. This mechanism should help the model learn from more reliable preference data.
4. **Leverage Iteration for Consistency Regularization:** A key practical motivation is to find a way to assess the reliability of self-generated preferences *without* external human annotations or expensive oracle models. The authors propose using the consistency of reward rankings across *different iterations* of the self-rewarding process as an intrinsic signal for regularization. If two successive model versions (current and previous) give inconsistent rankings for a pair of responses, it suggests that the preference for that pair is ambiguous and should be learned with less confidence.
5. **Develop a Practical and Efficient Algorithm (CREAM):** The ultimate objective is to translate these theoretical insights into a concrete, effective, and computationally feasible algorithm that can enhance the alignment performance and stability of SRLMs, particularly for broadly accessible 7B-size LLMs where prompt-based judging might be less reliable.

In essence, the motivation is to make self-rewarding LLMs more robust and reliable, transforming them into truly self-improving systems by addressing the fundamental issue of internal reward accuracy and bias propagation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CREAM (Consistency Regularized sElf-rewarding lAnguage Model) is built upon a principled theoretical framework and a novel regularization technique.

#### 3.1. Generalized Iterative Preference Fine-tuning Framework

The paper first formalizes a general iterative preference fine-tuning framework. The objective is to minimize a loss function `L(θ, z)`:
`L(θ, z) = L_SFT(θ; D_S) + E_{x~D_U; y,y'~π_θ_t(·|x)} [L_DPO(θ; y, y', x, z)]`

- `L_SFT`: Supervised Fine-Tuning loss on a labeled dataset `D_S`, aligning the model `π_θ` (parameterized by `θ`) with basic instruction-following abilities. This term can be zero if no SFT data is used.
- `L_DPO`: Direct Preference Optimization loss, which learns from preference data pairs `{y, y'}` generated from an unlabeled prompt dataset `D_U`.
- `z(y, y', x) ∈ {0, 1}`: A labeling function indicating preference (1 if `y` is preferred over `y'`).

The framework employs a **two-step optimization algorithm**, analogous to Expectation-Maximization or self-training:

1. **Preference-labeling step (E-step):** Given the current model `θ_t`, the labeling function `z` is determined by comparing the intrinsic reward scores of `y` and `y'`. For smaller LLMs (like 7B), `r_θ(x, y) ∝ [log π_θ(y|x) - log π_ref(y|x)]` is used as the reward model, rather than relying on LLM-as-a-Judge prompting, which is less reliable for smaller models.
 `z_{t+1}(y, y', x) = 1[log π_θ_t(y|x) - log π_ref(y|x) ≥ log π_θ_t(y'|x) - log π_ref(y'|x)]`
2. **Learning step (M-step):** With the preference labels `z_{t+1}` fixed, the model parameters `θ` are updated to `θ_{t+1}` by minimizing the overall loss `L(θ, z_{t+1})`.

The authors prove that this two-step process converges.

#### 3.2. Consistency Regularized Self-Rewarding (CREAM)

The core insight of CREAM is that the `z` labels generated by the model itself are not always reliable. Forcing a hard preference (`z=0` or `z=1`) when responses are of similar quality leads to overconfident mistakes and bias accumulation.

To address this, CREAM introduces a **regularization term `λL_Reg(θ; y, y', x)`** to the DPO loss, transforming the objective to:
`L(θ, z) = L_SFT(θ; D_S) + E_{x~D_U; y,y'~π_θ_t(·|x)} [L_DPO(θ; y, y', x, z) + λL_Reg(θ; y, y', x)]`

The `L_Reg` term is designed to push the model towards a uniform preference distribution for pairs of similar quality, effectively making it less confident. Theoretically, the authors show that minimizing `L_DPO + λL_Reg` is equivalent to minimizing a **soft-labeled DPO loss**:
`C_λ L_DPO(θ; y, y', x, z) + (1 − C_λ) L_DPO(θ; y, y', x, 1 − z)`
where `C_λ = (1 + λ) / (1 + 2λ)`. This implies that instead of just learning from the labeled preference `z`, the model also learns (to a lesser extent, weighted by `1-C_λ`) from the *reversed* preference `1-z`. This can be interpreted as a form of label smoothing. The `C_λ` factor represents the confidence in the label `z`.

**Crucially, how is `λ` (or `C_λ`) determined in a self-rewarding setting?** CREAM proposes to estimate the confidence in a preference label by measuring the *consistency* of rankings between the current model (`θ_t`) and the model from the *previous iteration* (`θ_{t-1}`). If `θ_t` and `θ_{t-1}` largely agree on the ranking of responses for a given prompt, the preference is deemed consistent (high `C_λ`); if they disagree, it's inconsistent (low `C_λ`).

#### 3.3. Proposed Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

1. **Initial SFT Training:** The base model `θ_0` is first fine-tuned on a seed SFT dataset `D_S` to obtain `θ_1`.
2. **Iterative Preference Training (Loop `t=1` to `T`):**
 - **Response Sampling:** For each prompt `x_j` in `D_U`, `N=5` responses `{y_{ij}}` are sampled from the policy model `π_θ_t`.
 - **Current Model Ranking (J):** Reward scores `r_{ij} = log π_θ_t(y_{ij}|x_j) - log π_θ_0(y_{ij}|x_j)` are computed (using `θ_0` as the `π_ref` model) and used to rank responses, yielding ranks `J_{ij}`.
 - **Previous Model Ranking (K):** Reward scores `r'_{ij} = log π_θ_{t-1}(y_{ij}|x_j) - log π_θ_0(y_{ij}|x_j)` are computed using the *previous iteration's model* (`θ_{t-1}`) to rank the *same* generated responses, yielding ranks `K_{ij}`. (Special case for `t=1`: `θ_0` is used for `K`).
 - **Consistency Estimation:** The Kendall's Tau coefficient `τ_j` (Equation 3.10) is calculated between the two rank lists (`J` and `K`) for each prompt `x_j`. Kendall's Tau measures the ordinal association between two rankings.
 - **Adaptive Consistency Regularization:** The overall consistency rate `C` for the current iteration is computed as the average of `(τ_j + 1) / 2` across all prompts. This `C` value becomes `C_λ`.
 - **Preference Dataset Composition:** The "best" (`y^+_j`) and "worst" (`y^-_j`) responses are selected based on the current model's ranking (`J`). This forms the `D_DPO` dataset `{(x_j, y^+_j, y^-_j)}`. A "reversed" dataset `D_RDPO` `{(x_j, y^-_j, y^+_j)}` is also prepared.
 - **Model Update:** The model `θ_{t+1}` is updated by minimizing the combined loss: `C * L_DPO(π_θ_t, D_DPO) + (1 - C) * L_DPO(π_θ_t, D_RDPO)`. This is the practical implementation of the soft-labeled DPO.

This methodology intrinsically integrates consistency as a means to self-regulate the learning process, making it more robust against internal biases without external supervision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of CREAM demonstrates its superiority over standard SRLM approaches, particularly for smaller LLMs. The main findings are derived from experiments on 7B-parameter Llama-2 and Llama-3 models across various natural language understanding benchmarks (ARC-Easy/Challenge, OpenBookQA, SIQA, GSM8K), and are supported by an in-depth analysis of rewarding consistency and accuracy.

#### 4.1. Overall Alignment Performance (Table 1 & Figure 3)

- **Standard SRLM Limitations:** For Llama-2 (a weaker foundation model), standard SRLM often shows performance degradation even after the first iteration (M2 vs M1), and a severe drop by M3. For Llama-3, while SRLM initially improves, it also degrades by M3, indicating bias accumulation and unstable self-improvement.
- **CREAM's Significant Improvement:** CREAM consistently achieves substantial performance gains over standard SRLM for both Llama-2 and Llama-3. For Llama-3, CREAM M3 outperforms SRLM M3 by a notable margin (83.37% vs 80.71% average accuracy), demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed regularization.
- **Comparison to Oracle:** The "Oracle" baseline (SRLM using an external, specialized reward model, InternLM2, sometimes enhanced with ground truth labels) represents an upper bound. For Llama-3, CREAM M3 achieves performance highly competitive with, and even slightly surpasses, Oracle M3 on average (83.37% vs 82.12%), a remarkable achievement given it uses *no external reward model*. This highlights CREAM's success in mitigating self-rewarding bias to near-oracle levels.
- **Consistent Iterative Improvement:** Unlike SRLM, which often degrades, CREAM shows consistent performance improvement across iterations (M1 to M2 to M3), particularly evident for Llama-3. This suggests the regularization effectively stabilizes the learning process and prevents reward hacking or bias accumulation. (Appendix C.1 further confirms this stability over 6 iterations).
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - "SRLM + KL" (simple KL divergence regularization) improves over baseline SRLM but is inferior to CREAM, indicating the specificity of CREAM's consistency regularization is more effective.
 - "CREAM w/o RC" (manually set consistency rate) performs worse than full CREAM, validating the benefit of dynamically calculated consistency via Kendall's Tau.
- **Alignment Arena (GPT-4o Judgments):** Win-rate comparisons show CREAM consistently beating SRLM and holding its own against Oracle, reinforcing its strong alignment performance from a qualitative perspective.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Rewarding (Table 2, Figure 4, Figure 5)

- **Rewarding Consistency:** SRLM exhibits low consistency in its reward rankings across iterations, confirming the presence of noise and unreliable preference data. In stark contrast, CREAM maintains significantly higher ranking consistency, which directly contributes to its stable performance.
- **Ranking Accuracy:** CREAM consistently achieves higher pairwise ranking accuracy on both self-generated preference data and external RewardBench datasets compared to SRLM. This indicates that while self-ranking is inherently challenging for smaller models, CREAM's approach yields more reliable preference signals for training.
- **DPO Rewarding vs. Prompt Rewarding:** The paper finds that the intrinsic DPO-based rewarding (used in CREAM) is more suitable and effective for 7B-level LLMs than traditional LLM-as-a-Judge prompting, which struggles with complex reasoning for reward assignment.

#### 4.3. Reliability of Self-Consistency (Table 3)

- Using an external Oracle reward model as the baseline for consistency estimation yields slightly better results (especially for Llama-2), but using the *last iteration's model* (`θ_{t-1}`) proves to be a reasonably reliable and practical proxy for consistency, especially for stronger foundational models like Llama-3. This is crucial for the self-rewarding paradigm where external models are not available.

#### 4.4. Consistency Measurement (Table 4)

- While other correlation metrics (Spearman, TopOrder) can also be used for consistency regularization, Kendall's Tau (used in CREAM) generally performs best, suggesting its robustness in capturing ranking agreement. This highlights the careful choice of consistency metric.

#### 4.5. Applicability (Appendix C.6)

- **Larger Models:** CREAM's effectiveness extends to Llama-2-13B models, demonstrating its generalizability beyond 7B parameters.
- **Unaligned Models:** While initial SFT is beneficial, CREAM can still effectively boost performance even when starting from pre-trained (non-chat-tuned) models, provided they undergo some initial alignment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CREAM framework represents a significant step forward in the field of Large Language Model alignment, particularly for the burgeoning area of self-rewarding LLMs. Its potential impact is multifaceted:

1. **Enabling Robust Self-Improvement for LLMs:** By directly addressing the critical issue of rewarding bias and overconfidence in self-generated preference data, CREAM allows LLMs to iteratively improve their alignment more stably and effectively. This mitigates the common problem of performance degradation after a few self-training iterations, making true "self-play" alignment more viable.
2. **Democratizing LLM Alignment:** CREAM's ability to achieve near-oracle performance (for Llama-3) without relying on expensive human annotations or powerful, often proprietary, external oracle reward models (like GPT-4) is a game-changer. This significantly lowers the barriers to entry for aligning LLMs, making advanced alignment techniques accessible to a wider range of researchers and organizations with limited computational resources, especially for broadly available 7B-parameter models.
3. **Enhancing Efficiency and Scalability:** The self-contained nature of CREAM, which only requires the latest two model checkpoints for consistency estimation, makes it computationally efficient compared to ensemble methods that require maintaining and evaluating multiple reward models. This translates to more scalable and efficient alignment pipelines.
4. **Theoretical Contributions and Future Research:** The generalized iterative preference fine-tuning framework provides a valuable theoretical foundation for understanding various iterative alignment methods. The insights gleaned from analyzing rewarding bias and the effectiveness of consistency regularization can inspire future research into more sophisticated self-correction mechanisms, uncertainty quantification, and adaptive learning rates in LLM training.
5. **Improved Model Reliability:** By training models to be less overconfident in ambiguous preference scenarios, CREAM is likely to lead to more robust and less brittle LLMs that are better aligned with nuanced human preferences, even when those preferences are difficult to discern.
6. **Practical Tool for the Community:** The public release of the code base ensures that researchers and practitioners can readily experiment with, reproduce, and build upon CREAM, accelerating advancements in open-source LLM alignment.

In summary, CREAM addresses a fundamental limitation in the self-rewarding paradigm, paving the way for more autonomous, efficient, and broadly accessible methods for aligning large language models with human values and intentions.
