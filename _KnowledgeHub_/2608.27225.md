# STEP: State-Aware Task Estimation and Planning with Multi-Modal LLMs for Human-Robot Collaboration

> [!summary] Summary
> STEP is a prompting pipeline that makes a multi-modal LLM assist a human assembler by first estimating the intended goal structure, then generating a structured JSON state of the workstation from an image, predicting remaining actions, and propagating future states. A goal-distance metric truncates and re-rolls plans. It raises action executability by 32.8% and cuts final-state error by 14.8% over an AntGPT-style baseline.

## Problem

- Multi-modal LLMs lack grounded interaction experience, so they do not track state transitions and hallucinate actions that drift away from the operator's intended goal.
- Prior state-tracking planners assume access to ground-truth initial state or an exploratory rule-learning phase, or treat state tracking separately from action planning entirely.
- Natural-language state descriptions are not expressive enough for a robot workspace, and 'verb, noun' action plans leave placement, orientation, and other execution parameters ambiguous.

## Method

- ==Task estimation== plus ==chain-of-thought== state representation generation: the model localizes each block in the image, then emits a JSON state with placement, orientation, top-face orientation, and relative position.
- ==Action prediction== from goal and action history only (image and state hurt this stage), followed by ==two-stage state propagation== that first narrates the change in language then re-serializes it as JSON.
- Rollouts: a hand-computed ==goal-distance metric== over the 5-block, 4-attribute state (range 0-20) truncates the plan at the lowest-distance state, which seeds the next rollout until distance reaches zero.

## Results

- On **495** teleoperated block-assembly instances across **8** target structures with GPT-4o, STEP beat the baseline by **32.8%** in action executability and **14.8%** in final-state error.
- Rollout ablation at **50%** completion: final error drops **4.40** to **4.05** over three rollouts while executability falls **88.98%** to **84.55%**, with plans growing **0.7** actions per rollout.
- Across GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, GPT-5.2 STEP wins on both metrics (GPT-5.1: **82.70%** vs **69.24%** executability, **3.71** vs **4.80** error); GPT-4o mini loses, with only **26.67%** task correctness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A structured, machine-readable state gives a quantitative goal distance, which turns open-ended language planning into a search that can be truncated and re-rolled toward convergence.
> - Modality is not uniformly helpful: adding image and state context degraded the action-prediction stage, so feed each pipeline stage only the inputs it needs.
> - Pipeline stages compound: correct goal estimation and error-free state estimation each measurably improve downstream plans, but the extra queries cost 8x runtime.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of human-robot collaboration (HRC), specifically focusing on improving robot autonomy in industrial settings where shared control and mutual understanding are essential. The increasing affordability and versatility of robots have expanded their applications, necessitating more sophisticated methods for robots to infer human intentions and assist with complex tasks.

A significant challenge in HRC is the inherent difficulty in training robots for tasks in human-centric environments due to the extensive data collection often required. Recent advancements in Multi-modal Large Language Models (MM-LLMs) have presented an alternative by leveraging their in-context learning capabilities for long-term action anticipation (LTA) from observations. Prior work has explored using MM-LLMs to infer human goals and generate high-level action plans in natural language, often based on sequences of human actions. Approaches like AntGPT and VidAssist utilize LLMs to predict overall goals and subsequent actions.

However, existing MM-LLM applications in robotics face several limitations. Firstly, MM-LLMs, due to their pre-training without real-world interaction, often lack a robust understanding of system states and state transitions. This deficiency can lead to "hallucinated actions" that do not converge to the intended goal. Some prior works (e.g., RAFA, LLM-State, Statler, Wall-E) have attempted to address this by explicitly querying LLMs to track system states, but these often rely on assumptions such as the availability of ground-truth initial states, the ability to comprehensively describe states in natural language, or focus solely on state tracking separate from action planning. Secondly, MM-LLMs typically generate high-level action plans (e.g., "verb, noun" pairs) that can be ambiguous in execution, necessitating predefined functions or continuous human-in-the-loop feedback.

STEP extends this prior research by relaxing some of these assumptions and addressing the limitations. It aims to integrate explicit state estimation and propagation directly into the task planning process, providing structured state representations and detailed assistance parameters. This work builds upon the foundation of shared autonomy and LTA using LLMs while seeking to overcome their noted deficiencies in state-awareness and action executability, particularly for structured industrial environments that are underrepresented in MM-LLM pre-training data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the effectiveness of human-robot collaboration in industrial settings by developing a method that enables robots to understand system states and plan actionable tasks more accurately. The paper proposes the State-aware Task Estimator and Planner (STEP) to achieve this.

The motivation for STEP arises from two key limitations identified in current MM-LLM-based approaches for robot task planning:

1. **Lack of System State Understanding and State Transition Tracking:** MM-LLMs, while capable of generating human-like text and interpreting multi-modal inputs, generally do not inherently maintain an internal model of the real-world system state or track how actions modify that state. This deficiency often leads to the generation of action plans that are logically inconsistent with the current environment or diverge from the intended goal, resulting in "hallucinated actions." For effective collaboration, a robot needs to anticipate the consequences of its actions and ensure progress towards a shared objective. Prior efforts to address this often require significant assumptions (e.g., ground-truth initial state access, simple natural language state descriptions) or treat state tracking as a separate module, limiting their real-world applicability.
2. **Ambiguity in Natural Language Action Plans:** MM-LLMs typically generate action plans in natural language, which, while intuitive for human understanding, can be high-level and ambiguous for robot execution. For example, a command like "place on table" lacks specific parameters such as the precise placement location, orientation, or collision avoidance information. This ambiguity necessitates additional mechanisms, such as predefined functions or iterative feedback, to translate high-level commands into low-level robot movements, which can be inefficient or prone to errors.

To address these motivations, STEP was developed with the following specific objectives:
- To prompt an MM-LLM to explicitly estimate the current state of a system and predict the state transitions that result from proposed actions.
- To forecast future states concurrently with action predictions, ensuring that generated plans are task-convergent and lead to the desired goal.
- To generate not only high-level actions but also additional assistance parameters necessary for the precise execution of these actions, thereby reducing ambiguity.
- To implement a mechanism (rollouts) that leverages structured state representations to quantitatively measure progress towards the goal, allowing for iterative refinement and truncation of action plans to ensure efficiency and correctness.
- To evaluate this approach in a structured industrial-like assembly scenario, demonstrating its applicability to human-robot collaboration tasks where human operators delegate planning to a robot.

By achieving these objectives, STEP aims to enable robots to take over task planning more reliably, reduce human workload, and facilitate seamless collaboration in scenarios such as machine assembly or modular fixture construction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

STEP employs a multi-staged pipeline that integrates multi-modal large language models (MM-LLMs) for state-aware task estimation and action planning in human-robot collaboration. The overall approach involves taking human operator actions and an image of the workstation as input, then iteratively estimating the task, current state, predicting future actions, and propagating future states.

The pipeline consists of the following components:

- **Input Data**: STEP takes as input the sequence of actions already performed by an operator (A1, A2..., Ak) and an image of the current workstation (Ik). A small curated set of in-context examples is also provided to the MM-LLM for guidance.

- **Task Estimation (§III-A)**:
 - This initial stage adopts a top-down approach to infer the operator's overall goal.
 - The MM-LLM is prompted with the observed actions (A1...Ak), the image (Ik), and in-context examples of (actions, <image>) -> Task.
 - A list of all possible tasks is provided to constrain the MM-LLM's output.
 - The output is the estimated overall task (T) the operator intends to complete.

- **State Representation Generation (§III-B)**:
 - Concurrently with task estimation, the MM-LLM is prompted with the workstation image (Ik) to generate a structured representation of the current environment state (S).
 - A chain-of-thought approach is used, where the model is queried multiple times to describe various aspects of the workspace. This includes identifying the locations of each block (e.g., top right, center left).
 - For each block, the model is queried for its placement location (e.g., on table, on block X), orientation (standing, lying face down, lying on side), top face orientation (parallel/perpendicular to camera), and specific relative location if placed on another block (e.g., left/right/center).
 - This detailed information is then combined and formatted by the MM-LLM into a JSON object, providing an explicit, structured state representation. An example structure for each block includes attributes like "location", "orientation", "top_face_orientation", and "specific_location".

- **Action Prediction (§III-C)**:
 - Using the estimated task (T) and the historical actions (A1...Ak), the MM-LLM predicts the future actions (Ak+1, Ak+2..., An) required to complete the task.
 - In-context examples of (performed actions -> future actions) relevant to the estimated task are included in the prompt.
 - During experiments, it was observed that including multi-modal data (state or image) in this stage degraded performance, so only textual inputs are used for action prediction.

- **State Propagation (§III-D)**:
 - This stage tracks changes to the environment state resulting from the execution of predicted actions.
 - The MM-LLM is prompted with the action history (A1...Ak), the current estimated state (S), and the next predicted action (Ak+1) to output the subsequent state (Sk+1) as a JSON object.
 - This process is iterative: the newly propagated state (Sk+1) becomes the "current state" for predicting Sk+2, and so on, until all predicted actions have corresponding future states (Sk+1...Sn).
 - A chain-of-thought approach is also applied here: the MM-LLM first describes state changes in natural language, then converts this description into a JSON representation.

- **Rollouts (§III-E)**:
 - To ensure progress towards the estimated goal and mitigate potential errors, STEP employs an iterative rollout mechanism.
 - A quantitative distance metric (dT) is calculated between any given structured state representation and the estimated task's completion state. dT is defined as the total number of differences in block characteristics, ranging from 0 to 20 for the 5-block, 4-characteristic representation.
 - For each rollout, the MM-LLM generates a sequence of future actions and states. The dT is calculated for all generated states.
 - The action and state sequences are then truncated at the first state (Sl) that exhibits the lowest dT. This state Sl is then set as the new current state for the subsequent rollout.
 - This iterative process, repeated for a maximum number of rollouts (a hyperparameter) or until dT=0, guides the action plan towards the inferred goal by ensuring that each iteration starts from a state closer to or at least not further from the goal.
 - After the final rollout, the predicted action sequence and the corresponding state sequence leading to the lowest dT are returned.

The OpenAI GPT-4o model was utilized for the main experiments, with 5 outputs sampled and the one with the maximum log-probability selected.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of STEP was conducted in a simulated environment using a robot assembly task, comparing its performance against a baseline inspired by AntGPT. The baseline involved task estimation followed by a single rollout of action prediction without explicit state information. The dataset comprised 495 instances of teleoperated block assembly sequences, where operators constructed one of eight different structures using five identical wooden blocks. The evaluation considered different task completion percentages (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%) to simulate varying levels of human delegation.

**Metrics Employed:**
- **Executability**: Percentage of predicted actions that passed all logical and execution constraints, with sequences truncated at the first failed action.
- **Final-state error**: The difference between the ground truth task completion state and the final state successfully achieved by executing the predicted actions.
- **Longest Common Subsequence (LCS)**: The length of the longest common subsequence of predicted and remaining recorded actions, normalized by the length of the longer sequence.
- **Task correctness**: Binary metric (0 or 1) indicating if the predicted task matched any possible ground-truth tasks at inference time.
- **Current state error**: Distance between the generated current state and the ground-truth state at inference.

**A. Comparison with Baseline (using GPT-4o)**

The results in Table I demonstrate the following:
- **Executability**: STEP consistently outperformed the baseline across all task completion percentages. For instance, at 50% task completion, STEP achieved 85.60% executability compared to the baseline's 71.68%. The average improvement in executability over the baseline was 32.8%.
- **Final-state error**: STEP resulted in a lower final-state error compared to the baseline at all task completion percentages, indicating that its predicted actions led to states closer to the intended goal. At 50% completion, STEP had a final error of 4.11, while the baseline had 4.40. The average decrease in final-state error was 14.8%.
- **LCS**: The baseline method generally exhibited a higher LCS than STEP. This was attributed to STEP's rollout mechanism, which truncates action plans after identifying a state closest to the goal. Consequently, STEP's predicted action sequences were, on average, shorter by approximately 3 actions compared to the baseline. While a higher LCS might suggest more shared actions, the baseline's longer sequences included more non-executable or incorrect actions, leading to lower executability and higher final-state error.
- **Trend with Task Completion**: Both methods showed improved final error with increasing task completion, as the starting state was closer to the goal. Task correctness initially declined with longer input action sequences (more noise/occlusion) but then increased as the target structure became more discernible. Current state error increased as tasks progressed, possibly due to increased structural complexity and block proximity in images.

**B. Ablations**

- **Number of Rollouts (Table II, 50% task completion)**:
 - Increasing the number of rollouts led to a decrease in final error (from 4.40 for 1 rollout to 4.05 for 3 rollouts), indicating that additional rollouts effectively guide the plan closer to the goal.
 - LCS increased with more rollouts (from 31.06% to 38.82%), correlating with the average increase of 0.7 actions per rollout.
 - Executability slightly decreased with more rollouts (from 88.98% to 84.55%), which could be attributed to the longer action sequences providing more opportunities for execution failures. However, the executing actions were more relevant to the goal.

- **Comparison of MM-LLMs (Table III, 50% task completion, 45 instances)**:
 - STEP generally outperformed the baseline in executability and final error across most tested OpenAI models (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, GPT-5.2), demonstrating its robustness.
 - For GPT-4o mini, the baseline showed slightly better executability, but GPT-4o mini exhibited a notably low task correctness score, suggesting that errors in the initial task estimation stage propagated throughout the pipeline for both methods. This indicates the importance of the MM-LLM's capability in the initial task estimation.

- **Impact of Task and State Estimation Correctness (Tables IV and V, 50% task completion)**:
 - **Task correctness (Table IV)**: When the task prediction was correct, executability improved (84.63% vs 84.37%) and final error decreased (3.64 vs 4.9).
 - **Current state error (Table V)**: When the current state estimation had no errors, executability improved (86.38% vs 84.41%) and final error decreased (3.93 vs 4.06).
 - These ablations indicate that improvements in earlier stages (task and state estimation) reliably lead to better performance in downstream action prediction and state propagation, highlighting the modularity of the STEP pipeline.

**Summary of Findings:**
STEP demonstrated superior performance over the baseline in generating executable and goal-convergent robot action plans, primarily due to its explicit modeling and propagation of structured environment states. While it sometimes produced shorter action sequences, these sequences were more efficient and effective in achieving the desired goal state. The modular design of STEP implies that further enhancements to its individual stages could yield additional performance gains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The STEP framework addresses key limitations in applying Multi-modal Large Language Models (MM-LLMs) to robotic task planning, particularly within human-robot collaboration scenarios in industrial settings. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy in HRC**: By enabling robots to explicitly estimate system states, predict state transitions, and forecast future states, STEP improves the robot's ability to act as an intelligent assistant. This allows human operators to delegate planning responsibilities more effectively, potentially reducing their cognitive load and enabling them to supervise multiple robots or focus on higher-level, creative tasks.
- **Increased Action Executability and Goal Convergence**: The research demonstrates a notable improvement in action executability (32.8% over baseline) and a reduction in final-state error (14.8% over baseline). This indicates that STEP generates more reliable and task-convergent action plans, which is critical for practical deployment in industrial environments where precision and success rate are paramount.
- **Structured State Representation for Ambiguity Reduction**: STEP's use of a structured JSON state representation allows for a more detailed understanding of the environment and the prediction of necessary assistance parameters beyond simple "verb, noun" pairs. This addresses the ambiguity inherent in high-level natural language commands, enabling robots to execute actions more precisely (e.g., specific placement locations, orientations).
- **Applicability to Data-Scarce, Structured Environments**: The approach is designed for industrial settings, which are often structured but lack extensive pre-training data relevant to MM-LLMs. Leveraging in-context learning with structured state tracking provides a mechanism to adapt MM-LLMs to these unique challenges.
- **Modular Pipeline Design**: The pipeline's modularity, with distinct stages for task estimation, state generation, action prediction, and state propagation, allows for independent development and improvement of each component. The ablation studies confirm that improvements in earlier stages (e.g., task correctness, current state error) reliably lead to better overall performance, facilitating future research and development.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its contributions, the paper identifies areas for further development:
- **Scenario Generalization**: The current implementation of STEP is specific to the block assembly scenario. Generalizing it to other industrial tasks would require modifications to prompts, state representation structures, and function implementations. Future research could explore methods for MM-LLMs to automatically adapt or "craft" these components for new scenarios, reducing the initial investment required.
- **Real-time Performance**: STEP's current runtime (mean of 148.07 seconds) is significantly slower than the baseline (mean of 18.24 seconds) due to multiple MM-LLM queries. This latency is a barrier to real-time industrial adoption. Future work will need to focus on optimizing response times, potentially through local hosting of MM-LLMs or fine-tuning smaller models for specific tasks.

Overall, STEP contributes to the ongoing effort to bridge the gap between high-level language understanding and low-level robot control by providing a more state-aware and contextually grounded planning framework. Its findings encourage continued exploration into the capabilities of MM-LLMs for robust, long-horizon reasoning in complex human-robot collaborative tasks.
