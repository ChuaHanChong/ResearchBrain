# VABench: Measuring Embodied Spatial Intelligence through Visual Demonstrations, Active Perception, and Metric Control

> [!summary] Summary
> VA-Bench is a RoboTwin physics-simulated benchmark testing whether general-purpose MLLMs can close the observe-reason-act-revise loop: learn procedure from RGB-only demonstrations, actively choose camera viewpoints, issue metric Cartesian arm commands, and revise from execution feedback, with no privileged poses or learned action head. Across 14 task families and 12 models, the best macro-average success is only 53.93%, despite 100% target localization, and active viewpoint control beats passive multi-view input.

## Problem

- Spatial benchmarks like SpatialVLM, CV-Bench and ManipBench score verbal or fixed-view spatial answers, so correct relation descriptions never get tested for contact-level metric precision in executed manipulation.
- Interactive benchmarks such as Theory of Space, ESI-Bench and SpatialWorld let agents explore, but grade spatial beliefs or high-level decisions rather than metric end-effector control with execution feedback.
- MLLM manipulation benchmarks like EmbodiedBench, ST-BiBench, IMBench and ESPIRE supply object coordinates, ground-truth poses, motor primitives or depth lifting, so none tests joint viewpoint selection and metric arm control.

## Method

- **Joint view-arm action space**: the MLLM issues ==metric world-axis translations== (1-100 mm), gripper-local rotations and aperture commands, interleaved with ==gripper-centered camera viewpoints==, executed by a fixed ==model-agnostic inverse-kinematics layer==.
- **Demonstration-conditioned re-grounding**: the model inspects frames of an ==RGB-only expert video==, writes its own ==textual procedure summary==, then must re-instantiate it metrically in unseen seeds without poses or trajectories.
- **Task suite and diagnostics**: 14 RoboTwin families (11 single-arm, 3 dual-arm) with 280 expert-verified seeds, seven held-out transfer variants, a five-object composition track, and nine human-annotated trajectory criteria spanning perception, manipulation and error recovery.

## Results

- Best three-run macro-average success is **53.93%** (Qwen3.8-max), with Opus-5 **52.86%** and GPT-5.6-sol **51.55%**; dual-arm tops out at **22.22%**, while top models score **100%** target localization but **70-79%** spatial relations.
- Removing active camera control, even with five passive views each step, cuts success by **9.64-29.64** points across four models; Qwen3.8-max falls **57.50%** to **27.86%**, place_cube_in_bowl **85%** to **5%**.
- Held-out geometry drops GPT-5.6-sol **32.14** points (**80.00%** to **47.86%**) versus **10.00** for Qwen3.8-max; long-horizon five-object task reaches **61/100** placements but zero strict episode successes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Perception and semantic scores saturate while execution fails: failures accumulate downstream in metric grounding and online correction, so fixed-view spatial QA overstates embodied spatial competence.
> - Task-conditioned looking beats broader coverage: choosing where to look for the current subgoal outperforms more simultaneous views, especially for placement tasks requiring targeted height and clearance evidence.
> - The best actor is not the best teacher: explicit, constraint-rich procedure summaries transfer across models, while permissive summaries sufficient for their author can underspecify tasks for other readers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

VA-Bench addresses a gap between conventional visual-spatial reasoning evaluations and embodied robot-control benchmarks. Earlier spatial reasoning datasets, including CLEVR, GQA, SpatialVLM, CV-Bench, and related 3D reasoning benchmarks, primarily assess whether a model can identify qualitative or quantitative relationships in static images or supplied multi-view observations. These evaluations can measure whether a model recognizes that one object is above, behind, or near another, but they generally do not require the model to acquire missing evidence or convert its interpretation into physical action.

More recent interactive benchmarks, such as Theory of Space, ESI-Bench, and SpatialWorld, introduce active exploration. In those settings, models must gather observations in partially observed environments and update spatial beliefs or make task decisions. However, the resulting outputs are often beliefs, answers, or high-level decisions rather than direct metric manipulation commands.

A separate line of research evaluates embodied manipulation using environments such as Meta-World, RLBench, CALVIN, LIBERO, ManiSkill, RoboCasa, and BEHAVIOR-1K. These benchmarks measure robot performance across grasping, placement, household activities, and long-horizon tasks. Many, however, evaluate learned visuomotor policies or specialized action-generation systems. Other MLLM-oriented benchmarks, including EmbodiedBench, ST-BiBench, IMBench, and ESPIRE, move closer to executable manipulation but may provide object states, 3D coordinates, depth-based lifting, parameterized motor primitives, or other structured information.

VA-Bench is positioned at the intersection of these areas. It requires a general-purpose multimodal large language model (MLLM) to interpret an RGB demonstration, decide which views to acquire, infer the relationship between the robot and the object, generate metric Cartesian movements, and revise actions in response to execution feedback. The benchmark therefore evaluates a complete view–reason–act loop rather than isolating perception, spatial question answering, or learned policy execution.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Central Research Question

The paper investigates whether general-purpose MLLMs possess sufficient embodied spatial intelligence to operate under incomplete visual observation and produce reliable physical manipulation. The authors argue that spatial intelligence should not be defined solely as the ability to describe object locations. In manipulation, a model must determine what information is missing, actively obtain it, express the scene in a consistent spatial frame, and use that representation to control an end effector.

The central question is whether models can close this loop independently when they are not given privileged object poses, precomputed waypoints, oracle trajectories, or a learned robot action head.

#### 2.2. Motivation From Manipulation Requirements

A single image may show an object but fail to reveal its depth, height, orientation, graspable region, or relation to the robot’s gripper. Pixel displacement does not directly specify a world-frame movement. For example, a model may correctly identify a bottle but still fail because it approaches from an unsuitable orientation, closes the gripper at the wrong height, or releases a cube outside the tolerance of a bowl.

The benchmark is motivated by several related requirements:

- **Active evidence acquisition:** The model must choose views that resolve ambiguity rather than relying on a fixed observation bundle.
- **Metric grounding:** Qualitative relations must be translated into signed world-axis translations and local rotations with numerical magnitudes.
- **Contact-level reasoning:** Successful interaction depends on grasp location, approach direction, orientation, clearance, and release height.
- **Feedback-based revision:** The model must interpret planner outcomes and physical consequences, then modify its subsequent actions.
- **Demonstration-conditioned re-grounding:** A demonstration supplies procedural information but not the metric solution for a new scene.
- **Compositional control:** Atomic manipulation skills must be combined in dual-arm and long-horizon settings.

The authors also aim to distinguish different failure points. A model may localize the target and understand the manipulation semantics but still fail at spatial calibration, pre-contact positioning, or recovery after an error.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Benchmark Structure

VA-Bench is implemented in the physics-based RoboTwin simulator. Its base suite contains **14 task families**, including **11 single-arm** and **three dual-arm** tasks. The tasks cover:

1. Direct grasp grounding, such as grasping bottles, cubes, and pens.
2. Placement and stacking, including placing objects in bowls, on cubes, or in upright configurations.
3. Functional contact and tool use, including pressing a bell and hammering a block.
4. Bimanual coordination, including shoe placement, handover, and pot lifting.

Each task family contains 20 deterministic, physically verified scene seeds. Every retained instance was confirmed to be solvable through a complete expert execution. The primary evaluation uses the same seeds across three independent runs for each model condition. Terminal success is computed by task-specific physics-based predicates rather than by human judgment.

The benchmark also includes seven held-out geometry or layout variants and a separate five-object long-horizon basket task.

#### 3.2. Model Inputs and Privilege Boundary

At test time, the MLLM receives:

- The task instruction.
- A textual summary derived from an RGB-only demonstration.
- The current clean RGB image.
- Filtered robot proprioception, including end-effector pose, gripper state, finger midpoint, and gripper-local axes in world coordinates.
- Interaction history and previous action-validity or trajectory-planning feedback.

The model does not receive object coordinates or poses, depth, segmentation masks, target markers, keypoints, contact state, task waypoints, camera calibration, or oracle trajectories. The demonstration shows a successful procedure but does not provide machine-readable actions, robot poses, contact labels, or a metric solution for the current seed.

Before the main episode, the evaluated model inspects multiple demonstration frames and writes a structured summary describing visible constraints such as grasp region, approach direction, arm roles, coordination order, and completion conditions.

#### 3.3. Active Observation and Robot Action

The model can issue camera actions and robot actions through a shared interaction budget. Camera actions select among bounded semantic viewpoints, such as top-down, side, front-oblique, side-top, and oblique views. It can also request local camera translations, zoom, yaw, and pitch adjustments. These actions change only the observation pose and do not alter the physical scene.

Robot actions are explicitly metric. The model specifies:

- World-axis translations of 1–100 millimeters.
- Gripper-local rotations of 1–90 degrees.
- Gripper aperture commands.
- Synchronized translation or aperture commands for dual-arm tasks.

A fixed, model-agnostic inverse-kinematics and trajectory layer executes the requested targets. It checks reachability and generates trajectories but does not select the object, grasp point, arm role, waypoint, or correction. This design makes the MLLM responsible for converting visual evidence into numerical actions.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics and Diagnostics

Terminal task success is the primary metric. Success requires the task-specific physical predicate to be satisfied within the action budget. The base score macro-averages success across task families, assigning each family equal weight.

The authors additionally annotate one complete base-suite run for each condition using nine trajectory-level behavioral diagnostics:

- **Spatial perception and understanding:** target localization, active exploration, and robot–object spatial relations.
- **Robot manipulation:** manipulation semantics, manipulation planning, and fine-grained pre-contact analysis.
- **Error recovery:** error detection, online correction, and post-failure adjustment.

Three annotators independently assess each episode, and majority voting determines the final labels. Agreement is reported as high overall, with 95.69% unanimity and Fleiss’ kappa of 0.942. Subtask completion is also measured to capture partial progress in multi-stage tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Base-Task Performance

The strongest three model conditions have similar performance. Qwen3.8-max achieves a three-run 14-task macro-average of **53.93 ± 3.17%**, followed by Opus-5 at **52.86 ± 2.79%** and GPT-5.6-sol at **51.55 ± 4.31%**. Given the three-run evaluation, the authors do not claim a reliable ordering among these models.

Single-arm tasks are substantially easier than dual-arm tasks. Qwen3.8-max reaches **65.61%** on single-arm tasks but only **11.11%** on dual-arm tasks. GPT-5.6-sol has the strongest dual-arm average at **22.22%**. Several weaker conditions achieve near-zero performance on dual-arm tasks, and no model completes the handover task in the reported base results.

The gap between spatial diagnostics and terminal success is a central result. In the annotated run, the best model achieves **100% target localization** and **100% manipulation-semantics understanding**, but only **78.9% robot–object spatial relations** and **73.9% fine-grained pre-contact analysis**. Thus, recognizing the correct object and choosing semantically appropriate actions does not guarantee sufficient metric accuracy for physical completion.

Error recovery is weaker than error detection. Qwen3.8-max detects errors in 73.6% of applicable episodes but performs online correction in only 46.7%. Opus-5 detects errors in 69.9% but corrects them online in 32.4%. These results indicate that models can sometimes recognize that an action failed without successfully revising the geometric estimate or action strategy.

#### 4.2. Active Versus Passive Observation

A matched comparison evaluates active camera control against a passive protocol that supplies five predefined views at every observation point. The passive condition provides broader fixed coverage but removes the model’s ability to select or refine views.

Active control improves success for all four tested conditions. For Qwen3.8-max, performance decreases from **57.50% active success** to **27.86% passive success**, a reduction of 29.64 percentage points. GPT-5.6-sol declines by 22.14 points, Opus-5 by 23.93 points, and Doubao-2.1 by 9.64 points.

The effect is particularly pronounced for placement tasks. For example, Qwen3.8-max’s success on *place_cube_in_bowl* falls from 85% with active observation to 5% with passive views. The authors suggest that fixed coverage does not substitute for selecting evidence relevant to the current subgoal, although the experiment does not isolate whether the cause is view relevance, cross-view integration, or context complexity.

#### 4.3. Geometry Transfer and Long-Horizon Composition

Held-out geometry and layout variants reveal substantial degradation. Qwen3.8-max falls from 77.86% on matched base tasks to 67.86% on held-out variants, a 10-point reduction. GPT-5.6-sol falls from 80.00% to 47.86%, a 32.14-point reduction. These results show that strong performance on familiar task configurations does not necessarily imply robust re-grounding when object geometry, containers, or layouts change.

The five-object long-horizon task is more difficult still. Qwen3.8-max completes 61 of 100 possible object placements, and GPT-5.6-sol completes 53, but neither model completes a strict long-horizon episode. Partial atomic progress therefore does not reliably compose into complete inventory-conditioned behavior.

#### 4.4. Demonstration-Summary Effects

The source and style of the demonstration-derived summary affect performance. A Qwen3.7-plus model using the Qwen3.8-max summary performs worse than with its own summary. In a three-task cross-model comparison, the Qwen3.8-max summary reduces the average performance of the other four readers by 31.25 points, whereas the Opus-5 summary improves them by 7.50 points on average.

The paper attributes this pattern cautiously to differences in explicitness. The Qwen3.8-max summary states fewer constraints and leaves more details implicit, which may be adequate for its author but less transferable to other models. More explicit summaries from GPT-5.6-sol and Opus-5 appear easier for other models to use, although the comparison is limited to three tasks and one run.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

VA-Bench provides a structured way to evaluate embodied spatial intelligence as an integrated capability rather than as isolated visual recognition or language reasoning. Its main significance lies in exposing failure modes that conventional spatial benchmarks may miss. A model can correctly identify a target and describe a demonstrated procedure while remaining unable to estimate contact geometry, maintain a consistent world-frame representation, revise metric actions, or coordinate two arms.

The benchmark may support research in several areas. It can be used to compare MLLM prompting and reasoning strategies, study active perception, evaluate demonstration summarization, and develop methods for metric spatial grounding and recovery. Its controlled transfer and long-horizon tracks are also useful for distinguishing memorization of task procedures from re-grounding and composition.

The results should be interpreted within the benchmark’s limits. It is a physics simulation with controlled object and camera settings, and the held-out variants do not establish open-ended or real-world generalization. The active camera interface evaluates bounded viewpoint selection rather than a physically moving camera arm. The direct-control design also does not measure modular systems in which an MLLM produces high-level instructions for a separately trained vision-language-action policy. Finally, the trajectory diagnostics identify observable behavioral breakpoints but do not causally determine whether a failure originated in perception, planning, numerical grounding, or execution.

Within those boundaries, VA-Bench offers an evaluation framework for testing whether multimodal models can transform visual procedures and actively gathered evidence into reliable, physically grounded manipulation.
