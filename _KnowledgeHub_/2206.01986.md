# Delving into the Openness of CLIP

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Peking University developed a framework to quantify the "openness" of Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) models using novel extensibility and stability metrics. Their evaluation revealed that CLIP's performance degrades considerably with expanding vocabularies and is vulnerable to adversarial class names, primarily due to issues in its feature space representation.

## Problem

- Traditional vision models struggle to adapt to dynamically expanding recognition targets, being limited by fixed class sets during training.
- While CLIP was designed for open-vocabulary recognition, existing evaluations primarily assessed its performance on static, closed vocabularies, overlooking its behavior in dynamic scenarios.
- There was a lack of systematic methods to quantify and diagnose CLIP's actual "openness" and its robustness to new or distracting classes.

## Method

- Introduced a novel evaluation protocol featuring "==extensibility (Acc-E)==" and "==stability (Acc-S)==" metrics to measure dynamic vocabulary performance.
- Acc-E quantifies a model's ability to handle simultaneously scaling target classes and input images over incremental expansions, while Acc-S assesses prediction consistency for existing classes when new, ==non-target distractors== are introduced.
- Analyzed the underlying feature space properties, including ==margin==, ==inter-modal alignment==, and ==intra-modal uniformity==, to diagnose observed performance shifts.

## Results

- Evaluated CLIP-like models exhibited significant reductions in extensibility (Acc-E), with up to a **12.9%** absolute drop on CIFAR100 and a **15.3%** average drop in cross-dataset expansion.
- Prediction stability (Acc-S) decreased substantially, showing, for instance, a **13.4%** drop on CIFAR100 and varying degrees of vulnerability across different semantic groups.
- A small set of adversarially chosen non-target classes could cause severe accuracy drops, reaching **52.7%** on CIFAR10, highlighting security risks.
- Retrieval-enhanced Prompt Engineering (REPE) improved extensibility and stability by an average of **1.2%** by creating more expressive text representations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Performance degradation in CLIP models is rooted in a small margin between positive and negative image-text similarity scores, leading to low distinguishability among classes.
> - Poor intra-modal uniformity, especially within the text feature space, indicates that textual descriptions of different categories are not adequately separated.
> - CLIP models are susceptible to "adversarial non-target vocabularies," where abstract, confusing class names can severely destabilize predictions for existing categories.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of computer vision has pursued the development of intrinsically open mechanisms for visual recognition, aiming for models that can adapt to dynamically expanding recognition targets, including both input images and their associated classes. Traditional supervised classifiers are typically trained on a fixed set of classes, limiting their ability to handle novel categories that emerge in real-world applications such as medical diagnosis or e-commerce.

Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) and similar open-vocabulary learning paradigms represent a notable advancement in this direction. CLIP pre-trains a vision-language model on extensive image-text datasets, enabling it to learn semantic alignments between visual and textual information. During inference, CLIP re-frames image classification as an image-to-text matching task, where class names are converted into natural language descriptions (e.g., "a photo of a [CLASSNAME]"). This allows CLIP to perform zero-shot inference on novel classes by incorporating their textual descriptions into the vocabulary.

Despite CLIP's theoretical capability for open-vocabulary recognition, previous evaluations primarily assessed its performance on static, closed vocabularies derived from downstream datasets. This approach did not fully explore the model's behavior in dynamic scenarios where the vocabulary of recognizable classes expands. This work addresses this gap by proposing a novel evaluation framework that specifically investigates the "openness" of CLIP-like models, differentiating itself from other paradigms like Open Set Recognition (OSR), which focuses on identifying unknown classes, and Open World Recognition (OWR), which involves incremental retraining. The study also positions itself within the context of recent advancements in CLIP variants (e.g., SLIP, DeCLIP, CoOp) and prompt engineering techniques, aiming to understand their inherent limitations in truly open environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the disparity between the theoretical promise of CLIP for open-vocabulary visual recognition and the practical absence of comprehensive evaluation protocols for this specific capability. While CLIP is designed to handle an "open class set" or "open vocabulary" in a zero-shot manner, its real-world performance as the vocabulary scales remained largely unexplored by existing benchmarks, which typically focus on static, closed-set accuracy. This gap, the authors argue, leads to an overestimation of CLIP's actual openness.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Systematically Quantify Openness:** The primary objective is to move beyond static accuracy metrics and develop a systematic method to quantify the "openness" of CLIP-like models. This involves defining new metrics that reflect a model's performance under dynamic vocabulary expansion, which is characteristic of real-world open recognition tasks.
2. **Design a Novel Evaluation Protocol:** To achieve the above, the authors aim to introduce an evaluation protocol that assesses openness from an "incremental perspective." This protocol simulates the real-world scenario where new classes are progressively introduced into the recognition vocabulary.
3. **Define Extensibility and Stability Metrics:** Specifically, two new metrics are defined:
 - **Extensibility (Acc-E):** To measure a model's ability to handle novel visual concepts as the recognition target, including both classes and their associated input images, scales simultaneously. This captures the model's overall performance as its operational vocabulary grows.
 - **Stability (Acc-S):** To assess how consistently a model predicts for existing classes when new, non-target classes (acting as distractors) are introduced into the vocabulary. This metric is crucial for understanding prediction shifts and robustness in an expanding class environment.
4. **Dissect CLIP's Feature Space:** The research aims to investigate the underlying reasons for observed performance changes (e.g., accuracy drops, instability) by analyzing CLIP's feature space. This involves examining properties such as "representation alignment" (how well image and text features of the same concept align) and "intra-modal uniformity" (how uniformly distributed features are within the image and text modalities). The goal is to identify specific representational characteristics that contribute to or hinder open-vocabulary performance.
5. **Facilitate Future Research:** By providing a new evaluation framework, empirical evidence of limitations, and insights into the underlying causes, the authors intend for this work to serve as a foundation and catalyst for future research aimed at improving the openness, robustness, and security of CLIP-like models.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between CLIP's theoretical potential for open-vocabulary recognition and its practical limitations, which are not captured by conventional evaluation, and to provide diagnostic insights into the causes of these limitations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted approach to evaluate and analyze the openness of CLIP-like models.

#### 3.1. Open-Vocabulary Recognition Paradigm (CLIP Review)

The paper first reviews CLIP's mechanism, which formulates image classification as an image-to-text matching task. Given an image `x` and a target vocabulary `V(T)` of class names `w_i`, CLIP predicts the label by computing the cosine similarity between the image feature `f_I(x)` and the text features `f_T(t_i)` of class descriptions (e.g., "a photo of a `w_i`"). The class with the highest similarity score is chosen. Traditional evaluation measures `Acc(V(T))` on a fixed vocabulary `V(T)` and corresponding dataset `D(T)`.

#### 3.2. Quantifying Extensibility and Stability for Open World

To address the limitations of static evaluation, the authors propose a novel evaluation protocol:

- **Extensibility (Acc-E):** This metric quantifies a model's ability to handle new visual concepts during vocabulary expansion. It is defined as the averaged classification accuracy across `N` incremental expansions. To implement this, `N` disjoint target vocabularies `{V(T)_1, ..., V(T)_N}` are used. For each permutation `s` of these vocabularies, accuracy is calculated as vocabularies are progressively unioned. `Acc-E` averages these accuracies over 100 random permutations.
- **Stability (Acc-S):** This metric explores how stable a model's predictions are for existing classes when new, non-target classes are introduced. It measures conditional classification accuracy `Acc(V(T) | V(T) U V(NT))`, where `V(T)` is the target vocabulary, and `V(NT)` represents non-target vocabularies whose classes serve as distractors but do not have corresponding input images. `Acc-S` is defined as the averaged conditional accuracy across incremental additions of `M` non-target vocabularies, again averaged over 100 permutations. For general stability, `Acc-S` is further averaged over each vocabulary `V_i` considered as `V(T)`, and the rest `V!=i` as `V(NT)`.

#### 3.3. Experimental Settings for Extensibility and Stability

- **Models Evaluated:** CLIP (ResNet-101, ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16), SLIP (ViT-B/16), DeCLIP (ViT-B/32), Prompt Ensemble (PE) (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16), and CoOp (ViT-B/16).
- **Datasets:** CIFAR100 (20 vocabularies, 5 classes each) and ImageNet (Entity13 with 13 vocabularies/20 classes, Living17 with 17 vocabularies/4 classes). Vocabularies were constructed based on superclass-class hierarchical structures.
- **Dataset-level Extensibility (Appendix):** A more rigorous test involving vocabulary expansion across different datasets, such as CIFAR10, Caltech101, SUN397, ImageNet, Flowers102, OxfordPets, and StanfordCars.

#### 3.4. Adversarial Non-Target Vocabulary

To explore the lower bound of stability, the concept of an "adversarial non-target vocabulary" `V(ANT)` is introduced. This is defined as a small set (e.g., 3 classes) of non-target words (nouns from WordNet or ImageNet class names) that maximally reduce `Acc-S` for a given target vocabulary. This method aims to identify highly confusing distractors.

#### 3.5. Dissecting the Extensibility of CLIP (Feature Space Analysis)

The paper investigates the underlying causes of observed performance degradation by analyzing CLIP's representation space:

- **Margin Analysis:** The margin between positive (image-ground truth text) and negative (image-other text) similarity scores is analyzed. A small margin implies low distinguishability.
- **Inter-modal Alignment ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$):** Measures the expected Euclidean distance between features of positive image-text pairs. Lower values indicate better alignment.
- **Intra-modal Uniformity ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{uniform}}$):** Measures how uniformly image and text features are distributed on the hypersphere. It is the sum of `uniform-I` (image uniformity) and `uniform-T` (text uniformity). Lower values (more negative logarithm of expectation of pairwise exponential distance) indicate better uniformity, meaning features are well-separated.
- **Visualization:** Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) is used to visualize feature distributions.

#### 3.6. Retrieval-enhanced Prompt Engineering (REPE) (Appendix)

As a potential solution, the authors propose REPE, a zero-shot inference method. Instead of using a single generic prompt ("a photo of a [CLASSNAME]"), REPE customizes class descriptions by retrieving diverse, semantically rich captions from the pre-training corpus (CC12M) that are similar to the original prompt. These retrieved captions are then mean-pooled with the original prompt's embedding to create a more expressive and distinguishable text representation `f_REPE_T(t_i)`. This aims to improve semantic consistency and distinguishability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The investigation into the openness of CLIP-like models yielded several key findings:

#### 4.1. Overestimated Openness and Performance Deterioration

- **Extensibility (Acc-E) Drops:** All evaluated CLIP-like models exhibited a significant decline in accuracy as the vocabulary expanded. For example, CLIP (RN101) on CIFAR100 experienced a 12.9% absolute drop in `Acc-E` compared to `Acc-C` (accuracy on closed vocabularies). While ImageNet datasets showed smaller drops (average 2.7-3.6%), they still demonstrated a clear performance degradation.
- **Dataset-Level Expansion:** More rigorous evaluations involving vocabulary expansion across multiple distinct datasets revealed even more dramatic declines, with an average drop of 15.3% in `Acc-E`. This suggests that the issue is more pronounced when classes come from diverse semantic domains.

#### 4.2. Poor Prediction Stability

- **Stability (Acc-S) Drops:** The stability metric (`Acc-S`) also showed significant decreases (e.g., 13.4% on CIFAR100 for CLIP RN101). This indicates that the introduction of new classes, even as mere distractors, leads to misclassifications of images belonging to previously known categories.
- **Varying Stability across Categories:** The degree of stability varied among different class groups. For instance, "Medium-sized Mammals" on CIFAR100 showed a 21.08% performance drop due to interference from non-target vocabularies, whereas "Flowers" exhibited a minimal drop (0.53%). This suggests that stability is influenced by the inherent properties and naming variations of the image categories.

#### 4.3. Vulnerability to Adversarial Non-Target Vocabularies

- **Dramatic Accuracy Drops:** Introducing a small number (e.g., three) of adversarially chosen non-target class names into the vocabulary led to severe performance degradation. For example, on CIFAR10, adding "bitmap," "automobile insurance," and "equidae" caused a 52.7% absolute accuracy drop.
- **Abstract Concepts as Adversaries:** The adversarial words identified were often more abstract than typical visual concepts (e.g., "bitmap" vs. "flower"), suggesting that CLIP struggles with semantic modeling of higher-abstraction objects. This finding highlights security risks for CLIP-like models in open recognition tasks.

#### 4.4. Root Cause: Feature Space Properties

- **Small Margin and Overlapping Similarities:** Analysis of cosine similarities revealed that positive image-text pairs had low similarity scores (average 0.26 for positive pairs), and critically, the similarities between positive and negative pairs were very close. The margin distribution between positive and largest negative similarity was centered around zero, indicating low distinguishability between classes and a high risk of prediction reversals when competing classes appear.
- **Poor Intra-modal Uniformity (Text):** CLIP models (especially vanilla CLIP) exhibited poor intra-modal uniformity, particularly in the text feature space. This implies that the textual descriptions of different competing categories were not sufficiently distinguishable from each other. Models incorporating intra-modal contrastive learning during pre-training (e.g., SLIP, DeCLIP) showed better uniformity.
- **CoOp's Performance:** CoOp, which performs prompt tuning, achieved better inter-modal alignment and the lowest intra-modal uniformity loss on the text side, correlating with its better extensibility. Visualization showed CoOp's prompt optimization dispersing prompt features while aligning with image features.

#### 4.5. Impact of Model Architectures and Training

- Stronger vision backbones (ViT vs. ResNet), widespread supervision (DeCLIP), and more pre-training data (CLIP vs. SLIP) generally improved extensibility.
- Prompt ensemble methods at inference time also provided performance boosts.
- CoOp achieved the highest extensibility, but this method requires access to downstream training data, limiting its applicability to truly zero-shot open tasks.

#### 4.6. Efficacy of Retrieval-enhanced Prompt Engineering (REPE) (Appendix)

- REPE consistently improved extensibility and stability across datasets (average 1.2% boost).
- Analysis showed that REPE effectively reduced text uniformity loss (`uniform-T`) and improved inter-modal alignment (`align`). It also increased the median margin between positive and negative similarity scores, making predictions more robust.
- REPE was demonstrated to be lightweight, model-agnostic, and combinable with other fine-tuning methods like adapter-tuning for further performance gains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the understanding of CLIP-like models in open-vocabulary visual recognition, with several implications:

- **Refined Evaluation Standards:** The introduction of "extensibility" and "stability" metrics provides a more realistic and comprehensive framework for evaluating open-vocabulary models. This moves beyond static, closed-set accuracy, encouraging the development of models that are robust to dynamic vocabulary changes. This framework can guide future benchmark design and comparative studies.
- **Identified Critical Limitations:** The study systematically quantifies and exposes the substantial performance degradation and instability of current CLIP-like models when faced with expanding vocabularies and novel classes. This challenges the prevailing perception of CLIP as "truly open" and highlights that its practical deployment in dynamic, open-world scenarios requires further advancements.
- **Mechanistic Insights into Failures:** By dissecting the feature space, the research offers a fundamental explanation for the observed limitations. The finding that a small margin between positive and negative class descriptions, coupled with poor intra-modal uniformity of textual features, leads to confusion among competing text features provides diagnostic insights. This moves the discussion from *what* goes wrong to *why* it goes wrong.
- **Highlighted Security and Robustness Concerns:** The demonstration of vulnerability to adversarial non-target vocabularies underscores potential security risks. Models could be misled by strategically chosen class names, raising concerns for applications where reliability and robustness are paramount.
- **Guidance for Future Research and Development:** The findings provide clear directions for improving open-vocabulary models:
 - **Pre-training Strategies:** Future work should focus on enhancing the quality of pre-training data and incorporating specific supervision signals (e.g., emphasizing alignment and uniformity) to create more robust and distinguishable feature spaces.
 - **Zero-Shot Inference Methods:** The effectiveness of Retrieval-enhanced Prompt Engineering (REPE) suggests that optimizing text representations during inference, even without fine-tuning, can significantly boost performance. This paves the way for developing more sophisticated prompt engineering techniques.
 - **Theoretical Foundations:** The paper calls for further investigation into the theoretical risks of open tasks and the development of evolving benchmarks that mirror real-world complexities.
- **Practical Implications:** The report serves as a cautionary note for practitioners relying on current CLIP models for critical open-world applications. It suggests that while CLIP offers impressive zero-shot capabilities, its inherent limitations regarding vocabulary expansion and stability must be addressed before widespread, unmitigated deployment in highly dynamic environments.

In essence, this research provides a rigorous assessment of CLIP's openness, revealing its current shortcomings and offering a valuable roadmap for developing the next generation of truly open and robust vision-language models.
