# Automatic Chain of Thought Prompting in Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Auto-CoT automates the creation of effective Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompts by leveraging question clustering and simple heuristics for demonstration sampling. This approach achieves performance comparable to or better than human-designed CoT examples across various reasoning tasks, eliminating the need for manual effort while outperforming Zero-Shot CoT.

## Problem

- Manual Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting requires substantial human effort to design task-specific examples, making it time-consuming and unscalable.
- Zero-Shot CoT prompting, while easy to implement, often produces suboptimal results compared to human-designed examples.
- Existing CoT methods faced a trade-off between human effort (Manual-CoT) and performance (Zero-Shot-CoT).

## Method

- **Auto-CoT** automates CoT prompt creation through a two-stage process: ==question clustering== and ==demonstration sampling==.
- It clusters questions using ==Sentence-BERT== and ==k-means== to ensure diversity in selected demonstration questions.
- For each cluster, representative questions are selected and processed using ==Zero-Shot-CoT== ("Let's think step by step") to generate reasoning chains, with simple ==heuristics== (e.g., question length under 60 tokens, fewer reasoning steps) filtering for quality.

## Results

- Auto-CoT consistently matches or exceeds the performance of Manual-CoT and significantly outperforms Zero-Shot-CoT across **ten diverse reasoning datasets**, including arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic tasks.
- The method demonstrates robustness to incorrect reasoning chains, maintaining higher accuracy even when **50%** of the automatically generated demonstrations contain errors.
- Auto-CoT performs effectively in a streaming data setting with new question batches, showcasing its practical applicability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diverse demonstrations are more effective than demonstrations similar to the test question, counterintuitively, as similarity can lead to a "misleading by similarity" phenomenon.
> - Auto-CoT's diverse sampling strategy, based on clustering, provides robustness against individual incorrect reasoning chains generated by the LLM.
> - Simple heuristics, such as selecting shorter questions and favoring shorter reasoning chains, significantly improve the quality of automatically generated demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in various tasks, but their performance on complex reasoning problems remains challenging. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting has emerged as an effective technique to improve reasoning in LLMs by encouraging them to generate intermediate steps before producing a final answer. However, existing CoT methods have significant limitations: they either require substantial human effort to design task-specific examples (Manual-CoT) or use simple prompts that produce suboptimal results (Zero-Shot-CoT).

This paper introduces Auto-CoT, a novel approach that automates the creation of effective CoT prompts by leveraging the strengths of LLMs while mitigating their weaknesses. Auto-CoT eliminates the need for human-designed examples while achieving performance comparable to or better than Manual-CoT across a range of reasoning tasks.

### 2. Background and Related Work

Chain-of-Thought prompting emerged as a technique to enhance reasoning in LLMs through the generation of intermediate reasoning steps. Two primary approaches have been established:

1. **Manual-CoT**: Requires human experts to craft high-quality examples with reasoning steps for each specific task. While effective, this approach is time-consuming and doesn't scale well.

2. **Zero-Shot-CoT**: Uses the simple prompt "Let's think step by step" to encourage reasoning without examples. While easy to implement, it often performs worse than Manual-CoT.

Related work in this area includes In-Context Learning (ICL), which shows that the performance of LLMs heavily depends on the quality and selection of demonstrations. Other methods like similarity-based retrieval for prompt construction and advanced problem decomposition techniques have also been explored, but they generally rely on manually created demonstrations.

### 3. The Auto-CoT Method

Auto-CoT automates the creation of effective CoT prompts through a two-stage process:

1. **Question Clustering**: Questions are encoded using Sentence-BERT and grouped into k clusters using k-means clustering. This ensures diversity in the selected demonstration questions.

2. **Demonstration Sampling**: For each cluster, representative questions are selected and processed using Zero-Shot-CoT to generate reasoning chains. Simple heuristics filter the demonstrations to favor shorter questions (less than 60 tokens) and reasonable reasoning length.

The resulting demonstrations are then used for in-context learning, where they are concatenated with a test question and the "Let's think step by step" prompt to generate reasoning and answers for new problems.

### 4. Key Components of Auto-CoT

#### 4.1. Question Diversity through Clustering

The clustering approach is crucial for creating diverse demonstrations. The paper visualizes the distribution of questions across various datasets, showing clear clustering patterns:

This diversity is essential because it helps mitigate the effect of incorrect reasoning chains. By selecting questions from different clusters, the system ensures that errors in one cluster don't propagate to similar questions in other clusters.

#### 4.2. Simple Heuristics for Quality Control

Auto-CoT employs simple but effective heuristics to improve the quality of generated demonstrations:

- Selecting questions shorter than 60 tokens
- Favoring reasoning chains with fewer steps
- Ensuring balanced representation from different clusters

These heuristics significantly improve performance across benchmark datasets.

### 5. Experimental Results

Auto-CoT was evaluated on ten diverse reasoning datasets, including arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks. The results show that Auto-CoT consistently matches or exceeds the performance of Manual-CoT while significantly outperforming Zero-Shot-CoT:

The method demonstrates effectiveness across various LLMs, including when using a different model (Codex) for generating demonstrations than the one used for testing (GPT-3).

### 6. Impact of Diversity in Demonstrations

The paper highlights the crucial role of diversity in mitigating the negative effects of incorrect reasoning. Two alternative methods are compared:

1. **Retrieval-Q-CoT**: Selects questions similar to the test question
2. **Random-Q-CoT**: Randomly selects questions without considering diversity

The results show that Retrieval-Q-CoT performs significantly worse than Random-Q-CoT:

This counter-intuitive finding is explained by the "misleading by similarity" phenomenon: when a demonstration contains incorrect reasoning for a question similar to the test question, the model tends to replicate those errors.

### 7. Robustness to Incorrect Reasoning

One of Auto-CoT's key strengths is its robustness to incorrect demonstrations. The paper investigates how performance degrades as the percentage of wrong demonstrations increases:

Auto-CoT maintains higher accuracy even when 50% of the demonstrations contain incorrect reasoning, highlighting the effectiveness of the diverse sampling approach.

### 8. Performance in Real-World Scenarios

The paper also evaluates Auto-CoT in a streaming setting, where data arrives in batches:

In this setting, Auto-CoT consistently performs well, often matching or exceeding Manual-CoT while maintaining a significant advantage over Zero-Shot-CoT.

This demonstrates the practical applicability of Auto-CoT in real-world scenarios where new types of questions continually emerge.

### 9. Conclusion

Auto-CoT represents a significant advancement in making complex reasoning with LLMs more accessible and effective. By automating the creation of CoT prompts through question clustering and filtered demonstration sampling, it achieves performance comparable to human-designed demonstrations while eliminating the need for manual effort.

The key insights from this work include:

1. **Diversity over similarity**: Counterintuitively, diverse demonstrations are more effective than demonstrations similar to the test question.

2. **Robustness through diversity**: Auto-CoT's clustering approach provides robustness against incorrect reasoning chains.

3. **Simple heuristics matter**: Basic filtering techniques significantly improve the quality of automatically generated demonstrations.

4. **Practical applicability**: Auto-CoT performs well in realistic streaming settings, making it suitable for real-world applications.

These findings have important implications for making LLMs more accessible for complex reasoning tasks across various domains, from education and scientific research to code generation and decision support systems.
