# WorldContact: A Contact-Centric World Model for Scalable Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> WorldContact is a contact-centric Lagrangian neural simulator for deformable-object manipulation that learns vertex dynamics from limited calibrated simulation trajectories, then generates extra training data. It tokenizes spatial, topological, robot, and environment contacts per vertex, uses hierarchical token merging for global attention, and predicts over time steps 20x the source solver's. Rollouts run 10x faster, and expanded data lifts real-robot bag-lifting success from 65% to 95%.

## Problem

- Numerical deformable simulators need tiny integration steps to resolve rapid motion and prevent interpenetration, making generation of policy-training trajectories slow and expensive to scale.
- Video and latent world models such as IRASim or Interactive World Simulator lack explicit material-point correspondences, so their dynamics cannot be checked for vertex error or penetration.
- Existing particle-based neural dynamics struggle with the continually changing contacts of deformables, between folds, support surfaces, and grippers, causing lost grasps and implausible deformation.

## Method

- ==Contact-centric vertex tokenizer==: learned ==compact-support kernels== aggregate neighbor geometry and motion across four contact types, ==spatial==, ==topological (UV rest-space)==, ==controllable (robot and held objects)==, and ==environmental==, into each vertex token.
- ==Hierarchical token encoder== and ==global-token decoder==, adapted from ==WorldParticle==: ==self-attention== with ==3D RoPE== plus ==bipartite cosine-similarity merging== halves tokens per level; vertex queries ==cross-attend== to global tokens to predict position and velocity deltas.
- ==Multi-step autoregressive training== with backprop through five-step rollouts at 20x the solver time step, then fine-tuning with a ==differentiable topology projection== (==Laplacian smoothing== plus ==edge-length constraints==) inside the loop.

## Results

- Against RoboCraft, RoboCook, and AdaptiGraph on identical splits, WorldContact cuts position MSE to **0.0002** from **0.0323-0.0598** and penetration rate to **0.35%** from **10-25%**.
- State rollouts on one H100 run **10x** faster than the source simulator, excluding rendering; hierarchical token compression cuts training step time **4-9%**, and dropping the contact tokenizer causes table and gripper penetration.
- Fine-tuning π0.5 on 150 WorldContact-expanded trajectories lifts real-robot single-attempt bag-lifting success to **19/20 (95%)**, versus **13/20 (65%)** with only the 25 source trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A learned simulator can outrun its own teacher by predicting state change over large intervals directly, trading small approximation error for an order-of-magnitude cheaper data generation.
> - Typing neighbors by contact kind, spatial, topological, robot, environment, is a stronger inductive bias than nearest-sample features alone; in ablation it separates stable grasps from penetration.
> - Separating explicit-state dynamics from rendering makes generated data verifiable, but evidence is one real task over 20 trials, so fidelity-versus-policy-gain scaling stays untested.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

WorldContact addresses a central problem in robot learning: acquiring sufficient interaction data for tasks involving deformable objects. Conventional approaches rely on real-world demonstrations or numerical physics simulation. Real-world data collection is expensive and potentially unsafe, while high-fidelity simulation can be computationally costly because small integration steps are needed to resolve rapid motion, collision, friction, and contact constraints.

The paper lies at the intersection of three research areas. The first is **learned world models for robotics**, in which a model predicts future states or observations conditioned on robot actions. Earlier world-model approaches, such as latent-space models and video-prediction systems, have been used for planning, policy optimization, and imagined experience generation. WorldContact differs by maintaining explicit object vertices and physical state variables rather than predicting only images or latent representations. This enables direct evaluation of position error, velocity error, and geometric penetration.

The second area is **neural simulation of deformable objects**. Prior methods, including particle-based graph networks, mesh-based simulators, and material-adaptive dynamics models, learn object motion from simulated or observed trajectories. WorldContact adopts a Lagrangian, vertex-based representation and extends it with explicit modeling of several contact types: interactions between object parts, topological neighbors, robot geometry, held objects, and environmental surfaces.

The third area is **simulation-to-real transfer and scalable demonstration generation**. Systems such as MimicGen and SoftMimicGen expand demonstrations by transforming or replaying existing trajectories, while deformable-object simulators provide varied synthetic experience. WorldContact instead learns a transition model from a limited set of calibrated, high-quality trajectories and uses that model to generate additional rollouts at a larger time step. The generated states are rendered into the observations required by a vision-language-action policy.

Thus, the paper’s primary contribution is not a new robot policy architecture. It is a learned data-generation mechanism intended to reduce the computational cost of producing training trajectories for deformable-object manipulation.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Main Objective

The main objective is to develop a contact-aware neural world model that can predict deformable-object dynamics under prescribed robot motion and environmental geometry. The model is intended to produce additional training trajectories from a relatively small source dataset.

The authors focus on shopping-bag manipulation because bags exhibit several difficult physical behaviors. Their geometry changes continuously, straps and bodies interact, the bag can collide with tables or other objects, and grasping forces are transmitted through deformable material. These properties make contact representation important for predicting whether an object remains attached to a gripper, penetrates a surface, or deforms plausibly during motion.

#### 2.2. Motivation for Contact-Centric Modeling

The paper argues that local contact geometry is a useful organizing principle for deformable dynamics. A vertex may be influenced by nearby vertices on the same object, nearby portions of another object, robot fingers, rigidly held tools, or a supporting surface. Treating these interactions explicitly should help the model distinguish, for example, between free motion and motion constrained by a gripper or table.

The model also needs to represent long-range dependencies. A force applied at one region of a bag can cause motion at vertices that are spatially distant but connected through the object’s topology. The authors therefore combine local contact features with hierarchical attention that communicates information across the entire object.

#### 2.3. Motivation for Larger Time Steps

The source numerical simulator uses a physical time step of 0.002 seconds to maintain numerical stability. WorldContact is trained to predict changes over 0.04 seconds, a 20-fold larger interval. This allows it to replace many small integration steps with one learned transition. The expected benefit is faster generation of complete trajectories, although this comes at the cost of approximating rather than explicitly solving the underlying physical dynamics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. State Representation

WorldContact divides the scene into a predicted object state and a prescribed state.

The predicted object state contains vertex positions, vertex velocities, object topology, and vertex attributes. Each vertex attribute includes an object identity and rest-state UV coordinates, which identify the vertex’s location in the object’s intrinsic topology.

The prescribed state includes robot commands, fixed robot and object geometry, and sampled environmental geometry. Robot commands consist of the six joint rotations and gripper opening for each of two arms. Environmental samples include positions, velocities, object or surface tags, and surface normals. Objects that are rigidly held by the robot are treated as part of the prescribed state, whereas released or freely moving objects belong to the predicted state.

The model predicts changes in position and velocity over the larger interval. These changes are added to the current state to obtain the next predicted state.

#### 3.2. Contact-Centric Vertex Tokenizer

For every predicted vertex, the tokenizer constructs four categories of local neighborhoods:

1. **Spatial contact:** Nearby vertices in Euclidean space across all predicted objects.
2. **Topological contact:** Vertices nearby in the rest-state UV topology of the same object.
3. **Controllable contact:** Nearby robot links, grippers, and rigidly held objects.
4. **Environmental contact:** Nearby samples from surfaces such as a table.

Learned compact-support kernels aggregate relative positions, velocities, and attributes from each neighborhood. The resulting features are combined with the current vertex position, velocity, and identity/topology attributes to produce a vertex token.

This design explicitly distinguishes geometric proximity from topological connectivity and from externally prescribed contact. That distinction is relevant for deformable objects, where two vertices may be close in space without being neighbors in the material surface, or may be linked topologically despite being far apart in space.

#### 3.3. Hierarchical Encoder and Decoder

The encoder first applies self-attention to vertex tokens and then repeatedly merges tokens using a bipartite matching procedure. The number of tokens is reduced by approximately half at each level, producing a smaller set of global tokens. These tokens summarize local geometry, motion, contact, and topology while allowing information to propagate across distant regions.

The decoder retains the original vertex-resolution tokens as queries. It applies attention among the global tokens and then cross-attention from vertex tokens to global tokens. An output multilayer perceptron predicts position and velocity corrections for every vertex.

The implementation contains approximately two million trainable parameters. It uses three encoder and three decoder attention levels, eight attention heads, and a 192-dimensional token width.

#### 3.4. Training and Projection

Training uses five-step autoregressive segments sampled from recorded trajectories. The loss averages squared position and velocity errors across all predicted vertices and rollout steps. Because the model is rolled out autoregressively during training, gradients pass through multiple prediction steps and can reduce accumulated error.

The authors further fine-tune the model with a differentiable topology projection. The unconstrained predicted positions are smoothed using four Laplacian iterations, followed by 32 edge-constraint iterations that keep each topology edge between 0.9 and 1.1 times its rest length. The projected state is both used for computing the loss and fed into the next prediction step. This is intended to improve long-horizon stability and reduce implausible edge distortion.

#### 3.5. Data Collection and Policy Integration

The training data come from a calibrated physical simulator used by SimWeaver. The authors reconstruct digital assets and calibrate parameters against real-world observations. The shopping bag contains 20,274 vertices; the robot gripper and table are represented by 1,114 and 16,900 sampled points, respectively.

An automated simulation agent configures tasks, samples initial states, varies interaction locations, speeds, and motion amplitudes, and executes the resulting controls. The dataset contains 16 shopping-bag manipulation and collision tasks, with 25 training trajectories per task and two validation and two test trajectories per task. Trajectories are rejected if they contain numerical failures, interpenetration, self-intersections, unintended adhesion, workspace violations, or camera-view failures.

For policy learning, predicted object states are rendered into the two-camera observation format consumed by a pretrained vision-language-action policy. Each rendered observation is paired with the action used to condition the world-model rollout. The policy is then fine-tuned on the resulting observation-action dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Dynamics Prediction

WorldContact substantially outperforms the three reported neural dynamics baselines on the aggregate test metrics. Its position MSE is **0.0002**, compared with 0.0598 for RoboCraft, 0.0575 for RoboCook, and 0.0323 for AdaptiGraph. Its velocity MSE is also **0.0002**, compared with 0.0106, 0.0105, and 0.0098, respectively.

The reported penetration rate is **0.35%** for WorldContact, versus 13.88% for RoboCraft, 10.28% for RoboCook, and 24.84% for AdaptiGraph. Qualitative examples indicate that the baselines sometimes lose bag-gripper contact during lifting or produce physically implausible deformation, whereas WorldContact more often preserves the intended interaction.

These comparisons should be interpreted within the paper’s evaluation setting: all methods use the same dataset split, and the data are generated from calibrated simulation with full vertex-state supervision.

#### 4.2. Computational Efficiency

On one NVIDIA H100 GPU, excluding rendering and disk input/output, WorldContact generates 0.4 seconds of simulated dynamics per second of wall-clock time. The source physical simulator generates 0.04 seconds per second. This corresponds to a reported **10-fold speedup** for state rollouts.

The larger learned time step is central to this result. WorldContact predicts a 0.04-second state transition directly, whereas the numerical simulator uses 20 smaller steps to cover the same interval.

Hierarchical token compression also reduces training cost. On a single H100, the reported optimization time for rollout lengths of 5, 10, and 15 steps decreases from 4.367, 11.236, and 15.873 seconds without compression to 4.184, 10.526, and 14.493 seconds with compression.

#### 4.3. Ablation Findings

Removing the contact-centric features produces unstable rollouts, including penetration into the table and gripper and failures in maintaining contact. The ablation supports the authors’ claim that explicit contact information is important for this task.

Replacing hierarchical attention with full-resolution attention increases training time. This indicates that token compression provides a computational benefit while retaining vertex-level predictions, although the paper does not report a separate accuracy comparison for this ablation.

#### 4.4. Real-Robot Policy Performance

The downstream evaluation uses 25 simulated bag-lifting trajectories as source data. The expanded dataset contains 150 trajectories generated with WorldContact. A pretrained Pi0 vision-language-action policy is fine-tuned using two RGB views and the instruction “right grasp and lift.”

On a real robot, the policy fine-tuned only on the 25 source trajectories succeeds in **13 of 20 trials**, or **65%**. The policy fine-tuned on the expanded dataset succeeds in **19 of 20 trials**, or **95%**. Success requires a single-attempt grasp, lifting the bag above the table, and holding it for two seconds without human intervention.

The result indicates that model-generated trajectories can improve policy adaptation in this task. However, the evaluation contains only 20 trials per condition, and no confidence intervals or statistical tests are reported.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

WorldContact provides a concrete approach for scaling deformable-object training data without requiring all additional trajectories to be generated by a slow numerical simulator or collected on a physical robot. Its explicit representation of contact, topology, and robot-conditioned geometry is particularly relevant to tasks where maintaining physical interaction is more important than producing visually plausible but physically inconsistent observations.

The real-robot result suggests that faster learned rollouts can have practical value for policy fine-tuning, not merely for prediction benchmarks. If the approach generalizes, it could support more efficient adaptation to new manipulation behaviors and reduce the cost of constructing task-specific datasets.

Several limitations remain. The model is trained on calibrated simulation data and evaluated mainly on shopping bags, so its ability to handle unseen materials, geometries, or tasks is not established. Autoregressive prediction errors may accumulate under distribution shift. Rendered observations may also differ from real camera observations. Finally, generated data inherit limitations in the source trajectory coverage and simulator calibration. The authors identify real-world training, broader object and material evaluation, and using policy failures to guide further data collection as important directions for future work.
