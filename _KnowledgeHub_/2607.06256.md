# Diagnosing Semantic Handoff Failures in Agent-Orchestrated Vision-Language-Action Skill Composition

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at SimpleAI developed an agent-orchestrated framework to diagnose failures in composing vision-language-action (VLA) skills for long-horizon household tasks, identifying that individual skill competence in isolation does not translate to robustness in chained execution due to the 'semantic handoff problem'. The system provides trace-backed diagnostics, attributing failures to specific issues like target grounding, control execution, and insufficient next-skill readiness.

## Problem

- VLA skills perform competently in isolation but often fail when composed sequentially for long-horizon tasks.
- The 'semantic handoff problem' occurs when a skill completes its local postcondition but leaves the environment in a state unsuitable for the next skill.
- Lack of systematic methods to diagnose why complex, chained robot tasks fail, moving beyond simply observing near-zero success rates.

## Method

- Developed an agent-orchestrated ==semantic execution harness== that employs a bounded ==plan-act-verify-replan loop== with multi-view VLM verification.
- Defined ==semantic skill contracts== that include explicit ==handoff-aware postconditions== (e.g., target within arm-reach, not just visible) and a step budget.
- Utilized **π0.5**-based VLA skill checkpoints and a multi-view VLM verifier (`gemini-2.5-flash`) to assess postconditions and readiness, generating replayable trace artifacts for diagnostics.

## Results

- Individual skills like `place_on` and `open_door` achieved `open_door``place_on``place_on`.`place_on`% success in isolated evaluations, confirming intrinsic competence.
- End-to-end task completion on BEHAVIOR-1K showed a mean progress score of **19.5%**, indicating near-zero overall task success despite individual skill strength.
- Ablation studies on the `move_to` skill's verifier showed that tightening handoff-aware postconditions (e.g., to 'within arm reach') led to increased re-navigation attempts and enabled the successful completion of a previously failed task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Individual VLA skills often possess high competence when evaluated from curated, ideal initial states, but this competence degrades significantly in compositional settings.
> - The 'semantic handoff problem' is a primary contributor to failures in long-horizon tasks, manifested as issues where skills successfully complete locally but fail to establish preconditions for subsequent skills.
> - Trace-backed diagnostics reveal specific, actionable failure modes such as target grounding errors (37 failures), control execution issues (58 failures), and next-skill readiness failures (35 failures), which are distinct from overall skill incompetence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of embodied AI and robotic manipulation, specifically addressing the challenge of enabling robots to perform complex, long-horizon tasks in household environments. The core problem is the composition of multiple vision-language-action (VLA) skills to achieve an overarching goal.

The broader research landscape for robot policy learning has seen significant advancements, moving from modular imitation learning systems like CLIPort and PerAct to large-scale generalist robot policies and VLA models such as Gato, RT-1, RT-2, Open X-Embodiment, Octo, Open-VLA, and π$_{0}$. These models leverage large datasets and advanced neural architectures to enable robots to understand language instructions and execute corresponding actions. This paper specifically utilizes a π$_{0.5}$-based VLA skill library.

Another relevant area is task and motion planning (TAMP), which traditionally combines symbolic task representations with continuous motion planning. More recent approaches, such as Code as Policies, MAESTRO, and TIGeR, enrich VLM agents with active perception, geometry, planning, and symbolic tools, aiming to provide more stable and inspectable semantics. This research departs from purely symbolic or hand-engineered planners by treating learned VLA skills as agent-callable tools, but it aims to provide a "missing runtime interface" for their robust composition.

Finally, the work relates to language-conditioned agents and semantic grounding, where systems like SayCan, Inner Monologue, and ReAct use language models to select, reason over, and call robot tools. Recent agentic robot systems further integrate structured workflows, multi-agent decomposition, and temporal verification. This paper focuses on addressing execution-time grounding, particularly at skill handoffs, where a step must advance only when the state is genuinely ready for the *next* skill, moving beyond mere visibility.

This work contributes to the integration and robust execution of learned VLA skills by introducing an agent-orchestrated framework for diagnosis, verification, and recovery, specifically addressing the "semantic handoff problem" that arises when chaining such skills.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to diagnose and understand the failures that occur when composing multiple language-conditioned vision-language-action (VLA) skills to complete long-horizon household tasks. This diagnostic framing aims to separate the intrinsic competence of individual skills from their robustness when chained together in complex sequences.

The motivation stems from the inherent challenge in executing long-horizon tasks. While individual VLA skills, such as navigating to an object or grasping it, might be well-trained and perform competently in isolation, their sequential composition often fails. The core issue, termed the "semantic handoff problem," is that the boundary between two skills is often implicit and underspecified. A skill might successfully achieve its local postcondition (e.g., "object is visible") but leave the robot, objects, or camera views in a state that is not suitable for the *next* skill in the sequence (e.g., "object is visible, but too far to grasp"). This results in a distribution shift: a skill, when called in an end-to-end rollout, starts from a "messy" chained terminal state of the previous skill, which differs significantly from the "clean" skill-boundary snapshots it was trained and benchmarked on.

Therefore, the authors are not primarily motivated by achieving state-of-the-art task success on benchmarks like BEHAVIOR-1K. Instead, their key objective is to build an agent harness that can systematically reveal *why* long-horizon rollouts fail. This harness is designed to turn near-zero task success rates into actionable diagnostics, identifying specific failure modes related to next-skill readiness, target grounding, or control execution from chained states. By pinpointing these issues, the research aims to inform what improvements are needed in VLA skill libraries, particularly concerning their robustness to the varied and often unpredictable initial conditions encountered during compositional execution.

The paper makes three specific contributions to address these objectives:
1. **Formulation of Semantic Skill Composition:** Defining long-horizon execution with VLA skills as semantic composition, where each skill call includes typed arguments, a budget, an expected postcondition, and an implicit "next-skill readiness" requirement. This formulation explicitly identifies semantic handoff failures where a skill appears complete but does not leave the state ready for the subsequent skill.
2. **Instantiation of a Semantic Execution Harness:** Developing a BEHAVIOR-1K-based harness that employs π$_{0.5}$-based skill checkpoints. This harness uses a bounded plan-act-verify-replan loop with multi-view VLM verification, handoff-aware postconditions, recovery mechanisms, and generates replayable trace artifacts for diagnostics.
3. **Trace-Backed Diagnostics:** Providing empirical evidence and analysis that demonstrates the gap between snapshot-state skill competence and chained-state robustness. The harness attributes failed skill attempts to specific causes (next-skill readiness, target grounding, control execution), offering actionable insights for improving VLA capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers around an agent-orchestrated semantic execution harness designed to execute and diagnose vision-language-action (VLA) skill compositions. This harness separates the overall system into an agent layer and an embodiment layer.

**A. System Architecture (Semantic Execution Harness)**
The *agent layer* is responsible for high-level control: selecting skill calls, maintaining task state, invoking verification, and triggering recovery mechanisms (retrying or replanning). The *embodiment layer* manages the low-level interactions: simulation environment, VLA inference, and primitive action execution. Figure 2 illustrates this architecture, showing offline training feeding into an online plan-act-verify-replan loop.

**B. Semantic Skill Contracts**
Each skill call is formalized as a "semantic skill contract," defined by:
- `name`: The type of skill (e.g., `move_to`, `pick_up_from`, `place_on`, `open_door`, `press`).
- `args`: Typed arguments binding specific task objects.
- `prompt`: The natural language instruction provided to the VLA policy.
- `B_s`: A maximum step budget for the skill, acknowledging that VLA policies lack a reliable stop signal and skill durations vary.
- `K_s`: The interval (in environment steps) at which verification is performed.
- `ϕ_t`: The expected natural language postcondition for the skill, which includes handoff-aware requirements. For instance, a `move_to(can)` postcondition is designed to require the target to be "close-up and at arm-reach distance," not merely visible.

**C. VLA Policy Training**
The underlying VLA policies are based on π$_{0.5}$ models, utilizing a PaliGemma 2B vision-language backbone combined with a 300M-parameter flow-matching action expert. This expert consumes three 224x224 RGB views (from a head camera and two wrist cameras) along with proprioceptive state to predict multi-step action chunks.
The training process uses cleaned BEHAVIOR-1K teleoperation demonstrations and involves two stages:
1. **Full-skill mid-training:** An initial finetuning of the base π$_{0.5}$ checkpoint on pooled skill data for 50k steps to establish shared visuomotor and language grounding.
2. **Skill-group post-training:** Specialization of separate checkpoints for different skill groups (e.g., navigation/orientation, switch/press, grasping, placement) from the mid-training initialization. Each skill type is then routed to its specific group's checkpoint during deployment.

**D. Step-Level Plan-Act-Verify-Replan Loop**
During online execution, for each skill:
1. **Act:** The harness executes a VLA action chunk for `K_s` environment steps (e.g., `K=200`).
2. **Observe:** Head and wrist observations are collected.
3. **Verify:** A multi-view VLM verifier (using `gemini-2.5-flash`) queries the postcondition using the collected observations. A skill advances only if the verifier returns a positive verdict above a confidence threshold of 0.6. Uncertain verdicts (below 0.6) prevent skill termination.
4. **Recover/Advance:**
 - If verification passes, the agent advances to the next skill.
 - If verification fails or is uncertain, execution continues for another `K_s` steps.
 - If the skill budget (`B_s`) is exhausted without successful verification, the agent triggers a replan, typically involving re-navigation to re-establish a valid handoff.
This loop generates a step-resolved verifier trajectory for every rollout, detailing skill attempts, verifier decisions, and replans.

**E. Diagnostic Evaluation Protocols**
The evaluation employs two complementary protocols to isolate and diagnose failures:
1. **Isolated Single-Skill Benchmark:**
 - **Purpose:** To assess the intrinsic competence of individual VLA skills from ideal, "curated" initial conditions.
 - **Procedure:** The simulator is reset to a snapshot corresponding to the start frame of a cleaned demonstration segment from the validation split. The corresponding skill-group checkpoint is run in isolation.
 - **Scoring:** Success is judged by the multi-view VLM verifier, with its decisions subsequently confirmed by human review to ensure accuracy.
2. **Composed End-to-End Rollout:**
 - **Purpose:** To evaluate skill robustness under "chained" and potentially "messy" initial conditions produced by preceding skills during continuous execution.
 - **Procedure:** The same skill-group checkpoints are composed via the agent loop, with each skill starting from the terminal state of the previous one. This is run on official BEHAVIOR-1K public test instances.
 - **Scoring:** Task-level success is determined by the BEHAVIOR-1K BDDL predicate. Additionally, a "progress score" is derived by human inspection of rollout videos against a reference skill sequence, providing a graded measure of completion given the often near-zero task-level success.
 - **Diagnostics:** Every failed skill attempt within the end-to-end rollouts is classified into specific diagnostic labels (target grounding, control execution, next-skill readiness) based on structured verifier reasons.

This dual evaluation strategy allows for a direct comparison of skill performance under ideal vs. realistic compositional settings, enabling the attribution of failures to the semantic handoff problem rather than fundamental skill incompetence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings that highlight the "semantic handoff problem" and provide diagnostics for failures in agent-orchestrated VLA skill composition.

**A. Isolated Skill Competence from Curated Snapshot States**
An initial benchmark of individual VLA skills, run from clean, curated skill-boundary snapshots, indicates that many skills are competent in isolation.
- Table I shows high success rates for several skills under human-reviewed VLM verification: `place_on` (100.0%), `open_door` (100.0%), `pick_up_from` (96.5%), `place_in` (84.6%), `turn_on_switch` (87.5%), and `move_to` (77.1%).
- Notably, placement skills, which often become bottlenecks in end-to-end tasks, demonstrate strong performance from these ideal starting states. This finding establishes that the observed failures in chained execution are not solely due to weak underlying skill policies.

**B. Competence Does Not Survive Composition**
Despite the competence of individual skills in isolation, their performance significantly degrades when composed in end-to-end rollouts.
- The same skill-group checkpoints that perform well from curated snapshots become unreliable when starting from the "messy" chained terminal states generated by previous skills. This constitutes a "skill-to-task gap" attributable to a distribution shift between the clean training distribution and the varied initial conditions encountered during compositional execution.
- Overall task-level predicate success in end-to-end BEHAVIOR-1K tasks is near zero. To provide a more granular understanding, a "progress score" (fraction of a task's reference skill sequence completed) was introduced. Across thirty rollouts, the mean progress score was 19.5%, with individual tasks varying significantly (e.g., "Turn on radio" at 50.0% vs. "Hide Easter eggs" at 0.0%). Progress tended to concentrate in early navigation, door opening, and grasping steps, but collapsed during contact-rich manipulation and multi-object placement tasks.

**C. Attributing Handoff Failures to Actionable Causes**
The agent harness provides trace-backed diagnostics, attributing failed skill attempts to specific VLA-side causes. These attributions are based on structured verifier reasons. On a representative round with one instance per task, out of 130 failed attempts, three dominant categories emerged (Table III):
1. **Target Grounding (37 failures):** The VLA policies failed to correctly identify or localize the target object, often because the object was out of view or the wrong instance was selected after navigation or door opening. The dominant failure signature was "target out of view or the wrong instance after navigation or door opening."
2. **Control Execution (58 failures):** The robot reached the target or interaction point but failed to execute the intended physical action successfully. This category includes:
 - `grasp` (31 failures): The gripper reached the object but failed to close or hold it properly.
 - `actuation` (15 failures): The robot reached a handle, switch, or button but failed to actuate it.
 - `placement` (12 failures): The robot approached a surface or container but mis-positioned the object.
3. **Next-Skill Readiness (35 failures):** The previous skill did not leave the environment in a state suitable for the subsequent skill to begin. The dominant failure signature was "navigation stops short of an arm-reach pose at the handoff," indicating that `move_to` skills often completed their local postcondition without bringing the robot close enough for the next manipulation step.

Crucially, these attributed failures represent *transfer failures* of skills that are competent in isolation, rather than raw incapacity. The harness's diagnostic capability allows for specific identification of where and why these transfers break down.

**D. Verifier Ablation and Audit**
A controlled ablation was performed on the `move_to` skill's navigation verifier criterion (Table VI). Tightening the postcondition from merely "reached the target area" to "the target is within arm reach" resulted in:
- An increase of 12 additional "next-skill readiness" failures.
- 25 more `move_to` attempts, indicating increased re-navigation to achieve the stricter arm-reach requirement.
- The recovery and successful completion of the "Turn on radio" task, which previously failed due to insufficient proximity for actuation.
This ablation demonstrates the effectiveness of handoff-aware postconditions in sharpening the readiness signal and improving task success.

A blinded human audit of the verifier's failure decisions (Table VII) confirmed its reliability, with 20 out of 21 verifier-flagged failures being judged as real failures by human annotators (an over-strict rate of 0.05). This indicates that the reported failure diagnostics are reliable indicators of actual execution problems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The presented work holds significance for the development and deployment of robust robotic systems, particularly those relying on learned VLA policies for long-horizon tasks.

**Diagnostic Framework for VLA Systems:** The most immediate impact is the introduction of the agent layer as a diagnostic instrument. By systematically executing, verifying, and recording step-resolved traces, the harness provides concrete evidence of where and why execution stalls. This capability transforms near-zero task success rates into actionable insights, moving beyond simply observing failure to understanding its root causes. This is critical for iterating on VLA models and their training data.

**Addressing the "Semantic Handoff Problem":** The research formally identifies and rigorously investigates the "semantic handoff problem," where individual skill competence does not translate to compositional robustness due to a distribution shift in initial states. This highlights a fundamental challenge for current VLA systems: they are often trained and evaluated on curated, isolated scenarios, making them brittle when faced with the varied and dynamic conditions of chained execution. Recognizing this distinction is key to designing more effective training methodologies.

**Guiding Future VLA Development:** The trace-backed diagnostics provide a clear roadmap for improving VLA skill libraries.
- **Targeted Data Collection:** The identification of "chained-state distribution" as a primary cause suggests a need for targeted data collection that explicitly covers the "messy" initial conditions skills encounter during composition, rather than just scaling up data from curated snapshots.
- **Improved Grounding:** The prevalence of target grounding failures indicates a need for VLA models with more robust instance-level binding and scene search capabilities, especially after significant robot movement or scene changes.
- **Robust Control Execution:** Specific breakdowns in grasping, actuation, and placement suggest the need for more robust, contact-rich manipulation policies that can reliably complete actions even with minor misalignments or dynamic object interactions.
- **Readiness-Aware Verification:** The proposed "next-skill readiness predicate" (ϕ_t ∧ ρ(s_t, s_t+1)) offers a principled direction for incorporating handoff awareness directly into the verification process. This could lead to more intelligent agents that actively ensure suitable conditions for the next skill, reducing failures and improving recovery.

**Enhancing Robot Reliability and Recovery:** The ability of the agent harness to trigger replanning and recovery within the execution loop (e.g., re-navigating when an arm-reach condition is not met) demonstrates a practical approach to increasing system reliability. By proactively addressing semantic handoff failures, robots can become more adaptable and persistent in completing complex tasks in unstructured environments.

In essence, this work shifts the focus from merely developing powerful VLA policies to rigorously understanding and mitigating the practical challenges of deploying them in real-world, long-horizon settings. It provides both a diagnostic tool and a conceptual framework for building more robust and reliable embodied AI agents.
