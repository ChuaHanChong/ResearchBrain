# Predictive Relative-Velocity Steering for Safe Robotic Manipulator Teleoperation in Dynamic Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> PARRY is a lightweight plug-and-play collision-avoidance layer for robot teleoperation that filters end-effector velocity commands. It predicts obstacle point clouds via a time-to-collision look-ahead with overshoot protection, then uses Rodrigues' rotation to deflect the relative velocity direction while keeping its magnitude, avoiding potential-field stagnation. It reached 82.7% collision-avoidance rate versus 79.1% for potential-field superposition, at 0.101 ms per cycle.

## Problem

- Operators miss suddenly appearing obstacles under network latency or divided attention, so teleoperated manipulators collide before an evasive command reaches the remote arm.
- Potential-field methods cancel attractive against repulsive forces, stalling the end effector; operators misread this stagnation as disconnection and issue erratic joystick commands.
- Virtual fixtures need predefined or interactively built constraints, and barrier-function filters demand smooth obstacle models plus online optimization, both costly on raw point clouds.

## Method

- ==Rodrigues-rotation velocity deflection== in the obstacle frame rotates the relative velocity about an axis normal to it and the repulsive force, preserving speed magnitude and forward progress.
- ==Overshoot-protected adaptive prediction== sets the look-ahead horizon from a ==time-to-collision==-style radial approach time, then clamps each predicted point so none crosses past the end effector.
- Preprocessing stack of ==hand-eye reprojection==, ==voxel downsampling==, ==capsule-based robot self-filtering==, and ==radius outlier removal==, with ==power-law distance-weighted aggregation== of pointwise repulsive forces.

## Results

- Across **21,000** paired Monte Carlo runs, PARRY cut end-effector collision rate **17.2%**, **27.3%**, and **38.7%** versus potential-field superposition, barrier-function QP, and separation monitoring (**p<0.01**).
- From **0** to **150 ms** observation latency, PARRY's collision rate rose only **2.6** points versus **5.4** and **4.3** for the potential-field and barrier-function baselines, keeping the best rate throughout.
- Overshoot protection dropped trials with **>90-degree** repulsive-force flips from **20.2%** to **3.6%** and wrong-way repulsion from **20.4%** to **0.1%**; compute was **0.101 ms** versus **0.729 ms**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rotating a command vector instead of summing forces onto it preserves operator-commanded speed, converting potential-field deadlock into smooth detours without altering the tracking controller.
> - Geometric look-ahead on raw point clouds is a cheap substitute for model-based delay compensation, and its benefit grows as perception latency rises.
> - Predicting obstacle motion without per-point overshoot clamping induces reverse repulsion; safeguards can matter more for motion stability and operator trust than for headline safety rate.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic teleoperation has advanced to enable dexterous manipulation in remote or hazardous environments, a capability that often surpasses fully autonomous systems in terms of precision and adaptability. However, ensuring safety in teleoperated systems, particularly in dynamic environments or under network latency, remains a significant challenge. Human operators may experience delays in perceiving or reacting to unexpected obstacles, leading to potential collisions.

Existing approaches to enhance safety in teleoperation include:
- **Reactive repulsion-based methods**, such as Artificial Potential Fields (APFs): These methods generate repulsive forces from obstacles to steer the robot away. A known limitation of classical APF methods is the potential for local minima or velocity stagnation, where attractive and repulsive forces cancel out, causing the robot to stop or oscillate. This can be problematic in teleoperation, as an operator might misinterpret such stagnation.
- **Virtual Fixtures (VFs) and haptic guidance**: These constrain robot motion to predefined safe regions or trajectories. While effective for known environments, they often require prior definition or interactive construction, limiting their robustness in unfamiliar or unstructured settings.
- **Control Barrier Function (CBF)-based safety filters**: These methods project operator commands onto a mathematically guaranteed safe set. However, they typically require smooth obstacle representations and online optimization, which can incur a substantial computational burden when applied directly to raw point-cloud data.
- **Standards-based approaches**: Methods like Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM) provide guidelines for maintaining safety distances in human-robot collaboration but may not fully address real-time, dynamic collision avoidance.

Addressing these limitations, a subset of research focuses on **rotational obstacle avoidance** to mitigate APF stagnation by guiding robots using tangential forces or directional modulation. Concurrently, efforts in **delay compensation and prediction** in teleoperation aim to improve system stability and transparency by employing techniques such as Smith predictors, model-mediated teleoperation, or shared control approaches that use kinematic models and model predictive control.

This paper introduces PARRY, which integrates predictive capabilities and a rotational steering mechanism into a lightweight, modular framework. PARRY directly operates on end-effector velocity commands and processes point clouds, positioning it as a real-time, reactive safety layer designed to overcome the stagnation issues of classical APFs and the computational demands of some CBF methods, while also mitigating the impact of perception latency inherent in teleoperation pipelines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a proactive collision avoidance framework for robotic manipulators in teleoperation settings that effectively addresses safety risks arising from human operator limitations, network latency, and dynamic environments.

The key motivations driving this work are:

- **Human operator limitations**: Despite their dexterity, human operators can fail to notice or react promptly to suddenly appearing obstacles. This risk is exacerbated when operators are focused on a complex manipulation task or when their attention is divided.
- **Impact of network latency and processing delays**: Teleoperation systems inherently involve network latency and complex data processing pipelines, particularly for perception (e.g., point cloud processing). These delays can cause a temporal mismatch between the operator's commands and the robot's actual environment, leading to outdated obstacle information and delayed execution of evasive maneuvers, thus increasing collision risk.
- **Limitations of existing safety mechanisms**:
 - **Artificial Potential Fields (APFs)**: While widely used, APFs are susceptible to local minima and velocity stagnation, where repulsive and attractive forces cancel each other out. In a teleoperation context, such stagnation can be misinterpreted by an operator as a system malfunction, potentially leading to abnormal and unsafe command inputs.
 - **Virtual Fixtures (VFs)**: VFs provide motion guidance but often rely on predefined geometric constraints, making them less robust or adaptable in unstructured or unfamiliar environments.
 - **Control Barrier Functions (CBFs)**: CBFs offer strong safety guarantees but typically require smooth obstacle representations and involve online optimization, leading to significant computational overhead that may hinder real-time application with raw point-cloud inputs.
- **Need for a lightweight, real-time solution**: Teleoperation requires high-frequency control updates. Therefore, any safety-assistance mechanism must be computationally efficient ("lightweight") and able to operate in real time without introducing undue delays into the control loop.
- **Addressing the deadlock problem**: The specific issue of velocity stagnation or "deadlock" in APF methods, where the robot effectively halts, is a critical motivation. The researchers aim to develop a method that steers the robot around obstacles without losing forward momentum or becoming stuck.

To address these motivations, the proposed PARRY framework aims to:
1. Provide proactive collision avoidance at the end-effector velocity-command level.
2. Compensate for obstacle-observation latency through a Time-To-Collision (TTC)-based prediction mechanism.
3. Mitigate the deadlock problem by rotating the relative-velocity vector, preserving its magnitude while changing its direction.
4. Maintain a lightweight design suitable for high-frequency teleoperation control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PARRY (Predictive Avoidance by Rotating Relative velocitY) is designed as a modular and lightweight framework that operates directly on end-effector velocity commands in teleoperation. The overall approach involves three main stages: point-cloud preprocessing, overshoot-protected adaptive prediction, and Rodrigues-rotation-based safe velocity generation.

#### 3.1. A. Problem Formulation and System Overview

The system takes an operator's desired end-effector velocity ($v_{ee}$) and a depth camera's raw point cloud as inputs. It outputs a modified end-effector velocity ($v_{cmd}$) that avoids obstacles. The core idea is to compute the end-effector velocity relative to an obstacle ($v_{rel} = v_{ee} - v_o$), predict the obstacle's future position, calculate a repulsive guidance force, and then rotate $v_{rel}$ using Rodrigues' rotation formula to obtain a safe relative velocity ($v_s$). This $v_s$ is then transformed back to the base frame to get $v_{cmd} = v_s + v_o$.

#### 3.2. B. Self-Filtering-Based Point-Cloud Preprocessing

The raw depth image from the camera is converted into 3D points in the camera frame, then transformed into the manipulator's base frame using hand-eye calibration. The point cloud undergoes several processing steps:
1. **Cropping and Voxel Downsampling**: Points outside the workspace and tabletop boundaries are removed, and the remaining points are downsampled using voxels to reduce computational load.
2. **Robot Self-filtering**: To distinguish obstacles from the robot's own body, the manipulator links are approximated by capsules. Points within a certain radius of any capsule are filtered out, assuming they belong to the robot itself.
3. **Radius-Based Outlier Removal**: This step suppresses depth noise and reduces fluctuations in subsequent velocity estimates by retaining only points that have a sufficient number of neighbors within a defined radius. The output is the preprocessed obstacle point cloud, $O$.

#### 3.3. C. Overshoot-Protected Adaptive Prediction

To compensate for sensing and processing delays in teleoperation, PARRY employs a geometric point-cloud prediction mechanism.
1. **Obstacle Velocity Estimation**: The most hazardous point (closest to the end-effector) in the current point cloud $O^{(k)}$ is identified. Its instantaneous velocity is estimated by differencing its position with the most hazardous point from the previous frame. An Exponential Moving Average (EMA) is applied to this estimate to reduce jitter, with a condition to retain the previous velocity if the nearest point displacement is too large.
2. **Adaptive Prediction Horizon**: A Time-To-Collision (TTC)-inspired metric, defined as the obstacle's radial approach time ($T_{app}$), is used to adaptively determine the prediction horizon ($t_p$). If the obstacle is approaching (radial approach speed $v_{app} > \epsilon_a$), $t_p$ is scaled by $T_{app}$ and clipped within a range $[t_{min}, t_{max}]$. Otherwise, $t_p$ defaults to $t_{min}$.
3. **Look-ahead Point Cloud Generation**: Each point in $O^{(k)}$ is projected forward by $\Delta o = v^{(k)}_o t_p$. The magnitude of $\Delta o$ is capped.
4. **Overshoot Protection**: This critical step prevents predicted points from moving beyond the end effector or further away. For each point $o^{(k)}_i$, its predicted radial coordinate $\ell_{i,p}$ is calculated. If $\ell_{i,p}$ falls below a minimum allowable distance $d_s$ or causes the point to effectively "pass through" the end effector (i.e., the predicted point is further than the original point from the end effector but still within an influence region), the point is corrected or reverted to its original position. This ensures repulsive forces are correctly directed and do not cause erroneous reactions. The result is the predicted point cloud, $\hat{O}$.

#### 3.4. D. Rodrigues-Rotation-Based Safe Velocity Generation

This module is responsible for modifying the end-effector velocity command based on the predicted obstacles.
1. **Repulsive Force Calculation**: Similar to APF, PARRY calculates a repulsive force from the predicted obstacle point cloud $\hat{O}$. Instead of a classical APF formulation, it uses a power-law pointwise repulsive force that decays with distance to provide adjustable sensitivity. The final aggregate repulsive force ($F_{rep}$) is computed using distance-weighted aggregation, preventing distant obstacles from dominating the avoidance direction.
2. **Rotation Axis Determination**: The rotation axis ($\hat{a}$) is computed as the normalized cross product of the relative velocity vector ($v_{rel}$) and the aggregate repulsive force ($F_{rep}$).
3. **Handling Degenerate Cases**:
 - No redirection is applied if there are no active obstacles, the repulsive force is negligible, the relative velocity is negligible, or the end effector is already moving away from the obstacle.
 - If $v_{rel}$ and $F_{rep}$ are approximately antiparallel (leading to a degenerate rotation axis), PARRY reuses the rotation axis from the previous time step. This maintains smooth temporal continuity and stability and, crucially, avoids velocity stagnation by preserving the magnitude of $v_{rel}$, allowing continuous motion that eventually moves the system out of the degenerate configuration. This contrasts with classical APFs, where antiparallel forces can lead to near-zero resultant velocity.
4. **Redirection Angle**: The redirection angle ($\theta$) is determined by the magnitude of $F_{rep}$, scaled by a gain $k_{rot}$ and clipped within $[0, \theta_{max}]$.
5. **Velocity Redirection**: Rodrigues' rotation formula is applied to $v_{rel}$ using the determined axis $\hat{a}$ and angle $\theta$ to obtain the deflected relative velocity $v_s$. This rotation only changes the direction of $v_{rel}$ while preserving its magnitude.
6. **Final Command Velocity**: The safe relative velocity $v_s$ is then transformed back to the base frame by adding the obstacle velocity $v_o$, yielding the final commanded end-effector velocity $v_{cmd} = v_s + v_o$. This $v_{cmd}$ is then sent to the robot's low-level controller.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of PARRY was evaluated through extensive Monte Carlo simulations and physical robot experiments.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiments Setup

- **Environment**: MuJoCo simulation with a UR5e manipulator, Robotiq 2F-85 gripper, workbench, and manipulated object.
- **Task**: Pick–transport–release task.
- **Obstacle**: A dynamic spherical obstacle with radius 0.10 m, launched with speeds sampled from [0.5, 3.5] m/s, at various times and target points near the end effector. Gaussian noise was added to point-cloud positions and obstacle velocity.
- **Latency**: Obstacle-observation latencies of $\tau \in \{0, 100, 150\}$ ms were simulated.
- **Trials**: 1,000 paired randomized scenarios for each latency setting, totaling 21,000 runs.
- **Metrics**: Collision Avoidance Rate (CAR), median minimum clearance, median computation time, and the percentage of trials with large-angle force flips or wrong-way repulsion.

#### 4.2. B. Baselines and Ablation Studies

- **Baselines**:
 - **APF-VS**: Adds repulsive force directly to nominal task velocity.
 - **CBF-QP**: Uses kinematic CBF quadratic program for obstacle constraints.
 - **SSM**: Scales nominal task velocity based on end-effector–obstacle distance and approach speed.
- **Ablations (from PARRY)**:
 - **PARRY w/o Rel**: Disables relative velocity processing.
 - **PARRY w/o Pred**: Disables TTC prediction.
 - **PARRY w/o Guard**: Disables overshoot protection.

#### 4.3. C. Simulation Results and Discussion

1. **Baseline Safety Comparison**:
 - PARRY achieved a higher CAR across all latency settings. At 0 ms latency, PARRY's CAR was 82.7%, compared to 79.1% for APF-VS, 76.2% for CBF-QP, and 71.8% for SSM.
 - The improvements were statistically significant (p < 0.01).

2. **Robustness to Obstacle-Observation Latency**:
 - As latency increased from 0 ms to 150 ms, PARRY's CAR degradation was 2.6 percentage points (pp), from 82.7% to 80.1%.
 - In contrast, APF-VS degraded by 5.4 pp (79.1% to 73.7%) and CBF-QP by 4.3 pp (76.2% to 71.9%).
 - PARRY maintained its CAR advantage, demonstrating greater robustness to latency.

3. **Ablation Study Insights**:
 - **Relative Velocity (PARRY w/o Rel vs. PARRY w/o Pred)**: Relative velocity processing showed a 23.2% relative reduction in collision rate at 0 ms latency, indicating its effectiveness in capturing dynamic relationships.
 - **TTC-Based Prediction (PARRY w/o Pred vs. PARRY w/o Guard)**: The introduction of TTC-based prediction led to significant CAR reductions (17.5% at 0 ms, 21.7% at 100 ms, 24.9% at 150 ms), particularly at higher latencies, highlighting its role in compensating for perception delays.
 - **Overshoot Protection (PARRY w/o Guard vs. PARRY)**: While only slightly improving CAR, overshoot protection substantially enhanced motion stability and operator experience. It reduced the percentage of trials with large-angle repulsive-force flips from 20.2% to 3.6% (at 0 ms) and erroneous repulsive-force directions from 20.4% to 0.1%. This suggests that overshoot protection primarily prevents erratic and unsafe force computations.

4. **Safety Margin and Computational Cost**:
 - PARRY achieved the largest median minimum end-effector clearance (0.188 m at 0 ms latency) compared to APF-VS (0.150 m) and CBF-QP (0.135 m), indicating a larger safety margin.
 - Its median computation time was 0.101 ms, which is considerably lower than CBF-QP (0.729 ms) and suitable for 100 Hz control frequencies.

5. **Local-Minimum Cases**: In symmetric two-sphere configurations prone to APF local minima, PARRY maintained the forward velocity component, traversed these regions smoothly, and reached the target earlier, whereas APF-VS exhibited persistent oscillations around zero velocity. This finding supports PARRY's ability to mitigate transient stagnation caused by APF velocity superposition.

#### 4.4. D. Physical Robot Experiments

- **Setup**: 7-DoF Flexiv Rizon 4 manipulator, Meta Quest 3 controller for operator commands, Intel RealSense D435 depth camera.
- **Task**: Operator grasps, stacks, and re-locates paper cups.
- **Scenarios**: Static obstacle blockage and dynamic moving obstacle intrusion.
- **Results**: PARRY successfully redirected the end-effector to bypass obstacles in both static and dynamic scenarios, allowing task completion. The baseline teleoperation framework, without PARRY, collided with obstacles in comparable scenarios. This demonstrated the framework's collision-avoidance effectiveness and real-time feasibility on a physical system.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that contributes to the field of teleoperation safety by offering a method to mitigate critical limitations of existing approaches, particularly concerning dynamic environments and system latency.

The significance of PARRY lies in several key aspects:

- **Enhanced Safety in Dynamic Teleoperation**: By proactively predicting obstacles and steering the end effector, PARRY reduces collision rates, especially in scenarios with unexpected or moving obstacles. This directly addresses a major safety concern in real-world teleoperation applications where the environment may not be fully known or static.
- **Robustness to Latency**: The adaptive TTC-based prediction mechanism allows PARRY to compensate for sensing and processing delays common in teleoperation pipelines. This robustness ensures that safety measures remain effective even when environmental information is not instantaneously available, which is crucial for remote operation.
- **Mitigation of APF Limitations**: PARRY's use of Rodrigues' rotation formula to redirect the relative velocity, rather than superimposing repulsive forces, effectively bypasses the local minima and velocity stagnation problems inherent in classical Artificial Potential Fields. This ensures continuous, purposeful motion around obstacles, preventing the robot from getting "stuck" and reducing the risk of operator misinterpretation.
- **Improved Operator Experience and System Stability**: The preservation of relative velocity magnitude during redirection, coupled with overshoot protection, contributes to smoother, more predictable robot movements. This enhances the operator's trust and reduces the likelihood of erratic or dangerous behavior caused by unexpected halts or sudden changes in repulsive force direction.
- **Lightweight and Real-time Operation**: The framework's design prioritizes computational efficiency, allowing it to operate at high control frequencies (e.g., 100 Hz). This makes it suitable for integration into existing teleoperation architectures as a plug-and-play safety layer without introducing significant computational overhead.
- **Practical Feasibility**: Validation through both extensive simulations and experiments on a physical robotic manipulator (Flexiv Rizon 4) demonstrates the real-world applicability and deployment feasibility of PARRY.

The potential impact of this work includes:
- **Increased adoption of teleoperation in hazardous environments**: By making teleoperation safer and more reliable, PARRY could facilitate its use in applications such as remote inspection, hazardous waste handling, deep-sea exploration, or medical surgery, where human presence is undesirable or impossible.
- **Reduced training burden for operators**: A more robust and intuitive safety system could potentially reduce the extensive training typically required for teleoperation, as the system can proactively prevent common collision scenarios.
- **Foundation for future safety layers**: The modular nature of PARRY, operating at the velocity-command level, allows for its integration with various teleoperation control strategies and perception systems, potentially serving as a baseline for developing more advanced, whole-body collision avoidance systems in the future.

While the current study focuses on end-effector collision avoidance, the approach lays groundwork for future work extending these safety constraints to all manipulator links and evaluating its effects on broader human-robot interaction metrics like teleoperation efficiency and overall operator experience.
