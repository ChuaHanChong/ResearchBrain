# InT: Self-Proposed Interventions Enable Credit Assignment in LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Intervention Training (InT) introduces a two-stage method enabling large language models (LLMs) to self-propose single-step corrective interventions within their reasoning traces. This approach allows LLMs to effectively learn from their own errors on complex, multi-step problems, leading to state-of-the-art performance on challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks by providing fine-grained credit assignment.

## Problem

- Standard outcome-reward Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces a significant "credit assignment problem" in multi-step LLM reasoning, as feedback is only provided at the final answer.
- This limitation results in inefficient learning signals, high-variance gradients, and a severe lack of useful data from predominantly incorrect rollouts on difficult tasks.
- Existing solutions like Process Reward Models (PRMs) are computationally expensive and complex to train, often requiring extensive data collection or external supervision.

## Method

- **InT** employs a two-stage paradigm: first, LLMs propose ==interventions== through self-verification; second, the model is trained via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and subsequent reinforcement learning.
- The LLM self-verifies its own incorrect reasoning traces against a reference solution, identifies the first critical error, and then generates a single-step corrective intervention.
- The model undergoes SFT on these collected problem-prefix-intervention pairs to internalize corrective patterns, creating a stronger initialization for subsequent outcome-reward RL.

## Results

- Self-proposed interventions dramatically improved success rates by 22 times (from **0.0713%** to **1.56%**) and problem coverage by ~**2.7x** (from 29/334 to 80/334) on a subset of hard problems.
- InT + RL achieved state-of-the-art performance across four challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with an average score of **33.72%**, representing a ~**59%** relative improvement over the base model (**21.17%**).
- On the IMO-AnswerBench, InT + RL (using a **4B** parameter model) achieved **25.62%**, more than doubling the base model's performance and outperforming larger open-source models like `gpt-oss-20b` (**23.36%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs are empirically more reliable at verifying individual reasoning steps against a reference than at generating a complete correct solution from scratch, a capability InT exploits for self-correction.
> - Extracting useful learning signals from otherwise discarded incorrect rollouts via targeted, single-step interventions is critical for improving LLM performance on hard problems where successful trajectories are rare.
> - SFT on short, targeted interventions effectively "patches" the base model and provides a more "on-policy" initialization for RL, preserving the model's entropy and avoiding the pitfalls of fine-tuning on full, potentially off-policy, reference solutions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has revolutionized many areas of artificial intelligence, particularly in tasks requiring human-like language understanding and generation. A critical area of ongoing research focuses on enhancing LLMs' reasoning capabilities, especially for complex, multi-step problems like mathematical reasoning. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for post-training LLMs, allowing them to learn from outcomes and refine their responses.

However, a fundamental challenge in applying RL to long-horizon reasoning tasks is the **credit assignment problem**. Standard outcome-reward RL, where feedback is only provided at the final answer, struggles to efficiently learn from complex trajectories. When a multi-step reasoning trace leads to an incorrect answer, the entire trace is penalized uniformly, even if many intermediate steps were correct. Conversely, in a successful trace, all steps are uniformly reinforced, potentially strengthening spurious or inefficient reasoning pathways. This leads to several issues: inefficient learning signals, increased verbosity, premature shifts in reasoning, and a complete lack of signal when no successful rollouts are produced for particularly hard problems.

The existing landscape of solutions to the credit assignment problem in LLMs includes:
- **Process Reward Models (PRMs):** These models are designed to provide dense, step-level rewards, theoretically offering more granular feedback. However, training high-quality PRMs is computationally expensive, requires vast amounts of data (often from "branched rollouts" or human annotations), and can suffer from stability issues or "reward hacking." Moreover, even with an accurate PRM, optimizing the policy to identify and generate alternative, higher-value steps remains a difficult task.
- **Learning from Natural Language Feedback:** Other approaches involve using human or stronger teacher models to provide textual critiques or generate refined solutions, which are then distilled back into the policy via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or used in off-policy RL. These methods often rely on external supervision or the availability of a superior model.
- **Hint-Guided RL:** This involves conditioning the LLM on partial solution prefixes or "hints" (often derived from expert solutions) to guide its generation toward more promising solution spaces, thereby improving exploration and success rates.

The work by Yang et al. with **Intervention Training (InT)** positions itself as a novel and efficient alternative that addresses the shortcomings of outcome-reward RL and the complexities of PRMs. InT sidesteps the need for explicit PRM training and expensive branched rollouts by leveraging an inherent asymmetry in LLM capabilities: it is often easier for an LLM to *verify* an existing reasoning step against a reference solution than to *generate* a correct step from scratch. By allowing the LLM to self-verify its own incorrect traces and propose single-step corrections, InT provides a targeted, fine-grained credit assignment mechanism without relying on external critics or complex reward models. It further differentiates itself by focusing specifically on extracting useful learning signals from *incorrect* rollouts, which dominate training on the most challenging problems. This approach is also shown to be complementary to hint-guided RL, suggesting a potential for synergistic improvements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective, scalable, and computationally efficient method for **credit assignment in LLM reasoning**, particularly for long-horizon and challenging problems where standard outcome-reward RL falls short.

The motivations driving this objective are multifaceted:
- **Addressing RL's Credit Assignment Problem:** The core motivation stems from the inherent limitations of outcome-reward RL in attributing success or failure to specific intermediate steps within a long reasoning chain. This "indiscriminate penalty" or "uniform reinforcement" leads to high-variance gradients, inefficient learning, and undesirable model behaviors such as excessive verbosity or premature truncation.
- **Extracting Signal from Failed Rollouts:** For sufficiently difficult tasks, LLMs often produce predominantly incorrect reasoning traces. Standard RL discards these failed rollouts as they yield zero reward, leading to a severe lack of learning signals. A key motivation for InT is to tap into this abundant source of "negative" supervision by pinpointing errors within these failed traces and learning from them.
- **Bypassing PRM Complexities:** While PRMs conceptually offer a solution to credit assignment, their practical implementation is fraught with challenges, including high computational cost for data collection (e.g., branched rollouts), training instabilities, and difficulty in precisely optimizing the policy to act on step-level rewards. The authors are motivated to find a simpler, more direct approach that avoids these complexities.
- **Leveraging LLM Asymmetries:** The research is motivated by the observation that LLMs are often better at verifying or critiquing existing text than at generating perfect text from scratch. This asymmetry provides an opportunity for self-correction without requiring a stronger external model.
- **Improving RL Initialization:** Recognizing that effective RL training often benefits from a good policy initialization, the authors aim to use fine-grained credit assignment to "patch" the base model, creating a superior starting point for subsequent RL, thereby enhancing its sample efficiency and final performance.
- **Tackling Hard Problems:** The focus on "hard problems" (e.g., Olympiad-level mathematics) where successful rollouts are rare, underscores the motivation to make LLMs more robust and capable in high-stakes, challenging domains.

In essence, the overarching goal is to enable LLMs to learn more effectively and efficiently from their own mistakes, particularly when tackling complex reasoning problems, thereby pushing the boundaries of their reasoning capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

InT (Intervention Training) proposes a two-stage training paradigm designed to facilitate fine-grained credit assignment in LLMs: (1) **Proposing Interventions through Self-Verification** and (2) **Training via Supervised Fine-Tuning on Intervention Data, followed by Reinforcement Learning.**

#### 3.1. Proposing Interventions through Self-Verification

This stage addresses the challenge of identifying and correcting errors within an LLM's reasoning trace without resorting to complex external reward models or expensive branched rollouts.
- **Leveraging Task Asymmetry:** The core idea is to exploit the empirical observation that LLMs are often more reliable at verifying individual steps against a reference solution than at generating a correct end-to-end solution.
- **Two-Stage Prompting Procedure:**
 1. **Error Identification:** Given a problem (`x`), a model-generated incorrect reasoning trace (`y`), and a human-written *reference solution*, the base LLM is prompted to act as an "expert mathematician" (using `p_InT`, detailed in Box 1 & 2 of the appendix). Its task is to perform a step-by-step verification, comparing its own trace against the reference solution. It identifies all potentially incorrect steps and, crucially, pinpoints the *first critical error* (`y_t*`) along with its step number (`t*`). The focus on the first error is strategic, as later errors are often consequences of earlier ones.
 2. **Intervention Generation:** Once the first critical error is identified, the model is then instructed to generate a *single-step corrective intervention* (`~y_t*`). This intervention is designed to replace `y_t*` and steer the subsequent reasoning trajectory toward the correct solution. It's critical that the intervention does not directly reveal the final answer but provides guidance to correct the reasoning path.
- **Self-Generated Feedback:** The key innovation here is that the same LLM that generated the original incorrect trace is also responsible for self-verifying and proposing its own corrective intervention. This enables credit assignment via self-generated feedback, making the process highly scalable and autonomous.
- **Efficacy Validation:** The generated interventions are empirically validated by concatenating the correct prefix (`y_<t*`) with the intervention (`~y_t*`) and sampling continuations. Results show a substantial increase in success rates and problem coverage when continuing from an intervention compared to continuing from the original error or simply resampling from the prefix. The critical role of reference solutions and the LLM's instruction-following capabilities in generating high-quality interventions are also empirically confirmed.

#### 3.2. Intervention Training (InT) for Credit Assignment

Once a dataset of problem-prefix-intervention pairs is collected, the model is trained in two phases:
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for Patching:**
 - The model undergoes SFT on the collected intervention data. The goal is to internalize the corrective patterns, allowing the model to spontaneously generate similar interventions during future reasoning, even without access to a reference solution at inference time.
 - **Optimal SFT Configuration:** Extensive ablation studies determined the most effective SFT strategy:
 - **Cloning the prefix (`y_<t*`) and the intervention (`~y_t*`):** This is crucial to ensure the model learns to associate the intervention with the specific preceding context.
 - **Excluding the suffix (`~y_>t*`):** Training on the full, successful continuation (`~y_>t*`) was found to be detrimental, potentially limiting exploration during subsequent RL training.
 - **Filtering interventions:** Only interventions that led to at least one correct continuation (out of 32 rollouts) were retained for SFT, ensuring quality and relevance.
 - The SFT objective is defined to maximize the likelihood of the correct prefix and the proposed intervention, effectively "patching" the model's erroneous reasoning pathways.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Fine-Tuning:**
 - After the SFT-based "patching" step, standard outcome-reward RL (specifically GRPO in this work) is applied.
 - The SFT-initialized model serves as a significantly better starting point for RL. Having internalized corrective behaviors, its rollouts are less prone to the identified errors, allowing RL to more effectively amplify successful trajectories, suppress unproductive ones, and extract learning signals from problems that previously yielded none.

This combined approach ensures that the model learns to correct its mistakes locally and effectively, leading to a more robust and capable reasoning agent.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings demonstrating the effectiveness of Intervention Training (InT):

#### 4.1. Efficacy of Self-Proposed Interventions

- **Increased Success Rates:** Rollouts conditioned on self-proposed, single-step interventions showed a dramatic improvement, increasing average reward by 22 times (from 0.0713% to 1.56%) on a subset of hard problems. Problem coverage (number of unique problems solved) also significantly increased (from 29/334 to 80/334).
- **Role of Reference Solutions:** Interventions generated with access to reference solutions were far more effective than those generated without, highlighting the critical role of privileged information in guiding self-correction.
- **Importance of Instruction-Following:** The instruction-tuned Qwen3-4B-Instruct model produced better interventions than its non-instruction-tuned counterpart, indicating that robust instruction-following capabilities are essential for the self-verification process.
- **Scaling with Model Capacity:** Interventions proposed by a larger model (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct) yielded approximately 2x higher accuracy, confirming that intervention quality scales with model capacity.
- **Complementary to Hint-Guided RL:** While interventions alone were effective, combining them with hint-guided rollouts (using partial solution prefixes) led to even stronger results, suggesting that these approaches are complementary.

#### 4.2. Impact of SFT with Interventions (Patching)

- **Improved `pass@k` as Initialization:** Supervised fine-tuning on intervention data consistently increased `pass@k` (for `k` up to 1024) on both training and held-out test sets. This indicates that InT successfully enriches the distribution of successful trajectories, providing a strong initialization for subsequent RL.
- **"On-Policy" Training Data:** InT-generated traces were found to be more "on-policy" (i.e., had lower negative log-likelihood under the base model) compared to other forms of SFT data like full reference solutions or self-reflection traces. This is crucial because fine-tuning on highly off-policy data can increase the entropy of the model's next-token distribution, leading to unstable and ineffective RL training. Interventions are short, preserving the base model's existing reasoning patterns.
- **Preserved Entropy:** Unlike SFT on full reference solutions, InT did not significantly increase the entropy of the next-token distribution, ensuring a more stable and effective base for RL exploration.

#### 4.3. Performance of InT + RL

- **Enhanced Learning from Hard Problems:** On the curated training set of difficult problems, InT + RL achieved higher average reward and a significantly lower "zero-advantage ratio" (the fraction of problems for which the model never produces a correct rollout) compared to standard RL or other SFT+RL baselines. This confirms that InT enables the model to extract useful learning signals from previously unsolvable problems.
- **State-of-the-Art on Benchmarks:** InT + RL achieved the strongest overall performance across four challenging, contemporary mathematical reasoning benchmarks (IMO-AnswerBench, HMMT 2025 Nov, AMO-Bench, Apex Shortlist).
 - It achieved an average score of 33.72%, representing a ~59% relative improvement over the base model (21.17%) and a ~19% improvement over the standard RL baseline (28.26%).
 - Notably, on IMO-AnswerBench (a high-quality dataset of Olympiad-level problems), InT + RL achieved 25.62%, more than doubling the base model's performance (11.68%) and outperforming larger open-source models like `gpt-oss-20b` (23.36%) despite using only a 4B parameter model.
- **Avoided Pitfalls of Off-Policy SFT:** Naïve SFT on full reference solutions performed poorly, even degrading performance compared to the untuned base model in some cases. This validates InT's approach of using targeted, on-policy interventions to avoid distorting the base model's distribution and hindering downstream RL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "InT: Self-Proposed Interventions Enable Credit Assignment in LLM Reasoning" paper introduces a highly significant contribution to the field of LLM training and reasoning. Its impact can be understood in several key areas:

- **Solving a Core RL Challenge for LLMs:** InT provides an elegant and effective solution to the long-standing credit assignment problem in reinforcement learning for long-horizon, multi-step LLM reasoning tasks. By allowing LLMs to self-correct at a granular step-level, it overcomes the limitations of sparse outcome-rewards without the prohibitive costs and complexities associated with training explicit process reward models (PRMs).
- **Boosting LLM Reasoning Capabilities:** The demonstrated performance improvements on challenging, Olympiad-level mathematical benchmarks (e.g., a ~14% absolute gain on IMO-AnswerBench, outperforming larger models) indicate a substantial leap in the practical reasoning capabilities of LLMs. This suggests that InT can enable smaller, more efficient LLMs to achieve performance levels previously only attainable by much larger or more specialized models.
- **Enhanced RL Training Efficiency and Robustness:** InT makes RL training for LLMs more sample-efficient and robust by extracting valuable learning signals from otherwise discarded incorrect rollouts. This is particularly crucial for hard problems where successful trajectories are rare. By providing a better-initialized model for RL, InT accelerates learning and helps avoid local optima or unstable training dynamics.
- **Leveraging Intrinsic LLM Strengths:** The method brilliantly capitalizes on the often-underutilized asymmetry between an LLM's generative capacity and its verification/critique capabilities. This self-correction mechanism reduces reliance on costly human annotation or larger, external oracle models, paving the way for more autonomous LLM self-improvement.
- **Simplicity and Scalability:** InT's approach is notably simple and computationally efficient. It avoids complex architectures, branched rollouts, or modifications to the core RL objective. This simplicity makes it highly scalable and accessible for practitioners to implement and integrate into existing LLM training pipelines.
- **Future Directions for Autonomous AI:** The work opens exciting avenues for future research into fully autonomous LLM self-improvement. By demonstrating effective self-verification and intervention, it lays groundwork for systems that can continually refine their own reasoning, potentially even generating their own training problems and verifying their solutions, leading to more robust, adaptable, and less brittle AI agents. Concepts like continual learning and improved memory architectures could further extend InT's principles to more dynamic and evolving reasoning contexts.

In conclusion, InT represents a practical and powerful paradigm shift in how LLMs can learn from their own reasoning errors. By enabling fine-grained, self-generated feedback, it significantly advances the state-of-the-art in LLM reasoning, particularly for complex, challenging tasks, and lays critical groundwork for future autonomous AI development.
