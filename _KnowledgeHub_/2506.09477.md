# On a few pitfalls in KL divergence gradient estimation for RL

> [!summary] Summary
> A paper from Meta FAIR and Meta GenAI identifies widespread implementation errors in KL divergence gradient estimation, a crucial component in reinforcement learning, particularly for large language models. It demonstrates that common methods yield biased gradients or optimize the unintended reverse KL, and provides correct, unbiased estimators, showing their efficacy in tabular and LLM experiments including Llama 3 models.

## Problem

- Widespread implementation practices for KL divergence gradient estimation in reinforcement learning, particularly in large language models, often result in incorrect or biased gradients.
- Current methods frequently fail to properly account for the auto-regressive sequential dependencies in LLM outputs when computing sequence-level KL divergence gradients.
- Many open-source projects and influential papers contain these pitfalls, leading to the intended KL regularization not being enforced effectively.

## Method

- The paper theoretically derives and explains the ==correct, unbiased gradient estimate for KL divergence==, showing it can be obtained by differentiating the ==squared KL estimate== or by using a ==stop-gradient operation on the ratio term==.
- It proposes ==unbiased sequence-level KL gradient estimates== for ==auto-regressive models==, including a vanilla sequence-level form and two ==variance-reduced variants== (==leave-one-out control variate== and ==cumulative estimate==) to account for full sequential dependencies.
- The authors provide empirical validation in both controlled tabular settings and realistic large language model fine-tuning experiments to demonstrate the impact of the identified pitfalls and the efficacy of the proposed correct methods.

## Results

- Tabular experiments demonstrated that common incorrect KL gradient estimates exhibit significant bias, fail to minimize the target KL, or inadvertently minimize the reverse KL, unlike correctly derived estimates.
- In LLM experiments (e.g., Llama 3), correct vanilla token-level gradient estimates effectively reduced KL divergence, whereas incorrect variance-reduced methods showed little progress on the target metric, especially in high-KL regimes.
- Properly implemented sequence-level KL gradient estimates (vanilla, leave-one-out, cumulative) significantly outperformed token-level implementations in minimizing sequence-level KL divergence for LLMs, showing faster convergence and better overall performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Auto-differentiating common Monte-Carlo estimates of KL divergence (e.g., vanilla or variance-reduced) as a loss function yields biased gradients, often resulting in no effective regularization or minimizing the unintended reverse KL divergence.
> - For sequence generation tasks like those with large language models, token-level KL losses produce only partial gradients, as they fail to account for the auto-regressive dependencies between tokens.
> - Correctly estimating the KL divergence gradient requires specific forms derived from the score function, and for sequences, unbiased sequence-level estimators that capture full temporal dependencies are crucial for effective optimization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper directly addresses a fundamental and widely-used component in modern reinforcement learning, especially within the context of large language models: KL divergence regularization. KL divergence is a cornerstone in many RL algorithms, such as Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), where it serves to prevent policies from drifting too far from a reference or previous policy, thus improving training stability and sample efficiency.

With the advent of powerful Large Language Models (LLMs), RL techniques, particularly Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), have become crucial for aligning LLMs with human preferences, safety guidelines, and desired behaviors. KL divergence plays a central role in RLHF pipelines, typically as a regularization term in the objective function to ensure that the fine-tuned LLM policy does not diverge excessively from its initial pre-trained state, preserving general language capabilities while adapting to specific feedback.

The authors explicitly point out that the pitfalls they identify are present in "a number of open source projects and papers," specifically naming widely adopted frameworks like TRL (Transformer Reinforcement Learning by Hugging Face) and Open Instruct, as well as being described in pseudocode from seminal works like GRPO and subsequent papers building upon them. This highlights that the issues are not obscure theoretical quirks but rather pervasive implementation errors affecting widely used and influential methodologies in the LLM and RL communities.

Unlike much research that proposes novel algorithms or architectures, this work focuses on a critical, yet often overlooked, aspect of *correctly implementing* existing, well-established theoretical concepts. By uncovering these subtle implementation bugs, the paper contributes to the robustness, reproducibility, and ultimately, the efficacy of RL and RLHF applied to LLMs. It acts as a crucial "sanity check" for current practices, ensuring that the intended mathematical properties of KL regularization are genuinely realized in practice. This also relates to a broader theme in ML research about the importance of rigorous empirical evaluation and meticulous implementation details, which can often be more impactful than new conceptual breakthroughs if they expose widespread inefficiencies or errors. The paper also touches upon the design space of regularization, relating its findings to discussions on alternative divergences, but strongly argues that the observed issues are *bugs* rather than intentional design choices.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research note is to **highlight and demonstrate several common pitfalls in the implementation of KL divergence gradient estimation, particularly as applied to reinforcement learning for large language models (LLMs).** The authors aim to clearly explain why these common implementations are incorrect and to show the practical impact of these errors, while also providing the correct ways to estimate the KL gradient.

The motivation for this work stems from observed inconsistencies and inefficiencies in real-world implementations:
- **Misconception in Gradient Calculation:** A widespread practice involves constructing a Monte-Carlo estimate of the KL divergence and then directly applying auto-differentiation (e.g., using PyTorch or Jax) to this estimate, treating it as a loss function to minimize. The authors contend that this straightforward approach "does not produce the right gradient in general," leading to the "targeted KL regularization not being enforced as intended." This is a significant issue because it means the theoretical benefits of KL regularization are not being realized.
- **Neglecting Sequential Dependencies:** For sequence generation tasks inherent to LLMs, the auto-regressive nature means that past tokens influence future tokens. Many current gradient estimates for LLM applications fail to account for this sequential dependency, resulting in only a "partial derivative of the full gradient." This omission can hinder the effectiveness of KL regularization.
- **Impact on Open-Source and Seminal Work:** The authors observe these conceptual pitfalls in popular open-source RLHF projects (like TRL and Open Instruct) and pseudocode from influential papers (e.g., GRPO), implying that these errors are widespread and potentially affecting the performance and understanding of many existing models.
- **Clarification, Not Innovation:** The paper explicitly states its purpose is not to advocate for the importance of KL divergence itself, but rather, *assuming* its necessity, to ensure it is implemented correctly. This highlights a commitment to robust scientific practice and reproducibility over novelty for novelty's sake.

In essence, the motivation is to bring theoretical rigor to practical implementation, ensuring that the tools and techniques used to build advanced AI models are fundamentally sound.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper combines rigorous theoretical analysis with empirical validation through both controlled tabular experiments and practical large language model (LLM) experiments.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Analysis

The paper starts by revisiting the definition of KL divergence and introducing three common Monte-Carlo estimates:
- **Vanilla Estimate:** $d_{KL}^{vanilla} = \log(\pi(y) / \pi_{ref}(y))$
- **Variance-Reduced Estimate:** $d_{KL}^{var-reduced} = \log(\pi(y) / \pi_{ref}(y)) + (\pi_{ref}(y) / \pi(y)) - 1$
- **Squared Estimate:** $d_{KL}^{squared} = \frac{1}{2} (\log(\pi(y) / \pi_{ref}(y)))^2$

The core of the theoretical analysis lies in demonstrating the *bias* that arises when one attempts to minimize KL divergence by simply differentiating these estimates as loss functions via auto-differentiation. The key insight provided is that the gradient of an expectation (like KL divergence) has two components: a path-wise derivative and a score function derivative. Simply differentiating the KL estimate only captures the path-wise part, leading to an incorrect or biased gradient in most cases.
- For the vanilla estimate, differentiating it yields a gradient with an expected value of zero, effectively providing no regularization.
- For the variance-reduced estimate, differentiating it yields a gradient that, in expectation, points towards minimizing the *reverse* KL divergence ($KL(\pi_{ref}, \pi)$) instead of the intended $KL(\pi, \pi_{ref})$.
- Crucially, the authors show that differentiating the *squared* KL estimate, despite the estimate itself being biased, yields an *unbiased* gradient estimate for the target $KL(\pi, \pi_{ref})$. This is then generalized to the "vanilla gradient estimate" $log(\pi(y)/\pi_{ref}(y))\nabla log\pi(y)$, which is the correct form.

The analysis is then extended to **sequential data** (relevant for LLMs). The authors explain that common token-level KL losses in LLMs lead to partial gradients because they fail to account for the auto-regressive dependencies where current tokens impact future tokens and their associated KL divergences. They then derive and propose unbiased estimates for the *full sequence-level* KL gradient, including a "vanilla sequence-level" estimate and two variance-reduced variants ("leave-one-out control variate" and "cumulative estimate").

#### 3.2. Empirical Validation (Tabular Experiments)

To substantiate their theoretical claims, the authors conduct a series of controlled experiments in a tabular multi-arm bandit setting, where analytical gradients can be computed as a "gold standard."
- **KL Divergence Estimation Accuracy:** They first validate the MSE of the vanilla, variance-reduced, and squared KL estimates, confirming that variance-reduced estimates indeed offer lower MSE for small sample sizes.
- **KL Gradient Estimation Accuracy:** They compare the MSE of different gradient estimates (incorrect vanilla, incorrect variance-reduced, correct vanilla with and without control variates) against the analytic gradient, demonstrating the bias of the "incorrect" implementations.
- **Minimizing KL Divergence:** They show how different gradient estimates perform when used in an optimization loop to minimize KL divergence, illustrating that incorrect estimates fail to properly converge to the target.
- **KL-Regularized Reward Maximization:** They set up an RL problem where KL regularization is applied alongside reward maximization. This experiment reveals that while correct vanilla estimates lead to convergence towards the intended optimal policy, the incorrect variance-reduced estimate leads to convergence to a *different* optimal policy, specifically one derived from the reverse KL divergence.

#### 3.3. Empirical Validation (LLM Experiments)

Finally, the paper demonstrates the practical impact of these pitfalls in realistic LLM settings, using Llama 3 models.
- **Low-KL Regime (KL-regularized Reward Maximization):** They fine-tune an 8B Llama 3 model on MATH prompts with string-match reward, which typically operates in a low-KL regime (policies stay close to $\pi_{ref}$ due to strong regularization). They observe that in this regime, different *correctly implemented* divergences might yield similar performance, suggesting that the choice of divergence or its precise implementation details might be less critical when regularization is very strong.
- **High-KL Regime (On-policy Distillation):** To explore a high-KL regime (where policies are expected to diverge more significantly), they conduct an on-policy distillation task, aiming to distill a 70B model ($\pi_{ref}$) into an 8B model ($\pi$) by directly minimizing KL divergence. This experiment clearly shows:
 - The correct vanilla token-level estimate successfully reduces KL divergence, while the incorrect variance-reduced token-level estimate makes little progress on the target metric.
 - Proper sequence-level gradient estimates (vanilla, cumulative, and leave-one-out variants) significantly improve the rate of KL minimization compared to token-level implementations, underscoring the importance of capturing full sequential dependencies and utilizing variance reduction techniques.

This comprehensive approach, combining analytical derivation with empirical evidence across different scales and problem types, robustly supports the paper's central claims about the prevalence and impact of these KL gradient estimation pitfalls.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper unveils two major pitfalls in common implementations of KL divergence gradient estimation, particularly relevant for RL and LLM training, and provides empirical evidence for their impact.

#### 4.1. Pitfall 1: Differentiating KL Estimates as Loss Functions Leads to Incorrect Gradients.

- **Vanilla KL Estimate:** Simply differentiating the Monte-Carlo estimate $\log(\pi(y) / \pi_{ref}(y))$ as a loss function yields a gradient whose expectation is zero. This means such an implementation provides no effective regularization; it amounts to adding zero-mean noise to the optimization, hindering the intended policy alignment.
- **Variance-Reduced KL Estimate (Commonly Used):** Differentiating the popular variance-reduced KL estimate (which adds a control variate term to the vanilla estimate) results in a gradient that, in expectation, minimizes the *reverse* KL divergence, $KL(\pi_{ref}, \pi)$, rather than the desired $KL(\pi, \pi_{ref})$. While both $KL(\pi, \pi_{ref})$ and $KL(\pi_{ref}, \pi)$ converge to $\pi = \pi_{ref}$ at their minimum, their behavior during optimization, especially when combined with other objectives like reward maximization, is fundamentally different. The paper argues this is an "incidental" regularization effect, not a deliberate design choice, and leads to convergence to a different optimal policy than intended.
- **Correct Vanilla Gradient:** Intriguingly, the paper demonstrates that differentiating the *squared* KL estimate, despite the estimate itself being biased, yields an *unbiased* gradient estimate for the target $KL(\pi, \pi_{ref})$. This correct gradient form is presented as $\log(\pi(y) / \pi_{ref}(y)) \nabla \log \pi(y)$, which can also be obtained by using a stop-gradient operation on the ratio term (i.e., $\text{sg}(\log(\pi(y)/\pi_{ref}(y))) \nabla \log\pi(y)$). This is highlighted as the correct way to estimate the KL gradient.

#### 4.2. Pitfall 2: Token-Level Losses Produce Partial Gradients for Sequences.

- For sequence generation (e.g., in LLMs), where the objective is sequence-level KL divergence, simply summing token-level KL gradients (even if each token-level gradient is correctly computed) results in a *partial* gradient. This is because it fails to account for the auto-regressive dependency: a token $y_t$ impacts future tokens $y_s$ ($s > t$) and, consequently, their associated KL divergences.
- The paper derives and proposes **unbiased sequence-level KL gradient estimates** that account for these temporal dependencies. These include a "vanilla sequence-level" estimate (which is essentially applying the correct single-variable gradient form to the entire sequence) and two variance-reduced variants: a "leave-one-out control variate" approach and a "cumulative estimate" (reminiscent of cumulative reward in RL).

#### 4.3. Empirical Validation Results

- **Tabular Experiments:**
 - Confirmed that incorrect gradient implementations (differentiating vanilla or variance-reduced estimates) exhibit significant bias and higher MSE compared to correct vanilla gradient estimates.
 - Showed that using the incorrect vanilla estimate for KL minimization leads to divergence, while the incorrect variance-reduced estimate minimizes the reverse KL, leading to a different convergence point than the desired KL.
 - In KL-regularized reward maximization, correct vanilla gradient estimates converge to the intended optimal policy, whereas the incorrect variance-reduced estimate converges to the optimal policy of the *reverse* KL regularization.
- **LLM Experiments:**
 - In the **low-KL regime** (e.g., strong regularization in RLHF), the performance-KL trade-off was similar across different (correctly implemented) KL regularization methods. This suggests that the precise choice of divergence or gradient estimation method might be less critical when policies are constrained to stay very close to the reference.
 - In the **high-KL regime** (e.g., on-policy distillation where policies might be far apart):
 - The correct vanilla token-level gradient estimate effectively decreased KL divergence, while the incorrect variance-reduced token-level estimate showed little progress. This underscores that in high-KL settings, the differences between correct and incorrect implementations become critically apparent.
 - Furthermore, properly implemented **sequence-level KL gradient estimates** (vanilla sequence-level, leave-one-out, and cumulative) significantly outperformed even the best token-level implementations in minimizing sequence-level KL divergence, demonstrating the importance of capturing full temporal dependencies and applying appropriate variance reduction techniques for better learning rates and convergence.

In summary, the paper's findings are a crucial warning to the RL and LLM communities: common implementation shortcuts for KL divergence gradients are often fundamentally flawed, leading to biased estimates, unintended regularization effects, and suboptimal training outcomes, particularly in scenarios where policies are allowed to diverge significantly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance and potential impact of this work are substantial, touching upon the foundational integrity and practical efficacy of reinforcement learning applied to large language models.

- **Correcting Widespread Errors:** The most immediate and profound impact is the identification and clear explanation of widespread, yet subtle, implementation errors in KL divergence gradient estimation. By explicitly naming popular open-source projects and pseudocode in influential papers, the authors highlight that these are not niche issues but rather pervasive pitfalls that could be affecting a significant portion of current RLHF and related LLM fine-tuning efforts. Correcting these fundamental errors can lead to more robust, efficient, and theoretically sound training procedures.

- **Improved Reproducibility and Reliability:** Different implementations of KL regularization can lead to vastly different optimization landscapes and final model behaviors. By clarifying the correct methods, this paper enhances the reproducibility of RL/LLM research. Researchers and practitioners will have a clearer guide on how to implement KL regularization faithfully, leading to more reliable experimental results and a better understanding of why certain approaches succeed or fail.

- **Enhanced LLM Alignment and Performance:** For applications like RLHF, where the goal is to align LLMs with complex human preferences or values, KL regularization is a critical component for maintaining model coherence and preventing "over-optimization" on a narrow reward signal. If the KL regularization is not enforced as intended, the fine-tuning process may be less effective, leading to models that are either under-regularized (drifting too far) or inconsistently regularized. Correct implementations could unlock better-aligned and more performant LLMs.

- **Practical Guidance for Developers:** The paper provides actionable insights and specific correct formulas for implementing KL gradients, both for single-step and sequential (LLM) scenarios. This is invaluable for framework developers and machine learning engineers who are building or contributing to RL/LLM training pipelines. The discussion of variance reduction techniques for sequence-level gradients further offers practical ways to improve training stability and speed.

- **Deeper Theoretical Understanding:** Beyond immediate practical benefits, the work contributes to a deeper theoretical understanding of gradient estimation for composite objectives, particularly when the objective function depends on the learned distribution in complex ways (both through sampling and the integrand). It underscores the importance of the score function component of gradients in such settings, which is often overlooked in simpler supervised learning contexts.

- **Re-evaluation of Existing Works:** The findings suggest that some past research results where KL regularization was used might need to be re-evaluated, considering that the true regularization effect might have been different from what was assumed. This could lead to new insights into why certain RLHF methods performed as they did, and potentially inspire improvements.

- **Focus on High-KL Regimes:** The empirical finding that the differences between correct and incorrect implementations become stark in "high-KL regimes" (where the policy deviates significantly from the reference) is particularly important. Many real-world RL problems, including complex LLM fine-tuning tasks, might naturally operate in or transition into such regimes. This highlights the importance of rigorous gradient estimation not just for theoretical purity but for practical performance in challenging settings.

In essence, this paper serves as a vital diagnostic and corrective piece for the rapidly evolving field of RL for LLMs. By ensuring the correctness of a fundamental building block, it paves the way for more robust, predictable, and effective development of advanced AI systems.
