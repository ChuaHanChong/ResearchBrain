# On Training in Imagination

> [!summary] Summary
> A theoretical framework is presented that decomposes the return error in model-based reinforcement learning's "training in imagination" paradigm into distinct dynamics and reward model contributions. The work also provides a closed-form solution for optimally allocating data samples between dynamics and reward learning, along with an analysis of how policy optimization tolerates different types of reward noise.

## Problem

- Existing theoretical bounds for model-based RL often treat learned dynamics and reward models as a single entity, not providing independent quantification of their error contributions.
- There is a lack of principled guidance for optimally allocating a fixed data budget between acquiring dynamics transitions and obtaining reward annotations, especially given varying costs.
- The impact of noisy or biased reward signals on policy optimization, particularly for algorithms like REINFORCE, and how to best manage reward fidelity, is not well understood theoretically.

## Method

- The authors derive a novel ==simulation error bound== that explicitly decomposes the return gap into separate, independently quantifiable dynamics-model error and reward-model error components.
- A ==closed-form expression== is developed for the optimal ratio of dynamics to reward samples that minimizes the return-error bound, considering differential sample costs and ==power-law error scaling==.
- The behavior of the ==REINFORCE policy gradient== estimator is analyzed under both zero-mean additive noise and systematic reward bias, deriving conditions for optimal reward fidelity allocation.

## Results

- A new decomposed upper bound on the return gap was derived, consistently holding in numerical experiments but showing significant looseness in practice, overestimating the actual gap by **29x to 286x**.
- Empirical evaluation demonstrated that reward model error can decay substantially faster with data (exponent **0.96**) than dynamics model error (exponent **0.11**) in synthetic environments.
- The analysis showed that the optimal strategy for managing reward fidelity with zero-mean noise depends on the specific cost-variance relationship, and systematic bias fundamentally differs by introducing an unremovable gradient bias.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Lower Lipschitz constants for the learned dynamics, reward, and policy functions lead to tighter return-error bounds, highlighting a desirable property for learned representations and their associated models.
> - The optimal allocation of data between dynamics and reward learning depends on the relative costs of samples, the exponents of their error decay, and the sensitivities of the return to these errors.
> - Zero-mean reward noise does not bias the REINFORCE gradient but increases its variance, which can be mitigated by more rollouts, whereas systematic reward bias introduces an irreducible gradient bias.
