# On Training in Imagination

> [!summary] Summary
> A theoretical framework is presented that decomposes the return error in model-based reinforcement learning's "training in imagination" paradigm into distinct dynamics and reward model contributions. The work also provides a closed-form solution for optimally allocating data samples between dynamics and reward learning, along with an analysis of how policy optimization tolerates different types of reward noise.

## Problem

- Existing theoretical bounds for model-based RL often treat learned dynamics and reward models as a single entity, not providing independent quantification of their error contributions.
- There is a lack of principled guidance for optimally allocating a fixed data budget between acquiring dynamics transitions and obtaining reward annotations, especially given varying costs.
- The impact of noisy or biased reward signals on policy optimization, particularly for algorithms like REINFORCE, and how to best manage reward fidelity, is not well understood theoretically.

## Method

- The authors derive a novel ==simulation error bound== that explicitly decomposes the return gap into separate, independently quantifiable dynamics-model error and reward-model error components.
- A ==closed-form expression== is developed for the optimal ratio of dynamics to reward samples that minimizes the return-error bound, considering differential sample costs and ==power-law error scaling==.
- The behavior of the ==REINFORCE policy gradient== estimator is analyzed under both zero-mean additive noise and systematic reward bias, deriving conditions for optimal reward fidelity allocation.

## Results

- A new decomposed upper bound on the return gap was derived, consistently holding in numerical experiments but showing significant looseness in practice, overestimating the actual gap by **29x to 286x**.
- Empirical evaluation demonstrated that reward model error can decay substantially faster with data (exponent **0.96**) than dynamics model error (exponent **0.11**) in synthetic environments.
- The analysis showed that the optimal strategy for managing reward fidelity with zero-mean noise depends on the specific cost-variance relationship, and systematic bias fundamentally differs by introducing an unremovable gradient bias.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Lower Lipschitz constants for the learned dynamics, reward, and policy functions lead to tighter return-error bounds, highlighting a desirable property for learned representations and their associated models.
> - The optimal allocation of data between dynamics and reward learning depends on the relative costs of samples, the exponents of their error decay, and the sensitivities of the return to these errors.
> - Zero-mean reward noise does not bias the REINFORCE gradient but increases its variance, which can be mitigated by more rollouts, whereas systematic reward bias introduces an irreducible gradient bias.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper contributes to the field of model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL), specifically focusing on the "training in imagination" paradigm. In this paradigm, a policy is optimized based on trajectories generated by a learned dynamics model and evaluated by a learned reward model, without direct interaction with the true environment during policy updates. This approach has gained prominence with recent successes in agents like Dreamer 3, Dreamer 4, and AlphaZero-style methods in board games and Atari, which demonstrate strong empirical performance on complex control tasks.

Prior theoretical work, such as the simulation lemma by Kearns and Singh and subsequent analyses by Asadi et al. [2018b], has provided bounds on the value gap between a true and approximate Markov Decision Process (MDP). However, these existing bounds often treat the learned dynamics model and reward predictor as a single, coupled object. Critically, they do not typically assign independently controllable coefficients to errors stemming from the dynamics model versus the reward model. Furthermore, the theoretical literature has not adequately addressed how a fixed sample budget should be optimally allocated between acquiring dynamics transitions and obtaining reward annotations, especially given that reward samples can be significantly more expensive (e.g., human preference labels).

This paper aims to address these identified gaps by providing a theoretical framework that decomposes return error into distinct dynamics-model and reward-model components. It also investigates the implications of these separate error sources for representation learning, sample budget allocation, and the robustness of policy optimization to noisy or biased reward signals. In doing so, it extends the theoretical understanding of MBRL beyond prior simulation bounds and integrates considerations of data cost and quality, which are increasingly relevant in practical applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from several open questions regarding the "training in imagination" paradigm, despite its empirical successes. The authors identify four specific questions that guide their investigation:

1. **Error attribution**: To what extent do errors in the learned dynamics model contribute to the overall return gap, versus errors in the learned reward model? Existing simulation-lemma-style bounds often do not provide a clear independent quantification of these two error sources. A detailed understanding of their individual contributions is crucial for targeted model improvement.
2. **Representation properties**: What characteristics of learned representations and the functions operating on them (dynamics, reward, policy) can lead to tighter bounds on return error? Identifying such properties could inform the design of more effective latent spaces and model architectures for MBRL. This relates to concepts like Lipschitz continuity and temporal smoothness in latent space.
3. **Budget allocation**: Given a finite budget for data collection, how should this budget be optimally split between acquiring dynamics transitions (e.g., state-action-next-state tuples) and obtaining reward annotations (e.g., human feedback for a given state-action pair)? Reward samples are often more expensive, necessitating a principled approach to this trade-off that current theoretical models lack.
4. **Reward fidelity**: How does policy optimization algorithms, specifically REINFORCE, tolerate noisy or biased reward annotations? In practical settings, obtaining perfectly accurate rewards can be costly or impossible. This raises a practical question: is it more beneficial to acquire many cheap, noisy annotations, or fewer, more expensive but accurate ones? Understanding the impact of different noise characteristics (zero-mean vs. biased) is critical for effective reward modeling.

By addressing these questions, the research aims to provide theoretical foundations and practical guidance for designing, analyzing, and optimizing model-based reinforcement learning systems that rely on training in imagination.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper combines theoretical derivations with numerical experiments to validate assumptions and predictions.

**Theoretical Approach:**

1. **Simulation Error Decomposition (Lemma 1, Corollary 1, Proposition 1):**
- The authors extend the work of Asadi et al. [2018b] by deriving a simulation error bound (Lemma 1) that explicitly incorporates a learned reward model. This bound provides separate, independently controllable coefficients for dynamics-model error (ε_dyn) and reward-model error (ε_rew) under Lipschitz assumptions for the dynamics (f), reward (r), and policy (π) functions. The bound holds under the condition γL_f(1 + L_π) < 1, where γ is the discount factor and L_f, L_π are Lipschitz constants.
- Corollary 1 analyzes this bound, demonstrating that the dynamics-error coefficient is monotonically non-decreasing with the Lipschitz constants L_f, L_r, and L_π. This identifies lower Lipschitz constants as a representation desideratum for tightening the return-error bound.
- Proposition 1 connects this Lipschitz perspective to the temporal-straightening objective of Wang et al.. It shows that the temporal-straightening curvature loss can be upper-bounded by a function of the Lipschitz constant of the latent velocity map, suggesting that a smoother latent velocity map (lower Lipschitz constant) promotes "straight" latent trajectories.

2. **Optimal Sample Allocation (Theorem 1):**
- The paper considers a scenario where dynamics-transition samples and reward samples have different per-sample costs (c_dyn, c_rew) and their respective errors decay according to power-law scaling laws: ε_dyn(N_dyn) = A_d * N_dyn^(-α) and ε_rew(N_rew) = A_r * N_rew^(-β).
- Theorem 1 derives a closed-form expression for the optimal ratio of dynamics samples to reward samples (N*_dyn / N*_rew) that minimizes the return-error bound from Lemma 1, given a fixed total sample budget. This ratio depends on the power-law exponents (α, β), the per-sample costs (c_dyn, c_rew), and the Lipschitz coefficients derived from Lemma 1.

3. **Cost-Effective Learning from Noisy Rewards (Theorem 2, Corollary 2, Proposition 2):**
- **Zero-mean noise:** Theorem 2 analyzes the behavior of the REINFORCE policy gradient estimator under additive, zero-mean, i.i.d. reward noise. It demonstrates that such noise does not bias the gradient estimator but adds a variance term that decreases with the number of rollouts (K). The variance inflation is bounded by a term proportional to σ^2_η * H * W^2_H / (K(1-γ)^2).
- **Optimal fidelity for noise-induced variance:** Corollary 2 builds on Theorem 2 by introducing a per-rollout cost 'c' for acquiring reward annotations and modeling the noise variance as σ^2_η(c). It reduces the problem of optimal fidelity allocation to minimizing a function Φ(c) = c * σ^2_η(c). The authors illustrate this with three examples: power-law fidelity, bounded fidelity, and irreducible noise floor, showing how the optimal strategy (more fidelity vs. more quantity) depends on the shape of Φ(c).
- **Systematic bias:** Proposition 2 addresses the case of systematic reward bias. It shows that non-zero reward bias introduces a gradient bias that cannot be removed by averaging over multiple trajectories, emphasizing the critical distinction between zero-mean noise and systematic bias.

**Numerical Experiments:**

1. **Numerical Illustration of Lemma 1 (Section 3.1):**
- The derived bound (Equation 1) is empirically tested on two benchmarks: a synthetic environment with globally Lipschitz dynamics and rewards, and a Linear-Quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) environment with locally Lipschitz quadratic rewards. The ratio R = LHS/RHS (realized return gap / theoretical bound) is computed for 525 configurations. This experiment verifies that the bound holds across diverse settings.

2. **Scaling of Dynamics and Reward Errors (Section 4.1):**
- This experiment investigates the power-law scaling assumptions of Theorem 1. Dynamics and reward errors are measured as a function of training sample counts (N_dyn, N_rew) in a synthetic continuous-control environment. The authors fit power-law curves ε ~ N^-α to the empirical data to estimate the exponents α and β, and the constants A_d, A_r.

3. **Empirical Evaluation of Equation (4) (Section 4.2):**
- The optimal sample ratio formula from Theorem 1 is empirically evaluated. The proportionality aspect (N*_dyn / N*_rew ∝ ε*_dyn / ε*_rew) is tested by replacing global Lipschitz constants with realized value-function sensitivities. The experiment assesses the looseness of the multiplier when instantiated with global Lipschitz constants versus realized sensitivities, primarily using LQG configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings addressing the stated objectives:

1. **Error Attribution and Representation Properties:**
- **Decomposition of Return Error (Lemma 1):** The core theoretical finding is a decomposed upper bound on the return gap between a true MDP and a learned MDP. This bound clearly separates the contributions of reward-model error (ε_rew) and dynamics-model error (ε_dyn), each with its own coefficient. Specifically, `J(π, M) - J(π, M̂) ≤ (1/(1-γ)) ε_rew + (γL_r(1+L_π) / ((1-γ)(1-γL_f(1+L_π)))) ε_dyn`. This decomposition allows for independent analysis and optimization of each error source.
- **Lipschitz Constants as Representation Desideratum (Corollary 1):** The dynamics-error coefficient in Lemma 1 is shown to be monotonically non-decreasing with the Lipschitz constants L_f (dynamics), L_r (reward), and L_π (policy). This implies that representations which inherently lead to lower Lipschitz constants for these learned models will tighten the return-error bound, suggesting a theoretical basis for designing "smooth" latent spaces.
- **Connection to Temporal Straightening (Proposition 1):** The temporal-straightening curvature loss, an objective function designed to encourage linear trajectories in latent space, is upper-bounded by a function of the Lipschitz constant of the latent velocity map. This formalizes a link between the smoothness of the latent velocity map and the "straightness" of imagined rollouts, connecting representation learning objectives to the stability of long-horizon predictions.
- **Empirical Bound Validation (Section 3.1):** Numerical experiments on synthetic and LQG benchmarks consistently demonstrated that the derived bound (Equation 1) holds for all tested configurations. However, the typical bound was observed to be loose, overestimating the actual return gap by factors ranging from approximately 29x (LQG) to 286x (synthetic) at the median.

2. **Optimal Sample Allocation:**
- **Closed-Form Optimal Ratio (Theorem 1):** Under power-law error scaling assumptions for dynamics and reward models (ε ~ N^-α), a closed-form expression for the optimal ratio of dynamics samples to reward samples (N*_dyn / N*_rew) was derived. This ratio is proportional to (α/β) * (c_rew/c_dyn) * (dynamics_coefficient) * (ε*_dyn / ε*_rew). This provides a quantitative rule for allocating resources based on learning rates, costs, and model sensitivities.
- **Factors Influencing Allocation:** The theorem indicates that the optimal allocation shifts towards reward samples if reward-sample cost (c_rew) decreases, reward error decays faster (higher β relative to α), or the planning horizon is shorter (smaller γ).
- **Empirical Power-Law Scaling (Section 4.1):** Experiments verified that dynamics and reward errors in a synthetic environment follow distinct power-law scaling laws. The reward error was found to decay significantly faster (exponent β ≈ 0.96) than the dynamics error (exponent α ≈ 0.11), with a ratio of approximately 9:1. This observation indicates that reward models become accurate with fewer samples than dynamics models in the tested setup.
- **Empirical Evaluation of Allocation Formula (Section 4.2):** The proportionality N*_dyn / N*_rew ∝ ε*_dyn / ε*_rew was largely validated empirically when global Lipschitz constants in the multiplier were replaced with realized value-function sensitivities. This suggests the formula's predictive content lies primarily in the proportionality. However, instantiating the multiplier with the global Lipschitz constants from Lemma 1 resulted in a significant overestimation (approximately three orders of magnitude) of the realized optimal sample ratios, indicating the looseness of worst-case global bounds in practice.

3. **Reward Fidelity:**
- **REINFORCE with Zero-Mean Noise (Theorem 2):** For finite-horizon REINFORCE, additive zero-mean i.i.d. reward noise was proven not to bias the policy gradient estimator. It only adds a variance term, which decreases with the number of independent rollouts (K).
- **Optimal Fidelity Allocation (Corollary 2):** When a budget needs to be split between reward fidelity (lower noise per annotation) and quantity (more rollouts), the optimal strategy depends on the function Φ(c) = c * σ^2_η(c). Examples demonstrated varied optimal choices:
- **Power-law fidelity (σ^2_η(c) = A c^-p):** If p > 1, higher fidelity is optimal; if p < 1, more cheap, noisy rollouts are optimal; if p = 1, cost-fidelity trade-off is constant.
- **Bounded fidelity (σ^2_η(c) = σ^2_0 (1 - c/c_max)):** Both extremely cheap (c → 0) and noise-free (c = c_max) annotations minimize the bound.
- **Irreducible noise floor (σ^2_η(c) = σ^2_floor + A/c):** The cheapest annotations (c → 0) are optimal.
- **Impact of Systematic Bias (Proposition 2):** Systematic reward bias, unlike zero-mean noise, introduces a gradient bias that cannot be eliminated by averaging over more trajectories. This highlights the fundamental difference between these two types of reward corruption.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a more granular theoretical understanding of the "training in imagination" paradigm in model-based reinforcement learning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized in several areas:

1. **Improved Model Analysis and Design:** By decomposing return error into distinct dynamics and reward model components, the paper offers a framework for analyzing the performance bottlenecks of MBRL systems. This decomposition allows practitioners to attribute performance gaps more precisely and focus development efforts on improving the most critical model component. The identified representation desideratum (lower Lipschitz constants) provides a theoretical motivation for developing smoother latent spaces and employing Lipschitz regularization techniques in model learning, potentially leading to more stable and reliable long-horizon planning.

2. **Principled Resource Allocation:** The derived optimal sample allocation rule (Theorem 1) offers practical guidance for managing data acquisition budgets in MBRL. It quantifies the trade-off between collecting dynamics data and reward annotations, considering their respective costs and learning rates. This is particularly relevant in domains where reward collection is expensive (e.g., human feedback in RLHF, expert demonstrations in robotics). The empirical observation that reward errors can decay much faster than dynamics errors suggests that, depending on costs and exponents, significant resources may be justifiably allocated to dynamics learning.

3. **Guidance for Reward Modeling Strategies:** The analysis of noisy rewards provides insights into how to handle imperfect reward signals. The distinction between zero-mean noise and systematic bias is critical: while the former can be mitigated by collecting more data (more rollouts), the latter introduces a fundamental gradient bias that cannot be averaged away. Corollary 2 offers concrete decision-making criteria for choosing reward annotation fidelity based on the cost-variance relationship, which can directly inform the design of reward collection pipelines in practice.

4. **Advancement of MBRL Theory:** This work extends foundational simulation lemma theory by explicitly modeling learned rewards and providing insights into the propagation of errors from both dynamics and reward models. While acknowledging the looseness of worst-case Lipschitz bounds, the work highlights their utility in establishing proportionality and general trends, pushing the theoretical understanding closer to practical application.

In summary, this paper contributes to both the theoretical foundations and practical application of model-based reinforcement learning by disaggregating error sources, providing guidance on resource allocation, and characterizing the effects of reward signal quality. These insights can inform the development of more efficient, robust, and interpretable MBRL systems.
