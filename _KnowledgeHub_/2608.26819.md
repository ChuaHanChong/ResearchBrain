# CLIPPER: Replayable Shortlisted Optimization for Repeated Spatial Coverage Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> CLIPPER is a greedy coverage planner for municipal micromobility parking that shortlists candidates into bounded per-round pools, yet still recomputes exact marginal gains and checks every hard constraint (exclusions, locks, spacing, caps) before each insertion, plus a replay record and a screening bound on omitted gain. Across three cities it stays within 0.245 percentage points of full-set greedy while running 13.6-28.9x faster.

## Problem

- Full-set greedy reoptimization costs tens of seconds per policy alternative at city scale, blocking rapid what-if comparison of edited planning states.
- Lazy, stochastic, and thresholded greedy speedups keep their own constraint models and do not enforce locks, area caps, conflict classes, and network spacing together.
- Dynamic submodular methods assume a cardinality constraint and maintain a solution across updates, so they cannot recompute each recorded municipal policy state exactly.

## Method

- ==Restricted selection==: candidates are partitioned into proposal groups, ranked once by singleton coverage, backfilled into a bounded pool, then scored by exact current marginals with full feasibility checks.
- Two budget modes: **CLIPPER-F** gives every group a fixed width K, while **CLIPPER-A** spreads one shared slot budget toward groups with more feasible candidates and higher singleton scores.
- Audit machinery: a ==cached-singleton residual screening bound== upper-bounds the pool's omitted gain per round to trigger expansion, plus offline full-set scans and a fingerprinted replay record.

## Results

- CLIPPER-F at K=**1024** averages a **0.245**, **0.003**, and **0.001** percentage-point coverage gap in Braunschweig, Munich, and Berlin, with **13.6x**, **15.4x**, and **28.9x** lower rollout time.
- CLIPPER-A with **8192** slots runs in **9-15%** of the relaxed-cap control's time, with mean gaps of **1.82**, **0.12**, and **0.27** percentage points across the three cities.
- Per-state speedups span **7.4-84.3x** and drop sharply once network spacing activates at E5; **99** full-data reruns reproduced identical coverage, counts, and termination flags.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separate the approximation from the constraint model: restrict which candidates get evaluated, but keep gains exact and feasibility checks complete, so guarantees survive.
> - Shortlisting quality is instance-specific; the shortlist width should be calibrated per city against a same-policy full-set control, not fixed universally.
> - Speed comparisons only mean something under matched policy; comparing a relaxed-cap fast method against a strict-cap control would confuse allocation rules with candidate restriction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of spatial decision support systems, specifically focusing on facility location problems and submodular optimization, with an application to urban micromobility planning. The problem of optimal facility placement to maximize coverage under various constraints is a well-established area, often addressed using models like the Maximal Covering Location Problem (MCLP) and broader monotone submodular function maximization.

Existing algorithmic approaches for submodular maximization include greedy methods, which are known for their approximation guarantees, and more advanced techniques designed to improve computational efficiency. These include "lazy greedy" algorithms that reduce marginal-query costs by skipping redundant gain calculations, as well as stochastic and thresholded greedy variants that trade query cost for approximation guarantees under their specific constraint models. Dynamic and prediction-augmented methods also exist, aiming to maintain solutions across sequences of element updates, though the paper notes that the closest dynamic methods typically assume simpler cardinality constraints, which do not align with the complex, multifaceted constraints of municipal planning.

Prior work in micromobility planning has explored aspects such as geofenced parking spaces and strategic facility placement, often using optimization or reinforcement learning techniques. However, the existing solutions frequently face computational challenges when applied to city-scale scenarios with rapidly changing operational requirements and complex, explicit policy constraints. Full-set greedy algorithms, while providing a benchmark for solution quality, can be computationally intensive, requiring tens of seconds for a single city-scale plan. This latency impedes iterative "what-if" exploration and rapid comparison of policy alternatives, which are critical for municipal planning processes.

CLIPPER addresses this gap by aiming to provide low-latency feedback for repeated spatial coverage planning under a comprehensive set of real-world municipal constraints, including geofenced exclusions, mandatory retained sites, spacing rules, and area-level caps. Unlike methods that dynamically update a solution, CLIPPER recomputes each recorded state, emphasizing deterministic replayability and explicit constraint enforcement within a bounded candidate evaluation framework. This positions CLIPPER as a practical tool for planning support systems, where transparency, auditability, and rapid iteration are as important as solution quality.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an optimization method, CLIPPER, capable of generating feasible spatial coverage plans for micromobility parking zones rapidly, specifically within seconds, for city-scale applications. This needs to be achieved while adhering to a complex set of operational constraints derived from municipal requirements and ensuring that the planning process is replayable and auditable.

The motivation for CLIPPER stems from practical challenges encountered in municipal micromobility planning, particularly through collaboration with the City of Braunschweig. Operational requirements in this context involve sophisticated policy rules such as:

- **Geofenced exclusions:** Areas where micromobility devices are not permitted.
- **Mandatory retained sites:** Specific locations that must be included in any plan.
- **Spacing rules:** Minimum distances between deployed facilities.
- **Area-level caps:** Limits on the number of facilities within defined administrative or geographical zones.

A significant operational challenge is that any revision to these policies—even minor edits like temporary no-return zones for events—necessitates the generation of a new feasible plan. Using full-set greedy optimization for such scenarios proves time-consuming, requiring "tens of seconds per alternative at city scale." This latency creates a barrier to effective "what-if" exploration and iterative deliberation, which are recognized as crucial components of planning support systems. Planners require quick feedback to compare multiple feasible alternatives under varying constraints and demand patterns.

Therefore, the core motivations are to:

1. **Reduce computation time:** Achieve "seconds-level feedback" for city-scale planning, moving away from "tens of seconds."
2. **Maintain explicit constraint enforcement:** Ensure that every encoded policy constraint (e.g., exclusions, locks, spacing, caps) is rigorously checked and satisfied during the optimization process, guaranteeing plan feasibility.
3. **Enable replayability:** Develop a system that can deterministically reproduce a planning state and its resulting plan, given identical inputs and configurations. This supports transparency and allows for post-hoc analysis and validation of decisions.
4. **Support auditability:** Provide mechanisms to inspect the optimization process, including the ability to measure "missed-gain" (the difference between the chosen candidate's gain and the best possible gain from the full candidate set) through offline audits or online screening bounds.
5. **Facilitate "what-if" exploration:** Allow planners to rapidly compare multiple policy scenarios and understand the impact of constraint revisions on coverage and facility placement, thereby aiding deliberation and decision-making.

By addressing these motivations, CLIPPER aims to transform micromobility parking zone design from a slow, batch-oriented process into a rapid, interactive, and transparent one.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CLIPPER (Constraint-exact Low-latency Iterative Planning with Pooled Evaluation and Replay) is designed to address the need for rapid, constrained spatial coverage planning. Its methodology centers on a restricted selection process combined with exact feasibility checks and mechanisms for auditability and replay.

#### 3.1. Planning Model

The underlying planning model uses a monotone submodular objective function to maximize demand coverage.
- Let `V` be the set of candidate locations and `D` be the demand points, each with a non-negative weight `w_d`.
- Each candidate `e ∈ V` covers a subset of demand points `C_e ⊆ D`.
- The objective function for a selected set of facilities `S ⊆ V` is `f(S) = Σ_{d∈D} w_d * 1[d ∈ ∪_{e∈S} C_e]`, where `1[·]` is an indicator function. This value is typically reported as a percentage of total demand weight.

A "recorded scenario" `Ω` defines the operational context, including:
- `V_Ω`: The set of active candidates.
- `L(Ω)`: A subset of `V_Ω` representing mandatory "locked" sites.
- `I(Ω)`: A family of feasible subsets of `V_Ω`. A set `S` is feasible if it contains all locked sites, excludes candidates within specified exclusion zones (unless locked), respects a global budget and accounting-group specific caps, permits at most one site per conflict class, and satisfies minimum network spacing rules. Locks override exclusions.

CLIPPER aims to find a feasible set `S ∈ I(Ω)` that maximizes `f(S)`. The performance of CLIPPER is compared against a full-set greedy algorithm operating under the identical policy.

#### 3.2. Restricted Selection with Exact Feasibility Checks

CLIPPER employs a restricted selection strategy to accelerate the optimization process.
1. **Candidate Grouping and Initial Ranking:** Candidates are initially partitioned into "proposal groups" (e.g., using K-means clustering). Within each group, candidates are ranked once based on their singleton coverage `f({e})`, using stable tie-breaking. This pre-computation reduces repetitive work.
2. **Iterative Selection:** The optimization proceeds iteratively, starting with an incumbent solution `S_{t-1}` (initialized as `S_0 = L(Ω)`). In each round `t`:
 - Candidates that are already selected or invalid (e.g., in an exclusion zone unless locked) are filtered.
 - A "bounded pool" `P_t` of candidates is formed by backfilling from the pre-ranked candidates within their respective groups. This pool is a subset of the currently feasible and unselected candidates.
 - From `P_t`, CLIPPER selects the candidate `e_t` that maximizes the marginal gain `Δ(e | S_{t-1}) = f(S_{t-1} ∪ {e}) - f(S_{t-1})`. Crucially, this selection is subject to an **exact feasibility check**: `S_{t-1} ∪ {e}` must be a member of `I(Ω)`. This ensures that every selected candidate maintains the overall feasibility of the solution according to all hard constraints.
 - The incumbent solution is updated to `S_t = S_{t-1} ∪ {e_t}`.
 This process continues until the budget is filled or no further feasible positive marginal gains can be found within the pool. This approach guarantees feasibility but not global optimality.

Two variants of CLIPPER are introduced based on how the bounded pool `P_t` is constructed and how accounting group caps are handled:
- **CLIPPER-F (Fixed per-group width):** Each proposal group is allocated a fixed number of candidate slots, `K`. This variant uses a policy that distributes the residual facility budget equally among accounting groups.
- **CLIPPER-A (Shared candidate budget):** This variant uses a single, total budget for candidates across all groups, distributing slots based on factors like the number of feasible candidates in a group and their maximum singleton scores. CLIPPER-A is evaluated under a "coverage-prioritized" policy that relaxes candidate-count-weighted accounting caps by a factor of `λ = 1.5`, while maintaining the global facility budget.

The computational work for marginal gain calculations is `O(c Σ W_t)` for CLIPPER (where `c` is max demand points covered by one facility, and `W_t` is pool size in round `t`), which is contrasted with `O(cT|V_Ω|)` for full-set greedy (where `T` is number of insertions, `|V_Ω|` is total candidates).

#### 3.3. Online Screening versus Offline Audit

To assess the quality of the restricted selection, CLIPPER includes auditing mechanisms:
- **Exact Omitted Gain (`δ_t`):** This is the difference between the best possible feasible marginal gain from the complete candidate set (`m*_t`) and the actual marginal gain of the chosen candidate (`m_t`). Computing `m*_t` requires a full-set scan and is computationally expensive; thus, it is typically used offline or at specific checkpoints and excluded from reported rollout times.
- **Conservative Residual Screening Bound (`ˆδ_t`):** For online monitoring, a cheaper upper bound `u_t` on possible gains from the full set is calculated using cached singleton scores. The bound `ˆδ_t = max{0, u_t - m_t}` indicates a potential missed gain without requiring a full marginal recomputation. This bound can trigger pool expansion or a full offline audit if it suggests that significant gains might be missed.

#### 3.4. Replay Contract

A core feature is the "replay contract," which stores all necessary information to deterministically reproduce an optimization run. This includes the scenario and policy settings, aggregate counters, termination status, and a selected-set fingerprint. Fixed ranking rules and deterministic tie-breaking ensure that identical inputs yield identical outputs, enabling transparent comparison of different policy states over time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CLIPPER was conducted using real-world micromobility trip data from three German cities: Braunschweig (BS), Munich (MUC), and Berlin (BER). The experiments utilized constructed scenarios (`E_0` to `E_{10}`) forming a stress chain, gradually introducing increasing numbers of exclusions, locked sites, network spacing constraints, and hotspot exclusions. Performance was measured by coverage (percentage of demand weight covered) and rollout time (time taken for pool construction, exact gain calculations, feasibility checks, and selection).

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup Details

- **Data:** 243,649 (BS), 4,958,963 (MUC), and 12,306,947 (BER) trips. Demand points were aggregated from snapped origin/destination endpoints.
- **Candidate Grids:** OSM-derived sidewalk/footway/parking support, 10m resolution (BS) and 25m (MUC, BER).
- **Proposal Groups:** 16-36 K-means clusters, each containing 1750-2750 candidates.
- **Coverage Parameters:** 200m coverage radius, facility budgets of 600 (BS), 1200 (MUC), 1600 (BER).
- **Control:** Full-set greedy algorithm under the *same policy* as the respective CLIPPER variant.
- **Hardware:** Intel Core Ultra 9 285K.

#### 4.2. CLIPPER-F Performance (Fixed per-group width, balanced policy)

- **Coverage Gaps:** For a fixed pool width `K = 1024`, CLIPPER-F consistently achieved coverage very close to the full-set greedy control. The mean coverage gap over all 11 states was within **0.245 percentage points** (Braunschweig), **0.003 percentage points** (Munich), and **0.001 percentage points** (Berlin). This indicates minimal loss in solution quality compared to the full-set approach.
- **Rollout Time and Speedup:** CLIPPER-F significantly reduced planning rollout times. Mean rollout times across the 11 states were **1.762 seconds** (Braunschweig), **1.493 seconds** (Munich), and **1.825 seconds** (Berlin). This represents a substantial speedup compared to the full-set control, which took **24.029 seconds** (Braunschweig), **22.943 seconds** (Munich), and **52.685 seconds** (Berlin). The speedups ranged from **13.6×** (Braunschweig) to **28.9×** (Berlin).
- **Chain Sensitivity (Table 2):** Per-state analysis showed that gaps remained small (e.g., -0.038 to 0.377 pp in Braunschweig). Speedups were highest in simpler scenarios (E0-E4, up to 84.3×) before network spacing constraints activated, but remained substantial (minimum 7.4×) even with complex edits (spacing and hotspot exclusions in E5-E10).

#### 4.3. CLIPPER-A Performance (Shared candidate budget, relaxed policy)

- **Coverage Gaps:** With a total budget of 8192 candidate slots and a coverage-prioritized policy (relaxed accounting caps), CLIPPER-A showed slightly larger, but still controlled, coverage gaps. Mean gaps were **1.82 percentage points** (Braunschweig), **0.12 percentage points** (Munich), and **0.27 percentage points** (Berlin).
- **Rollout Time and Speedup:** CLIPPER-A also provided significant speedups. Mean rollout times were **3.219 seconds** (Braunschweig), **3.360 seconds** (Munich), and **4.415 seconds** (Berlin). This translated to speedups ranging from **6.8×** (Munich) to **11.1×** (Berlin) compared to its respective relaxed-cap full-set control.

#### 4.4. Determinism and Audit Scope

- Multiple reruns (99 total across all states and cities for CLIPPER-F with K=1024) confirmed deterministic behavior. All reruns produced identical coverage values, step counts, gain counts, termination reasons, and terminal flags, demonstrating the replayability feature.
- The system did not prematurely stop due to a narrow pool; all runs exhausted all feasible positive gains.

In summary, CLIPPER achieved its objective of providing seconds-level feedback for complex spatial coverage planning, maintaining high solution quality (minimal coverage gaps) compared to full-set greedy controls, and ensuring full constraint enforcement and replayability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of CLIPPER represents a notable advancement in the domain of spatial decision support for urban planning, particularly for micromobility management. Its significance and potential impact derive from its ability to reconcile computational efficiency with the rigorous demands of real-world municipal policy and planning.

1. **Enabling Rapid "What-If" Analysis:** The primary impact of CLIPPER is its ability to reduce the time required for generating city-scale spatial plans from "tens of seconds" to "seconds." This acceleration facilitates iterative "what-if" exploration, allowing urban planners to quickly test the implications of various policy changes (e.g., new exclusion zones, adjusted spacing rules, budget reallocations) on demand coverage and facility placement. This immediate feedback supports a more agile and responsive planning process, moving away from slow, batch-oriented analyses.

2. **Maintaining Policy Compliance and Auditability:** Unlike some heuristic or approximation methods, CLIPPER is designed to enforce *every encoded model constraint* rigorously during selection. This "constraint-exact" approach ensures that all generated plans are practically feasible and compliant with complex municipal quality agreements, which is critical for legal and public acceptance. The integrated auditing mechanisms (offline full-set scans and online screening bounds) provide transparency into potential missed gains, allowing planners to understand the trade-offs between speed and solution quality, thereby fostering trust in the planning outcomes.

3. **Enhancing Transparency and Replayability:** The "replay contract" is a key contribution, ensuring that specific planning states, policies, and their resulting plans can be deterministically reproduced. This feature is vital for accountability, allowing previous decisions to be re-examined, understood, and communicated transparently. It provides a robust framework for comparing different planning alternatives not just by their final scores, but by a traceable, versioned policy state and its context.

4. **Flexibility for Policy Evolution:** The design of CLIPPER separates scenario design from optimization and allows the underlying proposal rules to change without altering the core feasibility or comparison semantics. This modularity means that as urban mobility policies evolve, the planning tool can adapt without requiring a fundamental re-engineering of its core principles, ensuring its long-term utility.

5. **Direct Application to Urban Micromobility:** By framing its development around the specific operational requirements of the City of Braunschweig, CLIPPER directly addresses a pressing need in urban micromobility management. Effective planning of parking zones for shared scooters and bikes is crucial for reducing urban clutter, ensuring equitable access, and managing public space. CLIPPER provides a tool that can help optimize these deployments under dynamic conditions, potentially leading to more efficient, user-friendly, and publicly acceptable micromobility systems.

6. **Broader Implications for Spatial Decision Support:** While demonstrated in micromobility, the core principles of CLIPPER—rapid, constrained optimization with auditability and replayability—are transferable to other spatial planning problems involving facility location under complex, dynamic constraints, such as emergency service placement, public utility planning, or retail network optimization.

In essence, CLIPPER contributes a practical and auditable optimization framework that empowers urban planners with the necessary speed and transparency to navigate the complexities of modern spatial planning challenges.
