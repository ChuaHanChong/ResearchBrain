# Mastering Multi-Drone Volleyball through Hierarchical Co-Self-Play Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Hierarchical Co-Self-Play (HCSP) framework from Tsinghua University enables autonomous multi-drone teams to master 3v3 volleyball, integrating an event-driven high-level strategy with low-level motion skills. This framework, through a three-stage population-based training pipeline, allows drones to acquire agile locomotion and complex team strategies from scratch, achieving an 82.9% average win rate against five baselines and a 71% win rate against a strong rule-based policy.

## Problem

- Autonomous multi-drone teams face challenges in 3v3 volleyball, requiring both high-level strategic coordination and precise low-level control of underactuated quadrotors.
- The turn-based nature of volleyball introduces long-horizon dependencies and tight inter-agent coupling, making it difficult to learn complex team strategies.
- The absence of expert demonstrations necessitates agents to discover fundamental motion primitives and cooperative strategies from scratch, leading to reward sparsity and exploration inefficiency.

## Method

- A **Hierarchical Co-Self-Play (HCSP)** framework is proposed, which decomposes the policy into an event-driven high-level strategy for tactical decisions and multiple low-level skills for continuous drone-specific motion control.
- A three-stage population-based training pipeline is employed: Stage I for low-level skill acquisition with ==policy chaining==, Stage II for high-level strategy pretraining using ==PSRO-Nash== with sample reallocation, and Stage III for co-self-play to jointly refine both layers.
- Low-level skills use ==Per-Rotor Thrust (PRT)== inputs for fine-grained control, while the high-level strategy makes centralized decisions at discrete game events, providing tactical parameters to the drones.

## Results

- HCSP achieved an average win rate of **82.9%** against five non-hierarchical game-theoretic self-play baselines and a **71%** win rate against a rule-based hierarchical 'Bot' policy.
- The Stage III co-self-play policy demonstrated a **71.5%** win rate over the Stage II policy in head-to-head evaluations, and improved its win rate against a Nash-averaged opponent from **31.4%** to **47.6%**.
- The framework facilitated the emergence of complex behaviors, such as a 'dump' shot (a two-touch maneuver) during co-self-play and a 'front-flip attack' during initial skill acquisition, without explicit engineering.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy chaining during low-level skill acquisition (Stage I) significantly improves the reliability of sequential skill execution and accelerates learning for complex, temporally adjacent maneuvers.
> - Sample reallocation for the high-level strategy (Stage II) effectively addresses reward sparsity and stabilizes training by extracting and attributing cumulative rewards at event-triggered timesteps.
> - The co-self-play stage (Stage III), with a KL divergence penalty and one-at-a-time refinement, enables the emergence of novel, sophisticated team behaviors and adaptations in low-level skills driven by game-outcome rewards.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Competitive tasks have historically served as benchmarks for evaluating progress in artificial intelligence, with notable advancements in domains such as Go, poker, and real-time strategy games. As research extends from virtual environments to physical settings, robot sports have emerged as a significant area for embodied intelligence. These physically grounded competitions, including robot soccer, table tennis, and multi-drone pursuit-evasion, require a combination of high-level strategic planning and low-level motion control.

This work addresses the 3v3 multi-drone volleyball task within the VolleyBots testbed. This task presents a convergence of several challenges: high-level strategic coordination among multiple agents, precise low-level control of underactuated quadrotors, and complex game dynamics. The turn-based nature of volleyball introduces long-horizon dependencies, while the multi-agent setting necessitates tight inter-agent coupling. The physically grounded nature of the task, combined with the absence of expert demonstrations, makes learning both basic motion primitives and complex team strategies from scratch a significant research challenge.

Hierarchical control architectures are a common approach to managing complexity in robotics by decomposing tasks into abstraction levels. This includes traditional behavior-based systems and more recent learning-based frameworks like HiREPS and SayCan. This paper builds upon these principles by proposing an event-driven hierarchical policy, specifically tailored for the multi-drone volleyball scenario. Furthermore, multi-agent reinforcement learning, particularly self-play, is a recognized paradigm for training agents in competitive zero-sum or general-sum games where expert data is unavailable. The proposed method integrates self-play within its hierarchical framework to facilitate the emergence of complex behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable autonomous multi-drone teams to master the 3v3 multi-drone volleyball task. This overarching goal is motivated by several specific challenges inherent in the task:

- **Long-Horizon Dependencies:** The turn-based nature of ball exchange in volleyball means that actions can have consequences that unfold over extended periods, making it difficult for agents to associate immediate actions with distant future rewards.
- **Tight Inter-Agent Coupling:** Each team comprises three drones that must coordinate their actions precisely to return the ball over the net within three touches. This requires synchronized decision-making and cooperative execution, dynamically switching between offensive and defensive roles.
- **Underactuated Quadrotor Dynamics:** Quadrotors are inherently underactuated systems, meaning they have fewer control inputs than degrees of freedom. This necessitates fine-grained, agile, and reactive low-level motor skills to perform maneuvers such as precise racket strikes and rapid 3D positioning.
- **Absence of Expert Demonstrations:** Unlike some reinforcement learning tasks that can leverage imitation learning from human or engineered expert data, this task requires agents to discover both fundamental motion primitives and complex team strategies entirely from scratch. This makes reward sparsity and exploration efficiency critical concerns.

To address these challenges, the authors propose the Hierarchical Co-Self-Play (HCSP) framework. The core motivation behind HCSP is to decouple the problem into distinct levels of abstraction:
- **Separating Strategic Reasoning from Motor Control:** A hierarchical structure allows a high-level policy to focus on sparse, strategic decisions (e.g., which drone performs which skill) while low-level policies manage the continuous, agile motor control required for drone movement and ball interaction. This decomposition aims to simplify the learning problem at both levels.
- **Event-Driven High-Level Strategy:** By activating the high-level strategy only at discrete game events (e.g., ball hit, ball crossing the net), the framework aligns with the turn-based nature of the game and manages the long-horizon dependencies by segmenting the decision-making process.
- **Emergent Behavior from Scratch:** The design of a multi-stage training pipeline is motivated by the need to enable both low-level skills and high-level strategies to emerge without any form of expert supervision, gradually building complexity and coordination.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Hierarchical Co-Self-Play (HCSP) framework is a hierarchical reinforcement learning method that decomposes the overall policy into an event-driven high-level strategy and multiple low-level skills.

### 4. HCSP Architecture

The policy architecture is designed with two layers:
- **High-Level Strategy:** This component is responsible for team-level tactical decisions. It is implemented as a centralized multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with three distinct output heads, each corresponding to one of the team's drones. This enables synchronized decision-making for the entire team. The high-level strategy is activated only at discrete game events, such as when a racket hits the ball or when the ball crosses the net. Its output comprises the selection of a specific low-level skill for each drone and corresponding tactical parameters (e.g., target positions, attack directions). The observation for the high-level strategy includes global information, such as the states of all six drones (three teammates and three opponents), the ball dynamics, and other game-relevant features.
- **Low-Level Skills:** These skills manage continuous, drone-specific motion control. Each drone independently executes its assigned low-level skill at a high frequency (50 Hz). The observation for a low-level skill includes the drone's own state, ball dynamics, and tactical parameters provided by the high-level strategy, but excludes information about other drones. Per-Rotor Thrust (PRT) inputs are used as the low-level action space, offering fine-grained control for agile maneuvers.

### 5. Three-Stage Training Pipeline

HCSP employs a three-stage population-based training pipeline to enable the emergence of both skills and strategies without expert demonstrations:

- **Stage I: Low-Level Skill Acquisition**
 - **Objective:** To develop a pool of diverse and robust motion primitives for individual drones. Seven fundamental skills are identified: Serve, Receive, Pass, Set, Attack, Hover, and GoTo.
 - **Approach:** Each skill is trained as a separate single-agent reinforcement learning task using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Reward shaping is applied to provide dense feedback and accelerate learning for these specific tasks.
 - **Policy Chaining:** To ensure smooth transitions between temporally adjacent skills, policy chaining is utilized. This method iteratively trains each new skill in the context of previously learned ones (e.g., the Attack skill is trained to follow a teammate's Set skill). This enhances both intra-drone motion continuity and inter-drone cooperation.

- **Stage II: High-Level Strategy Pretraining**
 - **Objective:** To acquire initial cooperative and adversarial team tactics.
 - **Approach:** The low-level skill pool obtained from Stage I is frozen. The high-level strategy is then trained through team-level iterative self-play using Policy-Space Response Oracles (PSRO) with a Nash meta-solver (PSRO-Nash). In each iteration, a candidate high-level strategy competes against opponents sampled from a population based on their Nash equilibrium distribution. A candidate strategy is added to the population if its win rate exceeds a predefined threshold.
 - **Reward:** The high-level strategy receives a sparse reward based on game outcomes (win/lose) and racket-ball contact events.
 - **Sample Reallocation:** To address the sparsity of event-driven high-level actions, a sample reallocation procedure is introduced. This extracts transitions at event-triggered timesteps and attributes cumulative rewards over the intervals between events, storing them in a dedicated buffer for more efficient training. This mitigates high-variance gradients and stabilizes learning.

- **Stage III: Co-Self-Play**
 - **Objective:** To jointly refine both high-level strategies and low-level skills through mutual adaptation, fostering advanced team behaviors.
 - **Approach:** This stage iteratively optimizes both layers. One team (candidate high-level strategy and low-level skills) is trained against a frozen opponent. When the candidate's win rate exceeds a threshold, the newly refined low-level policies are added to the existing low-level skill pool. Since the skill pool changes, a new high-level strategy is then trained from scratch to accommodate the augmented action set, competing against the previous high-level strategy and the original low-level skill pool.
 - **Low-Level Reward in Co-Self-Play:** Instead of reusing hand-crafted Stage I rewards, low-level skills in this stage directly adopt the high-level game-outcome reward. This encourages skill adaptations driven by strategic outcomes. To mitigate reward sparsity for low-level actions and prevent policy collapse, a KL-divergence penalty between the current low-level policy and its reference policy is incorporated into the low-level reward function.
 - **Refinement Schedule:** Ablation studies demonstrated that co-optimizing the high-level strategy with one low-level skill at a time (one-at-a-time refinement) is more effective than simultaneous co-optimization of all low-level skills due to the non-stationary nature of multi-agent learning.

### 6. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate HCSP's performance against baselines, quantify the impact of the co-self-play stage, and analyze the contribution of its core components.

### 7. Game Results Against Baselines

- HCSP achieved an average win rate of 82.9% across five baseline methods, indicating a competitive advantage.
- It significantly outperformed all four non-hierarchical game-theoretic self-play baselines: Self-Play (SP), Fictitious Self-Play (FSP), Policy-Space Response Oracles with uniform meta-solver (PSRO_Uniform), and PSRO with Nash meta-solver (PSRO_Nash).
- HCSP also attained a 71% win rate against the rule-based hierarchical "Bot" policy from VolleyBots, which represents a strong handcrafted baseline.

### 8. Analysis of Stage III Co-Self-Play

- **Quantitative Performance Improvement:** In head-to-head evaluations, the Stage III policy achieved a 71.5% win rate over the Stage II policy, demonstrating a clear performance gain from co-self-play. When evaluated against a Nash-averaged opponent (derived from a population including baselines and all HCSP stages), the Stage III policy improved its win rate from 31.4% (Stage II) to 47.6%, indicating enhanced absolute robustness and near-Nash competitive performance.
- **Emergent Low-Level Skills and Behaviors:** Stage III co-self-play led to the emergence of novel behaviors not explicitly designed or present in earlier stages. A notable example is the "dump" shot, where the setter drone itself redirects the ball over the net instead of performing a set for an attacker. This emergent two-touch maneuver showcases the evolution of low-level primitives beyond their initial definitions and the high-level strategy's ability to adapt its skill allocation to leverage these new behaviors.
- **Emergent Front-Flip Attack (Stage I Case Study):** During Stage I low-level skill acquisition, policy chaining facilitated the discovery of a "front-flip attack," a complex maneuver that allows the drone to impart additional momentum to the ball, resulting in higher ball speed.

### 9. Ablation Studies

- **Policy Chaining (Stage I):** Policy chaining was found to significantly improve the reliability of sequential skill execution and accelerate learning. For instance, it successfully enabled the learning of a Hover skill immediately following an Attack skill, whereas a single-policy approach failed to achieve this smooth transition, indicating its effectiveness in decomposing complex behaviors.
- **High-Level Strategy Sample Reallocation (Stage II):** Sample reallocation led to faster skill progression and more stable training for the high-level strategy, as evidenced by earlier and more consistent favorable win rates in policy population heatmaps, with lower win-rate variance. The population trained with sample reallocation achieved a 90.5% win rate against a population trained with standard sampling.
- **KL Divergence Penalty (Stage III):** A moderate KL divergence penalty coefficient (e.g., 1e-4) was crucial for effective low-level learning during co-self-play. A large penalty caused policy performance to collapse rapidly, while no penalty resulted in slow and ineffective learning due to sparse rewards.
- **High-Level Strategy Parameter Initialization (Stage III):** For the relearning step of the high-level strategy, training from scratch achieved a higher ultimate win rate compared to initializing newly added dimensions by averaging existing parameters. While requiring more steps, training from scratch avoided convergence to suboptimal local solutions.

### 10. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the fields of embodied artificial intelligence and multi-agent reinforcement learning by addressing a complex, physically grounded competitive task.

- **Advancement in Embodied AI and Robot Sports:** The paper presents the first systematic study of the 3v3 multi-drone volleyball task, which integrates a diverse set of challenges including strategic team play, precise motion control, high-speed aerial agility, and turn-based multi-agent interactions in a physical environment. This work expands the scope of robot sports benchmarks beyond isolated aspects to encompass a comprehensive set of capabilities.
- **Novel Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning Framework:** HCSP offers a hierarchical reinforcement learning method that effectively integrates a centralized high-level strategy for team tactics with decentralized low-level skills for agile control. This design strategy is shown to be effective for tasks demanding coordination and dexterity.
- **Demonstration-Free Skill and Strategy Emergence:** The proposed three-stage population-based training pipeline enables the emergence of both motion skills and tactical strategies from scratch, without reliance on expert demonstrations. This is a significant step towards developing autonomous systems in domains where expert data is scarce or impossible to collect.
- **Emergent Complex Behaviors:** The co-self-play stage of HCSP facilitates the emergence of sophisticated team behaviors, such as adaptive role switching, coordinated formations, and novel "dump" shots, without explicit design or additional reward engineering. This indicates a pathway for developing more adaptable and creative AI agents in complex multi-agent environments.
- **Performance Benchmarking:** HCSP's superior performance against both non-hierarchical self-play methods and a rule-based hierarchical baseline establishes a new benchmark for competitive multi-drone tasks.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The current approach is evaluated in a high-fidelity simulation environment. A primary limitation is the potential sim-to-real gap, which could hinder direct transfer to physical drones due to unmodeled sensor noise, latency, and real-world physics complexities. Preliminary real-world experiments on low-level skills show promise but highlight the need for further work in this area. Additionally, HCSP currently relies on full state information (drone poses, velocities, ball dynamics) rather than raw sensor inputs (e.g., onboard cameras), limiting its direct applicability in scenarios where only sensory data is available. Future research could focus on bridging the sim-to-real gap and integrating learning directly from raw sensor streams.
