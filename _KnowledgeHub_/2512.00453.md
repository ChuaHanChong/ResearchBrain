# Sample-Efficient Expert Query Control in Active Imitation Learning via Conformal Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> Conformalized Rejection Sampling for Active Imitation Learning (CRSAIL) introduces a method to selectively query experts based on state-space novelty, using conformal prediction to establish a data-driven threshold. This approach enables active imitation learning to achieve expert-level performance across continuous control tasks while significantly reducing the number of required expert queries.

## Problem

- Imitation Learning (IL) struggles with covariate shift, where the learner's state distribution deviates from the expert's, leading to accumulating errors.
- Active Imitation Learning (AIL) seeks to mitigate covariate shift by querying experts for labels but faces high costs from excessive queries, especially in complex simulations or human-in-the-loop systems.
- Existing AIL methods often over-query, require real-time expert interventions, or rely on less reliable action-uncertainty thresholds for identifying novel states.

## Method

- CRSAIL proposes a post hoc, batch-based querying strategy that identifies "under-represented" states by calculating a novelty score based on their K-th nearest neighbor distance to existing expert data.
- ==Conformal prediction== is employed to calibrate a robust, task-agnostic query threshold (R) from a calibration dataset, linking a user-specified miscoverage rate (α) to the expected query rate.
- The learner policy rolls out full trajectories, and only states exceeding the calibrated novelty threshold R are selected for expert labeling after the episode, eliminating real-time expert takeovers.

## Results

- CRSAIL consistently matched or exceeded expert-level reward across three MuJoCo continuous control tasks (Inverted Double Pendulum, Pusher, Hopper) with high convergence rates.
- The method substantially reduced expert queries, using approximately **3.4%** of DAgger's total queries and **34.7**-**52.2%** of the total queries of the best-performing baselines, while maintaining high performance.
- CRSAIL demonstrated empirical robustness to its main hyperparameters (α for query budget and K for novelty score), suggesting reduced tuning effort for new applications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly assessing state-space novelty provides a more efficient mechanism for expert querying compared to relying on action uncertainty.
> - Conformal prediction offers a statistically principled and distribution-free method to set a global query threshold, simplifying hyperparameter tuning across tasks.
> - A post hoc, batch-based querying approach removes the need for disruptive real-time expert interventions, making active imitation learning more practical for deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation Learning (IL) is a methodology that enables a policy to learn expert behavior directly from demonstrations, offering an alternative to reward engineering in Reinforcement Learning. Despite its successes in various domains, IL is susceptible to covariate shift. This phenomenon occurs when the learner agent's on-policy state distribution deviates from the expert's, leading to accumulating control errors during execution.

Active Imitation Learning (AIL) methods aim to mitigate covariate shift by querying an expert for additional action labels during the training process in states where the learner's performance is likely to degrade. However, a significant challenge in AIL is the cost associated with obtaining expert labels. This cost can be substantial in various scenarios, including computationally intensive high-fidelity simulators, human-in-the-loop annotation systems that induce operator fatigue, and safety-critical domains or robot fleets where frequent interventions are undesirable or prohibited.

Existing AIL approaches often exhibit limitations:
- **Query Inefficiency**: Some methods, such as DAgger, query the expert for labels for every state visited, leading to high annotation budgets and redundant data.
- **Real-time Expert Intervention**: Algorithms like SafeDAgger often require the expert to take real-time control when the learner is predicted to be unsafe, complicating deployment.
- **Action-Uncertainty Reliance**: Methods like LazyDAgger, EnsembleDAgger, and ThriftyDAgger base queries on action-uncertainty thresholds. These can be less reliable indicators of state novelty, as high uncertainty might arise from aleatoric randomness rather than truly novel, under-represented states, potentially leading to unnecessary queries.
- **Auxiliary Networks and Tuning**: Approaches like RNDAgger require training auxiliary networks and fine-tuning per-task thresholds, adding complexity.
- **Redundant Data**: Many current methods populate the expert dataset with numerous near-duplicate state-action pairs, which can slow training and provide limited informational value.

This research introduces Conformalized Rejection Sampling for Active Imitation Learning (CRSAIL), which positions itself within the AIL landscape by addressing the query efficiency bottleneck through a state-space novelty assessment. Unlike methods relying on action uncertainty or real-time interventions, CRSAIL directly measures how novel a state is relative to the existing expert data. It leverages conformal prediction to establish a data-driven, task-agnostic query threshold, distinguishing it from approaches like ConformalDAgger, which apply conformal methods for detecting expert policy drift rather than for query efficiency. CRSAIL's post hoc querying strategy, performed in batch after an episode, removes the requirement for real-time expert takeovers, making it distinct from many existing AIL methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that the cost of expert action labeling represents a major bottleneck in active imitation learning. This cost is particularly pronounced in several practical settings:
- **GPU-intensive simulations**: Running high-fidelity simulators for expert demonstrations consumes significant computational resources.
- **Human-in-the-loop settings**: Human operators providing labels experience fatigue, limiting scalability.
- **Robot fleets**: In scenarios where multiple robots operating under the same policy might encounter and revisit near-duplicate states, a naive querying strategy would lead to redundant and costly expert interventions.

Against this backdrop, the paper identifies several shortcomings in existing active imitation learning query-management schemes:
- **Over-querying**: Many methods query the expert excessively, even in well-covered state-space regions.
- **Intervention requirements**: Some techniques necessitate real-time expert takeovers, which can be disruptive and impractical in safety-critical or autonomous systems.
- **Suboptimal novelty assessment**: Relying on action-uncertainty thresholds may not accurately indicate state-space novelty, potentially leading to redundant labels.

To address these challenges, the key objectives of this work are:
1. **Develop a sample-efficient querying rule**: The central objective is to create a mechanism that queries an expert for action labels only when the encountered state is truly "under-represented" in the existing expert-labeled dataset. This aims to concentrate expert effort on informative data.
2. **Establish a robust, data-driven query threshold**: To overcome the need for manual, per-task hyperparameter tuning, the work aims to set a single, global query threshold. This threshold is determined using conformal prediction, providing a distribution-free calibration rule. This approach links a user-specified miscoverage rate `α` to the expected query rate, effectively making `α` a task-agnostic tuning knob for query budgeting.
3. **Eliminate real-time expert takeovers**: The method intends to allow for full trajectory rollouts with the learner policy, querying the expert on a subset of visited states afterward in batch. This post hoc querying strategy is designed to simplify deployment and avoid disruptive real-time interventions.
4. **Promote diverse and informative datasets**: By focusing queries on novel state-space regions, the approach aims to construct a more diverse and compact expert dataset, thereby improving generalization and preventing the replay buffer from being saturated with redundant near-duplicate data.
5. **Achieve expert-level performance with reduced queries**: Ultimately, the research seeks to demonstrate that this novel querying strategy can match or exceed the performance of an expert policy while substantially reducing the total number of required expert queries compared to existing AIL methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Conformalized Rejection Sampling for Active Imitation Learning (CRSAIL), instantiates a post hoc querying strategy that selectively requests expert action labels. This strategy focuses expert effort on regions of the state space that are not adequately represented by the current expert dataset.

**Problem Formulation Context:**
The environment is modeled as a discrete-time Markov Decision Process. The learner policy $\pi_\theta$ aims to minimize an imitation loss $J(\theta)$ by reproducing expert behavior. Initial training involves behavioral cloning (BC) on an initial expert dataset $D^{(0)}_{exp}$. Active learning proceeds in episodes, where the learner policy rolls out a trajectory, and a querying strategy $\psi$ determines which states from the trajectory will be sent to the expert for labeling. These newly labeled state-action pairs $Q_i$ augment the dataset $D^{(i)}_{exp}$, and the learner is updated. Training halts when a query budget $B$ or a step budget $T_{train}$ is reached. CRSAIL defines the strategy $\psi$ as $\psi_{R,K}$ based on state-space novelty and a calibrated threshold $R$.

**A. Querying by State-Space Novelty:**
The core of CRSAIL's querying mechanism is a novelty score, $s_K(x; D^{(i)}_{exp})$, which quantifies how "new" a state $x$ is relative to the current expert dataset $D^{(i)}_{exp}$.
1. **State Projection**: The expert dataset's states are projected as $D^{(i)}_X = \{x' : (x', u') \in D^{(i)}_{exp}\}$.
2. **Nonconformity Score**: For any state $x$, $s_K(x; D^{(i)}_{exp})$ is defined as the radius of the smallest closed ball centered at $x$ that contains at least $K$ elements from $D^{(i)}_X$. This is equivalent to the distance from $x$ to its K-th nearest neighbor in the expert state set. A larger score indicates lower local data density around $x$, signifying greater novelty. The parameter $K$ helps in making the score robust to isolated outliers and tolerant of duplicates.
3. **Query Rule**: Given a calibrated threshold $R > 0$, the expert is queried for a state $x^{(i)}_t$ in a trajectory $\tau^{(i)}$ if its novelty score $s_K(x^{(i)}_t; D^{(i)}_{exp})$ exceeds $R$. Otherwise, no query is made for that state. This strategy ensures that expert labeling effort is concentrated on under-covered state regions, avoiding redundant data.
4. **Post Hoc Batch Querying**: Crucially, queries are issued "post hoc in batch" after each episode is completed. This design choice eliminates the need for real-time expert interventions or takeovers during policy execution.

**B. Threshold Selection via Conformal Calibration:**
A key aspect of CRSAIL is the method for setting the threshold $R$ in a statistically principled and task-agnostic manner, using conformal prediction.
1. **Calibration Dataset**: An initial learner policy $\pi_{\theta_0}$ (obtained via behavioral cloning on $D^{(0)}_{exp}$) is rolled out for $M_{cal}$ episodes to collect a multiset of unlabeled on-policy states, $X_{cal}$. No expert labels are required for this phase.
2. **Calibration Scores**: For each state $x_j \in X_{cal}$, a nonconformity score $s_j = s_K(x_j; D^{(0)}_{exp})$ is computed.
3. **Calibrated Threshold**: A user-chosen miscoverage rate $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ is used to determine $R$. The threshold $R$ is set as the empirical $(1 - \alpha)$-quantile of the calibration scores $\{s_j\}$. Specifically, if $s_{(1)} \le \ldots \le s_{(N_{cal})}$ are the order statistics of these scores, then $R = s_{(m)}$, where $m = \lceil(N_{cal} + 1)(1 - \alpha)\rceil$.
4. **Interpretation and Robustness**: While classical conformal prediction guarantees exact coverage under exchangeability, the policy's evolution and temporal correlation of states in a trajectory mean this is an idealized reference, not a strict guarantee. However, $\alpha$ still serves as an effective knob to control the query rate: increasing $\alpha$ lowers the coverage level, decreases $R$, and thus labels more states as novel, leading to more queries. This mechanism provides principled budget control and adapts to the state-space density of each task without requiring per-task manual tuning of $R$.
5. **Fixed Threshold R**: The threshold $R$ is calibrated only once using the initial policy $\pi_{\theta_0}$ and initial expert dataset $D^{(0)}_{exp}$. It remains fixed throughout the entire training process. This is designed to allow the query rate to naturally decrease over time as the learner policy improves and the expert dataset $D_{exp}^{(i)}$ grows to cover more relevant regions of the state space.

**C. CRSAIL Training Algorithm:**
The CRSAIL training loop proceeds as follows:
1. Initial learner $\pi_{\theta_0}$ is obtained by behavioral cloning on $D^{(0)}_{exp}$.
2. The calibration step (Algorithm 1) is executed once to compute the threshold $R$.
3. The main training loop (Algorithm 2) iterates:
 - The current learner $\pi_{\theta_i}$ rolls out an episode.
 - The novelty scores for all visited states are computed using the current $D^{(i)}_{exp}$.
 - States with novelty scores greater than $R$ are selected for querying.
 - Expert labels are obtained for these selected states, forming $Q_i$.
 - $Q_i$ is added to $D^{(i)}_{exp}$ to form $D^{(i+1)}_{exp}$.
 - The learner $\pi_{\theta_i}$ is updated using $D^{(i+1)}_{exp}$ to produce $\pi_{\theta_{i+1}}$.
 - Episode steps and queries are tracked.
 - The loop continues until either the query budget $B$ or the total environment step budget $T_{train}$ is exhausted.

**Computational Notes**: The computation of K-th nearest neighbor distances involves constructing pairwise distance matrices between learner and expert states, typically accelerated using GPU resources, adding minor overhead.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of CRSAIL was evaluated on three MuJoCo continuous control tasks: Inverted Double Pendulum (InvDP), Pusher, and Hopper. The expert policies for these tasks were pre-trained RL models. CRSAIL's performance was compared against DAgger, EnsembleDAgger, and ThriftyDAgger. Metrics included convergence rate (achieving at least 95% of expert reward), queries-to-expert (number of queries until convergence), and total queries over a fixed training step budget ($T_{train}$). Experiments were conducted with varying initial expert dataset sizes ($M$), and results were averaged over five independent initial datasets. For CRSAIL, $K=5$ was fixed, and $\alpha$ was chosen from a coarse sweep (e.g., $0.93$ for InvDP and Pusher, $0.95$ for Hopper).

**A. Robustness to $\alpha$**:
Experiments on the Pusher task (Table II) demonstrated that CRSAIL exhibits robustness to the choice of $\alpha$. For $\alpha \geq 0.9$, all runs converged across all initial dataset sizes $M$. As anticipated from the conformal calibration rule, increasing $\alpha$ led to a higher total number of queries because it lowered the coverage target, marking more states as "novel." However, the number of queries required to reach expert-level performance showed weaker dependence on $\alpha$. This indicates that the choice of $\alpha$ primarily influences the querying behavior *after* convergence, offering a robust mechanism for budget control without significantly impeding initial learning progress. This robustness simplifies deployment by reducing the need for fine-grained per-task tuning of query thresholds.

**B. Robustness to $K$**:
A sweep of $K$ values on the Pusher task (Table III), with $\alpha = 0.93$, revealed that CRSAIL's performance is largely insensitive to the neighbor order $K$. All runs converged for every tested $K$ and initial dataset size $M$. Both the queries-to-expert and total queries remained relatively constant across different $K$ values. This suggests that, in these environments, even a small neighborhood size (e.g., $K=5$) is sufficient to capture the relevant local geometry of the expert data, and that isolated outliers are not a dominant factor affecting query decisions.

**C. Comparisons with Baselines**:
Table IV and V present a comprehensive comparison across tasks and initial dataset sizes.

- **Inverted Double Pendulum (InvDP)**: CRSAIL achieved 100% convergence across all $M$ values. It consistently required the fewest total queries and, in 4 out of 5 cases, the fewest queries to converge to expert-level performance compared to DAgger, EnsembleDAgger, and ThriftyDAgger. Specifically, CRSAIL used approximately 3.4% of DAgger's total queries and 34.7% of the best-performing baseline (ThriftyDAgger), while achieving comparable or superior best reward. Figure 2a for InvDP illustrates CRSAIL's faster convergence to expert-like behavior as a function of cumulative expert queries. Figure 2b shows CRSAIL's query rate starts high and then plateaus, contrasting with other methods' more linear querying. Figure 3 shows that CRSAIL queries fewer states in longer, successful episodes, demonstrating its ability to avoid redundant queries in well-covered regions.

- **Pusher**: CRSAIL again demonstrated 100% convergence for all $M$. It was uniformly the most query-efficient method, requiring the fewest queries to reach expert performance and the fewest total queries across all initial dataset sizes. CRSAIL's total queries were about 28.3% of DAgger's and 52.2% of the best baseline (EnsembleDAgger), while maintaining high reward. In contrast, ThriftyDAgger showed reduced convergence rates as $M$ increased (falling to 20%), despite a relatively high target query rate.

- **Hopper (Challenging Domain)**: The Hopper task, characterized by a mix of successful and failing behaviors, posed a greater challenge for CRSAIL's state-space novelty approach, as action-based gates could leverage the small action space. Despite this, CRSAIL still performed well, converging in 24 out of 25 runs. It achieved the lowest total query counts for the two smallest initial datasets ($M=1k, 2k$) and remained competitive at larger $M$. CRSAIL's total queries were 36.7% of DAgger's, though 132.9% of the best baseline (ThriftyDAgger). However, CRSAIL achieved a higher mean peak reward (102.2% vs. 98.4%) than ThriftyDAgger, suggesting a better performance-to-query trade-off in this specific instance.

Overall, the findings indicate that CRSAIL consistently matches or exceeds expert-level reward while substantially reducing the number of required expert queries across various robotic tasks. Its robustness to hyperparameter choices ($\alpha$ and $K$) was also empirically demonstrated.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of CRSAIL contributes to the field of active imitation learning by addressing a critical bottleneck: the cost of acquiring expert labels. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Reduced Expert Query Costs**: By focusing expert queries on genuinely novel and under-represented regions of the state space, CRSAIL demonstrably reduces the total number of expert labels required to achieve expert-level performance. This has direct economic implications for applications involving expensive simulators, human-in-the-loop systems, or large-scale robot deployments. The reported reductions of up to 96% against DAgger and 65% against prior AIL methods highlight this efficiency.

- **Enhanced Data Efficiency and Diversity**: CRSAIL's mechanism of avoiding redundant labels in well-covered areas helps in populating the expert dataset with a more diverse and compact set of informative states. This can lead to faster training and potentially better generalization, as the learning algorithm is exposed to varied and high-value data rather than near-duplicates.

- **Simplified Deployment and Integration**: The post hoc, batch-based querying strategy of CRSAIL eliminates the need for real-time expert takeovers or interventions during policy execution. This simplifies the operational requirements for AIL systems, making them easier to integrate into existing robotic platforms and autonomous systems where real-time control transfer can be complex or undesirable.

- **Robust and Principled Thresholding**: The use of conformal prediction to set a single, global query threshold ($R$) is a notable contribution. This data-driven calibration provides a distribution-free method for linking a user-specified miscoverage rate ($\alpha$) directly to the expected query rate, effectively transforming $\alpha$ into a task-agnostic tuning knob. The empirical robustness to choices of $\alpha$ and $K$ further streamlines the deployment process on novel systems with unknown dynamics, reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning.

- **Broader Applicability of Active Imitation Learning**: By making AIL more sample-efficient and easier to deploy, CRSAIL has the potential to expand the applicability of imitation learning to a wider range of real-world scenarios, particularly in domains like dexterous robot control, autonomous driving, and multi-agent systems, where expert supervision is costly or interventions are constrained.

- **Foundational Contribution to Conformal Prediction in RL**: This work demonstrates a novel application of conformal prediction for assessing state-space novelty and controlling query budgets in an active learning context, distinct from prior uses in IL for expert policy drift. This expands the practical utility of conformal methods within reinforcement learning and control.

In conclusion, CRSAIL offers a practical and principled solution to the expert querying problem in AIL, making imitation learning a more feasible and cost-effective approach for learning complex behaviors.
