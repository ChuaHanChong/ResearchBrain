# Training High-Level Schedulers with Execution-Feedback Reinforcement Learning for Long-Horizon GUI Automation

> [!summary] Summary
> A multi-agent framework called Coordinator-Executor-State Tracker (CES) is presented to enhance long-horizon GUI automation by decoupling planning, execution, and state management. The framework, trained with a staged execution-feedback reinforcement learning algorithm, improved action type accuracy by an average of 10.38% on various long-horizon benchmarks compared to single-agent baselines.

## Problem

- End-to-end single-agent GUI models face "responsibility coupling and capability conflict," struggling to simultaneously master diverse abilities like planning, reasoning, and precise execution in long-horizon tasks.
- Existing GUI agents often lack task state awareness in multi-step processes, relying on low-level historical actions or raw visual information which can lead to context loss and errors.
- Current multi-agent systems for LLM-driven agents often use general VLMs with prompt engineering, which may not be deeply optimized for individual specialized roles in complex GUI automation.

## Method

- A ==Coordinator-Executor==-State Tracker (CES) multi-agent framework is introduced to structurally decouple high-level planning and state management from low-level execution.
- A dedicated State Tracker agent is implemented to provide dynamic context compression and maintain a high-semantic, natural language representation of the task state.
- A staged execution-feedback reinforcement learning algorithm is used to optimize the high-level Coordinator and State Tracker, leveraging verifiable reward signals from a frozen, plug-and-play Executor.

## Results

- The CES framework, when integrated with a GUI-R1-**7B** Executor, improved average action type accuracy by **10.38%** across long-horizon benchmarks (AITZ, AMEX, GUI-Odyssey) compared to the standalone baseline.
- CES demonstrated substantial and consistent performance improvements across various Executor models (UI-R1-**3B**, GUI-Owl-**7B**, GUI-Owl-**32B**), validating its generalizability as a plug-and-play solution.
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of both the Coordinator and State Tracker components and the efficacy of RL training, with CES nearly eliminating "State Loss" errors (reduced from **14%** to **2%**) and significantly reducing "Planning Errors" (from **12%** to **4%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling high-level strategic planning and state management into specialized agents effectively resolves the responsibility coupling and capability conflict inherent in monolithic GUI automation models.
> - Maintaining an explicit, high-semantic representation of the task state through a dedicated State Tracker significantly improves an agent's ability to track progress and make informed decisions in long-horizon tasks.
> - Optimizing high-level planning and state management components using execution-feedback reinforcement learning yields superior and more generalizable performance compared to generic prompting or pure supervised fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Graphical User Interface (GUI) agents are a prominent area of research, aiming to automate complex tasks across various applications. The field has evolved from early rule-based or API-driven approaches, which were often platform-specific and lacked generalization, to pure-vision paradigms leveraging large vision-language models (VLMs). Initial pure-vision methods primarily relied on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), learning mappings from environmental states to actions through imitation. However, SFT-based approaches are limited by their heavy dependence on large-scale, costly, and meticulously annotated trajectory data, often exhibiting poor generalization in unseen environments.

Recent advancements have shifted towards Reinforcement Learning (RL) to enhance the planning and decision-making capabilities of GUI agents. RL offers an alternative by utilizing verifiable reward functions, reducing the reliance on extensive manual annotations. While RL has shown effectiveness in simpler tasks, existing single-agent architectures, whether SFT- or RL-based, face two significant challenges when confronted with long-horizon GUI automation:

1. **Responsibility Coupling and Capability Conflict:** End-to-end single-agent models attempt to integrate diverse capabilities—such as long-term task planning, multi-step reasoning, GUI element grounding, and precise action execution—within a unified policy network. This coupling presents optimization difficulties, as a model with finite parameters may struggle to simultaneously master these heterogeneous abilities, leading to potential performance degradation as task complexity increases.
2. **Lack of Task State Awareness:** In multi-step, long-horizon tasks, agents require an accurate understanding of their current progress to make informed decisions. Many existing methods rely on low-level historical action sequences or visual information from screenshots. These representations are often insufficient or unreliable for maintaining semantic context or determining the agent's position within a complex task, leading to context loss, errors, and difficulties in navigating ambiguous or out-of-distribution (OOD) interfaces.

Multi-agent systems represent an emerging trend in LLM-driven agents, offering a way to address complex tasks through specialized division of labor. While some multi-agent frameworks exist, many employ general VLMs with prompt engineering to assign roles, which may lack deep optimization for individual roles. Furthermore, existing research sometimes overlooks dedicated task state management and efficient information compression in long-horizon contexts, often relying on passive storage of redundant information.

This work addresses these identified limitations by proposing a multi-agent framework and a specialized RL training strategy. It distinguishes itself by structurally decoupling high-level planning and state management from low-level execution and by introducing a staged execution-feedback reinforcement learning algorithm specifically designed to optimize these high-level scheduling components. The framework aims to be generalizable and plug-and-play, integrating with various low-level Executor models to enhance their long-horizon capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the ability of GUI agents to handle complex, long-horizon tasks by addressing two fundamental challenges inherent in current single-agent architectures.

The first key motivation stems from the **responsibility coupling and capability conflict** observed in unified policy networks. Existing end-to-end GUI agent models often attempt to merge a wide range of capabilities—from abstract strategic planning and multi-step reasoning to concrete GUI element grounding and precise action execution—into a single model. This design choice creates a significant optimization burden. The paper posits that a model with a finite parameter count struggles to simultaneously excel at both high-level cognitive tasks (like decomposing complex instructions and tracking overall task progress) and low-level perceptual-motor tasks (like identifying specific UI elements and executing precise clicks). As the horizon of a task expands and its complexity increases, this inherent coupling can lead to conflicting optimization objectives, ultimately hindering the model's performance and potentially causing a "catastrophic collapse" of its diverse capabilities. The objective is therefore to decouple these responsibilities, allowing specialized agents to focus on distinct sets of capabilities.

The second core motivation arises from the **lack of task state awareness** in long-horizon tasks. For an agent to successfully navigate a multi-step process, it must maintain an accurate understanding of its current progress and overall task context. Traditional methods often rely on low-level historical action sequences (ee.g., "Click(x, y)") or raw visual information from screenshots to infer task state. The paper argues that these representations are insufficient and unreliable. Low-level actions provide minimal semantic context regarding task progress, while screenshots can be ambiguous (e.g., repeated "Home" screens) or unfamiliar (e.g., Out-of-Distribution interfaces). This limitation makes it difficult for agents to determine their position within a long task, leading to errors, redundant actions, and loss of progress. To address this, a key objective is to introduce a mechanism that explicitly maintains and updates a high-semantic representation of the task state.

To achieve these objectives, the research proposes a staged execution-feedback reinforcement learning algorithm and instantiates it within a **Coordinator-Executor-State Tracker (CES) multi-agent framework**. This framework aims to:
- **Structurally decouple** the complex automation process into specialized agents to resolve responsibility coupling.
- **Introduce a dedicated State Tracker** for dynamic context compression and state summarization to address the lack of task state awareness.
- **Focus training on high-level scheduling models** (Coordinator and State Tracker) while treating the low-level Executor as a frozen, plug-and-play component, providing verifiable feedback for optimization. This approach seeks to optimize specific capabilities without interference from low-level execution details, promoting generality.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology is centered around a multi-agent framework called Coordinator-Executor-State Tracker (CES) and a staged execution-feedback reinforcement learning algorithm.

#### 3.1. Task Setup

The GUI task is formally defined as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) ⟨S, A, F, R⟩, where S is the state space, A is the action space, F is the state transition function, and R is the reward function. The agent's policy π generates a chain of thought and an action based on the user instruction, current screen state, and historical information. The goal is to learn an optimal policy that maximizes cumulative reward. The paper argues against directly learning an end-to-end policy due to inherent challenges, proposing instead a structured decomposition into a multi-agent collaborative loop.

#### 3.2. CES Collaborative Loop

The CES framework conceptualizes GUI automation through an operating system analogy: the Coordinator acts as the "CPU" for planning, the Executor as the "I/O Device" for actions, and the State Tracker as the "Dynamic Memory" for state management. This design separates high-level strategic planning from low-level precise execution.

- **Coordinator (π_c):** This agent is the strategic decision-making core. It takes the user's high-level natural language instruction (q), the compressed high-semantic state summary from the State Tracker (m_t-1), and the current screenshot (s_t) as input. Its role is to translate the ambiguous high-level instruction into a clear, executable atomic instruction (l_t) for the Executor. The Coordinator is also designed to reflect and re-plan in case of exceptions or errors, guiding corrective actions.

- **Executor (π_e):** This agent serves as the action endpoint. Its function is limited to translating the atomic instruction (l_t) from the Coordinator into a physical operation on the GUI interface. The Executor does not manage long-term intent or complex task context. Given an atomic instruction (l_t) and the current screen (s_t), it outputs a chain of thought (th_t) and a standardized action (a_t). In this framework, the Executor is treated as a frozen, pre-trained, and swappable component, meaning its parameters are not updated during the training of the high-level schedulers.

- **State Tracker (π_s):** This agent is crucial for addressing long-horizon challenges. It functions as the framework's dynamic memory unit, performing dynamic context compression and state updating. It shifts the agent's state understanding from processing high-dimensional historical screenshots to a low-dimensional, high-semantic natural language space. As a language model, it infers and generates a new state summary (m_t) by integrating the user intent (q), the previous task state (m_t-1), and the Executor's output (u_t = (th_t, a_t)). This natural language-based state evolution aims to compress information, filter visual noise, and provide a coherent basis for the Coordinator's decisions, thereby mitigating context loss.

The overall flow is a loop: Coordinator plans based on State Tracker's summary, Executor executes, State Tracker updates its summary based on execution, and the Coordinator uses the new summary for the next planning step.

#### 3.3. Staged Execution-Feedback Reinforcement Learning

The training process for the Coordinator and State Tracker is divided into two distinct stages, leveraging a frozen Executor for reward signals.

- **Warm-up SFT:** Both the Coordinator and State Tracker undergo an initial Supervised Fine-Tuning phase. This stage aims to teach each agent its basic role, responsibilities, and expected output format. High-quality (input, output) data pairs are constructed from existing datasets through automated scripts for this preliminary fine-tuning.

- **Rule-based RL (GRPO):** To enhance generalization and strategic decision-making, the models are further optimized using the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm. GRPO is a policy optimization method that utilizes an importance sampling ratio and a clipping mechanism to ensure stable updates, along with a KL penalty against a reference policy to prevent large policy shifts. For each input, the model generates N candidate outputs, which are then scored by a rule-based reward function to calculate a relative advantage for policy updates.

- **Execution-Feedback Reward:** A core aspect of this approach is the design of the reward function. Instead of directly evaluating the Coordinator's atomic instructions or the State Tracker's state summaries, the reward signal originates from the Executor's final action. These intermediate outputs are passed through the CES loop, and the Executor's action is objectively scored using a verifiable, rule-based reward function (termed Execution-Feedback Reward). This reward signal is then back-propagated to optimize the Coordinator and State Tracker policies. The reward function (R) is a weighted sum of a format reward (R_format, binary for correct output tags) and an executor reward (R_executor). The executor reward, in turn, is a weighted sum of a type reward (R_type, for correct action type) and a parameter reward (R_param, for correct action parameters, e.g., point within bounding box, scroll direction, text F1 similarity). This indirect reward mechanism ensures that the high-level agents are optimized towards verifiable task success.

- **Staged Optimization:**
 - **Stage 1: Optimizing the Coordinator’s Planning Capability:** In this stage, the Executor model is frozen. The reward is calculated based on the Executor's execution results, which are used to update the Coordinator's policy network. During this stage, the required task state (m_t-1) is provided directly from ground-truth annotated states in the preprocessed dataset. This ensures the Coordinator learns to map a "perfect" state to optimal atomic instructions.
 - **Stage 2: Optimizing the State Tracker’s State Evolution Capability:** After the Coordinator is trained and its parameters are frozen, the focus shifts to optimizing the State Tracker. The State Tracker generates task states (m_t-1), which are then passed to the fixed Coordinator and Executor, influencing the entire decision chain. The final Execution-Feedback Reward from the Executor is exclusively used to optimize the State Tracker. This process teaches the State Tracker to generate state summaries that are most useful for the fixed Coordinator to make optimal decisions.

This staged approach aims to prevent conflicting optimization objectives, allowing each high-level agent to specialize in its respective role while being guided by the ultimate success of the low-level execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that the CES framework significantly enhances the planning and state management capabilities of GUI agents, particularly for long-horizon tasks.

#### 4.1. Long-Horizon Task Performance

Evaluations were conducted on three long-horizon benchmarks: AITZ, AMEX, and GUI-Odyssey, with average steps ranging from 7.5 to 15.3. The metrics used were Type (action type accuracy), GR (grounding accuracy), and SR (success rate, requiring both correct action type and parameters).

- **CES with GUI-R1-7B Executor:** The proposed CES framework, when integrated with a GUI-R1-7B Executor, demonstrated substantial performance improvements over the standalone GUI-R1-7B baseline. Across all benchmarks, CES improved the Type accuracy by an average of 10.38%. This indicates that the Coordinator's ability to decouple high-level planning and generate clear atomic instructions allows the Executor to focus on perception and localization, leading to more accurate and stable execution.
- **Comparison with Generic LLMs (GPT-5 via prompting):** When GPT-5 was used (via prompting) as the Coordinator and State Tracker, the performance showed only minor improvements (average 4% increase in Type accuracy) compared to the GUI-R1-7B baseline, with some metrics even degrading. This result suggests that while powerful general-purpose models can offer some benefits through prompt engineering, they are less effective and stable than the specialized, RL-trained Coordinator and State Tracker of the CES framework, which are specifically optimized for GUI task planning and state understanding.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness and Generalization

Further experiments assessed the efficiency and generality of CES using three Executor models of varying sizes: UI-R1-3B, GUI-Owl-7B, and GUI-Owl-32B. Three configurations were tested: (i) Baseline (model used directly), (ii) CES-P (same base model acts as all three roles via prompting), and (iii) CES (full framework with specialized Coordinator and State Tracker, and the base model as Executor).

- **Architectural Superiority (CES-P vs. Baseline):** The CES-P setup generally outperformed the baseline across all model sizes. This finding supports the hypothesis that structural decoupling and explicit state management mechanisms, even when implemented through prompting a single model, contribute to addressing issues like state awareness and cognitive load in long-horizon tasks.
- **Validation of Capability Conflict Hypothesis:** The performance of CES-P varied with model size. For the smaller UI-R1-3B model, CES-P resulted in a performance degradation (e.g., an 18.05% drop in SR on GUI-Odyssey), suggesting that its limited parameter capacity struggled with learning distinct planning and execution capabilities simultaneously. In contrast, the larger GUI-Owl-32B model showed a significant improvement (e.g., 13.18% in SR on AMEX) with CES-P. This supports the paper's hypothesis that training conflicting capabilities within a single network is challenging, especially for smaller models, but larger models might tentatively acquire these latent high-level skills.
- **Efficacy of Specialized, Trained Components (CES vs. CES-P):** The full CES framework, with its specialized and RL-trained Coordinator and State Tracker, significantly outperformed CES-P. For example, on GUI-Odyssey, CES boosted GUI-Owl-32B's SR from 39.60% (baseline) to 56.75%, and GUI-Owl-7B's SR from 37.53% (CES-P) to 46.65%. This indicates that the staged execution-feedback RL strategy effectively trains the Coordinator and State Tracker to develop planning and state understanding strategies that are superior to generic prompting. The paper notes that a 7B Coordinator and 4B State Tracker with CES achieved comparable effects to a much larger 32B single model, illustrating the effectiveness of decoupled training.
- **Generality of the CES Framework:** CES consistently provided substantial performance improvements over the baseline for every Executor model tested (UI-R1-3B, GUI-Owl-7B, GUI-Owl-32B). This confirms that CES functions as a robust and generalizable plug-and-play solution capable of enhancing the long-horizon task automation capabilities of diverse underlying Executor models.

#### 4.3. Analysis

- **Component and Training Ablation Study:**
 - **Without Coordinator:** Removing the Coordinator and feeding high-level instructions directly to the Executor led to a significant performance drop (e.g., SR dropped by 12.77% on GUI-Odyssey). This confirms the necessity of decoupling planning from execution.
 - **Without State Tracker:** Replacing the State Tracker with a simple mechanism of recording the last four action histories resulted in a significant performance drop. This highlights the critical role of information compression and high-semantic state management in long-horizon tasks, as opposed to low-level action history.
 - **Without RL (SFT only):** Models trained with only SFT warm-up performed significantly worse than RL-optimized models. This demonstrates that while SFT teaches basic roles, execution-feedback RL is essential for acquiring generalizable and optimal planning policies.
- **Failure Case Analysis:** A detailed analysis showed that the CES framework nearly eliminated "State Loss" errors (reduced from 14% to 2%) and significantly reduced "Planning Error" (from 12% to 4%). This confirms that the trained Coordinator and State Tracker successfully address the core challenges of planning and state management. The remaining errors were primarily attributable to the frozen Executor's inherent perceptual limitations (e.g., "Perception Error" and "Generalization Failure"), indicating a shift in the performance bottleneck.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of GUI automation by addressing two fundamental and pervasive challenges in long-horizon tasks: responsibility coupling within single-agent architectures and the lack of task state awareness. The proposed Coordinator-Executor-State Tracker (CES) multi-agent framework, coupled with its staged execution-feedback reinforcement learning strategy, offers a structured and effective solution.

The key significance lies in its systematic approach to decoupling high-level strategic planning and state management from low-level execution. By introducing specialized agents—a Coordinator for strategic planning and task decomposition, and a State Tracker for dynamic, high-semantic context compression—the framework mitigates the cognitive load and capability conflicts observed in monolithic GUI agents. The use of a frozen, plug-and-play Executor provides a stable foundation for back-propagating verifiable execution-feedback rewards, enabling efficient and targeted optimization of the high-level schedulers.

The experimental results consistently demonstrate significant improvements in planning and state management capabilities across various long-horizon GUI benchmarks and with different Executor models. This not only validates the architectural superiority of the CES framework but also confirms its generalizability as a robust, plug-and-play solution that can enhance existing Executor models. The finding that a relatively smaller, specialized CES system can achieve comparable performance to much larger single-policy models suggests a path towards more efficient and performant GUI agents.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for the development of more reliable, autonomous, and versatile GUI automation systems. By solving the core problems of planning and state awareness, the CES framework can lead to:
- **Increased task success rates:** Agents will be less prone to getting lost or making suboptimal decisions in complex, multi-step scenarios.
- **Improved robustness:** Enhanced state awareness allows agents to recover more effectively from unexpected GUI changes or errors.
- **Broader applicability:** The generalizable nature of the framework means it can be readily integrated with various underlying execution models, extending its utility across different platforms and applications.
- **More efficient development:** By decoupling responsibilities, future research can focus on optimizing specific components without retraining the entire system.

Furthermore, this research opens new avenues for future work, particularly in exploring synergetic evolution and joint training of multi-agent systems for GUI tasks, which could lead to even more sophisticated and adaptive automation capabilities. The shift in performance bottleneck from cognitive errors to the Executor's perceptual limitations also highlights a clear direction for subsequent research efforts in the field.
