# Reinforcement Learning-Based Control for an Inline Skating Humanoid Robot

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich developed a reinforcement learning control policy enabling a humanoid robot to perform dynamic inline skating with passive consumer skates, demonstrating robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer and achieving up to a 50% reduction in Cost of Transport compared to walking at higher speeds. The robot learned emergent stroke-and-glide propulsion without human motion data, adapting to varied surfaces and external perturbations.

## Problem

- Achieving stable and dynamic bipedal locomotion for humanoid robots using inherently unstable passive inline skates.
- Generating propulsion with unactuated inline skates requires precise 6-DoF control and inferring critical states like wheel speed and slippage from secondary sensors due to partial observability.
- Mitigating simulation contact artifacts (e.g., "sticky contact," "edge clipping") when modeling narrow-profile passive wheels in physics engines.

## Method

- Implementing an ==asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning== framework to develop a control policy for a humanoid robot equipped with passive inline skates.
- Employing a novel approach for ==contact modeling==, using ==spherical primitives== for wheel contacts during training and ==ellipsoidal primitives== for validation to mitigate simulation artifacts.
- Designing a specialized ==kinematic wheel-rolling reward== function and a ==success-based command curriculum== to enable the agent to learn complex, energy-efficient skating gaits from scratch.

## Results

- The trained policy successfully transferred zero-shot to the physical Booster T1 robot, demonstrating dynamic balance, agile turning, and robustness to external perturbations and varied floor surfaces.
- Achieved up to a **50% reduction** in Cost of Transport (CoT) compared to a walking policy at higher forward velocities (e.g., **0.326** CoT for skating vs. **0.665** for walking at **1 m/s**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the novel contact modeling, kinematic rolling reward, and success-based command curriculum were essential for effective and robust skating, resulting in the lowest velocity tracking errors (**0.43 ± 0.26 m/s** for v_x).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling wheel geometries in simulation (spherical for training, ellipsoidal for validation) is effective in overcoming contact artifacts and ensuring robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for passive inline skates.
> - Reinforcement learning can autonomously discover and synthesize dynamic, energy-efficient locomotion strategies, such as emergent stroke-and-glide propulsion, on highly unstable platforms without human motion data.
> - Equipping humanoids with passive inline skates can significantly reduce the Cost of Transport at higher velocities compared to traditional walking gaits, enhancing operational efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has witnessed significant advancements, particularly in dynamic locomotion for humanoid robots, often leveraging Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques. This work contributes to this landscape by addressing the complex problem of bipedal locomotion using unactuated (passive) inline skates, an area that has been largely underexplored.

Previous efforts to integrate skating with robotics have primarily focused on quadrupedal platforms, which inherently offer greater stability, or have relied on specialized hardware such as feet that transform into wheels or high-friction claws for propulsion. Some bipedal skating robots have circumvented the challenge of passive propulsion by incorporating partially actuated wheels. A notable concurrent development is the SKATER robot, a custom humanoid designed with integrated passive wheels, which utilizes deep RL to achieve a continuous-contact "swizzle" gait for stability. In contrast, the current research targets a general-purpose humanoid robot modified with off-the-shelf consumer inline skates, thereby tackling the control complexity of unmodified passive wheels.

Controlling passive wheels, especially those arranged in a single line like inline skates, introduces significant instability. Unlike actively driven wheeled-legged systems, propulsion with passive inline skates requires precise six Degrees of Freedom (6-DoF) control over the skate's position and orientation to effectively engage the wheel edges for force generation. This task is further complicated by partial observability, as passive wheels lack encoders, requiring the system to infer critical states like wheel speed and slippage from secondary sensors. Traditional control strategies, such as Zero Moment Point (ZMP) methods, often impose conservative constraints like continuous ground contact, which limit dynamic maneuvers. While some prior work has used precomputed kinematic control for pushing off wheel edges, these methods typically lack dynamic robustness against disturbances and require re-adaptation for different trajectories.

This paper extends the application of RL, which has proven effective in complex whole-body movement tasks such as humanoid parkour, martial arts, table tennis, and skateboarding. The innovation lies in applying RL to the 6-DoF control required for bipedal inline skating, specifically by relaxing the continuous-contact constraints seen in prior work. This approach aims to enable the robot to learn dynamic balancing and edge control, facilitating complex maneuvers like human-like stroking, turning while accelerating, and rapid deceleration, without relying on human motion data, imitation learning, or precomputed trajectories. A key technical challenge addressed is the mitigation of simulation contact artifacts inherent in physics engines when modeling narrow-profile passive wheels.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to enable humanoid robots to combine their versatile agility with the high-speed, energy-efficient locomotion strategies observed in human inline skaters. Equipping humanoids with passive inline skates offers the potential for efficient traversal over long, flat distances with minimal hardware complexity, despite the inherent instabilities compared to standard walking or actuated wheeled locomotion. Mastering these locomotion strategies could expand the capabilities of humanoids into dynamic and agile domains such as inline hockey or figure skating. Additionally, such advancements could lead to multimodal robotic platforms that seamlessly transition between conventional stepping for rough terrain and high-speed rolling for flat environments, thereby exploiting the advantages of both locomotion types.

The paper articulates several key objectives:
1. **Develop an RL Control Policy:** Train and deploy a reinforcement learning control policy that enables a humanoid robot to execute novel, edge-driven propulsion strategies using passive inline skates. The goal is for these strategies to emerge purely from the reward structure, without reliance on external human motion data, imitation learning, or kinematic priors.
2. **Hardware Modification and Integration:** Successfully modify a bipedal humanoid platform (Booster T1) to integrate completely passive, off-the-shelf consumer inline skates using custom-designed stereolithography (SLA) mounts.
3. **Address Simulation Challenges:** Overcome the inherent instability of passive wheels and the common simulation contact artifacts in physics engines. This involves developing an RL pipeline that utilizes distinct geometric primitives (spherical for training, ellipsoidal for validation) for wheel modeling to ensure dynamic realism and robust sim-to-real transfer.
4. **Incentivize Authentic Skating:** Formulate a kinematic wheel-rolling reward that specifically incentivizes gliding and synthesizes a feasible skating policy without needing pre-programmed foot trajectories or motion priors.
5. **Achieve Energy Efficiency:** Demonstrate a significant reduction in Cost of Transport (CoT) compared to standard walking gaits, quantifying the efficiency benefits of the learned skating locomotion.
6. **Validate Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Successfully deploy the learned policy directly from simulation to the physical Booster T1 hardware, demonstrating dynamic balance, rejection of active physical perturbations, and agile locomotion strategies, including turning at speed, across various floor surfaces.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a reinforcement learning framework to train a control policy for a humanoid robot equipped with passive inline skates. The methodology encompassed mechanical modifications, an asymmetric actor-critic RL architecture, specialized contact modeling, a tailored reward function, and a curriculum-based learning approach.

**Hardware Platform and Modifications:**
The base platform utilized was the Booster T1, a humanoid robot with 23 Degrees of Freedom (DoF). Its legs, comprising 3-DoF hips, 1-DoF knees, and 2-DoF ankles, were well-suited for precise control over skate position and wheel edge angles. The standard feet of the Booster T1 were removed and replaced with consumer children's inline skates. Custom-designed Stereolithography (SLA) mounts, optimized for weight efficiency and impact resistance (using Formlabs' Tough 1500 resin), connected the skate wheels directly to the robot's heel joint. The skate bearings were upgraded to high-quality Rollerblade Bearings ILQ-9 Twincam Pro to minimize friction, and the wheels were replaced with less compressible Hydrogen Street 60/92A Roller Blade Wheels to better align with rigid-body simulation properties. The robot's internal sensor package, including high-resolution dual-encoders for joint positions and velocities, and an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) in the torso for base orientation estimation, provided proprioceptive feedback.

**Reinforcement Learning Framework:**
An asymmetric actor-critic architecture was implemented within a modified Booster Gym environment, designed for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. This architecture separates the information available during simulated training (critic) from what is deployable on hardware (actor).
- **Problem Formulation:** The environment was modeled as an infinite-horizon partially observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP).
- **Observation Space (Actor):** The actor network received a 74-dimensional observation vector, comprising noisy measurements of projected gravity, base angular velocity, velocity commands, gait phase clock, joint positions and velocities, and previous actions. These observations mirrored hardware-available sensor measurements.
- **State Space (Critic):** The critic network utilized an 88-dimensional state vector, which included noiseless, ground-truth versions of the actor's observations, augmented with privileged variables such as base mass, center of mass (CoM) offset, base linear velocity, base height, and external forces and torques, all inaccessible to the physical robot's onboard sensors.
- **Action Space:** The actor output a 21-dimensional vector representing target joint positions, which were then fed to a low-level Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller operating at a higher frequency to generate motor torques.

**Contact Modeling for Passive Wheels:**
A critical aspect of the methodology was addressing simulation artifacts in physics engines when modeling passive inline skates. Standard high-resolution meshes can cause "sticky contact," while cylindrical primitives lead to "edge clipping" and artificial restitution impulses that the RL agent could exploit.
- **Training Environment (Isaac Gym):** Spherical primitives were used to model wheel contacts, fitting the bottom curvature of the physical wheels to ensure a stable single-point contact patch.
- **Validation Environment (MuJoCo):** Ellipsoidal primitives were employed during sim-to-sim testing to validate the policy and prevent overfitting to the spherical abstraction. This dual-geometry approach aimed to ensure robustness and generalization to the true physical wheel geometry.

**Reward Formulation:**
To overcome the agent's tendency to default to unstable stepping or shuffling behaviors, a specialized kinematic rolling reward was introduced alongside standard regularization terms.
- **Kinematic Rolling Reward (r_wheel):** This reward incentivized gliding by penalizing the squared error between the robot's base forward linear velocity and the effective linear velocity of its wheels in contact with the ground. The effective linear wheel velocity was calculated by mapping the angular velocities of contacting wheels to linear motion and taking the maximum absolute average between the left and right feet. A Gaussian kernel was applied to this error.
- **Regularization:** Additional terms penalized excessive joint torques, velocities, and accelerations, as well as base orientation errors, promoting smooth, energy-efficient motions and hardware protection.

**Success-Based Command Curriculum:**
To address the sparse reward problem caused by the inherent instability of passive wheels, a success-based command curriculum was implemented.
- **Command Discretization:** The velocity command space (linear forward velocity v_cmd_x and angular yaw velocity ω_cmd_z) was discretized into a 2D grid.
- **Phased Learning:** Training began with commands strictly localized to zero velocity, allowing the agent to master basic balancing.
- **Dynamic Unlocking:** At the end of each episode, average velocity tracking performance and episode duration were evaluated against thresholds. If successful, adjacent higher-velocity command bins were dynamically unlocked, enabling the policy to incrementally learn complex skating behaviors, from standing to gliding and eventually dynamic turning.

**Training Details:**
The policy was trained with a simulation time step of 0.002 seconds. It required approximately 100 million steps to learn standing balance and around 3 billion steps (20,000 PPO iterations), equivalent to roughly 1.9 years of simulated experience, to master skating across the full command range.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated its methodology through sim-to-sim ablation studies, a Cost of Transport (CoT) analysis, and successful zero-shot sim-to-real deployment on the physical Booster T1 robot.

**Sim-to-Sim Ablation Study:**
An ablation study confirmed the necessity of the proposed contact modeling, reward formulation, and curriculum.
- **Cylindrical Wheels:** A baseline policy trained with standard cylindrical wheel primitives in Isaac Gym exploited simulation artifacts (clipping through the ground and artificial restitution impulses). This policy demonstrated poor forward velocity tracking (1.34 ± 0.33 m/s tracking error) and angular velocity tracking (0.56 ± 0.32 rad/s tracking error) when validated sim-to-sim in MuJoCo with ellipsoidal wheels. This result supported the design choice of decoupled wheel geometries.
- **No Rolling Reward:** Policies trained without the custom kinematic rolling reward exhibited occasional falls and poor forward velocity tracking, often resulting in a "stepping" or "shuffling" gait rather than gliding. (v_x error: 1.47 ± 0.28 m/s, ω_z error: 0.25 ± 0.13 rad/s).
- **No Curriculum:** Training without the success-based command curriculum led to less stable skating techniques, limiting the policy's ability to track the full velocity command range effectively (v_x error: 0.70 ± 0.21 m/s, ω_z error: 0.13 ± 0.10 rad/s).
- **Deployed Policy (Full Pipeline):** The complete pipeline achieved the lowest tracking errors and consistent 60-second episode durations in MuJoCo (v_x error: 0.43 ± 0.26 m/s, ω_z error: 0.12 ± 0.08 rad/s), indicating its robustness and effectiveness.

**Cost of Transport (CoT) Analysis:**
The efficiency of the learned skating gait was quantified by comparing its CoT to that of a conventional walking locomotion policy. CoT was defined as the total mechanical power expended per unit of gravitational potential energy and horizontal distance traveled.
- At low forward velocities (below 0.5 m/s), the walking policy exhibited a lower CoT.
- However, at higher forward velocities, the skating policy demonstrated significantly improved efficiency. For instance, at a command of 1 m/s linear velocity and 0 rad/s angular velocity, the walking policy resulted in a CoT of 0.665, while the skating policy achieved 0.326, representing approximately a 50% reduction.
- At even higher commanded velocities, the skating policy maintained a low CoT, whereas the walking policy's efficiency substantially decreased. The CoT analysis also highlighted that the skating policy struggled with quick turns on the spot due to non-holonomic constraints and exhibited asymmetry in yaw CoT due to a "dominant leg" propulsion strategy.

**Hardware Deployment (Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer):**
The trained policy successfully transferred zero-shot to the physical Booster T1 robot without further fine-tuning.
- **Dynamic Locomotion:** The robot demonstrated dynamic balance, forward acceleration, deceleration, and agile turning while in motion. It was also capable of performing zero-radius turns.
- **Robustness:** The policy exhibited high robustness to active physical perturbations (e.g., continuous external pushes) and unmodeled variations in the environment, such as uneven flooring and different surface types (wooden, brick paving, padded mats), despite not being exposed to these conditions during simulation training.
- **Real-world CoT:** Real-world measurements on padded mats (a suboptimal surface for inline skating) confirmed that the inline skating policy became significantly more energy-efficient than standard walking beyond approximately 0.6 m/s, sustaining lower CoT while achieving higher overall speeds.
- **Emergent Behavior:** The robot displayed emergent stroke-and-glide propulsion, which the authors indicate parallels human skating behavior. However, the policy converged on a "dominant leg" strategy rather than an alternating push-off, which was noted as a potential area for future improvement for optimal efficiency and human-like symmetry.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research represents a notable advancement in humanoid robot locomotion by demonstrating the first successful real-world deployment of a bipedal humanoid robot executing emergent stroke-and-glide propulsion with passive consumer inline skates, without reliance on predefined motion trajectories or human motion data.

The significance of this work stems from several key contributions:
- **Overcoming Simulation Challenges:** The novel approach of decoupling wheel geometries for training and validation addresses a long-standing challenge in simulating passive, narrow-profile wheels and their complex edge contacts. This method enhances the fidelity of physics simulations for such systems, leading to robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, which is a critical bottleneck in robot learning.
- **Novel Locomotion Strategies:** The development of a specialized kinematic rolling reward and a success-based command curriculum enabled the reinforcement learning agent to autonomously discover and synthesize complex, energy-efficient skating gaits from scratch. This demonstrates the potential of RL to unlock dynamic and agile behaviors that are difficult to engineer through traditional control methods.
- **Enhanced Energy Efficiency:** The quantitative demonstration of up to a 50% reduction in Cost of Transport compared to conventional walking gaits at higher speeds highlights the practical advantages of passive wheeled locomotion for humanoids. This efficiency gain is crucial for extending operational duration and range in real-world applications.
- **Increased Robustness and Agility:** The deployed policy exhibited dynamic balancing, the ability to reject external disturbances, and adaptability to varied, unmodeled terrain and surface properties. This robustness indicates the learned control policy's capacity to operate reliably in diverse environments, a key attribute for practical robotic systems.

The potential impact of this research is substantial:
- **New Application Domains:** By enabling humanoids to skate dynamically, this work opens possibilities for their participation in high-speed, agile domains previously inaccessible, such as inline hockey, figure skating, or other dynamic sports and tasks requiring rapid, efficient movement.
- **Multimodal Locomotion Systems:** The integration of passive skates as modular tools could facilitate the development of multimodal humanoid platforms. These robots could seamlessly transition between versatile legged locomotion for navigating rough or cluttered environments and energy-efficient skating for rapid traversal of flat surfaces, combining the strengths of both paradigms.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework established in this paper provides a robust foundation for future investigations. Acknowledged areas for future work include refining reward formulations to incentivize more human-like alternating-leg propulsion for potentially greater efficiency, adapting the framework to newer simulation environments, and exploring more complex scenarios such as ice skating, which involves intricate friction and phase-change dynamics. Developing autonomous transition capabilities between walking and skating control policies also represents a compelling avenue for advancing humanoid agility.
