# Response Wide Shut? Surprising Observations in Basic Vision Language Model Capabilities

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UBC, Vector Institute, IIT Hyderabad, and Microsoft Research India introduce a "three-space analysis" to diagnose Vision-Language Model (VLM) limitations, revealing that fine-grained visual information often exists in early processing stages but is lost by the language decoder. The study shows that VLMs exhibit a stronger reliance on texture over shape for object recognition, suggesting key areas for improving VLM architectures and training strategies.

## Problem

- Despite advanced capabilities, VLMs often exhibit surprising shortcomings in basic visual understanding (e.g., counting, fine-grained recognition, spatial relations).
- Existing evaluations primarily focus on final VLM textual responses, offering limited insight into which architectural component (visual encoder, projection, or language decoder) is responsible for deficiencies.
- Prevailing hypotheses sometimes attribute VLM visual shortcomings solely to limitations of their visual encoders, without detailed investigation of later stages.

## Method

- The paper introduces a =="three-space analysis"== methodology, dissecting VLMs into ==visual latent==, ==vision-language shared latent==, and ==language response== spaces to probe information flow.
- Linear probes are trained on intermediate feature representations and output token representations to assess what information is implicitly captured at each stage for object recognition, counting, and spatial understanding tasks.
- A diverse set of VLMs (contrastive, encoder-decoder generative, instruction-tuned) are evaluated, and robustness to visual corruptions, background transformations, and the impact of language priors and model scale are analyzed.

## Results

- Fine-grained object recognition accuracy drops by at least **45%** in the response space compared to **>90%** in intermediate spaces, attributed to ineffective joint fine-tuning.
- Object counting similarly shows at least a **14%** performance degradation in the response space despite high accuracy in earlier stages.
- For spatial understanding, visual latent representations perform worst, with performance improving in the VL projection and response spaces, suggesting the language decoder's role in compensating for initial visual encoder weaknesses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - For fine-grained recognition and counting, crucial visual information is well-encoded in the visual and vision-language projection spaces but is significantly lost or poorly utilized by the language decoder.
> - Visual encoders initially exhibit weaker performance for spatial understanding, but the language decoder, when appropriately fine-tuned, can partially compensate.
> - VLMs demonstrate a critical reliance on texture cues over shape information for object recognition, a divergence from human perception that impacts generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have rapidly emerged as transformative general-purpose tools capable of addressing a wide array of complex computer vision and language tasks, ranging from comprehending charts to interpreting humor in images and videos. Despite their impressive capabilities, a growing body of evidence paradoxically highlights their shortcomings in seemingly basic visual understanding skills, such as recognizing simple negations, accurately counting objects, or understanding spatial relationships. These contradictory observations have sparked debate regarding the underlying mechanisms of VLM intelligence, with some suggesting reliance on large-scale pattern matching and memory recall rather than human-like, step-by-step reasoning.

Previous research in analyzing VLM capabilities has predominantly focused on evaluating the final textual response generated by these models. While this approach provides a holistic measure of performance, it offers limited insights into *where* deficiencies arise within the complex, modular architecture of a VLM. For instance, some prior work (e.g., Tong et al., 2024) has attributed VLM shortcomings primarily to the limitations of their visual encoders.

This paper critically intervenes in this ongoing discussion by proposing a systematic and nuanced analysis that goes significantly beyond simply measuring final VLM performance. It aims to pinpoint the specific components (visual encoder, vision-language projection, or language decoder) responsible for observed limitations. By dissecting VLMs into their constituent intermediate feature spaces, the authors provide a more granular understanding of information flow and knowledge representation at different stages of processing. This approach directly challenges and refines existing hypotheses about VLM limitations, offering a more targeted diagnostic tool for understanding their strengths and weaknesses. It therefore contributes to the broader effort of building more robust, reliable, and interpretable multimodal AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are driven by the observed discrepancies between the advanced capabilities of state-of-the-art VLMs and their surprising deficiencies in fundamental visual understanding tasks. The authors seek to:

1. **Systematically Understand VLM Limitations:** Uncover the specific shortcomings of prominent VLMs when confronted with core visual capabilities essential for high-level visual reasoning, namely object recognition (coarse and fine-grained), object counting, and spatial arrangement understanding.
2. **Pinpoint Sources of Deficiency:** Move beyond surface-level performance metrics to diagnose *which components* within a VLM's architecture—the visual encoder, the vision-language (VL) projection module, or the language decoder—are responsible for observed performance drops. This addresses the critical gap in existing literature that primarily evaluates end-to-end VLM outputs.
3. **Analyze Information Processing Flow:** By probing intermediate feature spaces, the study aims to understand how visual information is captured, transformed, and potentially lost as it propagates through the VLM network. This includes investigating robustness to visual corruptions and the processing of foreground versus background information.
4. **Guide Future VLM Development:** Provide actionable insights that can inform the design of better VLM architectures, more effective training strategies, and targeted efforts to improve specific areas of visual understanding, ultimately fostering progress in the field.
5. **Challenge Existing Hypotheses:** Specifically, the paper aims to provide evidence that, contrary to some prevailing views (e.g., Tong et al., 2024), visual encoders are often quite proficient at basic visual tasks, and that significant information loss frequently occurs later in the VLM pipeline, particularly at the language decoder stage.

The motivation stems from the recognition that while VLMs are powerful, their lack of basic skills, despite processing large datasets, suggests a fundamental difference from human cognition. Understanding *why* these models fail at simple tasks is crucial for developing truly intelligent and general-purpose multimodal AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this paper's methodology revolves around a novel "three-space analysis" design, which enables a granular investigation of information processing within VLMs.

**A. Three-Space Analysis:**
The authors dissect VLMs into three distinct conceptual spaces, corresponding to their typical modular architecture:
1. **Visual Latent Space:** The output features of the visual encoder, representing the initial visual understanding.
2. **Vision-Language (VL) Shared Latent Space:** The output of the vision-language projection or alignment mechanism, where visual information is prepared for the language decoder.
3. **Language Response Space:** The final output generated by the language decoder, representing the VLM's explicit textual response.

**B. Choice of Vision-Language Models (VLMs):**
To ensure comprehensive analysis, the study selects a diverse set of representative VLMs from three major architectural categories:
- **Contrastive Multi-Encoder Models:** CLIP, ALBEF, CoCa (models with separate encoders aligned via contrastive learning).
- **Encoder-Decoder Generative Models:** BLIP-2 (models that project images to a low-dimensional representation for a language decoder).
- **Instruction Fine-tuned Models:** InstructBLIP, LLaVA v.1.5, LLaVA-NEXT (models additionally fine-tuned to follow human instructions).
The choice is motivated by their popularity, open-source availability (allowing intermediate feature access), and their use as starting points for more sophisticated VLMs.

**C. Choice of Visual Tasks and Benchmarks:**
The research focuses on fundamental visual capabilities deemed crucial for higher-level reasoning:
- **Object Recognition:**
 - *Coarse-grained:* PaintSkills and Pascal VOC datasets.
 - *Fine-grained:* Stanford Dogs and CUB (Caltech-UCSD Birds-200-2011) datasets.
- **Object Counting:** PaintSkills dataset, focusing on 1–4 instances.
- **Spatial Arrangement Understanding:** PaintSkills dataset, probing relationships like "above," "below," "left," and "right."

**D. Task Design:**
All visual tasks are formulated as classification problems with discrete choices. For VLMs that generate textual responses, tasks are presented as Visual Question Answering (VQA) queries (e.g., "What is the central object in the image? Choices - dog, cat...?"). Specific prompts are tailored to VLM types (e.g., explicit "answer in one word" for instruction-tuned models).

**E. Evaluation Design:**
- **Visual and VL Projection Space:** Linear probes (single-layer MLP logistic regression models) are trained on frozen, average-pooled feature representations from these spaces. This method assesses what information is implicitly captured by these early stages.
- **Response Space:** Evaluated in two ways:
 1. **Probe (Probe):** A linear probe is trained on average-pooled output token representations from the language decoder, similar to the other spaces.
 2. **Direct Textual Response (Text):** The VLM's direct textual output to the VQA query is evaluated using regex and fuzzy matching against ground truth labels. This represents the default way VLMs are typically used.

**F. Control Task for Probes:**
To validate that linear probes are genuinely capturing information encoded in the representations rather than learning the task from training data, a control task with randomly shuffled labels is included. Low performance on this control task confirms the validity of the probing results.

**G. Robustness Analysis:**
- **Visual Corruptions:** The study assesses VLM performance under various noise types (Gaussian, uniform, shot, impulse), blurs (motion, defocus), and weather changes (snow) applied to the Pascal VOC dataset.
- **Background Transformations:** Using COCO images and object masks, experiments are conducted to understand how foreground vs. background information is processed. Transformations include black/white backgrounds, silhouettes, "blur reverse" (blurred background), adding a red circle, edge maps (shape only), and patch shuffling (texture disruption).

**H. Impact of Language Priors:**
To isolate the role of visual reasoning, the performance of VLMs is compared against a "blind" language-only LLM (Vicuna) that receives only the textual question without the image.

**I. Impact of Model Size:**
The study investigates how scaling the language decoder (e.g., LLaVA-NEXT 7B, 13B, 34B variants) influences performance across the three spaces.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The comprehensive three-space analysis yielded several "surprising observations" regarding VLM capabilities and limitations:

1. **Object Recognition (Coarse-grained):** Visual and VL projection spaces consistently demonstrate high performance (above 95% accuracy) across all VLMs. While the response space shows a slight performance dip (at least 5% average), this is attributed to factors like the effectiveness of instruction tuning (e.g., InstructBLIP significantly outperforms BLIP-2 in response space).

2. **Object Recognition (Fine-grained):** A drastic performance drop (at least 45%+) is observed in the response space compared to the strong performance (>90%) of the visual and VL projection spaces. This suggests that while early stages capture fine-grained distinctions, this knowledge is not effectively translated by the language decoder into the final response. The authors attribute this to **ineffective joint fine-tuning** of the projection layer and language decoder, potentially due to insufficient fine-grained samples in the training data (e.g., LLaVA-665k having only 0.17% fine-grained dog breed samples). This directly contradicts claims that visual encoders are primarily at fault for such deficiencies.

3. **Impact of Language Decoder Scale:** While scaling up the LLM decoder size generally improves response space performance to some extent, it does not fully bridge the significant gap for fine-grained object recognition, where accuracy remains at least 35% lower than intermediate spaces.

4. **Object Counting:** Similar to fine-grained recognition, the visual and VL projection probes show high accuracy (>90%), but there is a considerable performance degradation (at least 14% drop) in the response space. This again points to limitations in the language decoder's ability to process and articulate quantitative visual information accurately.

5. **Spatial Understanding:** Contrary to other tasks, the trend reverses: visual latent representations perform the worst, with performance gradually improving in the VL projection space and further in the response space (especially for LLaVA-1.5). This suggests that **visual encoders initially lack robust spatial understanding capabilities**, but the language decoder, aided by relevant fine-tuning data (e.g., LLaVA-665k having ~35% spatial samples), can partially compensate and generate a more accurate final response.

6. **Impact of Language Priors:** A "blind" language-only LLM (Vicuna) baseline showed very low accuracy across tasks, indicating that language priors play a relatively insignificant role in the observed VLM performance for the carefully selected tasks and datasets (like PaintSkills, designed to minimize spurious correlations).

7. **Robustness to Visual Corruptions:** The response space is surprisingly the most robust to common visual corruptions. However, the authors interpret this as a consequence of the **deficiencies in information flow**: if visual information (and thus the effects of corruption) is not fully transferred to the response space, the response appears spuriously robust. Conversely, the VL projection space is identified as the least robust, particularly for LLaVA-family models, suggesting vulnerabilities in their projection mechanisms compared to Q-former-based architectures (BLIP family).

8. **Background Transformations and Visual Prompting:**
 - **Role of Background:** Clean backgrounds (black or white) significantly improve performance in visual and VL projection spaces (~11-12% gain), but less so in the response space (~4-5% gain), indicating information loss. When objects are masked (silhouette), background context becomes crucial, highlighting VLMs' ability to leverage environmental cues for recognition.
 - **Visual Prompting:** "Blur reverse" (blurring the background while keeping the object sharp) yields the best overall performance, providing the highest gains in the response space (~7-8%), suggesting it effectively helps VLMs focus. Simple interventions like adding a red circle yield smaller gains.
 - **Shape vs. Texture Reliance:** VLMs show a drastic performance drop with edge maps (shape preserved, texture removed) compared to patch shuffling (shape distorted). This strongly suggests that VLMs, unlike humans, rely more on **texture cues than shape information** for object recognition, a critical deficiency impacting generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes a significant contribution to the field of multimodal AI by providing the first systematic, component-wise analysis of VLM capabilities across intermediate processing stages. Its key significance lies in challenging prevailing assumptions and offering targeted insights for future VLM development.

**A. Refined Understanding of VLM Limitations:**
The most impactful finding is the identification that for many fundamental visual tasks (fine-grained recognition, counting, and the benefits of visual prompting), the necessary visual information is **present and well-encoded in the early visual and vision-language projection stages, but is significantly lost or poorly translated by the language decoder** into the final VLM response. This fundamentally shifts the diagnostic focus from solely blaming the visual encoder (as suggested by some prior work) to critically examining the alignment mechanisms and the language decoder's ability to effectively utilize multimodal inputs.

**B. Targeted Improvement Strategies:**
The insights derived from the three-space analysis directly suggest concrete avenues for improvement:
- **Enhancing Joint Fine-tuning:** Future research should prioritize developing better joint fine-tuning strategies for the vision-language projection layer and the language decoder. This includes incorporating more diverse and representative training data for tasks like fine-grained recognition and counting to ensure the knowledge captured visually is effectively verbalized.
- **Improving Visual Encoders for Spatial Reasoning:** For spatial understanding, where visual encoders were found to be weaker, the paper reaffirms the need for developing visual encoders that are inherently better at capturing and representing relational information between objects.
- **Addressing Texture Bias:** The observation that VLMs rely more on texture than shape is a critical deficiency. Future work should explore architectural modifications or training methodologies to induce a stronger reliance on shape, which is crucial for human-like generalization and robustness to variations in appearance.
- **Optimizing Information Flow:** The "surprising robustness" of the response space to corruptions, paradoxically linked to information loss, highlights the need to ensure that relevant visual information is not only encoded but also robustly transferred and utilized throughout the VLM pipeline.

**C. Broader Impact on VLM Development and Trust:**
By offering a methodology to pinpoint the "where" and "why" of VLM failures, this research can accelerate the development of more reliable and trustworthy multimodal AI systems. Understanding which modules contribute to specific capabilities or shortcomings is a crucial step towards:
- **Responsible AI:** Mitigating biases and inaccuracies by identifying their source within the model, potentially enabling targeted "editing" of specific modules to remove unwanted behaviors.
- **Interpretability:** Moving beyond black-box evaluations, this modular analysis contributes to a more interpretable understanding of VLM decision-making processes.
- **Benchmarking:** The proposed three-space evaluation can serve as a more rigorous benchmark for future VLM architectures, prompting developers to not only aim for high final performance but also to ensure robust information flow and utilization across all stages.

In essence, this paper provides a valuable diagnostic toolkit and a roadmap for building the next generation of VLMs that are not only capable of complex tasks but also grounded in a solid understanding of fundamental visual concepts.
