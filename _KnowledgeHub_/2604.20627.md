# Occupancy Reward Shaping: Improving Credit Assignment for Offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Occupancy Reward Shaping (ORS) enhances offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning by extracting long-horizon temporal information from learned generative world models through optimal transport to create policy-preserving dense rewards. The method improves performance by up to 2.2x over sparse-reward baselines across various complex locomotion, manipulation, and real-world Tokamak control tasks, while also reducing non-monotonicity in learned value functions.

## Problem

- Long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks in offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning suffer from difficult credit assignment due to significant temporal delays between actions and outcomes.
- Existing methods relying on local temporal distance estimators or graph constructions struggle with scalability and can accumulate errors in complex, long-horizon settings.
- Learned goal-conditioned value functions frequently show high non-monotonicity, hindering effective policy learning and trapping agents in sub-optimal states.

## Method

- Learn a state-action conditioned ==occupancy measure== using a recursive Bellman-like formulation and a flow matching model to capture multi-modal future state distributions.
- Extract global temporal dependencies by defining a policy-preserving reward function as the negative squared Wasserstein-2 distance between the goal and the learned occupancy measure.
- Train a goal-conditioned policy using the dense, shaped rewards with standard offline GCRL algorithms, leveraging the improved credit assignment signal.

## Results

- Achieved an average **2.2x** improvement in success rate over sparse-reward GCIQL across 13 challenging OGBench locomotion and manipulation tasks.
- Outperformed state-of-the-art long-horizon GCRL baselines, including graph-based and hierarchical methods, and demonstrated consistent superiority in three real-world Tokamak control tasks.
- Led to significantly lower value non-monotonicity error and smoother value function estimates, particularly in long-horizon antmaze navigation, providing a dense and structured reward signal.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generative world models, by learning the occupancy measure, implicitly capture the global geometry and long-term temporal dependencies of the environment.
> - Optimal transport provides a formal framework to extract goal-reaching temporal information from a learned occupancy measure, which can be directly translated into a reward signal.
> - The proposed reward shaping mechanism is provably policy-preserving, ensuring that the optimal policy derived from the shaped rewards aligns with the true optimal policy in the original sparse-reward setting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) frequently encounters the challenge of credit assignment, particularly when learning goal-directed behaviors from data characterized by a significant temporal delay between actions and their eventual long-term consequences. This challenge is pronounced in Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL), especially when agents are trained using sparse rewards from large offline datasets.

Traditional approaches to mitigate credit assignment issues in GCRL with sparse rewards include:
- **Manual Reward Design**: This is often infeasible due to the diversity and complexity of goals.
- **Learning Local Temporal Distance Estimators**: Methods such as those by Hartikainen et al. (2019) estimate local distances, which are then used to construct semi-parametric or non-parametric graphs (Savinov et al., 2018; Mezghani et al., 2023) to infer temporal information. However, these graph-based methods face limitations in scalability with increasing task complexity, as composing local information to infer global temporal dependencies can lead to compounding errors.
- **Hierarchical RL (HRL) and N-step Returns**: Other strategies for long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks involve hierarchical policy structures (Chane-Sane et al., 2021; Park et al., 2023) or using n-step temporal difference learning (De Asis et al., 2018; Ahn et al., 2025) to effectively reduce the temporal horizon.

This paper addresses a gap in these existing methods by proposing a direct approach to extract global, long-term temporal dependencies. It posits that generative world models (Janner et al., 2020b; Schramm & Boularias, 2024; Farebrother et al., 2025), which capture the multi-modal distribution of future states an agent might visit (the occupancy measure), inherently encode the geometry of the environment. The challenge that this work specifically tackles is how to effectively extract this implicit temporal information from a learned world model and leverage it for credit assignment in offline GCRL, rather than relying on local distance estimates or graph constructions. The approach falls under the domain of reward shaping (Ng et al., 1999), specifically policy-preserving reward shaping, distinguishing it from methods that use reward shaping primarily as an exploration bonus.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve credit assignment in offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) tasks characterized by sparse rewards and long temporal horizons. The motivation for this objective stems from a critical observed limitation in current GCRL approaches.

Specifically, the authors identify that in long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks, the estimated goal-conditioned value function, `V^(s,g)`, frequently exhibits high non-monotonicity. This means that the value function does not consistently increase as the agent gets closer to the goal, leading to inaccurate credit assignment and causing policies to become trapped in sub-optimal regions of the state space. The paper empirically supports this hypothesis by analyzing the value non-monotonicity error (`δ_V`) in the `antmaze-giant-navigate` task. Their experiments show that `δ_V` is high even with small value estimation errors and increases with the magnitude of noise in value estimates (`σ_v`) and the task horizon (Figure 3, 4). This observation motivates the need for reward functions that can provide a more consistent and informative signal across long horizons.

The central question addressed by the paper is: "How do we design reward functions that encode long-horizon temporal structure and thereby mitigate value non-monotonicity with effective credit assignment, leading to performative policies?"

To achieve this, the authors propose leveraging the inherent capabilities of generative world models. They hypothesize that these models, by capturing the *occupancy measure* (the distribution of future states an agent may visit), possess latent information about the underlying geometry and temporal dependencies of the environment. The key objectives are therefore:
1. To formally establish how the temporal information stored in world models encodes the underlying geometry of the world.
2. To develop a method to extract this geometry from a learned model of the occupancy measure into a reward function that explicitly captures global, long-horizon goal-reaching information.
3. To ensure that this extracted reward function, when used for reward shaping, provably preserves the optimal policy, thereby maintaining theoretical soundness.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to provide a scalable and robust solution to the credit assignment problem in offline GCRL, overcoming the limitations of methods that rely on local approximations or graph constructions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Occupancy Reward Shaping (ORS)**, operates in three main stages: learning a generative model of the occupancy measure, extracting temporal information from this model into a reward function using optimal transport, and finally, training a goal-conditioned policy using the shaped rewards.

#### 3.1. Learning the Occupancy Measure

The first stage focuses on learning the state-action conditioned occupancy measure, denoted `d_π_D(s+|s,a)`, which represents the distribution of future states reachable from `(s,a)` under the dataset policy `π_D`.
- **Recursive Formulation**: The occupancy measure is learned using a recursive Bellman-like form:
 `d_π_D_θ(s+|s,a) = (1-γ) p(s'|s,a) + γ d_π_D_θ-(s+|s',a')`
 This formulation effectively "stitches" together future states across trajectories in the offline dataset `D`. `θ` parameterizes the current occupancy model, while `θ-` represents a time-delayed, target version for bootstrapping.
- **Flow Matching for Learning**: To capture the multi-modal nature of `d_π_D` and facilitate efficient computation of the reward function, a flow matching model is employed. The model learns a velocity field `v_θ(t,x)` that transforms a simple base distribution to the target data distribution. The training objective `L_flow(θ)` is composed of two parts:
 - `L_next(θ)`: A standard flow matching loss that learns to map a base distribution to the immediate next state `s'` (i.e., the transition function `p(s'|s,a)`).
 - `L_future(θ)`: A bootstrapping loss that regresses the velocity field `v_θ(t,s,a,x_t)` towards a stop-gradient estimate of the velocity field of the target occupancy measure `d_π_D_θ-(s+|s',a')`.
- **Warm-start Pretraining**: Optionally, an initial warm-start phase trains the occupancy model to predict future states sampled from a geometric distribution over trajectory timesteps without bootstrapping (Eq. 8), though consistent improvements were not reported for this phase.

#### 3.2. Extracting Temporal Information from the Occupancy Measure

The core of ORS lies in extracting the inherent world geometry from the learned occupancy measure into a goal-reaching reward function.
- **Formalizing Geometry with Optimal Transport**: The paper establishes a connection between the occupancy measure and state-space geometry using the squared Wasserstein-2 distance. For a given state-action pair `(s,a)` and goal `g`, the term `W_2^2(δ_g, d_π_D(s+|s,a))` measures the average squared Euclidean distance from the goal `g` to the future states `s+` predicted by the occupancy measure.
- **Proposition 1**: This proposition formally states that this squared Wasserstein-2 distance is monotonically *increasing* with respect to the shortest-path distance to the goal `g` (i.e., states further from the goal have larger `W_2^2` values). Crucially, it also states that this distance is *minimized* by the optimal action `a*` at any given state. This property is fundamental for defining a goal-reaching reward.
- **Reward Function Definition**: Based on Proposition 1, the goal-conditioned reward function `r_W(s,a,g)` is defined as the negative of the squared Wasserstein-2 distance: `r_W(s,a,g) = -W_2^2(δ_g, d_π_D(s+|s,a))`. This ensures that states leading to future occupancy distributions closer to the goal receive higher rewards.
- **Estimating `W_2^2` with Velocity Fields**: Directly computing `W_2^2` can be intractable. **Proposition 2** provides a solution by demonstrating that `W_2^2(δ_g, d_π_D(s+|s,a))` is upper bounded by the mean squared error between the learned velocity field `v_θ(t,s,a,x_t)` (from the flow matching model) and a target velocity field that directly moves samples from a base distribution to the goal `g` (`x_1 - x_0` where `x_1=g`).
- **Reward Network Training**: A neural network `ψ` is trained to approximate `r_W(s,a,g)` by minimizing `L_rew(ψ)` (Eq. 5). This loss function measures the squared difference between the network's output `r_W_ψ(s,a,g)` and the negative of the mean squared error of the velocity field when `x_1` is set to the goal `g`. This directly converts the temporal information captured by `v_θ` into a scalar reward signal.

#### 3.3. Policy Preservation (Theorem 1)

A crucial aspect of reward shaping is ensuring that it does not alter the optimal policy. **Theorem 1** formally proves that, under reasonable assumptions regarding goal reachability, dynamics, and dataset coverage, the greedy policy derived from the optimal action-value function `Q*(s,a,g)` (which is induced by the ORS reward `r_W(s,a,g)`) coincides with the true optimal shortest-path policy `π*(a|s,g)`. This theoretical guarantee indicates that ORS provides a dense, informative reward signal without introducing sub-optimal behaviors.

#### 3.4. Method Summary (Algorithm 1)

The overall ORS method proceeds in three sequential stages:
1. **Occupancy Model Training**: The flow matching model `d_π_D_θ` is trained using `L_flow(θ)` on the offline dataset.
2. **Reward Function Training**: The reward network `r_W_ψ` is trained using `L_rew(ψ)` to approximate the policy-preserving goal-conditioned reward.
3. **Policy Training**: Any standard offline GCRL algorithm (e.g., GCIQL), using a TD-learning critic, is then trained using the learned `r_W_ψ` as its reward function to learn the final goal-conditioned policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Occupancy Reward Shaping (ORS) was evaluated through extensive empirical analysis across diverse tasks and compared against multiple baseline methods.

#### 4.1. Performance on OGBench Tasks (Locomotion and Manipulation)

ORS was tested on 13 challenging long-horizon offline GCRL tasks from OGBench, encompassing maze navigation, cube manipulation, puzzle manipulation, and scene tasks. Datasets included both noisy expert (`*-play`, `*-navigate`) and highly sub-optimal (`*-noisy`, `*-explore`) data. Performance was measured by the average binary success rate on 5 test-time goals over 8 random seeds.
- **Overall Superiority**: ORS achieved the best performance on most tasks. On average, ORS demonstrated a 2.2× improvement in performance over its sparse-reward base algorithm, Goal-Conditioned Implicit Q Learning (GCIQL).
- **Comparison to General Baselines**: ORS consistently outperformed general offline GCRL baselines such as GCBC, GCIVL, QRL, and CRL across both locomotion and manipulation domains.
- **Comparison to Long-Horizon Specific Methods**:
 - **Go-Fresh**: ORS generally outperformed Go-Fresh, a graph-based reward shaping method, on the majority of tasks, including complex ones like `antmaze-giant-navigate` and `cube-triple-play` which involve long horizons (up to 2000 steps). Go-Fresh showed better performance on `antmaze-large-explore`, potentially due to its additional local reward component.
 - **Hierarchical & N-step Methods**: Compared to hierarchical methods (HIQL, SAW) and n-step return approaches (n-step GCIQL, GCIQL-OTA), ORS achieved 2.2× and 1.9× higher average performance, respectively. While HIQL and SAW were effective on `antmaze` locomotion, they struggled with manipulation tasks. SMORE was largely ineffective (mean success rate of 2.8%). The strong performance of ORS with a non-hierarchical policy emphasizes the effectiveness of its reward shaping.
- **Robustness to Data Quality**: ORS demonstrated effectiveness even with highly sub-optimal data, such as `*-noisy` and `*-explore` datasets.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness in Real-World Tokamak Control Tasks

ORS was evaluated on three real-world actuator control tasks in a nuclear fusion reactor (Tokamak) (control `β_N`, Electron density, Ion rotation). These tasks are characterized by non-linear, stochastic dynamics. Performance was measured as the cumulative negative L2 distance (reward) per episode across 10 test-time goals over 4 seeds.
- **Consistent Superiority**: ORS consistently achieved the best performance across all three Tokamak control tasks, outperforming all long-horizon specific baselines.
- **Stochastic Dynamics**: Go-Fresh performed poorly, suggesting that its graph-based reward shaping may be less effective in environments with highly stochastic dynamics compared to ORS's occupancy-based approach.

#### 4.3. Influence on Value Learning

The impact of ORS on value learning dynamics was analyzed using the `antmaze-giant-navigate` task.
- **Reduced Non-Monotonicity**: ORS led to a significantly lower average value non-monotonicity error (`δ_V`) at lower noise levels (`σ_v`) compared to sparse rewards or directly using `r_W(s,g)`.
- **Smoother Value Estimates**: ORS induced much less noisy estimates of the approximate value function (`V^(s,g)`) over long horizons (Figure 5, center).
- **Structured Reward Signal**: Visualizations showed that ORS rewards smoothly decay in magnitude with increasing temporal distance from the goal, providing a dense and continuous signal crucial for effective credit assignment (Figure 5, right).

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies on Design Choices

- **L2 Rewards**: A baseline using L2 distance to the goal for reward shaping (L2) was ineffective, performing worse than even sparse-reward GCIQL (Table 4). This highlights the importance of the occupancy-based distance for encoding meaningful temporal structure.
- **State-Only Rewards vs. Q-function Learning**:
 - ORS (learning a Q-function as a cumulative sum over `r_W(s,a,g)`) performed best.
 - ORS-s (using state-only rewards `r_W(s,g)`) performed worse, indicating the importance of action-conditioned rewards.
 - ORS-r (directly using `r_W(s,a,g)` as the Q-function) performed the worst, suggesting that a Q-function that accumulates rewards via TD learning is crucial for stitching trajectories and robustness against noisy reward estimates (Figure 6).
- **Expectile Parameter κ**: For locomotion tasks, ORS achieved higher performance with lower `κ` values, suggesting that the dense reward signal from ORS reduces the need for aggressive expectile regression. For manipulation tasks, the optimal `κ` increased with task complexity (Table 5).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Reinforcement Learning by offering a novel approach to address the persistent challenge of credit assignment in offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning, particularly in scenarios with sparse rewards and long temporal horizons.

The significance of Occupancy Reward Shaping (ORS) lies in several key aspects:
- **Novel Theoretical Insight**: The work formally establishes a connection between generative world models and state-space geometry, demonstrating how the temporal information implicitly captured by a learned occupancy measure can be explicitly recovered using optimal transport. This provides a rigorous foundation for extracting goal-reaching information.
- **Policy-Preserving Reward Shaping**: A crucial theoretical contribution is the proof that ORS provably does not alter the optimal policy. This ensures that the shaped rewards guide the agent towards the same optimal behavior as would be learned with a perfectly designed sparse reward function, while providing a richer and denser learning signal.
- **Empirical Performance and Scalability**: ORS achieves state-of-the-art results across a diverse suite of challenging locomotion and manipulation tasks, including complex, long-horizon scenarios where traditional and existing specialized methods struggle. Its effectiveness extends to real-world applications, as demonstrated by its superior performance in Tokamak control tasks. Unlike prior graph-based reward shaping methods, ORS leverages a global understanding of the state space from the occupancy measure, enabling it to scale more effectively with task complexity without accumulating errors from local approximations.
- **Mitigation of Value Non-monotonicity**: By providing dense, structured rewards that smoothly decay with distance from the goal, ORS demonstrably reduces the non-monotonicity of learned value functions. This leads to more stable and efficient learning, which is critical for solving tasks requiring extensive planning.
- **Simplicity and Adaptability**: ORS is designed to be implemented on top of existing offline GCRL algorithms, requiring minimal modifications to the policy learning pipeline. This makes it a broadly applicable and accessible technique for enhancing existing methods.

The potential impact of ORS is substantial. By providing a robust and scalable method for credit assignment, it can accelerate the application of offline GCRL to real-world problems characterized by limited interaction and complex goal-directed behaviors, such as robotics, autonomous systems, and advanced scientific control (e.g., nuclear fusion). The approach opens new avenues for leveraging the information stored within learned world models for various RL challenges beyond reward shaping.

A noted limitation by the authors is that ORS might be less effective in extremely sparse, highly combinatorial state spaces where few trajectories reach the goal, as the occupancy measure itself might provide limited signal. Future work could explore augmenting ORS with local rewards or learning occupancy over a more "useful" subset of future states to address these scenarios.
