# FutureRTC: Real-Time Robot Execution with Anticipatory-Conditioned Action Chunking

> [!summary] Summary
> FutureRTC, a plug-and-play framework, enhances real-time robotic execution using Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies by proactively anticipating future visual observations and proprioceptive states. This approach achieves superior robustness to inference delays, demonstrating only a 5.7-6.4% drop in success rate on LIBERO benchmark tasks when delays increased from 5 to 20 steps, while generating smoother and more efficient robot trajectories in both simulation and real-world scenarios.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models face significant computational requirements, leading to inference latency and prediction-execution misalignment in real-time asynchronous robot control.
- This misalignment causes VLA policies to generate actions based on stale observations and states, resulting in inter-chunk discontinuities, jerky motions, and reduced task success.
- Existing solutions are limited, either introducing 'stop-and-go' behaviors (synchronous), relying on heuristic post-processing (inference-time), or requiring costly retraining/fine-tuning (training-time), often neglecting the critical issue of stale visual observations.

## Method

- **FutureRTC** is developed as a plug-and-play adaptation layer that anticipates execution-time visual observations and proprioceptive states for existing, frozen VLA policies.
- A ==State Correction Module (SCM)== predicts a compensatory residual to accurately correct rolled-forward proprioceptive states, accounting for discrepancies between commanded and actual robot motion.
- An ==Observation Prediction Module (OPM)== predicts execution-time visual latent features by leveraging robot motion as an explicit physical prior for spatial warping and content synthesis, while a ==Policy Consistency Loss== ensures alignment with the base policy's expectations.

## Results

- FutureRTC demonstrated superior robustness to inference delays, with average success rates on Kinetix experiencing only a **3.0%** drop from 0 to 4 steps of delay and a **5.7-6.4%** drop on LIBERO for delays from 5 to 20 steps, outperforming all baselines.
- The framework produced the smoothest action velocity and acceleration profiles, significantly reducing inter-chunk discontinuities and requiring fewer execution steps across simulated and real-world tasks.
- In real-world bimanual manipulation, FutureRTC consistently achieved the highest task success rates, fewest execution steps, and shortest execution times, confirming robust transferability with minimal computational overhead (e.g., **~3-6M** parameters and **~3-3.6ms** added latency).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurate prediction of future visual observations, in addition to proprioceptive states, is critical for robust asynchronous VLA execution, as stale visual context significantly degrades performance.
> - Leveraging robot motion as a physical prior for visual feature evolution provides a constrained and effective mechanism for anticipating future visual scene changes.
> - Significant improvements in real-time asynchronous VLA control can be achieved by an external adaptation layer that conditions the policy with anticipated inputs, without requiring costly modification or retraining of the large VLA model itself.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has seen advancements with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which integrate deep visual-linguistic reasoning with action generation capabilities. These models offer potential for generalist robot control by unifying complex perception and control tasks. However, the substantial computational requirements of VLA models present a significant challenge for real-time robotic deployment, where low-latency and high-frequency control are critical.

To address the computational bottleneck and the accumulation of errors from step-wise execution, action chunking has been widely adopted. This strategy enables VLA policies to generate sequences of future actions in a single forward pass, amortizing inference overhead through open-loop execution. While this approach helps manage computational costs, it introduces a trade-off: executing longer chunks reduces responsiveness to environmental changes, while shorter chunks can lead to inter-chunk discontinuities and jerky motions.

The challenge of inference latency is further exacerbated when VLA models are deployed on resource-constrained edge platforms or remote servers. Two main execution paradigms attempt to manage this:
- **Synchronous execution**: The robot pauses and waits for the policy to compute the next action chunk, ensuring actions are conditioned on the most recent observations and states. However, this introduces "stop-and-go" behaviors, significantly degrading real-time responsiveness.
- **Asynchronous execution**: The policy computes the next action chunk concurrently with the robot executing the current chunk, thereby eliminating stalls. This improves computational efficiency but creates a "prediction-execution misalignment." Actions are generated based on observations and proprioceptive states from an earlier time step, which become stale by the time the actions are executed. This misalignment results in inter-chunk discontinuities, accumulating errors, and potential task failures.

Existing methods to mitigate these issues fall into two categories:
- **Inference-time strategies**: These methods, such as Temporal Ensembling (TE) or Real-Time Chunking (RTC), apply heuristic post-processing to smooth chunk boundaries without modifying the policy parameters. However, they can introduce additional computational overhead during runtime and do not fundamentally address the staleness of conditioning inputs.
- **Training-time strategies**: These methods, such as VLASH or Training-Time RTC (T-RTC), explicitly adapt the policy to asynchronous execution by requiring costly retraining or fine-tuning of the VLA model. VLASH attempts to reduce misalignment by forward-predicting future proprioceptive states but still relies on outdated visual observations. This approach is limited because the visual scene evolves alongside robot motion during the delay interval, making state correction alone insufficient.

FutureRTC positions itself within this landscape by addressing the core issue of prediction-execution misalignment in asynchronous VLA execution. It aims to overcome the limitations of prior work by anticipating both execution-time visual observations and proprioceptive states, thus providing a more complete and accurate context for the VLA policy without modifying the underlying policy itself. This "plug-and-play" nature distinguishes it from training-time methods that require policy re-optimization, while its comprehensive approach to predicting future context differentiates it from inference-time and state-only prediction methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient framework for real-time robot execution using Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies under asynchronous control, specifically addressing the "prediction-execution misalignment." This misalignment occurs because, in asynchronous execution, the robot's VLA policy generates future action chunks based on observations and proprioceptive states that are "stale" due to inference latency, while the robot is concurrently executing previously generated actions. By the time the newly computed chunk is ready, the robot's actual state and the visual scene have evolved, leading to actions being executed in an outdated context. This manifests as inter-chunk discontinuities, jerky movements, slower task completion, and reduced task success rates.

The key motivations for developing FutureRTC are:
1. **Rectifying Prediction-Execution Misalignment**: The fundamental issue in asynchronous VLA control is the temporal gap between when conditioning inputs (observations and states) are acquired and when the actions derived from them are actually executed. This work aims to bridge this gap by enabling the policy to condition on inputs that accurately reflect the robot's state and environment at the *time of execution*, rather than the time of inference initiation.
2. **Comprehensive Context Prediction**: Previous attempts, such as VLASH, have focused on predicting future proprioceptive states but largely neglected visual observations. The paper argues that visual observations are equally, if not more, critical as the visual scene changes significantly with robot motion during the delay period. Therefore, a core motivation is to predict *both* execution-time proprioceptive states and visual representations.
3. **Plug-and-Play Adaptation**: Many training-time methods require costly fine-tuning or re-training of the entire VLA model to adapt to asynchronous execution. This work aims to develop a "plug-and-play" framework, an adaptation layer that works with existing, pre-trained VLA policies without modifying their internal parameters. This approach seeks to reduce computational overhead associated with policy adaptation and increase the flexibility and applicability of the solution across various VLA backbones.
4. **Improving Real-Time Performance Metrics**: The ultimate motivation is to enhance the practical deployment of VLA models by achieving:
 - **Robustness to inference delays**: Ensuring consistent performance even with varying and significant latencies.
 - **Smoother trajectories**: Eliminating inter-chunk discontinuities and jerky motions.
 - **Faster execution**: Reducing overall task completion time and steps.
 - **Higher task success rates**: Improving the reliability of robot operations.
5. **Leveraging Physical Priors**: Recognizing that robot motion is a primary cause of visual change in manipulation tasks, the research is motivated to explicitly leverage this physical prior for more constrained and effective visual observation prediction, rather than relying solely on data-driven synthesis.

In summary, FutureRTC aims to provide a reliable, efficient, and broadly applicable solution for deploying VLA policies in real-time robotic systems by proactively anticipating the true execution-time context, thereby making asynchronous execution as accurate and smooth as delay-free synchronous execution, but without the associated stalls.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FutureRTC is presented as a plug-and-play adaptation framework designed to anticipate execution-time observations and states for real-time Vision-Language-Action (VLA) control. The framework achieves this by integrating a State Correction Module, an Observation Prediction Module, and a Policy Consistency Loss, all operating without modifying the underlying VLA policy parameters.

**Preliminaries and Problem Formulation:**
The core problem arises from asynchronous execution of a VLA policy `π_θ`, which generates a chunk of `H` actions `A_m` from observation `o_t` and state `s_t`. A subset of `K` actions is executed. Due to inference latency `δ`, quantified by delay `d = ⌊δ/Δt⌋`, the subsequent chunk `A_m+1` is conditioned on stale inputs `(o_t+K-d, s_t+K-d)` but is intended for execution at time `t+K`. This `d`-step temporal gap constitutes the prediction-execution misalignment. The first `d` actions of `A_m+1` are conventionally discarded, leading to actions `A_m+1[d:d+K-1]` being executed. FutureRTC aims to provide the VLA policy with an anticipated pair `(ˆz_t+K, ˆs_t+K)` (where `z` is the latent feature of `o`) so that `A_m+1` can be generated and directly executed from `A_m+1[0:K-1]`.

**1. State Correction Module (SCM):**
The execution-time proprioceptive state `s_t+K` is unobservable at the chunk generation time `t+K-d`. A naive approach involves rolling forward the stale state `s_t+K-d` using the committed actions `[a_t+K-d : a_t+K-1]` to estimate `˜s_t+K`. However, this forward integration accumulates bias due to the discrepancy between commanded and actual robot motion.
The SCM, `ϕ_SCM(·)`, is an MLP-based module designed to predict a compensatory residual `˜s_∆`. This residual is calculated from the rolled-forward state `˜s_t+K` and the normalized inference delay `d/d_max`. The final corrected execution-time state is then computed as `ˆs_t+K = ˜s_t+K ⊕ ˜s_∆`, with orientation updated through relative-rotation composition.
During training, a state correction loss `L_state = ||(s_t+K ⊖ ˜s_t+K) - ˜s_∆||^2_2` is used to minimize the difference between the predicted residual and the ground-truth residual, which is derived from actual execution-time and rolled-forward states from demonstrations.

**2. Observation Prediction Module (OPM):**
Correcting the state alone is deemed insufficient, as stale visual observations also contribute significantly to performance degradation, especially with increasing delays. The OPM, `ϕ_OPM(·)`, predicts the execution-time visual representation `z_t+K` (latent feature of `o_t+K`) from the stale latent `z_t+K-d`, committed actions `[a_t+K-d : a_t+K-1]`, and the corrected state `ˆs_t+K`. The OPM operates in the latent space of the VLA's vision encoder to ensure compatibility and limit computational overhead.

The module leverages robot motion as an explicit physical prior for feature evolution:
- **Motion Prior Encoding**: The sequence of committed actions `a_i` within the delay window is processed to create a physical feature vector `u_i` for each step, encoding instantaneous command, cumulative displacement, action increment, accumulated path length, and temporal progress. These `u_i` are concatenated into a trajectory feature matrix `U`, which is then passed through a temporal self-attention block to generate an action-conditioned motion prior `c_motion`.
- **Feature Fusion and Warping**: The motion prior `c_motion`, stale latent `z_t+K-d`, camera embedding `e_cam`, position embedding `e_pos`, and corrected state `ˆs_t+K` are fused into a conditioning feature `F`. This `F` is projected through linear and convolutional layers to predict a 2D flow displacement `∆p` and a transport gate `α ∈`. The stale feature `z_t+K-d` is spatially warped using differentiable bilinear grid sampling, `W(z_t+K-d, p - ∆p)`, and blended with the original stale feature using the transport gate: `z_trans = (1 − α) · z_t+K-d + α · W(z_t+K-d, p - ∆p)`. The `α` gate allows static regions to retain their original representation while moving regions rely more on warped features.
- **Feature Synthesis**: To account for newly exposed content, occlusions, or deformations not explained by geometric warping, a gated residual branch is introduced. It takes `F`, the transport residual `z_trans - z_t+K-d`, and an initial observation `z_0` (as a static visual reference) to predict a synthesis feature `r` and a synthesis gate `β ∈`. The final execution-time latent is reconstructed as `ˆz_t+K = z_trans + β · r`. The `β` gate assigns higher weights to areas requiring content synthesis.
During training, an observation prediction loss `L_obs = ||z_t+K - ˆz_t+K||^2_2` minimizes the discrepancy between the predicted latent and the ground-truth latent.

**3. Policy Consistency Loss:**
To ensure that the predicted `(ˆz_t+K, ˆs_t+K)` are effectively utilized by the frozen VLA policy, a policy consistency loss `L_policy` is introduced. This loss encourages the action chunk generated by the untouched policy using the anticipated pair `π_θ(· | ˆz_t+K, ˆs_t+K)` to align with the chunk generated from the ground-truth execution-time pair `π_θ(· | z_t+K, s_t+K)`. A single-step flow approximation is used during training for computational efficiency. `L_policy = ||π_θ(z_t+K, s_t+K) - π_θ(ˆz_t+K, ˆs_t+K)||2_2`.

**Overall Objective Function:**
The total objective function for training FutureRTC is `L_total = L_state + L_obs + λL_policy`, where `λ` is a hyperparameter (set to 10). The framework is trained using the Adam optimizer, with a learning rate decay, and delays `d` are uniformly sampled from `U[1, d_max]` to ensure robustness across a range of latencies. Once trained, the VLA policy uses the anticipated `(ˆz_t+K, ˆs_t+K)` to generate `A_m+1`, which is then executed directly from its beginning (`A_m+1[0:K-1]`).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FutureRTC encompassed simulated benchmarks (Kinetix and LIBERO) and real-world robotic manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against both inference-time and training-time asynchronous execution methods.

**Simulated Benchmark Results:**

- **Kinetix (d ∈):**
 - FutureRTC demonstrated superior robustness to inference delays. The average success rate across 12 tasks experienced only a 3.0% drop from d=0 to d=4, while requiring fewer execution steps when d ≥ 2. This suggests more decisive and continuous control compared to baselines.
 - Naive Asynchronous execution showed a significant performance collapse with increasing delays. Inference-time methods provided partial mitigation, and training-time methods exhibited stronger robustness but still suffered degradation under large delays due to reliance on outdated visual information.
 - VLASH, which predicts only future proprioceptive states, was inferior to FutureRTC, indicating the importance of visual observation prediction.
 - FutureRTC's performance was competitive with, and in some cases surpassed, training-time methods that fine-tune or retrain the policy, despite FutureRTC operating as a plug-and-play module without modifying the base VLA policy.

- **LIBERO (d ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20}):**
 - FutureRTC was evaluated with two VLA backbones, `π_0.5` and `SmolVLA-450M`, across four task suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long).
 - Across all evaluated delays and VLA backbones, FutureRTC consistently achieved the highest average success rates and required the fewest execution steps.
 - Its success rate decreased by only 5.7% for `π_0.5` and 6.4% for `SmolVLA` from d=5 to d=20, demonstrating strong delay tolerance in scenarios with substantial latency.
 - Existing methods deteriorated significantly with increased delay, further highlighting FutureRTC's stability.
 - The results reinforced that compensating for proprioceptive state alone (as in VLASH) is insufficient when visual observations remain stale, as FutureRTC consistently outperformed VLASH.

**Ablation Studies (LIBERO, SmolVLA-450M):**
Ablation studies quantified the contribution of each proposed component:
- **Baseline (stale observation + stale state, executing `A[0:K-1]`)**: Showed a rapid decline in success rate with increasing delay (e.g., 26.1% at d=5 to 0.7% at d=20), confirming the severe impact of prediction-execution misalignment.
- **+SCM (State Correction Module)**: Improved performance at low delays but was insufficient at high delays (e.g., 45.9% at d=5 to 5.8% at d=20), indicating that stale visual observations remain a bottleneck.
- **+OPM (Observation Prediction Module)**: Provided substantial performance improvements (e.g., 74.1% at d=5 to 67.5% at d=20), underscoring the critical role of execution-time visual context.
- **+L_policy (Policy Consistency Loss)**: Further enhanced overall performance (e.g., 75.8% at d=5 to 69.4% at d=20), suggesting its effectiveness in aligning the predicted context with the policy's expectations.

**Computational Efficiency:**
FutureRTC introduced minimal computational overhead. For `π_0.5`, it added 6.45M parameters and 3.64ms inference latency. For `SmolVLA-450M`, it added 5.19M parameters and 3.04ms inference latency. This indicates that the performance improvements are achieved with limited additional computational resources.

**Trajectory Smoothness Analysis (LIBERO):**
Analysis of action velocity and acceleration profiles demonstrated that FutureRTC produced the smoothest trajectories. Baselines like RTC, VLASH, and REMAC exhibited pronounced acceleration spikes at chunk boundaries, indicative of inter-chunk discontinuities. VLASH partially mitigated these spikes but was less smooth than FutureRTC, further supporting the necessity of predicting execution-time visual observations.

**Real-World Evaluation (Dual-arm AgileX Cobot Magic, d=5 and d=10):**
Deployed on a physical robot for three bimanual manipulation tasks (Stack Plates, Fold Towel, Hang Cups), FutureRTC consistently achieved:
- Highest task success rates.
- Fewest execution steps.
- Shortest execution times.
This confirmed that the robustness observed in simulations effectively transferred to real-world operations, demonstrating smoother, more decisive motions with fewer hesitations compared to Naive Asynchronous, RTC, and VLASH.

**Detailed Analysis (Appendix):**
The appendix provided further insights into the impact of prediction-execution misalignment, revealing that discarding the first `d` actions (common in naive asynchronous methods) is only beneficial when conditioning inputs are stale. When execution-time context is available, executing the chunk from its first action (`A[0:K-1]`) is optimal, with `o_t+K` contributing more significantly to performance recovery than `s_t+K` for some backbones. Crucially, providing both ground-truth execution-time observation and state resulted in delay-invariant performance, confirming that asynchronous execution's penalty lies in stale inputs, not the paradigm itself.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FutureRTC framework addresses a fundamental challenge in real-time robotics: the practical deployment of computationally intensive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies under asynchronous execution with inherent inference delays. Its contributions carry several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Real-Time VLA Deployment**: By effectively mitigating prediction-execution misalignment, FutureRTC allows VLA policies to operate reliably in real-time environments where latency is unavoidable. This is crucial for expanding the applicability of powerful, large-scale VLA models to practical robotic applications, especially on resource-constrained edge devices or over networks to remote servers.

2. **Plug-and-Play Adaptability**: The framework's design as a plug-and-play adapter that operates without modifying the underlying VLA policy parameters is a major advantage. This reduces the significant computational cost and engineering effort associated with re-training or fine-tuning large foundation models for specific deployment scenarios. It offers a practical and scalable solution for integrating real-time capabilities into existing and future VLA architectures.

3. **Improved Robot Performance**: The demonstrated improvements in task success rates, execution speed, and trajectory smoothness are direct benefits for robotic systems. Robots employing FutureRTC can perform tasks more reliably, efficiently, and with more fluid, human-like motions, enhancing overall operational quality and reducing wear and tear on hardware.

4. **Comprehensive Context Anticipation**: The research highlights the critical importance of anticipating both proprioceptive states and, crucially, visual observations. By showing that stale visual information significantly degrades performance even with corrected states, FutureRTC provides a more holistic and robust approach to future context prediction, guiding future research in this area.

5. **Robustness to Varying Latency**: The framework's ability to maintain high performance across a wide range of inference delays, including significant ones, is vital for real-world scenarios where network conditions, computational load, or model complexity can cause variable and unpredictable latencies. This robustness contributes to more dependable robot operations.

6. **Insights into Asynchronous Control**: The detailed analysis of prediction-execution misalignment and the impact of different input modalities provides valuable insights for the robotics and machine learning communities. It quantifies the individual contributions of stale observations and states, reinforcing the understanding that accurate, current context is paramount for effective control, irrespective of the execution paradigm.

7. **Foundation for Future Work**: While FutureRTC focuses on specific VLA architectures and manipulation tasks, its core methodology of anticipatory conditioning could potentially be extended. Future research directions, such as adapting the framework to other policy architectures (e.g., autoregressive, discrete diffusion) or addressing highly dynamic environments with external agents, can build upon the principles established here.

In conclusion, FutureRTC represents a significant step towards bridging the gap between advanced VLA model capabilities and the demanding requirements of real-time robotic deployment. Its practical, effective, and efficient approach offers a pathway for making sophisticated robot intelligence more accessible and reliable in real-world applications.
