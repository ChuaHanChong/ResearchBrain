# Optimization of sim-to-real transfer in the humanoid robot NICO

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed and evaluated methods for optimizing sim-to-real transfer for tabletop object grasping on the NICO humanoid robot, utilizing only its built-in low-resolution stereo cameras. A visual feedback model for iterative hand-object alignment achieved the highest overall grasping success rate of 72.7% across the robot's workspace, surpassing calibration-based correction models which peaked at 57.6% overall.

## Problem

- The "sim-to-real gap" frequently causes robotic manipulation failures on physical platforms despite successful planning in simulation, particularly for grasping tasks.
- Achieving reliable robotic grasping often necessitates expensive external sensors, which are not suitable for low-cost humanoid robot platforms.
- Accumulated minor positioning errors from mechanical inaccuracies, actuator imperfections, and sensor noise impede stable grasps with existing methods.

## Method

- The authors extended their prior 2D reaching ==calibration== method to a comprehensive pipeline for 2D tabletop object grasping on the NICO humanoid robot.
- A vision module was developed, integrating ==YOLO== for object/hand detection with ==stereo vision== for 3D localization, relying exclusively on the robot's built-in low-resolution fisheye cameras.
- A novel ==iterative visual feedback== model, termed **M4**, was implemented to dynamically align the robot's hand with the detected object immediately before grasp execution, distinct from fixed calibration-based corrections.

## Results

- The fully nonlinear calibration model (M3) achieved a **96.7%** grasping success rate within its neural-network correction area and **57.6%** overall across the tested workspace.
- The visual feedback model (M4) demonstrated the highest overall grasping success rate of **72.7%** across the entire tabletop workspace, showing better generalization.
- When stereo vision accurately localized the hand, the visual feedback model's success rate increased to **94.1%**, indicating its high potential with improved hand localization robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low-cost calibration models can achieve high grasping accuracy within their specifically trained regions, with a fully nonlinear model reaching 96.7% success in its calibrated area.
> - An iterative visual feedback mechanism provides superior generalization across the full robot workspace, achieving the highest overall grasping success rate.
> - The robustness of stereo vision-based hand localization is a critical factor for the performance of visual feedback approaches, as errors in hand detection can impair alignment and grasping.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the pervasive challenge of the "sim-to-real gap" in robotics, particularly in the context of robotic grasping for humanoid platforms. Robotic grasping necessitates the accurate integration of visual perception, object localization, inverse kinematics (IK), and precise hand control. The discrepancy between simulated and real-world robot performance, caused by factors such as mechanical inaccuracies, actuator imperfections, sensor noise, and simplified robot models, can lead to failed manipulation tasks even when movements are successfully planned in simulation.

The work builds upon the authors' previous research, which introduced a low-cost haptic calibration method to improve 2D reaching accuracy for the Neuro-Inspired COmpanion (NICO) humanoid robot. This paper extends that foundational work from simple reaching tasks to the more complex domain of tabletop object grasping.

Within the broader landscape of robotic grasping, many contemporary methods rely on sophisticated sensors like RGB-D cameras, point clouds, or extensive datasets (e.g., GraspNet-1Billion, Dex-Net 4.0, or those utilizing foundation models). Learning-based approaches often predict grasp poses or quality directly from visual input, but can exhibit sensitivity to variations in perception setup, object appearance, background, illumination, and camera noise. In contrast, this study differentiates itself by focusing on a low-cost approach, exclusively utilizing the robot's built-in, low-resolution fisheye stereo cameras, thereby avoiding the need for dedicated depth sensors, motion capture systems, or complex external tracking. The methodology employs explicit localization, IK, and calibration-based correction, rather than end-to-end learning of grasping policies.

Object detection, often a precursor to grasping, commonly employs frameworks like YOLO, which have been integrated with grasping networks for collaborative robots or for target localization and grasping in other humanoid robots like NAO. This research similarly integrates YOLO for object and hand detection within its vision module.

Furthermore, the paper situates itself within the field of sim-to-real transfer, an active area of research where techniques such as randomized-to-canonical adaptation, domain randomization, and domain adaptation have been explored to bridge the reality gap. Unlike these approaches that often involve training end-to-end grasping policies, this work focuses on a calibration-based correction mechanism. The inclusion of a visual feedback model for hand alignment is inspired by closed-loop visual feedback strategies that aim to enhance grasp robustness by compensating for residual localization and execution errors, yet it proposes a simpler, calibrated control pipeline rather than direct grasp pose prediction from depth images.

Thus, this research contributes to the development of robust, low-cost robotic grasping solutions for humanoid robots, specifically by advancing calibration-based sim-to-real transfer and incorporating visual feedback using limited onboard sensing capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties in achieving reliable robotic grasping on physical platforms despite successful planning in simulation. This "sim-to-real gap" is a critical bottleneck in deploying autonomous manipulation capabilities. Even minor positioning errors, accumulating from mechanical inaccuracies, actuator imperfections, sensor noise, or simplified robot models, can prevent a successful grasp. While the authors' previous work demonstrated improvements in 2D reaching accuracy through a low-cost haptic calibration method, grasping presents a significantly more complex task, requiring not only accurate reaching but also robust object detection, precise localization, appropriate hand positioning, and reliable grasp execution.

A core motivation of this work is to continue pursuing a "low-cost direction." This involves developing solutions that do not necessitate expensive or complex external hardware such as RGB-D cameras, motion capture systems, or advanced tracking setups. Instead, the focus is on maximizing the utility of the robot's existing, built-in sensor capabilities, specifically its low-resolution fisheye stereo cameras. This approach aims to enhance the accessibility and practicality of robotic manipulation, particularly for humanoid robots often constrained by cost and integrated sensor suites.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this research were:

1. **Extension of Calibration-Based Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To adapt and extend the previously developed calibration method, which improved 2D reaching accuracy, to the more complex task of 2D tabletop object grasping.
2. **Development of a Comprehensive Grasping Pipeline:** To design and implement a complete system for tabletop object grasping that integrates multiple components: calibrated correction models, object and hand detection based on the YOLO framework, stereo vision-based object and hand localization, inverse kinematics (IK) control, and a defined grasping procedure.
3. **Implementation of a Visual Feedback Model:** To develop a novel visual feedback mechanism that leverages the robot's built-in stereo cameras and the developed perception and localization components to dynamically align the robot's hand with the detected object immediately prior to grasp execution. This model was intended to operate without relying on calibration-based target correction.
4. **Evaluation of Grasping Performance:** To experimentally evaluate and compare the effectiveness of different calibration models (linear, partially nonlinear, fully nonlinear) and the proposed visual feedback model in terms of grasping success rates. This evaluation aimed to assess performance both within a specific calibrated region and across the robot's full tabletop workspace, providing insights into the generalization capabilities of each approach.

Ultimately, the research sought to develop and validate a robust, low-cost, and integrated grasping solution for humanoid robots that effectively mitigates the sim-to-real gap using primarily onboard sensing and calibration-based control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research builds upon the authors' previous work, adapting and extending it for the task of tabletop object grasping.

**Robotic Setup and Software:**
Experiments were conducted using the NICO humanoid robot, specifically its right arm and four-finger child-sized hand. A paper grid was utilized to provide a pseudo-ground truth for consistent object placement and performance evaluation across the robot's reachable area. Visual perception was facilitated by the robot's built-in stereo camera setup, which captured low-resolution fisheye images at 640x480 pixels. The software framework was based on NicoIK, which interfaces the simulated robot model with the physical NICO robot and provides target positions for movements. The PyBullet physics engine, with a modified NICO Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) file and its inverse kinematics solver, handled simulation, while the real robot was controlled via the NicoMotion library.

**Calibration Models:**
To address the sim-to-real discrepancy, three pre-trained calibration models, originally developed for 2D reaching, were incorporated into the grasping pipeline. These models transform the estimated real-world object position into a corrected simulated target position for the IK module:
- **M1 (Baseline Model):** A piecewise linear mapping based on manual simulation alignment and interpolation of measured height values. This model did not apply horizontal correction.
- **M2 (Partially Nonlinear Model):** Utilized a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to predict horizontal (x, y) target coordinates, while the height (z) component was still obtained through interpolation.
- **M3 (Fully Nonlinear Model):** Employed a neural network to directly predict the complete corrected three-dimensional (x, y, z) target position.
These models were used as fixed correction modules, without retraining, to evaluate their effectiveness in object grasping.

**Vision Module:**
The vision module comprised two main components:
- **Object and Hand Detection:** The YOLO framework was selected for its balance of speed and accuracy. A custom dataset of 442 images was created and manually annotated into eight classes, including six objects and distinct classes for the left and right hands. YOLO12s was chosen after evaluating several YOLO variants, including YOLO12n/m and YOLO11n/s/m, based on mean average precision (mAP50–95) and inference time. The experiments primarily used a plush tomato due to its optimal size, high-contrast color, and regular shape. Bounding boxes for hands were intentionally focused on the central palm area to ensure the centroid accurately represented the hand's position for alignment.
- **Stereo Vision-based Localization:** Both cameras were calibrated using a chessboard pattern to obtain intrinsic parameters and lens distortion coefficients. Images were then undistorted and rectified, enabling the computation of a disparity map to estimate the depth of detected objects. The 3D position of the detected object's center was reconstructed in the camera coordinate frame and subsequently transformed into the robot's coordinate frame via the kinematic chain of the robot head. A localization drift, attributed to camera overheating, necessitated an additional correction: predicted positions were shifted towards a fixed pivot point in the table plane. With a fixed head position during inference, the stereo vision module achieved a mean 2D localization error of 0.76 cm.

**Hand Alignment using Visual Feedback (Model M4):**
An additional visual feedback approach was implemented to align the robot hand with the detected object before grasping, distinct from the calibration-based models. This method operates as follows:
1. The robot estimates the object's position using the vision module.
2. The robot moves its hand to an approximate position above the object.
3. The vision module is re-engaged to detect the robot hand and reconstruct its position using stereo vision.
4. An iterative alignment procedure is initiated. In each iteration, the next IK target position (`p_k_IK`) is adjusted by shifting it from the detected hand position (`p_hand`) towards the estimated object position (`p_obj`): `p_k_IK = p_{k-1}_IK + a_k * (p_obj - p_hand)`, where the step size `a_k = 1/(2^(k-1))` gradually decreases to prevent oscillations.
5. This alignment continues until the horizontal distance between the detected hand and object positions falls below a predefined threshold (1 cm).
6. To improve hand detection reliability, the palm was rotated towards the camera during alignment, making the central part of the palm more consistently visible.
After alignment, the hand is lowered, and fingers are closed to execute the grasp. This method, M4, relies solely on visual feedback for alignment and does not incorporate the calibration-based target correction used in M1, M2, and M3.

**Grasping Pipeline and Experimental Evaluation:**
For successful grasp execution, two task-specific corrections were applied: vertical target correction (z-value) and palm yaw correction. These values were manually measured at reference points across the workspace and then estimated for arbitrary target positions using radial basis function (RBF) interpolation. Roll and pitch angles of the palm were kept fixed for a top-down grasping pose.

The complete grasping pipeline involved: image capture -> YOLO detection (image coordinates) -> stereo vision (depth, robot coordinates) -> transformation to robot's coordinate frame -> application of a correction model (M1, M2, M3) or the hand alignment model (M4) -> yaw correction -> IK solver (computes joint angles) -> NicoMotion library (executes movement on the robot).

Four grasping approaches (M1, M2, M3, and M4) were evaluated. A plush tomato was placed on 22 grid positions distributed across the robot's reachable workspace. For each position and model, three grasping attempts were performed, totaling 66 attempts per model. Performance metrics included the overall grasping success rate and the success rate specifically within the original neural-network correction area (10 target positions), allowing for an assessment of both calibrated region performance and generalization across the full workspace.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation assessed the grasping performance of the three calibration-based models (M1, M2, M3) and the visual feedback model (M4) across a grid of 22 target positions on the NICO robot's tabletop workspace.

**Calibration-Based Grasping Performance:**

- **Baseline Model (M1):** This purely linear model, which lacked additional horizontal (x, y) correction, exhibited the lowest performance. Its overall grasping success rate across the entire tested workspace was 16.7% (11 successful grasps out of 66 attempts). Within the neural-network (NN) correction area, its success rate was 10.0% (3 out of 30 attempts). Although the robot hand frequently approached the object, the residual positional errors were generally substantial enough to prevent a stable grasp.

- **Partially Nonlinear Model (M2):** This model, which used an MLP for horizontal coordinates but interpolated height, achieved an overall success rate of 48.5% (32 out of 66 attempts). Inside the NN correction area, its success rate was 93.3% (28 out of 30 attempts).

- **Fully Nonlinear Model (M3):** This model, which employed a neural network to predict the complete corrected 3D target position, including the z-coordinate, yielded the best performance among the calibration-based approaches. Its most significant improvement was observed within the NN correction area, where it achieved a 96.7% success rate (29 out of 30 attempts). This region corresponded to where the training data for the neural network was available. Across the entire tested workspace, M3 achieved an overall success rate of 57.6% (38 out of 66 attempts). Analysis of its predictions indicated that the mean displacement between input and predicted simulated positions was 2.14 cm inside the correction area but increased to 5.45 cm outside it. This suggests that M3 did not reliably extrapolate beyond its training data region, often overshooting the required correction in uncalibrated areas.

**Visual Feedback Model (M4) and Overall Comparison:**

- **Visual Feedback Model (M4):** This approach, which relied on iterative visual feedback to align the robot's detected hand with the detected object, achieved the highest overall grasping success rate across the full tabletop workspace. It attained a 72.7% success rate (48 successful grasps out of 66 attempts). Unlike the calibration-based models, successful grasps were more broadly distributed and not concentrated solely within the neural-network correction area, indicating better generalization across the workspace. However, the performance of M4 was constrained by the reliability of stereo vision for hand localization. Stereo vision correctly localized the hand in 77.3% (17 out of 22) of target positions. In the remaining positions, particularly when the background behind the hand was distant, stereo vision substantially overestimated the hand's position, impairing alignment. When considering only the attempts where stereo vision accurately localized the hand, the visual feedback model achieved a 94.1% success rate (48 out of 51 attempts), suggesting its potential for even higher performance with improved hand localization robustness.

**Summary of Grasping Success Rates:**

| Statistic | Baseline M1 | M2 | M3 | Visual feedback (M4) |
| :----------------------- | :---------- | :--------- | :--------- | :------------------- |
| Hand SV OK | – | – | – | 77.3% (17/22) |
| Grasp Rate (SV OK) | – | – | – | 94.1% (48/51) |
| Grasp Rate (NN Area) | 10.0% (3/30) | 93.3% (28/30) | 96.7% (29/30) | 83.3% (25/30) |
| Grasp Rate (All) | 16.7% (11/66) | 48.5% (32/66) | 57.6% (38/66) | 72.7% (48/66) |

In conclusion, the fully nonlinear calibration model (M3) demonstrated superior performance within its calibrated region. The visual feedback model (M4), despite challenges with stereo vision-based hand localization, achieved the highest overall grasping success rate across the entire tabletop workspace, suggesting its effectiveness for generalized tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by advancing sim-to-real transfer capabilities for humanoid robots, particularly in the domain of object grasping. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer for Manipulation:** The study successfully demonstrates that a low-cost haptic calibration method, previously validated for 2D reaching, can be effectively extended to improve the accuracy and success rate of 2D object grasping in a humanoid robot. This validates a generalizable approach for bridging the sim-to-real gap in more complex manipulation tasks, moving beyond simple point-to-point movements.

- **Promotion of Low-Cost Robotic Solutions:** A key impact of this work is its adherence to a low-cost paradigm. By exclusively utilizing the NICO robot's built-in, low-resolution fisheye stereo cameras and avoiding reliance on expensive RGB-D cameras, motion capture systems, or external tracking solutions, the methodology enhances the accessibility and affordability of sophisticated robotic manipulation capabilities. This can broaden the potential for deployment in research, education, and applications where cost is a significant constraint, especially for humanoid platforms.

- **Improved Robustness and Generalization through Hybrid Approaches:** The findings highlight the complementary strengths of calibration-based correction and visual feedback. The fully nonlinear calibration model (M3) demonstrated very high accuracy (96.7%) within its specifically calibrated region, showcasing the effectiveness of precise, localized corrections. Concurrently, the visual feedback model (M4) achieved the highest overall success rate (72.7%) across the full workspace, indicating its superior generalization capabilities even with current limitations in hand localization. This suggests that hybrid approaches, combining calibration for accuracy in known areas with visual feedback for robustness and generalization, could yield further improvements.

- **Advancement of Humanoid Robot Capabilities:** By providing a more reliable grasping pipeline, this research directly contributes to enhancing the autonomous manipulation capabilities of humanoid robots like NICO. Improved grasping is fundamental for a wide range of human-robot interaction scenarios, service robotics, and general object manipulation tasks. The ability to grasp objects robustly, even with intrinsic hardware limitations, moves these platforms closer to practical real-world deployment.

- **Identification of Future Research Directions:** The work also identifies specific areas for further development. The primary limitation of the visual feedback model—the robustness of stereo vision-based hand localization, particularly in challenging background conditions—provides a clear direction for future research. Improving hand segmentation or depth estimation techniques could significantly bolster the performance of visual feedback mechanisms and further increase overall grasping success.

In summary, this research offers a practical and low-cost approach to improving robotic grasping performance, demonstrating that careful calibration combined with intelligent visual feedback can effectively overcome the sim-to-real gap, thereby advancing the capabilities and applicability of humanoid robots in diverse environments.
