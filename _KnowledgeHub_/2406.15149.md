# Gaussian Splatting to Real World Flight Navigation Transfer with Liquid Networks

> [!summary] Summary
> The method builds a quadrotor simulator by fusing a 3D Gaussian Splatting radiance field of a real flight room with PyBullet flight dynamics, then behavior-clones Closed-form Continuous-time Liquid networks on expert approach-and-turn trajectories with irregular time-step augmentation. Trained only on one indoor simulated scene, the 34-neuron policy transfers zero-shot to a real drone, hiking five outdoor checkpoints.

## Problem

- Sim-to-real visual navigation usually leans on compute-heavy domain randomization or real-world fine-tuning, since photorealism alone fails to bridge sensor, lighting, and actuator mismatches.
- Behavior cloning suffers compounding error off-distribution, and recovery demonstrations that would fix it are impractical to collect on real flying hardware.
- Discrete-time recurrent policies like LSTMs assume a fixed inference rate, so they degrade when deployed hardware runs at frequencies unlike the training data.

## Method

- ==Dynamics-augmented Gaussian Splatting simulator==: a 430-image splat of the flight room plus splatted sphere targets, gravity-aligned to a 240 Hz ==PyBullet== quadrotor solver that drives the render camera.
- ==Implicit closed-loop augmentation==: expert PID trajectories start with the target at the image border and randomized range, baking recovery and edge cases into approach-then-turn demonstrations without DAgger-style relabeling.
- ==Irregularly time-sampled training== of ==Closed-form Continuous-time Liquid networks==: a CNN backbone feeds 34 liquid neurons that also receive elapsed time delta, with logging frequency randomized between 1 and 10 Hz.

## Results

- In the Gaussian-Splatting sim, the time-augmented Liquid policy hit **96%** first-target success and **1.72** of two checkpoints, versus **72%** and **1.04** for a **25x** larger LSTM.
- Transferring to the real DJI M300 indoors, Liquid dropped only **96%** to **75%** success over **40** trials while the LSTM collapsed from **72%** to **12.5%**.
- Outdoors on an unseen sunlit lawn, Liquid still scored **50%** over **40** runs and chained **five** consecutive checkpoints, whereas LSTM succeeded **zero** times; fixed-**9 Hz** training averaged **2.3** simulated hike steps versus **56.1**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuous-time recurrent models trained on jittered timestamps buy frequency robustness, decoupling policy behavior from the deployment platform's inference rate.
> - Photorealism plus careful expert-trajectory design can substitute for scene randomization, but only when the architecture itself extracts task-causal features rather than scene appearance.
> - Caution: splat-based simulators only capture static scenes, and per-color success asymmetries reveal that small visual biases in curated data survive transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This work addresses the challenge of transferring autonomous robot learning from simulation to the real world, a persistent difficulty in robotics often referred to as the "sim-to-real gap." Current approaches to mitigate this gap typically involve computationally intensive domain randomization methods or further model fine-tuning. The paper integrates recent advancements in several key areas to propose a novel solution for visual quadrotor navigation.

Firstly, it leverages **neural scene reconstruction** techniques, specifically **3D Gaussian Splatting (3D GS)**, to create photorealistic and detailed 3D models of real environments within a simulator. This builds upon prior work in neural radiance fields (NeRF) and GS for rendering and scene reconstruction, which have been explored for virtual reality, augmented reality, and autonomous driving. While existing research has connected Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) with radiance field models primarily for optimizing data collection for these models or for SLAM applications, this work utilizes GS *within* a flight simulator to generate high-fidelity training data for quadrotor control policies. It acknowledges the PEGASUS system as a close predecessor in combining physics engines with GS rendering for manipulation data generation.

Secondly, the paper employs **Imitation Learning (IL)**, specifically behavior cloning, a common approach in robotics where agents learn from expert demonstrations. This method is often preferred for its sample efficiency compared to Reinforcement Learning (RL) and its ability to capture implicit navigation knowledge. However, IL typically faces challenges related to policy stationarity, compounding errors, and generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, which the paper aims to address.

Thirdly, the research incorporates **Liquid Neural Networks (LNNs)**, particularly Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC) networks, a family of continuous-time neural networks known for their stable behavior, expressivity, and demonstrated promise in end-to-end imitation and reinforcement learning tasks, especially for mapping high-dimensional visual inputs to robust control decisions and exhibiting out-of-distribution generalization capabilities in prior flight tasks. This work further explores enhancing their robustness through irregularly time-sampled data training.

By combining these elements—photorealistic simulation via Gaussian Splatting, a refined imitation learning protocol, and robust Liquid Neural Networks—the study seeks to advance the state-of-the-art in zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for autonomous quadrotor navigation, aiming to reduce the reliance on extensive domain randomization typically required to bridge the reality gap.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a methodology that improves the generalization and robustness of autonomous quadrotor navigation policies, enabling successful transfer from a single simulated scene directly to diverse real-world environments without requiring scene randomization or extensive fine-tuning.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in autonomous robot learning:
- **The Sim-to-Real Gap:** While simulators offer scalable data generation and flexible trajectory optimization, transferring behaviors learned in simulation to the real world remains difficult. This discrepancy is often attributed to differences in visual fidelity, physical properties, sensor noise, and actuator dynamics between simulated and real environments.
- **Limitations of Existing Sim-to-Real Solutions:** Current approaches, such as domain randomization, are often computationally expensive or require significant effort to define relevant randomization parameters. Moreover, they do not fundamentally address the problem of learning robust representations that generalize across significant distribution shifts.
- **Need for Photorealistic and Dynamically Accurate Simulators:** Traditional simulators may lack the photorealism and dynamic accuracy required to generate training data that adequately prepares an agent for real-world visual complexities and physical interactions. The advent of neural scene reconstruction techniques like Gaussian Splatting offers a potential solution for creating such high-fidelity environments.
- **Enhancing Policy Robustness and Generalization:** Standard neural network architectures often struggle with out-of-distribution generalization, which is crucial for real-world deployment where conditions may vary significantly from training data. Liquid Neural Networks have shown potential in this regard, motivating their application in this context.

To achieve its objective and address these motivations, the research is structured around three main schemes and five key pillars:

**Three Main Schemes:**
1. **High-fidelity Simulator Development:** Integrating a 3D Gaussian Splatting radiance field with a quadrotor flight dynamics engine to create a realistic simulator for generating high-quality visual training datasets.
2. **Imitation Learning Pipeline with LNNs:** Designing an imitation learning protocol to train Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) networks (specifically CfC models) on the generated data, leveraging their known generalization capabilities.
3. **Real-World Deployment and Validation:** Deploying the trained networks on a physical drone to demonstrate robust generalization to real-world environments, including out-of-distribution scenarios.

**Five Key Pillars (Contributions):**
1. **Dynamics Augmented Gaussian Splatting Simulator:** Building a simulator that combines photorealistic rendering from Gaussian Splatting with high-fidelity flight dynamics, providing a rich training environment.
2. **Implicit Closed-Loop Augmentation:** Crafting expert demonstration trajectories that inherently include edge cases and recovery scenarios, thereby reducing the need for explicit, additional closed-loop augmentation or model fine-tuning.
3. **Liquid NNs Augmentation with Irregularly Time-Sampled Data:** Enhancing the adaptability and robustness of Liquid Neural Networks by training them with irregularly time-sampled data, making them more resilient to diverse temporal dynamics and varying inference frequencies.
4. **Simulation and Real-World Validation:** Conducting extensive quantitative flight tests in both simulated and real-world environments to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method.
5. **Zero-Shot Real-World Transfer:** Demonstrating the pipeline's capability for zero-shot transfer, enabling a quadrotor to perform complex navigation tasks in unseen outdoor environments after training solely in a single indoor simulated scene.

These objectives collectively aim to establish a complete and effective pipeline for learning robust autonomous quadrotor navigation policies that bridge the sim-to-real gap more effectively than prior methods.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The research proposes an end-to-end visual navigation model, denoted as $\pi$, which takes sequences of RGB images from a drone's onboard camera as input and outputs control commands. The training process uses behavior cloning, where the network learns to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between its predicted output ($\mu_t$) and ground truth labels ($y_t$), which consist of triaxial velocities and yaw rate in the quadrotor body frame.

#### 3.1. High-fidelity Vision and Dynamics Simulation

To create a realistic training environment, the authors developed a simulator combining Gaussian Splatting with quadrotor flight dynamics.

- **Gaussian Splatting (GS) Model of the Indoor Flight Room:** An indoor flight room was selected as the environment. Images (430 total, 1024x768 pixels) were captured using an iPhone 12 Pro camera via the Polycam app. These images were then processed with COLMAP to generate depth maps. A 3D GS model was trained on these COLMAP outputs at half resolution (512x384), using a densification interval of 500 and 30,000 iterations. This process resulted in photorealistic renderings despite some visual artifacts.
- **Adding Models of Task-Related Objects:** Two task-specific objects, red and blue spheres, were integrated into the environment. These were defined using the geometry object file format (obj) and rendered from various angles in Blender. Separate GS models were trained for each object. Their size and intensity were manually tuned to blend into the indoor environment.
- **Dynamics for GS Trajectories:** The visual GS world was aligned with a dynamics solver to generate trajectories reflecting quadrotor physics. The PyBullet physics engine was used, adapted from an existing codebase for Bitcraze's Crazyflie 2.x nano-quadrotor dynamics. Scaling was manually tuned to ensure consistent object positioning between PyBullet and the GS environment. The physics simulation ran at 240 Hz. During data collection or inference, the GS camera's position and attitude were updated based on the relative displacement calculated by PyBullet, rendering new views. This allowed for the generation of trajectories visualized in both environments.

#### 3.2. Task Description

The defined task is visual navigation through multiple checkpoints. The quadrotor must approach a target sphere and then turn based on its color: left for red and right for blue. This simulates following trail blazes. Each expert demonstration trajectory consists of two phases:

- **Approach:** To address the challenge of naive imitation learning failing on out-of-distribution trajectories, a strategy of "implicit closed-loop augmentation" was employed. Instead of explicitly adding recovery trajectories, the quadrotor's initial positions for each trajectory were randomized. Targets were positioned to partially appear at the image border, and initial target distances were varied. This exposed the network to diverse approach angles and distances. PID controllers guided the quadrotor's velocities and yaw rate to center the target in the frame, effectively incorporating recovery information by design.
- **Turn:** Once the drone hovered at a fixed distance from the target, a constant yaw rate command (approximately 12° per second, nearly double the approach phase rate) was issued, corresponding to the target's color. This distinct rate was intended to clearly delineate the turning behavior from the approach maneuver.

#### 3.3. Irregularly Sampled Data Augmentation

The research utilized Liquid Neural Networks, specifically Closed-form Continuous-time (CfC) models, which can handle irregularly sampled data. Unlike discrete-time recurrent models that assume constant time steps, CfC networks explicitly incorporate time ($t$) into their formulation:
$x(t) = \sigma(-f(x, I; \theta_f)t) \odot g(x, I; \theta_g) + [1 - \sigma(-[f(x, I; \theta_f)]t)] \odot h(x, I; \theta_h)$
Here, $x(t)$ is the hidden state, $I(t)$ is the external input, and $t$ is the time sampled by input time-stamps. This explicit time dependence eliminates the need for complex numerical solvers and allows for flexible data handling.

The authors implemented an irregularly sampled data augmentation scheme where the time steps ($\delta t$) between training samples were randomized. This was achieved by sampling $\delta t$ from a custom probability mass function (a bimodal distribution of Gaussians) that covered frequencies between 1 and 10 Hz. This augmentation was intended to enhance the networks' generalization across different frequency domains, improving adaptability to varying inference rates in deployment.

#### 3.4. Hardware Platform and Models Deployed

- **Hardware:** Real-world evaluations were conducted on a DJI M300 RTK quadcopter, interfacing with a DJI Manifold 2 companion computer (equipped with an NVIDIA Jetson TX2 GPU) for onboard inference. A Zenmuse Z30 camera and gimbal, providing stabilized forward-facing input images, were mounted. The system achieved a runtime frequency of approximately 2.6 Hz.
- **Models:** All models shared a common architecture: a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) backbone for feature extraction from 256x144 pixel input images, followed by a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) head for sequential decision-making. The models were trained with MSE loss.
 - **Liquid Variants:** Three CfC models were trained:
 - **Liquid:** Trained with time-scale augmentation, where data points were recorded at random time differences corresponding to frequencies between 1 and 10 Hz. Inputs included images, labels, and the time delta since the last input.
 - **Liquid 3Hz:** Trained on a dataset with a constant sampling rate of 3 Hz.
 - **Liquid 9Hz:** Trained on a dataset with a constant sampling rate of 9 Hz.
 All Liquid models used a skeleton of 34 liquid neurons and had approximately 12.3k trainable parameters in their recurrent part (excluding the shared 103.7k parameter CNN backbone).
 - **LSTM Baseline:** A Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was used as a baseline, chosen for its performance in similar visual flight navigation tasks among non-liquid RNNs. It was trained on the 3 Hz dataset to match the typical operating frequency, as LSTMs cannot handle irregularly sampled data without modification. The LSTM architecture comprised over 300k parameters in its recurrent part, making it approximately 25 times larger than the Liquid counterparts.

The training procedure involved using Tree-structured Parzen estimators (TPE) with the Optuna library for hyperparameter optimization, including learning rate, decay rate, and model-specific parameters. Each architecture was trained five times with optimized hyperparameters, and the best-performing checkpoint on validation loss was selected for testing.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research evaluated the performance of the trained navigation policies through a series of quantitative flight tests in various simulated and real-world scenarios. Performance was assessed based on maneuver success rates, with specific criteria for valid checkpoints (hovering at target distance, correct turning direction and rate).

#### 4.1. Sim-to-Sim Evaluation (PyBullet Simulator)

Models were first evaluated in the non-photorealistic PyBullet simulator on a 100-step hiking task with randomly initialized targets, operating at an inference frequency of 3 Hz.
- **Liquid and Liquid 3Hz** models demonstrated consistent performance, achieving mean hike lengths of 56.1 and 83.4 checkpoints, respectively. They successfully performed both single-target tasks and chronological composition.
- **Liquid 9Hz** averaged only 2.3 checkpoints, indicating challenges in closed-loop inference when the testing frequency differed significantly from its training frequency (9Hz). It particularly struggled with blue targets.
- The **LSTM** model performed poorly in generalization, with an average hike length of 1.4, generating erratic commands beyond the single-target task.

#### 4.2. GS-to-GS Evaluation (Photorealistic GS Simulator)

Models trained on GS datasets were evaluated in the photorealistic GS-rendered simulator at 3 Hz on a 2-target hike, limited by scene constraints.
- **Liquid** agents demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving an average of 1.72 steps out of 2. It showed balanced success rates for both red (94%) and blue (98%) target maneuvers. This model also exhibited efficient and smooth maneuvering.
- **Liquid 3Hz** achieved an average of 1.35 steps. It showed a bias, performing well on blue targets (96%) but struggling with red targets (64%).
- **Liquid 9Hz** performed the worst among Liquid models, with an average of 1.00 steps, indicating that training frequency significantly impacted performance at different inference rates.
- **LSTM** achieved an average of 1.04 steps. It also exhibited a significant bias, performing well on red targets (98%) but poorly on blue targets (43%).

The results suggest that time-scale augmentation (Liquid model) contributes to balanced and higher performance compared to fixed-frequency training for Liquid networks, and Liquid networks generally outperform LSTM in this complex simulated environment. An additional test (Half duration GS-to-GS) showed that the Liquid agent maintained its performance even with half the time budget, indicating higher efficiency.

#### 4.3. GS-to-Real Evaluation (Indoor Flight Room)

The sim-to-real transfer was evaluated on a DJI M300 quadrotor platform in the actual indoor flight room used for GS model training, using cardboard disks as targets.
- The **LSTM** agent experienced a substantial performance drop, from 72% success in GS-to-GS to 12.5% in the real world, epitomizing the difficulty of direct sim-to-real transfer for this architecture.
- **Liquid neural networks** demonstrated greater robustness to the sim-to-real gap.
 - The **Liquid** agent (time-scale augmented) showed the highest success rate at 75% (80% for red, 70% for blue), maintaining balanced performance across colors despite a drop from its GS-to-GS performance (96%).
 - **Liquid 3Hz** and **Liquid 9Hz** both achieved success rates of 55%. Liquid 3Hz retained its bias (25% red, 85% blue), while Liquid 9Hz showed less bias (50% red, 60% blue).

These findings indicate that Liquid networks, particularly the time-scale augmented variant, exhibit superior robustness in transferring learned policies directly from simulation to the real world compared to LSTM.

#### 4.4. GS-to-Real + Generalization (Outdoor Campus Lawn)

The best-performing Liquid model and the LSTM model were further evaluated on an outdoor campus lawn, representing a completely unseen environment with challenging conditions (sunlight, tree shades, building reflections).
- The **LSTM** agent was unable to complete the task even once in 40 attempts, demonstrating a complete lack of generalization to out-of-distribution real-world scenarios.
- The **Liquid** agent demonstrated zero-shot task completion capability, achieving a 50% success rate (40% for red targets, 60% for blue targets) in this highly challenging environment.
This was further validated by a successful demonstration of the Liquid agent navigating through a series of 5 consecutive checkpoints in an alternative outdoor campus location.

#### 4.5. Other Findings (Appendices)

- **Sim-to-GS** evaluation showed that models trained only on non-rendered simulation data struggled significantly to generalize to the photorealistic GS simulation, reinforcing the importance of photorealism for transfer.
- **Importance of Recovery Trajectories:** Experiments comparing different initial position sampling strategies confirmed that maximizing recovery trajectories by presenting targets at image borders significantly improved robustness, leading to an 18% average improvement in success rates for challenging "target-in-corner" tests.
- **Effect of Irregularly Sampled Data Augmentation on LSTM:** Modifying LSTM to include time difference ($\delta t$) as an input improved its performance slightly compared to the baseline LSTM, but it still performed significantly worse than Liquid networks, suggesting that architectural properties, not just input augmentation, are key to Liquid networks' performance.
- **Performance Repeatability:** Liquid models trained with irregular time-sampling (Liquid 1-10Hz) showed higher repeatability across multiple training instances compared to fixed-frequency Liquid models or LSTM.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research presents a framework for achieving zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for autonomous quadrotor navigation, which represents an advancement in robot learning. The findings demonstrate that policies trained entirely in a single photorealistic simulated environment can be deployed directly onto real hardware and generalize to out-of-distribution real-world scenarios, including complex outdoor multi-step tasks.

The key aspects of its significance and potential impact include:

- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap with Gaussian Splatting:** The integration of 3D Gaussian Splatting with quadrotor flight dynamics offers a method for generating high-fidelity, photorealistic visual training data at scale. This capability reduces the reliance on costly and time-consuming real-world data collection and extensive domain randomization, which typically aim to bridge the visual discrepancy between simulation and reality.
- **Validation of Liquid Neural Networks for Robust Control:** The study reinforces the capabilities of Liquid Neural Networks, particularly CfC models, for learning robust visual-to-control policies. Their demonstrated out-of-distribution generalization, coupled with irregularly time-sampled data augmentation, highlights their suitability for real-world autonomous systems where sensor data rates and environmental conditions can be variable.
- **Zero-Shot Transfer Without Scene Randomization:** A notable contribution is the achievement of zero-shot transfer without resorting to extensive scene randomization. This suggests that the combination of photorealistic data, implicitly augmented expert trajectories, and the inherent robustness of Liquid Networks enables learning fundamental task representations that are resilient to significant domain shifts. This could simplify the development pipeline for autonomous systems, reducing the engineering effort traditionally required for sim-to-real deployment.
- **Implications for Autonomous System Development:** The proposed end-to-end imitation learning protocol provides a proof-of-concept for training autonomous agents with potentially lower computational resources and less real-world data collection. This could accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous systems in various applications, from navigation and exploration to inspection and delivery, particularly for UAVs.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, which also highlight areas for future impact:
- **Dynamic Scenes:** Gaussian Splatting, like other neural rendering techniques, currently performs best in static scenes. Its effectiveness in generating dynamic data for scenarios involving moving objects or changing conditions is limited, which restricts its applicability to highly dynamic real-world environments like urban air mobility.
- **Trajectory Augmentation Complexity:** As tasks become more complex, designing efficient and comprehensive trajectory augmentation schemes to cover all possible edge cases and recovery situations becomes increasingly challenging, a general limitation of imitation learning.
- **Distribution Shifts:** While the approach achieved zero-shot transfer without *scene* randomization, large distribution shifts between training and real-world environments can still hinder performance. The paper notes that domain randomization might still be essential for robustness across completely novel real-world environments not implicitly covered during training.

Addressing these limitations would further enhance the robustness and applicability of visual end-to-end autonomous navigation systems, expanding their utility to a wider array of complex and dynamic real-world scenarios. The research opens avenues for further exploration into how photorealistic simulation and novel neural network architectures can contribute to more generalizable and robust robotic autonomy.
