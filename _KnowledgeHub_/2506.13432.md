# Adaptive Model-Base Control of Quadrupeds via Online System Identification using Kalman Filter

> [!summary] Summary
> A Kalman filter-based approach is introduced for online identification of a quadruped robot's total mass and center of mass, integrated into an adaptive model-based control architecture. This method enables the robot to maintain consistent base height and orientation tracking performance with varying or unknown payloads, exhibiting improved robustness over nominal and RLS-based controllers.

## Problem

- Model-based control for legged robots relies on fixed plant models, limiting their adaptability to variable or unknown payloads in dynamic environments.
- Existing online system identification methods, such as Recursive Least Squares (RLS), are susceptible to measurement noise and biases, often requiring extensive, ad-hoc heuristics for stable estimation.
- Many adaptive control approaches lack a direct, quantitative measure of the reliability of their parameter estimates, hindering informed decisions about when to apply model updates.

## Method

- A Kalman filter (KF) formulation is developed for online identification of the total mass and the x and y components of the center of mass (COM) of a quadruped's floating base.
- The KF is integrated into an adaptive model-based control architecture comprising a heuristic-based gait sequencer, ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC), and Whole-Body Control (WBC).
- Parameter updates from the KF to the controller are gated by thresholds applied to the estimation covariance matrix, ensuring that only sufficiently reliable estimates are used to adapt the robot's dynamic model.

## Results

- The KF-based adaptive controller significantly reduced base height tracking error by up to **61%** (e.g., from **4.92** cm to **1.89** cm) and its standard deviation by up to **57%** for the heaviest payload (**5.1** kg) compared to the nominal controller.
- During dynamic payload switching experiments, the adaptive controller with KF maintained consistently lower height and orientation errors throughout the 0 kg to 5 kg load changes, effectively adapting to varying payloads.
- The RLS-based adaptive controller did not consistently improve performance and sometimes degraded it, highlighting the KF's superior robustness to noisy and biased contact forces through its reliability assessment capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Kalman filter's inherent estimation covariance matrix provides a direct, quantitative measure of parameter reliability, allowing the controller to make informed decisions on when to apply model updates, reducing reliance on heuristics.
> - Dynamic locomotion, such as stepping or walking, significantly enhances the observability of mass and COM parameters, leading to a substantial decrease in estimation covariance and improved reliability compared to standing still.
> - A simplified robot model, assuming mass concentrated in the floating base and neglecting angular acceleration terms, is sufficient for effective online mass and COM identification during typical quadrupedal gaits without compromising overall performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Model-based control has significantly advanced the capabilities of legged robots, making them suitable for complex tasks. However, a pervasive challenge in deploying these robots in real-world scenarios, such as carrying diverse payloads in industrial or hostile environments, is the reliance of most model-based control architectures on fixed plant models. These models necessitate manual updates for known payloads and struggle with variable or unknown ones, limiting the robots' adaptability and autonomy.

The existing research landscape for addressing this limitation can be broadly categorized into five approaches:
1. **Model updates based on least-squares fitting:** This category involves fitting plant models to real-system measurements, often utilizing recursive least squares (RLS) techniques. While standard in system identification, these methods are noted for their susceptibility to noise and biases in system state and ground reaction forces (GRFs), often requiring the incorporation of heuristics for stability and convergence. Previous work on quadrupeds in this category has focused on online mass and center of mass (COM) estimation using GRFs.
2. **Model updates based on tracking error evaluation:** Approaches in this category estimate model parameters by minimizing the tracking error. Some methods in this area offer stability guarantees through Lyapunov analysis but may incur high computational costs due to added constraints. They do not require contact force information, but their performance is intrinsically linked to the overall tracking capabilities of the system.
3. **External force/disturbance estimation:** This involves estimating externally applied forces or disturbances rather than directly altering model parameters. The estimated information is then fed to the controller for appropriate planning. Similar to least-squares methods, these can be based on measurement fitting or tracking error evaluation and share some of their limitations regarding noise and versatility.
4. **Machine learning (ML) based approaches:** These methods aim to mitigate modeling inaccuracies by learning residual or disturbance models using ML techniques. The learned models can then compensate for the discrepancies between the nominal model and the real-world dynamics.
5. **Model updates based on Kalman Filters (KFs):** KFs are widely used for state estimation and have demonstrated effectiveness in estimating model parameters in various domains, including satellite control, flight control, automotive systems, and robot manipulators. They are generally considered more reliable and resistant to noise than RLS for online and offline system identification in these applications.

The presented work positions itself within the fifth category by applying a Kalman filter formulation to the problem of online mass and COM identification for legged robots. The authors highlight a gap, stating that despite the proven benefits of KFs in other fields, this method has "not yet been utilized for the system identification or adaptive control of legged robots." By leveraging the KF's ability to provide a measure of estimation reliability (via the covariance matrix), this research aims to overcome key limitations of RLS and other methods, such as susceptibility to noise and the need for extensive heuristics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a Kalman filter (KF) formulation for the online identification of the total mass and center of mass (COM) of a four-legged robot. This method is then integrated into an adaptive model-based control architecture for quadrupeds.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical limitations inherent in current model-based control strategies for legged robots:
- **Lack of adaptability to variable payloads:** Many real-world applications require legged robots to operate with varying, often unknown, payloads. Traditional model-based controllers rely on fixed plant models, which means they can only handle known payloads through manual parameter incorporation. This significantly restricts their applicability in dynamic environments.
- **Susceptibility of existing methods to noise and biases:** Current online system identification methods, particularly those based on recursive least squares (RLS), are highly susceptible to measurement noise and biases in system state and ground reaction forces (GRFs). This necessitates the use of numerous heuristics to achieve stable and convergent estimates, complicating their robust deployment.
- **Absence of reliability measures in parameter estimation:** Many existing online adaptation methods do not provide a direct measure of the reliability of their parameter estimates. Without this, controllers cannot make informed decisions about when to apply updates, potentially leading to instability if unreliable estimates are used.
- **Computational expense of some adaptive methods:** Certain adaptive control strategies that offer stability guarantees, such as those based on tracking error minimization, often introduce computationally expensive non-linear constraints into the control optimization problem, making them less suitable for real-time applications on resource-constrained platforms.

By proposing a KF-based approach, the authors aim to address these motivations by:
- **Enhancing robustness to measurement noise:** KFs are known for their superior noise resistance compared to RLS methods, which is crucial given the noisy sensor data typically encountered in legged robot systems.
- **Improving tracking performance with varying payloads:** Online adjustment of model parameters (mass and COM) using reliable estimates is expected to maintain consistent and accurate tracking performance even with significant and dynamic payload changes.
- **Providing a direct measure of estimate reliability:** The KF inherently provides an estimation covariance matrix, offering a quantitative measure of the uncertainty in the estimated parameters. This enables the controller to reason about the reliability of the current estimates and to apply updates only when they are deemed sufficiently reliable, thereby reducing the need for extensive, often ad-hoc, heuristics.
- **Maintaining computational efficiency:** The proposed KF formulation is designed to be computationally inexpensive, avoiding the complex optimization constraints found in some other adaptive control methods.

Ultimately, the goal is to enable quadrupeds to operate more autonomously and robustly in real-world scenarios where payloads may vary unpredictably, thereby expanding their practical utility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves developing a Kalman Filter (KF) for online system identification of a quadruped's mass and center of mass (COM), and then integrating this KF into a model-based control architecture.

#### 3.1. Robot Model for Parameter Estimation

The robot is simplified to a floating base in SE(3) with four legs, each having three degrees of freedom. For the purpose of system identification, a key simplification is made: all mass is assumed to be concentrated in the robot's floating base, and the legs are considered massless. This reduces the complexity of the estimation problem by focusing on the floating base dynamics, where payload effects are encoded.

The equations of motion (EOM) for the floating base are expressed in terms of linear and angular momentum balance:
- **Linear momentum:** $m(\dot{v} + g) = \sum_{i=0}^3 F_i$, where $m$ is total mass, $\dot{v}$ is linear acceleration, $g$ is gravity, and $F_i$ are ground reaction forces (GRFs).
- **Angular momentum:** $R^T (I_c - m[c]_{\times}^2)R \dot{\omega} + c \times mg = \sum_{i=0}^3 (r_i \times F_i)$, where $R$ is rotation from body to inertial frame, $I_c$ is rotational inertia about COM, $\dot{\omega}$ is angular acceleration, $c$ is COM position, and $r_i$ are foot positions.

For system identification, the angular acceleration term $R^T (I_c - m[c]_{\times}^2)R \dot{\omega}$ is neglected, assuming the robot does not perform high rotational acceleration movements. This simplifies the angular momentum equation to $c \times mg = \sum_{i=0}^3 (r_i \times F_i)$.

#### 3.2. Parameter Vector and Regressor Form

The parameters to be estimated are the total mass ($m$) and the x and y components of the COM offset, which are assumed to be the most observable during typical quadrupedal walking (the z-component of COM is assumed to be zero due to low observability from gravity in flat terrain locomotion). These are concatenated into a parameter vector $\pi = [m \ h_x \ h_y]^T$, where $h = m[c_x \ c_y]^T$.

The simplified EOM (linear and angular momentum) are rearranged into an affine form with respect to the parameter vector: $\Phi\pi = [\sum F_i \ \sum (r_i \times F_i)]^T$. Here, $\Phi$ is the $6 \times 3$ regressor matrix, effectively the Jacobian of the EOM with respect to $\pi$. It depends only on measurable quantities such as robot pose, acceleration, and the gravity vector.

#### 3.3. Kalman Filter Design

The KF is designed for parameter identification, treating the parameter vector $\pi$ as the state to be estimated.
- **Prediction Step:** The parameter vector is assumed to be stationary (no deterministic model for payload changes), so the process model is $A=I$ (identity matrix) and $B=0$. A random walk is introduced via the process noise covariance matrix $Q$, modeling unexpected parameter changes (e.g., added payload) as a Gaussian random distribution.
 - $\hat{\pi}_k^- = \hat{\pi}_{k-1}^+$
 - $P_k^- = P_{k-1}^+ + Q$
- **Update Step:** The measurement model is derived directly from the EOM in regressor form.
 - The measurement model Jacobian $H$ is the regressor matrix $\Phi_k$.
 - The measurement vector $z_k$ is the right-hand side of the regressor equation, $[\sum F_i \ \sum (r_i \times F_i)]^T$.
 - The update equations are:
 - Kalman Gain: $K_k = P_k^- \Phi_k^T (\Phi_k P_k^- \Phi_k^T + R)^{-1}$
 - State Update: $\hat{\pi}_k^+ = \hat{\pi}_k^- + K_k(z_k - \Phi_k \hat{\pi}_k^-)$
 - Covariance Update: $P_k^+ = (I - K_k \Phi_k) P_k^-$
 - $R$ is the measurement covariance matrix.

#### 3.4. Adaptive Model-Based Control Architecture

The KF is integrated into a control architecture consisting of four main components (Figure 3 in the paper):
1. **Robot:** The physical Unitree Go2 quadruped.
2. **State Estimation:** Computes the robot's floating base state (including accelerations) and foot contact forces from proprioceptive sensors. Ground Reaction Forces (GRFs) are obtained from leg kinematics and inverse dynamics.
3. **Model-Based Controller:** Based on the current estimated state and user input, it plans a gait schedule and generates joint commands. This controller combines a heuristic-based gait sequencer, Model Predictive Control (MPC), and Whole-Body Control (WBC).
4. **Model Adaptation (KF):** This component is responsible for online parameter estimation.
 - It determines the regressor matrix $\Phi_k$ (using the Pinocchio library) and measurement vector $z_k$ based on the estimated robot state.
 - To prevent faulty force measurements from contaminating estimates, GRFs are only used if both a physical contact is detected and a contact is scheduled by the gait planner. Otherwise, the force is set to zero.
 - The KF processes $\Phi_k$ and $z_k$ to update the estimated parameter vector $\hat{\pi}$ and its covariance $P$.
 - **Thresholding for Reliability:** A critical aspect is the application of thresholds on the diagonal elements of the estimation covariance matrix $P$. This mechanism ensures that parameter updates are only sent to the model-based controller when the estimates are sufficiently reliable, addressing the issue of noisy and biased measurements, especially during standing.
 - The contribution of the legs (known from the robot's digital model) is subtracted to isolate the floating base parameters.

#### 3.5. Experimental Validation

The approach is validated on a Unitree Go2 quadruped (16.21 kg nominal mass). The MPC and model adaptation run at 100 Hz, WBC at 500 Hz, and state estimation at 1000 Hz. The performance of the KF-based adaptive controller is compared against:
1. A nominal controller (fixed model parameters).
2. An adaptive controller using a classical RLS parameter estimator (with a forgetting factor of 0.8).
The KF's process noise $Q$ and measurement noise $R$ covariances are tuned, along with specific thresholds for the estimation covariances of mass and COM components.

Experiments include:
- **Base Tracking under Load:** Robot standing and stepping in place, or walking, with varying payloads (0.0 g, 1042.0 g, 2544.5 g, 5105.5 g) attached to the torso.
- **Dynamic Payload Switching:** Robot stepping in place while payloads are changed successively between 0 kg, 1 kg, 2.5 kg, and 5 kg.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation on a Unitree Go2 quadruped demonstrated several key outcomes regarding the performance of the proposed Kalman Filter (KF) based adaptive control, in comparison to nominal control and a Recursive Least Squares (RLS) estimator.

#### 4.1. Covariance Behavior and Update Thresholding

Figure 4 illustrates the diagonal elements of the KF's estimation covariance matrix ($P$) for mass ($m$), COM x-component ($h_x$), and COM y-component ($h_y$).
- When the robot is standing still, the covariances are relatively high, indicating higher uncertainty in the estimates due to biased ground reaction force (GRF) measurements (e.g., from joint friction).
- Upon initiation of stepping motions (trotting gait), the covariances abruptly decrease, signifying that the force measurements become more reliable during dynamic locomotion when joint friction is overcome.
- The ability to set thresholds on these covariances (shown by dashed lines) allows the model adaptation component to apply parameter updates only when the estimates are sufficiently reliable. This mechanism is central to the KF's robustness.
- In contrast, the RLS estimator's error covariances were found not to be similarly descriptive for reliability assessment, preventing meaningful thresholding.

#### 4.2. Base Tracking under Load

Table I summarizes the mean absolute errors and standard deviations for base height ($\tilde{p}_z$), roll ($\tilde{\phi}$), and pitch ($\tilde{\theta}$) for different payloads during both in-place stepping and walking experiments.
- **Base Height Tracking:**
 - The KF-based adaptive controller generally outperformed both the nominal controller and the RLS-based controller in base height tracking when payloads were present. For payloads of 2544.5 g and 5105.5 g, the KF significantly reduced both the mean height error and its standard deviation (e.g., from 3.43 cm to 1.84 cm for 2544.5 g, and from 4.92 cm to 1.89 cm for 5105.5 g, compared to nominal).
 - The KF maintained a relatively constant height error across different payload magnitudes, demonstrating consistent performance.
 - An exception was observed without any payload, where the KF resulted in a slightly increased mean height error and standard deviation (1.91 ± 0.92 cm) compared to the nominal controller (1.30 ± 0.73 cm). This is attributed to a bias in the mass estimation.
 - The RLS estimator did not consistently improve base height tracking and sometimes even degraded it (e.g., 2.91 ± 1.79 cm error without payload compared to 1.30 ± 0.73 cm for nominal).
- **Orientation Tracking (Roll and Pitch):**
 - The nominal controller exhibited robust roll and pitch tracking performance, with minimal impact from increasing payloads.
 - The KF-based controller slightly reduced the roll error for the heaviest payload (5105.5 g) and showed minor reductions in pitch error across most payload scenarios.
 - The RLS results for orientation errors were inconclusive, showing inconsistent improvements or degradations.

#### 4.3. Dynamic Payload Switching

Experiments involving dynamic payload changes (0 kg, 1 kg, 2.5 kg, 5 kg, and back to 0 kg) while the robot stepped in place provided insights into the adaptability of the KF.
- **Mass Estimation (Figure 5a, top):**
 - The estimated total mass fluctuated by approximately $\pm 500$ g around a mean value, with a consistent bias of about 1 kg higher than the true mass.
 - Upon adding payload, the estimate quickly increased by approximately the correct amount.
 - When payload was removed, the estimate converged to a value lower than the original mass, indicating some residual estimation error or persistent bias.
- **COM Estimation (Figure 5a, bottom):**
 - The estimated COM x-component ($c_x$) fluctuated by approximately $\pm 5$ mm, while the y-component ($c_y$) fluctuated by approximately $\pm 2.5$ mm.
 - The weight changes were not strongly observable in the COM estimations, likely due to the payloads being placed close to the robot's nominal center of mass during experiments.
- **Tracking Errors (Figure 5b):**
 - Compared to the nominal controller, the adaptive controller with KF maintained a more consistent and lower level of height and orientation errors throughout the dynamic payload switching experiment. The KF effectively adapted to the changing mass, preventing significant performance degradation that occurred with the fixed-model nominal controller.

#### 4.4. Discussion Summary

- The KF's ability to utilize its estimation covariance to gate updates based on reliability proved effective.
- Mass estimation bias, potentially due to biased force measurements, degraded KF performance when no payload was present (where the nominal model was already accurate). This suggests the need for strategies to handle estimation errors near nominal parameters or improved force measurement accuracy.
- The RLS estimator's poor performance was attributed to its inability to provide meaningful reliability measures via covariances, making it more susceptible to noisy and biased contact forces.
- The limited changes in COM estimates were likely due to the specific payload placement, which did not significantly shift the COM. Future experiments with broader payload positioning would be necessary for a more comprehensive evaluation of COM estimation.
- Noise in parameter estimates, particularly from footstep impacts affecting contact forces and acceleration signals, was identified as a factor. Potential solutions include improved state estimation, alternative problem formulations that avoid accelerations, or increased robustness to regressor matrix uncertainties.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a novel application of Kalman filters for online system identification in adaptive model-based control of quadrupedal robots. Its significance and potential impact can be delineated as follows:

- **Enhanced Adaptability to Variable Payloads:** The core contribution enables legged robots to operate effectively with variable and unknown payloads at runtime. This directly addresses a major limitation of traditional model-based controllers, which rely on fixed plant models. By continuously adapting the robot's dynamic model (mass and COM), the proposed method allows quadrupeds to maintain stable and accurate locomotion even when carrying different or dynamically changing loads. This expands their applicability in real-world scenarios such such as logistics, inspection, or sample collection, where payload variations are common.

- **Improved Robustness to Measurement Noise and Biases:** The Kalman filter formulation demonstrates superior resistance to measurement noise and biases compared to conventional recursive least squares (RLS) methods. This is crucial in robotics, where sensor data (e.g., joint torques, accelerations, GRFs) can be inherently noisy or subject to errors. The KF's ability to effectively filter these uncertainties leads to more reliable parameter estimates.

- **Quantifiable Reliability of Estimates:** A key novel aspect is the KF's inherent capability to output an estimation covariance matrix. This matrix provides a direct, quantitative measure of the reliability of the estimated parameters. This is a significant improvement over many state-of-the-art methods that lack such a feature. This reliability metric allows the adaptive controller to make informed decisions about *when* to update the model parameters, applying updates only when confidence in the estimates is high. This capability reduces the reliance on numerous, often empirical, heuristics typically needed for stable online adaptation.

- **Computational Efficiency:** The algorithm avoids the introduction of computationally expensive non-linear constraints into the control optimization problem, which are often required by other adaptive methods that offer stability guarantees. This makes the KF-based approach suitable for real-time deployment on robotic platforms with finite computational resources.

- **Foundation for Advanced Adaptive Control:** The work lays a foundation for more sophisticated adaptive control strategies. The ability to reason about estimate reliability (via covariance) can be leveraged to inform other parts of the control system, such as the controller or state estimation, making them more robust to model uncertainties.

- **Paving the Way for Broader Parameter Estimation:** The authors suggest future work involving the estimation of second moments of inertia with physical consistency constraints, which would further enhance model accuracy. The framework for handling observability and excitation conditions provided by the KF is conducive to such extensions.

- **Practical Demonstrability:** The open-source implementation for the Unitree Go2 robot signifies the practical applicability and accessibility of the developed method, facilitating further research and adoption by the robotics community.

In essence, this research significantly advances the field of adaptive control for legged robots by providing a robust, efficient, and reliable method for online system identification, thereby moving closer to truly autonomous and versatile quadrupedal locomotion in dynamic and unstructured environments.
