# Fine-Tune an SLM or Prompt an LLM? The Case of Generating Low-Code Workflows

> [!summary] Summary
> Research from ServiceNow found that fine-tuning a Small Language Model (SLM) on domain-specific data yields 10% higher quality outputs, measured by Flow Similarity, compared to prompting large language models for generating low-code workflows. This approach proved more effective for creating structured, precise outputs in complex enterprise automation tasks.

## Problem

- Generating precise, functional low-code workflows from natural language requirements within enterprise systems often leads to quality issues with general-purpose models.
- Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently struggle with domain-specific knowledge, strict structural output rules, and are prone to hallucinations when creating complex, structured outputs.
- Deploying LLMs for high-volume enterprise tasks faces practical challenges regarding inference costs, speed, and infrastructure requirements.

## Method

- A ==two-stage pipeline== decomposes low-code workflow generation into outlining and populating step inputs, enhancing manageability.
- ==Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)== is integrated to supply domain-specific steps and database artifacts, reducing hallucinations and grounding outputs.
- A ==Small Language Model (Mistral-Nemo-12B-Base) fine-tuned on custom enterprise workflow datasets== is compared against carefully prompted state-of-the-art LLMs.

## Results

- The fine-tuned SLM achieved an average of **10%** higher Flow Similarity (FlowSim) for complete workflow generation, with a **12.4%** advantage on out-of-domain datasets.
- Prompted LLMs like GPT-4o demonstrated significant structural error rates (e.g., **17.3%** on the TEST set), compromising the usability and reliability of their generated workflows.
- Fine-tuned SLMs excelled at handling enterprise-specific features and implicit actions, while LLMs showed some strength in complex structural logic like `FOREACH` or `PARALLEL`.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fine-tuning an SLM on specialized data yields higher quality structured outputs for domain-specific tasks compared to prompting much larger, general-purpose LLMs.
> - Domain-specific training data is crucial for generating accurate and reliable enterprise workflows, outperforming even instruction-tuned SLMs without such specialization.
> - Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) primarily reduces hallucinations by providing valid entities, but the majority of errors stem from the model's intrinsic logical structuring and input population capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses several critical and timely themes in the broader artificial intelligence and natural language processing research landscape, particularly concerning the practical application of large language models (LLMs) and small language models (SLMs) in enterprise settings.

Firstly, the core question of "Fine-Tune an SLM or Prompt an LLM?" is a central debate in current generative AI research and deployment strategies. As LLMs like GPT-4o become increasingly powerful and their per-token costs decrease, organizations must evaluate whether the benefits of fine-tuning smaller, more specialized models (e.g., faster inference, lower operational costs, improved domain relevance) still outweigh the convenience and generalized capabilities of powerful off-the-shelf LLMs. This work contributes to this ongoing discussion by providing empirical evidence for a specific, complex, domain-specific task.

Secondly, the research directly contributes to the burgeoning field of **domain-specific AI applications**. While general-purpose LLMs excel at a wide array of tasks, their performance can sometimes be suboptimal for highly specialized contexts requiring deep knowledge or adherence to strict structural rules. This paper highlights that for enterprise systems with unique terminologies, data structures, and operational logic, a general LLM might "hallucinate" or fail to produce the required precise and structured outputs. The focus on "low-code workflows" is a prime example of such a domain-specific task, where understanding enterprise system entities (steps, tables, columns, conditions) is crucial.

Thirdly, this work is situated within the growing interest in **low-code/no-code development via generative AI**. Automating the creation of software components, such as workflows, from natural language descriptions is a significant step towards democratizing software development and enhancing productivity for business users. This paper directly explores the technical feasibility and quality considerations for building such a system, contributing to the broader trend of AI-assisted software engineering.

Fourthly, the methodology heavily relies on **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** and **Task Decomposition**. RAG has emerged as a crucial technique for grounding LLMs in external, up-to-date, or domain-specific knowledge, thereby reducing hallucinations and improving factual accuracy. The paper demonstrates RAG's necessity for structured output generation by feeding the model with available steps and database artifacts. Task decomposition, breaking a complex problem into manageable sub-tasks (workflow outline generation and input population), is also a common and effective strategy for improving the performance of generative models on intricate tasks.

Finally, the paper contributes to the area of **evaluation methodologies for structured output generation**. Developing custom metrics like Flow Similarity (FlowSim), which quantifies the tree edit distance between generated and expected workflows, and designing systematic error analysis approaches (using a binary feature matrix) are vital for accurately assessing model performance in complex, structured domains where standard NLP metrics might fall short. The correlation of FlowSim with human evaluation further strengthens its validity as a reliable metric. This comprehensive evaluation framework offers valuable insights for other researchers and practitioners grappling with similar challenges in evaluating generative AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically determine whether fine-tuning a Small Language Model (SLM) can achieve superior quality compared to carefully prompting much larger, state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) for a highly domain-specific task: generating low-code workflows in JSON format from natural language user requirements. The authors aim to provide clear evidence that, despite the increasing capabilities and decreasing costs of LLMs, SLMs still hold a quality advantage for tasks demanding structured outputs and deep domain knowledge.

The motivation behind this investigation stems from several practical challenges faced during the development of "Flow Generation," an application at ServiceNow designed to automate workflow creation. These motivations include:

- **Achieving Desired Software Quality:** For an enterprise application, the generated workflows must be precise, functional, and adhere to strict system rules. The authors questioned whether prompting off-the-shelf LLMs would be sufficient to meet these rigorous quality standards for domain-specific, structured outputs.
- **Addressing Domain Specificity:** Low-code workflows within an enterprise system are inherently complex, requiring knowledge of available steps, specific data types, conditions, loops, and database elements (tables, columns, values) that vary by installation and can include custom components. General LLMs might struggle with these nuanced, proprietary details.
- **Optimizing Resource Utilization:** The authors explicitly state their consideration of SLMs (under 15 billion parameters) to "reduce infrastructure needs." This indicates a practical motivation concerning inference speed, computational costs, and deployment complexity, which are crucial factors for enterprise-scale AI applications. As LLM token costs decrease, the other operational advantages of SLMs (faster inference, lower total cost of ownership for high-volume tasks) become more relevant.
- **Reducing Hallucinations and Ensuring Adherence to Rules:** Generating structured outputs like JSON workflows necessitates strict adherence to predefined schemas and rules. LLMs are known to "hallucinate," generating syntactically correct but functionally incorrect or nonsensical outputs, which is unacceptable for enterprise automation. The motivation was to find an approach that minimizes such errors.
- **Understanding Model Limitations and Guiding Future Development:** Beyond a simple comparison, the authors were motivated to perform a systematic error analysis. This helps to identify specific weaknesses of both fine-tuned SLMs and prompted LLMs, providing actionable insights for future model improvements, data collection, and pipeline enhancements.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the need to build a high-quality, efficient, and reliable generative AI solution for a critical enterprise function, while pragmatically navigating the trade-offs between powerful general-purpose LLMs and specialized, fine-tuned SLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a comprehensive methodology centered on a pipeline architecture combining task decomposition, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and a comparative study between fine-tuned SLMs and various prompted LLMs.

#### 3.1. Task Definition and Decomposition

The core task is to generate low-code workflows in JSON format from natural language user requirements. To manage the complexity of this structured output, the task is decomposed into two distinct stages:
1. **Generate Workflow Outline (createFlow):** Given a natural language requirement, the model identifies step names, their execution order, and extracts an annotation (description) for each step. The output is a JSON outline.
2. **Populate Step Inputs (populateInputs):** For each step in the generated outline, using its annotation, the model gathers necessary data from the environment and generates the detailed step inputs in JSON format. Annotations serve as a bridge, providing context for input generation.

#### 3.2. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

RAG is integrated into the pipeline to reduce hallucinations and provide domain-specific context. The RAG system suggests:
- **Available Steps:** A dynamic list of steps specific to the system installation, including custom user-added steps.
- **Database Artifacts:** Relevant table names, column names, and values used in steps that interact with the system's database.
The retriever, along with the SLM, is fine-tuned using standard supervised fine-tuning and contrastive loss training, respectively.

#### 3.3. Pipeline Architecture

The system architecture consists of three layers:
- **UI Layer:** Receives user requirements and displays the generated workflow outline and inputs.
- **AI Layer:** Contains the language model (LLM/SLM) and the retriever.
- **Data Layer:** Stores indexed sources of system data (steps, tables, columns, values) that the retriever queries to provide suggestions to the LLM. This layer is adaptable for different customer installations.
Crucially, a workflow with *N* steps involves a maximum of *N+1* LLM calls: one for the outline and one for each step's inputs.

#### 3.4. Datasets

Custom datasets were created for both training and evaluation due to the domain-specific nature of the task:
- **Training Data:**
 - **Internal (1,512 workflows, 4,709 populateInputs examples):** Extracted and labeled from existing internal enterprise system deployments.
 - **Synthetic (766 workflows, 2,310 populateInputs examples):** Artificially created by domain experts to cover a broader distribution and simpler workflow structures.
- **Evaluation Data:**
 - **TEST (108 workflows, 367 populateInputs examples):** Generated by expert enterprise users interacting with the application, simulating real customer usage, often representing unfinished workflows.
 - **Out-of-Domain (OOD) (1,072 workflows, 4,958 populateInputs examples):** Labeled workflows extracted from 10 different customer deployments spanning diverse domains (e.g., retail, banking), thus containing different steps and table names than the training data.

#### 3.5. Models

- **Fine-tuned SLM (Our Approach):** Mistral-Nemo-12B-Base, chosen for its architectural support and optimization within ServiceNow's system. Fine-tuned on the custom training datasets.
- **Baseline SLM:** Mistral-Nemo-12B-Instruct, an instruction-fine-tuned model of the same architecture and size but *not* on workflow domain data, used to validate the impact of domain-specific training.
- **Prompted LLMs:**
 - **Closed-source:** GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, Gemini-2.0-Flash.
 - **Open-source:** Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct.
 - **Reasoning-focused:** o3-mini with medium reasoning.
For LLMs not fine-tuned, two detailed prompt templates (one for `createFlow`, one for `populateInputs`) were crafted. These templates included sections for Context, Task definition, Inputs, Guidelines, Constraints (with examples), and Output format, dynamically populated with system data and RAG suggestions.

#### 3.6. Metrics

- **Flow Similarity (FlowSim):** A custom metric based on tree edit distance, treating workflows as trees. Higher scores indicate better similarity to the expected workflow. This metric was validated to correlate well with human evaluation (Pearson and Spearman correlations > 0.75). It is reported for both "Outline" and "Outline and Inputs."
- **Structure Errors:** Percentage of generated examples with basic structural rule violations (e.g., `ELSE` without `IF`), which could severely impact usability.
- **Perfect RAG Simulation:** To understand RAG's impact, experiments were conducted where all steps in the expected workflow were guaranteed to be suggested by the retriever.

#### 3.7. Error Analysis

A systematic, iterative approach was developed for fine-grained error analysis:
1. **Qualitative Analysis:** Initial review of production model outputs to identify patterns.
2. **Feature Identification:** Based on patterns, features in the ground truth dataset that negatively impacted output quality were identified (e.g., presence of `FOREACH`, `SLA`, `implicit actions`).
3. **Binary Matrix Creation:** A matrix where each ground truth sample is characterized by the presence (1) or absence (0) of identified features, aligned with model scores.
This method allows for streamlined model comparison on specific features, quicker identification of underperforming areas, and extensibility for new error patterns.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings that underscore the advantages of fine-tuning SLMs for domain-specific, structured output generation in an enterprise context.

#### 4.1. Fine-tuned SLM Outperforms Prompted LLMs in Quality

The most significant finding is that **fine-tuning an SLM (Mistral-Nemo-12B-Base) consistently results in higher quality outputs** compared to prompting much larger, state-of-the-art LLMs.
- On average, the fine-tuned SLM achieved a **10% higher Flow Similarity (FlowSim)** score when generating complete workflows (outline and inputs).
- The performance gap was more pronounced for the more complex task of generating both outline and inputs, especially on the **Out-of-Domain (OOD) dataset (12.4% higher)**, demonstrating its robustness across varied customer environments.
- For the TEST set (user-generated requirements), the gap was 7.2%, indicating that the fine-tuned SLM also handled the nuances of real-world user input better.

#### 4.2. Importance of Domain-Specific Training Data

The baseline model, Mistral-Nemo-12B-Instruct (an instruction-fine-tuned SLM without domain-specific training), performed poorly across all metrics. This clearly validates that **in-domain training data is a crucial ingredient** for achieving high quality in such specialized tasks, regardless of model size or general instruction-following capabilities.

#### 4.3. LLM Performance and Limitations

- Among the prompted LLMs, **Gemini-2.0-Flash and GPT-4o performed the best**, yielding competitive but ultimately inferior results to the fine-tuned SLM.
- Smaller LLMs and those emphasizing reasoning (GPT-4o-mini, o3-mini) generally performed poorly, indicating that sheer size or general reasoning capabilities are not sufficient for this task without specific domain adaptation.
- **Structural Errors:** A critical finding was the prevalence of structural errors in LLM-generated outputs. While GPT-4o had reasonable FlowSim scores, it exhibited a high percentage of structural errors (17.3% on TEST, 12.0% on OOD), significantly impacting usability. Gemini-2.0-Flash performed better in this regard, even surpassing the fine-tuned SLM on the TEST set in terms of structure errors. When FlowSim was re-calculated with structure validation (assigning zero to broken workflows), GPT-4o's performance degraded substantially, highlighting the fine-tuned SLM's superior reliability in adhering to structural rules.

#### 4.4. RAG's Role: Addressing Hallucinations vs. Model Errors

Experiments with "perfect RAG" (where the retriever always provided all necessary steps) showed only a **maximum improvement of 4%** in outline generation for LLMs. This suggests that while RAG is essential for grounding and reducing hallucinations by providing valid system entities, the majority of errors are **model-intrinsic failures** (e.g., poor logical structuring, incorrect input population) rather than a lack of retrieved information. This implies that improving the generative capabilities of the model itself through fine-tuning is more impactful than merely improving retrieval.

#### 4.5. Granular Insights from Error Analysis

The systematic error analysis provided detailed insights into model strengths and weaknesses:
- **Structural Logic:** The fine-tuned SLM **underperformed LLMs (Gemini-2.0-Flash, GPT-4o) on workflows involving complex structural logic** like `FOREACH`, `PARALLEL`, and `TRY/CATCH`. The SLM often missed dependency steps or failed to generate complete logical blocks.
- **Domain-Specific Features:** Conversely, the fine-tuned SLM **consistently outperformed LLMs on enterprise-specific features** (e.g., Service Catalog, SLA, Glide datetime) and input-related features (worknotes, trigger conditions, multiple conditions). The average FlowSim score for the fine-tuned SLM was 12.16% higher than GPT-4o and 5.35% higher than Gemini-2.0-Flash on the ENTERPRISE set. This highlights the effectiveness of fine-tuning for internal system intricacies.
- **Implicit Actions:** The fine-tuned SLM was significantly better at handling user requirements where workflow steps and conditions were **expressed implicitly**, suggesting that the fine-tuning process enabled it to learn complex mappings from natural language to structured actions based on labeled examples.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for both academic understanding and practical application of generative AI, particularly within enterprise environments.

#### 5.1. Practical Guidance for Enterprise AI Development

The most immediate impact is the **strong empirical guidance it provides for companies developing domain-specific generative AI applications**. It makes a compelling case that, even with the advancements in general-purpose LLMs, investing in fine-tuning SLMs for tasks requiring highly structured, precise, and domain-aware outputs can yield substantially better quality. This is crucial for applications where errors can have significant operational or business consequences, such as automating critical enterprise workflows. The finding challenges the notion that prompting ever-larger LLMs is always the optimal or simplest path for complex industrial use cases.

#### 5.2. Economic and Efficiency Benefits of SLMs

By demonstrating the quality advantage of SLMs, the paper reinforces their role as a viable, and often superior, alternative to large LLMs for specific tasks. This has considerable economic and efficiency implications for enterprise deployments. SLMs require **less computational power for inference**, leading to **faster response times** and **lower operational costs** per query. In high-volume enterprise settings, these savings can be substantial, making advanced AI features more scalable and cost-effective to deploy. This work suggests that "smaller is better" when appropriately specialized.

#### 5.3. Advancing Low-Code/No-Code Automation

The "Flow Generation" application, which translates natural language into low-code workflows, directly contributes to the burgeoning field of **AI-assisted software development and automation**. By improving the quality of generated workflows, this research enables business users to more effectively automate complex processes, reducing the reliance on specialized developers and accelerating digital transformation within organizations. This democratizes access to automation capabilities, allowing a broader range of users to create sophisticated applications.

#### 5.4. Methodological Contributions to Evaluation and Error Analysis

The paper introduces and validates **Flow Similarity (FlowSim)** as a robust metric for evaluating structured output generation, particularly for tree-like structures such as workflows. Its demonstrated correlation with human judgment makes it a valuable tool for future research in similar domains. Furthermore, the systematic error analysis approach, utilizing a binary feature matrix, offers a reproducible and extensible framework for gaining granular insights into model performance. This methodology can be adopted by other researchers to efficiently diagnose model limitations and guide targeted improvements in generative AI systems.

#### 5.5. Informing Future Research and Product Development

The detailed error analysis provides a clear roadmap for future research. Identifying specific weaknesses (e.g., SLMs struggling with complex structural logic, LLMs making basic structure errors) allows for targeted improvements, such as specialized architectural enhancements, better training data augmentation for specific patterns, or incorporating post-processing steps. For ServiceNow, these findings will directly inform the iterative development of their Flow Generation application, leading to more robust and user-friendly products. The insights into RAG's limitations also suggest that while external knowledge is vital, improving the generative model's internal understanding and adherence to complex logic remains paramount.
