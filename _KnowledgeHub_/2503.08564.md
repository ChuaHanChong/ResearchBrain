# MoE-Loco: Mixture of Experts for Multitask Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> A Mixture of Experts (MoE) framework, MoE-Loco, was developed to enable a quadruped robot to achieve robust locomotion across diverse challenging terrains and switch between quadrupedal and bipedal gaits. This single policy achieved an 0.879 success rate in a mixed-task simulation benchmark, outperforming standard approaches, and successfully transferred zero-shot to a physical robot while alleviating gradient conflicts inherent in multitask learning.

## Problem

- Training a single robot locomotion policy that generalizes across a wide variety of terrains and distinct locomotion modes, such as quadrupedal and bipedal gaits.
- Mitigating gradient conflicts in multitask reinforcement learning (MTRL), which arise when tasks have fundamentally different dynamics and hinder performance or cause divergence.
- Current MTRL policies for robot locomotion are often limited to specific gaits or simpler terrains and struggle with robust real-world deployment.

## Method

- Developed **MoE-Loco**, a Mixture of Experts (MoE) framework, to train a single policy for multitask locomotion across diverse terrains and gaits.
- Employed a two-stage training process: an initial oracle policy with privileged information and MoE, followed by adaptation to proprioceptive-only observations using ==Probability Annealing Selection (PAS)==.
- Incorporated task-specific reward functions for 9 locomotion tasks and extensive domain randomization to enhance robustness and generalization.

## Results

- MoE-Loco achieved superior performance in a mixed-task simulation benchmark with an **0.879** success rate, significantly surpassing standard methods, and demonstrated robust zero-shot transfer to a Unitree Go2 robot across diverse real-world terrains.
- The MoE architecture quantitatively reduced gradient conflicts, particularly between bipedal and quadrupedal tasks, showing higher cosine similarity and lower negative gradient ratios compared to a standard policy.
- Analysis revealed clear expert specialization for different locomotion tasks and gaits, enabling the framework to synthesize novel gaits like a "dribbling gait" and rapidly adapt to new constraints such as three-footed walking.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture successfully mitigates gradient conflicts in multitask reinforcement learning, particularly when training for diverse terrains and gait types.
> - Different experts within the MoE framework naturally specialize in distinct locomotion behaviors and gaits, as evidenced by gating weight distributions and t-SNE visualizations.
> - The modularity of MoE allows for interpretable skill decomposition and the zero-shot composition of novel gaits or rapid adaptation to new physical constraints by adjusting expert contributions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a dominant paradigm for training robot locomotion policies, demonstrating success in enabling legged robots to learn various behaviors in both simulated and real-world environments. Existing research has shown success in tasks such as traversing complex terrains, achieving high-speed running, and performing extreme maneuvers like bipedal walking or parkour. However, a common characteristic of much of this work is a focus on single-task policies or a limited number of terrains and specific skills.

The broader research landscape for robot locomotion faces a significant challenge in developing a unified policy that can generalize across a wide variety of terrains and different locomotion modes (e.g., bipedal and quadrupedal gaits). Multitask learning (MTL) aims to address this by training a single network for multiple tasks, allowing for shared knowledge. However, a known issue in multitask reinforcement learning (MTRL), particularly when using simple neural network architectures, is the occurrence of gradient conflicts. These conflicts can lead to suboptimal model performance and even divergence, especially when tasks involve fundamentally different dynamics or objectives, such as diverse terrains coupled with distinct bipedal and quadrupedal gaits.

Previous MTRL efforts in robotics have often focused on manipulation tasks or specific aspects of locomotion, such as learning policies for different categories of quadruped robots (e.g., ManyQuadrupeds) or constructing policies from pre-trained expert models for basic skill acquisition (e.g., MELA). Some approaches attempt hierarchical RL for multi-terrain locomotion (e.g., MTAC), but often remain limited to a single gait, relatively simple terrains, and may not demonstrate real-world deployment.

This work addresses the aforementioned challenges by integrating the Mixture of Experts (MoE) framework into multitask legged locomotion. MoE, a long-standing concept that has seen renewed interest and application across fields like natural language processing and computer vision, has also been explored in reinforcement learning for scaling. By applying MoE to robot locomotion, MoE-Loco aims to specifically mitigate the pervasive problem of gradient conflicts in MTRL for robots navigating complex, diverse environments with multiple gaits. This represents an advancement in creating robust and adaptable general-purpose locomotion policies capable of zero-shot transfer to real-world scenarios, moving beyond specialized single-task or limited multi-task approaches. The authors position their work as leveraging MoE to enable a single policy to manage diverse terrains and gait switching, while also providing mechanisms for skill decomposition and composition, which contrasts with opaque, black-box standard neural network policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a single neural network policy that enables a quadruped robot to perform robust and versatile locomotion across a variety of challenging terrains and switch between fundamentally different locomotion modes, specifically quadrupedal and bipedal gaits. This goal is motivated by the real-world requirement for robots to operate in diverse, unstructured environments where a wide range of movement capabilities is necessary.

The key motivations behind pursuing this objective and integrating the Mixture of Experts (MoE) framework are detailed as follows:

1. **Overcoming Gradient Conflicts in Multitask Reinforcement Learning (MTRL):** A central motivation is to address the significant challenge of gradient conflicts that typically arise when training a single neural network policy for multiple, disparate tasks in reinforcement learning. These conflicts occur when the optimal gradient updates for one task interfere with those for another, leading to reduced training efficiency and degraded overall performance. For locomotion, this problem is exacerbated when tasks involve highly varied terrains (e.g., bars, pits, stairs, slopes, baffles) and distinct gaits (quadrupedal vs. bipedal), potentially causing model divergence. The MoE architecture is proposed as a mechanism to mitigate these conflicts by allowing different "experts" within the network to specialize in distinct behaviors, thereby directing gradients more appropriately.

2. **Improving Training Efficiency and Performance:** By alleviating gradient conflicts, the authors aim to improve both the efficiency of the training process and the ultimate performance of the learned policy across the diverse set of locomotion tasks. A more efficient training allows for faster convergence and better exploitation of the environment's rewards.

3. **Enabling Skill Decomposition and Composition:** The modular nature of the MoE framework is leveraged to facilitate the natural emergence and analysis of specialized locomotion behaviors within different experts. This modularity is motivated by the potential to then recombine these specialized skills with adjustable weights to synthesize new, novel locomotion patterns or adapt to new tasks without requiring extensive additional training. This capability would enhance the adaptability and reusability of learned skills, moving beyond a black-box policy approach.

In summary, the research seeks to advance the field of robot locomotion by demonstrating a unified policy capable of complex, multi-modal locomotion, addressing a core limitation of conventional MTRL, and providing a foundation for more adaptable and interpretable robot control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce MoE-Loco, a Mixture of Experts (MoE) framework designed for multitask locomotion in legged robots. The methodology centers on training a single policy to handle diverse terrains and switch between quadrupedal and bipedal gaits.

**Task Definition:**
The problem is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where tasks are characterized by varying state spaces ($S_\tau$), reward functions ($R_\tau$), and termination conditions. The robot learns a policy $\pi(a|s)$ to maximize cumulative reward across 9 challenging locomotion tasks:
- **Quadrupedal tasks:** Bar crossing, pit crossing, baffle crawling, stair climbing, slope walking.
- **Bipedal tasks:** Standing up, plane walking, slope walking, stair descending.
The control input includes velocity commands and a one-hot vector indicating the desired gait (quadrupedal or bipedal). The approach focuses on blind locomotion, using only proprioceptive information, but is noted to be extensible to visual RL.

**MoE Based Multitask Locomotion Learning:**
The training framework employs a two-stage process, building upon prior work and utilizing Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

- **State Space:** Observations include:
 - **Proprioception ($\mathbf{p}_t$):** Projected gravity, base angular velocity (IMU), joint positions, joint velocities, and the last action.
 - **Explicit privileged state ($\mathbf{e}_t$):** Base linear velocity and ground friction. These are typically available in simulation but not directly on a real robot.
 - **Implicit privileged state ($\mathbf{i}_t$):** Contact forces, encoded into a low-dimensional latent representation to manage the sim-to-real gap.
 - **Command ($\mathbf{c}_t$):** A velocity command ($v_x, v_y, v_{yaw}$) and a one-hot gait vector ($g=0$ for quadrupedal, $g=1$ for bipedal).
- **Action Space:** Consists of 12 desired joint positions for the robot's joints.
- **Reward Design:** Task-specific reward functions are implemented, differing for quadrupedal ($\mathbf{r}_{quad}$) and bipedal ($\mathbf{r}_{bip}$) gaits. These functions incorporate tracking rewards, regularization terms for joint limits, velocities, accelerations, and gait-specific balancing and termination penalties (detailed in Appendix V-A).
- **Termination:** Gait-dependent termination conditions are defined. For quadrupedal gait, excessive roll or pitch terminates the episode. For bipedal gait, contact of any link other than the rear feet and calf (after 1 second) leads to termination.

**Training Stages:**

1. **Stage 1: Oracle Policy Training:**
 - All observation states ($\mathbf{p}_t, \mathbf{e}_t, \mathbf{i}_t, \mathbf{c}_t$) are accessible.
 - The implicit state $\mathbf{i}_t$ is encoded into a latent representation. This latent, along with $\mathbf{e}_t$ and $\mathbf{p}_t$, forms a dual-state representation $\mathbf{l}_t$.
 - An LSTM integrates historical information from $\mathbf{l}_t$ and $\mathbf{c}_t$ into a state representation $\mathbf{h}_t$.
 - **MoE Architecture:** The MoE framework is integrated into both the actor and critic networks. A gating network $g(\mathbf{h}_t)$ outputs scores, which are normalized by a softmax function to produce expert weights $\hat{g}_i$. The final action $\mathbf{a}_t$ is a weighted sum of the outputs from $N$ individual experts $f_i(\mathbf{h}_t)$. The actor and critic MoE modules share the same gating network to ensure consistency.
 - An estimator module is pre-trained in this stage using an L2 loss ($L_{recon}$) to reconstruct the privileged information (`[Enc($\mathbf{i}_t$), $\mathbf{e}_t$]`) from proprioception and command (`Estimator($\mathbf{p}_t, \mathbf{c}_t$)`).
 - The overall optimization objective for this stage is $L_{surro} + L_{value} + L_{recon}$, combining PPO's surrogate and value losses with the reconstruction loss.

2. **Stage 2: Policy Adaptation with Proprioception:**
 - The policy's observations are limited to proprioception and command ($\mathbf{p}_t, \mathbf{c}_t$).
 - Weights of the estimator, LSTM, and MoE modules are initialized from Stage 1.
 - Probability Annealing Selection (PAS) is used to gradually shift the policy's reliance from the full privileged state to the estimator's output, allowing adaptation while minimizing performance degradation.

**Skill Decomposition and Composition:**
The MoE architecture is leveraged for its ability to dynamically decompose tasks into specialized skills.
- **Expert Specialization:** Quantitative analysis is performed on expert coordination across tasks to understand how experts specialize.
- **Skill Composition:** Pretrained experts can be recombined by manually or dynamically adjusting their gating weights ($w[i] \cdot \text{softmax}(g(\mathbf{h}_t))[i]$). This allows for the synthesis of new skills or gaits without additional full training, by selectively blending expert contributions.

**Experiment Setup:**
- **Simulation Environment:** IsaacGym, running 4096 robots concurrently on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.
- **Training Schedule:** 40,000 iterations for plane walking (both gaits), followed by 80,000 iterations for challenging terrains, and a final 10,000 iterations of PAS.
- **Control Frequency:** 50Hz in both simulation and real-world.
- **Real-world Deployment:** Unitree Go2 quadruped robot with an NVIDIA Jetson Orin. PD control is used for low-level joint execution ($K_p=40.0, K_d=0.5$).
- **Number of Experts:** Six experts are used ($N_{exp}=6$).
- **Benchmark:** A 5m x 100m runway with various obstacles (bars, pits, baffles, stairs, slopes) for both quadrupedal and bipedal gaits.
- **Robustness Measures:** Domain randomization is applied to parameters like base mass, mass position, friction, initial joint positions, and motor strength. Gaussian noise is added to observations (linear/angular velocity, gravity, joint position/velocity). Fractal noise is applied to the ground plane to prevent leg dragging and ensure robustness on uneven terrains.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results from both simulated and real-world experiments to evaluate MoE-Loco.

**Multitask Performance (Simulation):**
- **Overall Performance:** MoE-Loco demonstrated superior performance in the mixed-task benchmark across all three metrics: success rate, average travel distance, and average passing time. It achieved a success rate of 0.879, outperforming "Ours w/o MoE" (0.571) and RMA (0.000).
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - **"Ours w/o MoE" (standard MLP):** This baseline, with a similar total parameter count but lacking the MoE architecture, exhibited significantly lower performance in mixed tasks and struggled particularly with challenging terrains such as baffle crawling (0.264 success rate vs. 0.924 for MoE-Loco), quadrupedal stair walking (0.568 vs. 0.684), bipedal slope walking (0.504 vs. 0.961), and bipedal stair descending (0.453 vs. 0.964). This indicated that gradient conflicts substantially impacted the performance of standard neural networks.
 - **RMA:** This baseline, using a 1D-CNN as an asynchronous adaptation module, showed very limited success across the benchmark, achieving 0% success on the mixed task and failing on most individual challenging terrains.
- **Single-Task Performance:** MoE-Loco outperformed baselines in nearly all individual challenging tasks, with the exception of quadrupedal slope walking, which the authors suggest might be due to its relative simplicity.

**Real-World Experiments:**
- **Zero-Shot Deployment:** The MoE policy was successfully deployed zero-shot (without additional training) on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot.
- **High Success Rates:** In real-world tests involving a mix of challenging terrains and separated terrains for both gaits, MoE-Loco achieved higher success rates across all tasks compared to baselines (Figure 4). For instance, in the challenging mixed terrain sequence (bar, baffle, stairs, pits, slopes, then bipedal slope up/down), MoE-Loco demonstrated a significantly higher success rate.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** The policy's robustness and ability to generalize were further demonstrated through successful locomotion in various outdoor environments (Figure 5).

**Gradient Conflict Alleviation:**
- **Quantitative Metrics:** Experiments measured gradient cosine similarity (higher indicates less conflict) and negative gradient ratio (lower indicates less conflict).
- **Reduced Conflict:** The MoE policy consistently showed higher cosine similarity and lower negative gradient ratio compared to the standard policy (without MoE). This effect was pronounced between bipedal and quadrupedal tasks, and also significant between quadrupedal tasks requiring distinct skills (e.g., bar crossing vs. baffle crawling). This finding supports the hypothesis that MoE effectively mitigates gradient conflicts.

**Training Performance:**
- During the pretraining stage for plane walking (both gaits), the MoE policy exhibited improved training performance. It achieved higher mean rewards and longer mean episode lengths compared to the standard policy with a similar parameter count (Figure 6), indicating enhanced learning efficiency and stability.

**Expert Specialization Analysis:**
- **Gating Weight Distribution:** Qualitative analysis of the mean gating weights revealed that different experts were activated with varying prominence across different tasks (Figure 7), confirming that experts naturally specialize.
- **t-SNE Visualization:** t-SNE visualization of the gating network outputs (Figure 8) demonstrated that bipedal and quadrupedal tasks formed distinct clusters. Tasks requiring similar gaits, such as quadrupedal slope walking and pit crossing, clustered together, while tasks with more distinct movement patterns (bar crossing, baffle crawling, stair climbing) clustered further apart. This quantitatively confirms the emergence of specialized expert behaviors.

**Skill Composition:**
- **Dribbling Gait Synthesis:** Leveraging the identified specialization of experts (e.g., a "balancing expert" and a "crossing expert"), the authors manually adjusted gating weights (doubling the crossing expert's weight and masking others) to synthesize a new "dribbling gait" zero-shotly. This demonstrated the interpretability of MoE-based policies and the ability to compose novel skills from existing expert knowledge (Figure 9).
- **Three-Footed Gait Adaptation:** In an adaptation experiment, a new expert was initialized, and the original experts' parameters were frozen. By only updating the gating network, the robot quickly learned to walk on three feet on various terrains (flat ground, slopes). This showcases MoE's potential for rapid adaptation to new tasks by leveraging existing skills and focusing learning on the gating mechanism (Figure 10).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MoE-Loco framework represents a notable contribution to the field of robotic locomotion control, with several key aspects of significance and potential impact:

1. **Unified Policy for Multitask, Multi-gait Locomotion:** The most significant contribution is the demonstration of a single neural network policy capable of controlling a quadruped robot across a diverse array of challenging terrains (bars, pits, stairs, slopes, baffles) and switching between fundamentally different locomotion modes, specifically quadrupedal and bipedal gaits. This addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics of developing versatile and generalizable locomotion skills, moving beyond single-task or limited-scope multi-task policies.

2. **Effective Mitigation of Gradient Conflicts:** The research provides empirical evidence that the Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture effectively mitigates gradient conflicts inherent in multitask reinforcement learning. This is a critical technical advancement, as gradient conflicts often hinder the performance and stability of multi-task learning systems. By demonstrating improved training efficiency and superior task performance attributed to MoE, this work offers a robust solution for a pervasive problem in MTRL.

3. **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The successful zero-shot deployment of MoE-Loco on a real Unitree Go2 robot, coupled with its performance in various outdoor environments, underscores its robustness and adaptability to real-world complexities. This capability is crucial for robots operating in unstructured or dynamic settings where pre-programmed behaviors are insufficient.

4. **Interpretability and Reusability of Learned Skills:** The analysis of expert specialization, confirmed by varying gating weights and t-SNE visualizations, highlights the interpretability offered by the MoE architecture. Unlike opaque black-box neural networks, MoE allows for the identification and understanding of distinct sub-skills developed by individual experts. This interpretability opens avenues for more deliberate skill composition and task migration.

5. **Facilitating Skill Composition and Rapid Adaptation:** The ability to synthesize novel gaits (e.g., the dribbling gait) by manually adjusting expert contributions and to rapidly adapt to new physical constraints (e.g., three-footed walking) by updating only the gating network demonstrates a powerful mechanism for skill reusability and efficient adaptation. This modular approach could significantly reduce the training effort required to introduce new behaviors or adapt to changes in robot morphology or environment.

**Potential Impact:**
The principles demonstrated by MoE-Loco could have a broad impact on the development of highly autonomous and versatile legged robots. Future robots could leverage such policies to:
- Operate effectively in highly variable and complex environments without requiring task-specific policies or extensive re-training.
- Exhibit more human-like adaptability, drawing upon a repertoire of specialized skills to tackle unforeseen challenges.
- Accelerate the deployment of robots in fields such as exploration, disaster response, and logistics, where navigating diverse terrains and performing multiple functions are essential.

The authors also suggest extending this approach to incorporate sensory perception (camera, Lidar), which implies that MoE-Loco could serve as a foundational control framework for even more complex and perceptive robotic tasks in the future.
