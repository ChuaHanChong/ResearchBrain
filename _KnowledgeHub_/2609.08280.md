# Seeing is Not Believing: Breaking the Physical-to-Digital Trust Boundary in Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> This work exposes a ROS 2 vulnerability where setting LD_PRELOAD loads a hook that overrides rcl_publish, intercepting telemetry and control messages before Secure ROS 2 encrypts them. An Isaac Sim digital twin plus physics-grounded latent micro-motion synthesizes hardware-realistic joint states, letting an attacker hijack a Franka arm while spoofing verifiers, with sub-3 ms delay and 87% bypass of an autoregressive detector.

## Problem

- Downstream verifiers confirm task handovers from reported sensor telemetry alone, assuming reported joint states and camera frames faithfully mirror a robot's true physical execution.
- Secure ROS 2 authenticates, access-controls and encrypts messages only after they enter the DDS stack, so it never attests that published content matches physical reality.
- ROS 2 deployments lean on unverified third-party containers and repositories, a decentralized long tail that offers a plausible distribution channel for user-space malicious code.

## Method

- ==Pre-publication function interposition==: a Hook.so exported via LD_PRELOAD shadows rcl_publish, edits target-topic messages, then forwards them to the genuine symbol, leaving official binaries untouched.
- A ==hijacker-plus-spoofer pipeline== mirrors intercepted control signals into an Isaac Sim twin, samples its telemetry as the spoofed stream, and injects teleoperated commands into the physical arm.
- ==Latent micro-motion== perturbs the simulated trajectory with damped stochastic oscillators, ==Ornstein-Uhlenbeck drift== and stick-slip jumps, mass-matrix-shaped inter-joint covariance, and ==inverse-dynamics torque derivation==.

## Results

- Hijack-plus-spoof succeeded in **100 of 100** pick-and-place trials with and without Secure ROS 2, bypassing range, position-velocity, cross-joint and temporal detectors at **98%**, **100%**, **95%** and **87%**.
- Interception added under **3 ms** delay against the **33 ms** period of **30 Hz** joint states, roughly **4 ms** jitter and a **0%** message drop rate, leaving the task undelayed.
- Ablations show coupling lifts cross-joint bypass from **5%** to **92%** and micro-motion lifts temporal bypass from **13%** to **66%**; full synthesis cuts shoulder-joint velocity error **34%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cryptographic integrity in transit proves only provenance, never physical truthfulness; trust must be anchored at acquisition by signed, counter-stamped sensing hardware.
> - Realistic sensor forgery comes from perturbing the underlying physical state, not each channel, since shared-trajectory derivation automatically satisfies cross-channel consistency checks.
> - Statistical anomaly detectors are a weak defense once an attacker models the generative physics; an ensemble of four still left most attacks undetected.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses security in networked and autonomous robotics, particularly the reliability of telemetry used for remote monitoring, task verification, and multi-robot collaboration. Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) is widely used as middleware for distributed robotic systems, including warehouse robots, autonomous vehicles, agricultural platforms, and space-exploration systems. Its communications can be secured through Secure ROS 2 (SROS 2), which relies on DDS Security to provide authentication, authorization, and encryption.

Existing security research generally distinguishes between protecting messages during communication and verifying that software components are authentic. SROS 2 provides protection once a message has entered the DDS communication layer. The paper examines a different issue: whether a message accurately represents the physical state of the robot before it is transmitted. This is relevant to remote attestation and physics-based verification, where a verifier may infer task completion from joint states, camera images, velocities, or other sensor data.

The paper’s central contribution is to identify a pre-publication trust boundary in ROS 2. It argues that an attacker operating in the same user-space environment as a ROS 2 application can alter outgoing telemetry and control messages before SROS 2 authenticates and encrypts them. The resulting messages can therefore be cryptographically valid while being physically false.

The work also connects robotics security with software supply-chain security. ROS 2 deployments frequently use external packages, tools, and Docker containers. The authors’ ecosystem analysis suggests that many such artifacts are maintained by relatively unknown or unverified sources. The analysis does not show that these artifacts are malicious, but it demonstrates that third-party software could provide a plausible route for introducing user-space attack code into robotic deployments.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to determine whether an attacker can break the correspondence between a robot’s physical behavior and the telemetry observed by a downstream verifier. The authors focus on settings in which a verifier cannot continuously observe the robot through an independent trusted sensor and must instead rely on ROS 2 messages.

The paper considers two main attack objectives. The first is hijacking combined with spoofing. The attacker changes control signals so that the physical robot performs unauthorized actions, while replacing the robot’s telemetry with synthesized data consistent with the intended task. A verifier may then conclude that the robot completed its assigned task even though the physical execution was different. The second objective is denial of service. In this case, the attacker injects inconsistent or abnormal data to cause a verifier to reject valid executions, trigger diagnostics, or halt a workflow.

The motivation is that cryptographic security alone does not establish sensor truthfulness. Authentication can confirm that a message came through an authorized ROS 2 participant, and encryption can protect it from network interception. Neither property proves that the data originated directly from a physical sensor or that it was not modified by software operating before the communication layer.

The authors investigate five research questions: whether the attack works under ROS 2 and SROS 2; whether it operates in real time; how closely synthesized telemetry resembles genuine telemetry; whether the approach extends to camera data; and how individual synthesis components contribute to evading verification.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Threat Model

The attacker is assumed to obtain ordinary user-space execution within the environment running the target ROS 2 application. The proposed access route is a compromised third-party package, container, or auxiliary tool. The attacker is not assumed to have root privileges, kernel access, physical access, control of robot firmware, or the ability to modify official ROS 2 binaries. The attacker also does not compromise SROS 2 credentials or alter messages after they have entered the DDS communication layer.

The verifier is assumed to be uncompromised but dependent on ROS 2 telemetry. It may be a human operator, a peer robot, or a monitoring module. The model excludes systems with an independent trusted sensor that continuously observes the robot.

#### 3.2. Interception Mechanism

The authors trace the ROS 2 publication path from a Python client through the ROS 2 client library and the core `rcl` library. They identify `rcl_publish` as a common point where a message is still accessible before it is passed to the underlying middleware through `rmw_publish`.

The attack uses Linux dynamic function interposition. By setting the `LD_PRELOAD` environment variable to load a malicious shared library, the attacker causes the target process to resolve calls to `rcl_publish` through an attacker-controlled implementation first. The hook can inspect the publisher and message, identify selected topics, modify the message, and then invoke the legitimate publication function. This approach leaves the official ROS 2 binaries unchanged.

Because the modification occurs before DDS Security, SROS 2 subsequently authenticates and encrypts the altered message through its normal process. The verifier therefore receives a message that is valid from the communication system’s perspective but does not necessarily describe physical reality.

#### 3.3. Hijacking and Telemetry Spoofing Pipeline

The attack contains a hijacking component and a spoofing component. The hook intercepts control signals and telemetry. The authors use Isaac Sim on a separate attacker-controlled computer to simulate the intended robot behavior. The intercepted legitimate control signals are applied to the simulated robot, and the simulation produces telemetry with the same message types and structure as the physical robot.

The attacker simultaneously teleoperates the physical robot using alternative control inputs. These malicious control signals replace the intended commands before publication. The physical robot therefore follows the attacker’s commands, while the policy and downstream verifier receive data associated with the simulated execution. The paper describes this as two parallel execution traces: the physical robot follows one trajectory and the digital simulation follows another.

#### 3.4. Realistic Telemetry Synthesis

The paper argues that raw simulator output is distinguishable from physical telemetry because real robots exhibit vibration, friction, drift, and correlated motion across joints. To address this, the authors add latent micro-motion to simulated trajectories rather than independently adding noise to each telemetry channel.

The perturbation includes three components: vibration modeled with damped stochastic oscillators, slowly varying drift modeled with an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, and small discontinuities representing stick–slip friction. Inter-joint correlations are introduced by shaping disturbance covariance using the robot’s mass matrix. Position, velocity, and torque are then derived from the same perturbed trajectory. Torque is calculated using inverse dynamics, including inertial, Coriolis, gravity, and friction terms. This design is intended to preserve temporal structure, inter-joint relationships, and consistency between related telemetry channels.

The authors state that parameters can be calibrated from a short recording of stationary physical-robot telemetry, potentially collected during homing.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Design

The experiments use a physical Franka Emika robotic arm running ROS 2 Humble on Ubuntu, with SROS 2 authentication enabled in relevant experiments. The victim system includes an RViz visualization interface, while the attacker uses a separate computer running Isaac Sim. The main task is a repeated pick-and-place operation.

The evaluation includes four verification methods: range checking for position, velocity, and effort; mathematical consistency between position and velocity; cross-joint correlation analysis; and an autoregressive temporal anomaly detector. The authors also measure latency, jitter, message loss, telemetry similarity using RMSE and Dynamic Time Warping, torque variation, multimodal image synthesis, and the effects of removing individual synthesis components.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The attack successfully hijacked the robot in all 100 trials conducted without SROS 2 and all 100 trials conducted with SROS 2. In each case, the robot performed unauthorized actions while the RViz interface continued to display apparently normal task execution. This result supports the paper’s claim that the attack operates independently of DDS-level message protection.

Against the four verification methods, the complete synthesis pipeline achieved bypass rates of 98% for range checking, 100% for position–velocity consistency, 95% for cross-joint correlation analysis, and 87% for the temporal anomaly detector. The temporal detector was therefore the most effective of the evaluated defenses, but it still failed to identify most attack instances in the reported experiments.

The attack operated without message loss and with low reported communication overhead. The evaluation states that message delivery delay remained below 3 milliseconds, compared with a 33-millisecond interval for 30 Hz joint-state data. However, the paper gives somewhat inconsistent jitter figures: the abstract and introduction refer to approximately 2–3 milliseconds, while the evaluation text describes a standard deviation of around 4 milliseconds. This discrepancy should be clarified, although all reported values indicate that the attack did not disrupt normal message timing.

Telemetry comparisons showed that Real–Fake differences fell within the variability observed between two genuine physical-robot runs. Position RMSE, velocity DTW distance, and torque standard deviation exhibited similar per-joint patterns for genuine and synthesized data. The shoulder and wrist joints showed the greatest variation in both cases, while distal joints showed lower variation.

The ablation study indicates that each synthesis component contributed to detector evasion. For joint J2, position RMSE decreased from 0.1248 for raw simulation output to 0.1117 with the full pipeline. Velocity DTW distance decreased from 0.0029 to 0.0019, and torque standard deviation decreased from 0.3618 to 0.2793. Micro-motion substantially improved temporal anomaly performance, inter-joint coupling improved correlation-based evasion, and deriving all channels from one trajectory produced exact position–velocity consistency. Only the complete pipeline bypassed all four detectors at the reported rates.

The paper also demonstrates qualitative camera-frame manipulation. A vision-language model was used to generate final-state images that preserved the real background while depicting the simulated robot in the intended final configuration. The evaluation focuses on a final image rather than continuous video. The authors acknowledge that synchronously fabricating multiple live camera feeds would be more difficult and computationally demanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s principal significance is its distinction between communication integrity and physical provenance. A system can authenticate, authorize, and encrypt telemetry while still accepting data that was falsified inside the robot’s application environment. This has implications for remote task handovers, safety monitoring, industrial automation, delivery confirmation, and multi-robot coordination.

The findings suggest that statistical telemetry checks alone may not provide a dependable trust anchor once an attacker controls the pre-publication software path. The proposed defense is to move the root of trust closer to the physical sensing process. A secure microcontroller or trusted execution environment could sign measurements at acquisition time and attach monotonic counters to prevent modification and replay by ordinary user-space software.

The results should be interpreted within the study’s scope. The experiments use one robotic platform, one ROS 2 distribution, a specific application architecture, and a limited set of detectors. The threat model assumes a compromised artifact has already been installed, and the camera evaluation is qualitative and limited to final-state imagery. The supply-chain measurements demonstrate exposure but do not estimate the frequency of real-world malicious packages. Future work would need to examine broader hardware and middleware configurations, secure boot and process-isolation defenses, trusted sensor architectures, and independent physical observation.

Overall, the paper presents evidence that the physical-to-digital relationship in ROS 2 can fail before conventional communication security mechanisms apply. It supports a security architecture in which message confidentiality and authenticity are supplemented by trusted sensing, provenance attestation, and stronger controls over software introduced into robotic execution environments.
