# Drift is a Sampling Error: SNR-Aware Power Distributions for Long-Horizon Robotic Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Chongqing Institute of Green and Intelligent Technology introduce Context-Aware Power Sampling (CAPS), a training-free, inference-time framework that addresses instruction drift in long-horizon robotic tasks by re-conceptualizing it as a systematic sampling error. CAPS uses an adaptive dual-process system with global power distributions and an SNR-based metacognitive control to iteratively refine action trajectories, achieving up to 18% higher success rates in real-world benchmarks compared to prior methods.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models suffer from "instruction drift" in complex, long-horizon robotic tasks, where robots lose contextual grounding with the ultimate goal.
- Prior solutions like prompt engineering are limited by open-loop generation, and inference-time scaling methods often rely on inefficient parallel sampling and re-ranking for continuous action spaces.
- Local greedy sampling is prone to collapsing into "Negative Pivotal Windows" – irreversible local optima that lead to globally erroneous actions despite high local probability.

## Method

- Context-Aware Power Sampling (**CAPS**) is a training-free, inference-time framework that re-frames inference as sampling from a global power distribution to sharpen the probability landscape for long-horizon consistency.
- It employs an adaptive dual-process system that switches between "fast intuition" (greedy execution) and "slow deliberation" (global optimization via MCMC) using a Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) based metacognitive control.
- When drift risk is detected by a drop in SNR (high entropy), a Metropolis-Hastings (MCMC) algorithm is activated for iterative trajectory refinement, using randomized resampling and power distribution-based acceptance checks.

## Results

- CAPS achieved a **15.2%** increase in average success rate on RoboTwin 1.0, reaching **47.4%**, and outperformed TACO by **6.1%**.
- On the Libero-long benchmark, CAPS demonstrated a **97.6%** average success rate, significantly outperforming OpenVLA (**49.8%**) and RoboMonkey (**56.5%**).
- Real-world evaluation on XLeRobot showed CAPS improved the average success rate by **18.0%** over TACO (**53.0%**), achieving **71.0%**, with its adaptive strategy incurring only **2.15x** latency compared to **8.5x** for an 'Always-On' variant.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Instruction drift in long-horizon robotics can be fundamentally re-conceptualized as a systematic sampling error inherent in local greedy strategies, leading to "myopia of single-step prediction."
> - Applying global power distributions mathematically sharpens the model's probability landscape, enabling the system to "see through" locally tempting but globally detrimental actions.
> - An adaptive, context-aware mechanism, triggered by an increase in Shannon entropy (drop in SNR), allows for efficient allocation of computational resources, activating deliberation only at critical decision points.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research operates within the field of embodied artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic control. These models have demonstrated capabilities in various robotic tasks, but a persistent challenge in complex, long-horizon scenarios is "instruction drift." This phenomenon describes situations where robots lose contextual grounding with the ultimate goal as tasks progress, leading to locally reasonable but globally erroneous actions.

Prior work has attempted to address instruction drift through two primary avenues:
- **Prompt Engineering:** Techniques like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and ReAct aim to enhance logical coherence by inducing intermediate reasoning steps. However, these methods are often limited by their open-loop, unidirectional generation nature.
- **Inference-Time Scaling:** Approaches such as TACO model inference as a contextual bandit problem, using verifiers to re-rank parallel candidate actions. While these methods introduce a form of deliberation, they typically rely on independent parallel sampling and subsequent re-ranking, lacking the capacity for iterative refinement of a single trajectory. If the initial set of candidates lacks high-quality solutions, simple re-ranking proves ineffective.

The authors identify a critical gap in the application of inference-time scaling to continuous visuo-motor control. Unlike discrete symbolic reasoning tasks where exhaustive sampling might be feasible, robotic policies operate in high-dimensional continuous action manifolds governed by physical consequences. This "topological mismatch" makes unconstrained exhaustive sampling computationally prohibitive and renders greedy policies prone to collapsing into "Negative Pivotal Windows"—irreversible local optima that, despite high local probability, sever pathways to global success.

This work builds upon the theoretical foundation of power distributions and energy models, which have been explored in machine translation and mathematical reasoning to sharpen probability landscapes and facilitate implicit planning over future trajectories. The current research extends this theoretical lineage to embodied intelligence, specifically continuous action spaces, aiming to bridge the identified gap by developing an adaptive, inference-time computational framework that can dynamically allocate resources to overcome the limitations of greedy sampling in long-horizon robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recurring failure mode of "instruction drift" observed in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models when applied to complex, long-horizon robotic tasks. The authors reconceptualize instruction drift not merely as a phenomenon of model forgetting or diluted attention, but fundamentally as a systematic sampling error inherent in local greedy strategies. They posit that these strategies lead to "myopia of single-step prediction," causing generated trajectories to be locally smooth but globally inconsistent. Specifically, local greedy sampling is susceptible to collapsing into "Negative Pivotal Windows"—states that possess high local probability but irreversibly sever global success pathways.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Theoretical Reframing of Instruction Drift:** To reinterpret long-horizon robotic instruction drift from the perspective of systematic sampling error. This involves introducing global power distributions as a mathematical framework for analyzing this phenomenon, contrasting it with traditional local temperature sampling, and providing a theoretical explanation for why greedy policies fall into undesirable local optima. The goal is to demonstrate that by sharpening the model's probability landscape, the system can "see through" locally tempting but globally detrimental actions.
2. **Development of Context-Aware Power Sampling (CAPS):** To design and implement a training-free, inference-time computation framework, CAPS, that can mitigate instruction drift. This framework aims to:
 - **Reformulate Inference:** Shift the inference objective from simple autoregressive generation to sampling from a global power distribution, which intrinsically reweights conditional generative trajectory distributions towards trajectories with higher long-horizon consistency.
 - **Dynamic Compute Allocation:** Introduce a metacognitive control mechanism based on Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) to dynamically allocate inference-time computational resources. This mechanism should enable a context-aware transition from "intuitive fast thinking" (greedy execution) to "rational slow search" (global optimization) only when drift risk is detected (i.e., at "Topological Bifurcation Points" or "Negative Pivotal Windows" where decision uncertainty spikes).
 - **Iterative Trajectory Refinement:** Integrate a Metropolis-Hastings (MCMC) algorithm with randomized resampling and power distribution-based acceptance checks within the "slow search" phase. This allows for iterative rectification and refinement of candidate trajectories, improving robustness while preserving practical inference efficiency.
3. **Empirical Validation of Performance and Efficiency:** To rigorously evaluate the CAPS framework on established long-horizon robotic benchmarks (RoboTwin, Simpler-WindowX, Libero-long, and real-world tasks). The objective is to demonstrate substantial improvements in success rates over strong baselines, including OpenVLA and TACO, without requiring parameter updates or incurring prohibitive computational overhead. This empirical validation aims to support the effectiveness of adaptive inference-time computation for enhancing long-horizon robustness and consistency in embodied control.

In essence, the research seeks to equip VLA models with a "search-when-necessary" capability, enabling them to deliberate and correct course in uncertain or critical situations, thereby overcoming the inherent limitations of myopic greedy sampling in continuous action spaces.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Context-Aware Power Sampling (CAPS) framework is designed as a dual-process architecture for continuous robotic control, aiming to align the high-dimensional action manifold with long-horizon task objectives. It operates entirely at inference time without requiring model retraining.

#### 3.1. Problem Reconceptualization and Objective Reframing

The paper redefines instruction drift as a failure to account for the "Global Topology of Action Manifold" in traditional greedy autoregressive generation ($p_\theta(\tau|I, H_t)$). Instead of directly predicting the next action greedily, CAPS re-frames the inference objective as sampling from a **global Power Distribution**:
$\pi(\tau) \propto p_\theta(\tau|I, H_t)^\alpha$, where $\alpha \ge 1$ is a sharpening coefficient.
This power distribution mathematically sharpens the base model's conditional generative trajectory distribution, enhancing the probability of trajectories that exhibit higher long-horizon consistency and discouraging paths that lead to "Negative Pivotal Windows."

#### 3.2. Adaptive Computation as Optimal Control (Dual-Process System)

CAPS employs a metacognitive control mechanism to dynamically allocate computational resources, balancing inference precision and efficiency. This mechanism governs the transition between two systems:

- **System 1: Fast Intuition (Greedy Low-Temperature Sampling):** This is the default mode, executing actions based on the initial greedy path derived from the base VLA model.
- **System 2: Slow Deliberation (Global Optimization via MCMC):** This system is activated only when necessary, specifically when the risk of instruction drift is detected.

##### 3.2.1. Metacognitive Signal: KL Divergence-Based SNR

To detect drift risk, CAPS quantifies the "signal decay" using a **Contextual Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)**. Instruction drift is conceptualized as the policy distribution gradually degrading into random noise. SNR is defined using Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence:
$SNR_t \triangleq D_{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|H_t) || U_{unif}) = \log |A| - H(\pi_\theta(\cdot|H_t))$, where $U_{unif}$ is the uniform distribution over the action space $A$, and $H(\pi_\theta(\cdot|H_t))$ is the Shannon entropy of the policy.
A sharp increase in entropy (and thus a drop in SNR) indicates a "Bifurcation Point" on the probability manifold, signifying high uncertainty and a critical decision moment where the model faces multiple potential paths. Entropy serves as an efficient proxy for drift detection; if entropy is high, System 2 is activated.

##### 3.2.2. Optimal Switching Policy

The selection of the sampling strategy is modeled as a resource-constrained decision problem aimed at minimizing total expected loss (Error + $\lambda \cdot$ Computational Cost). This formulation leads to a hard-threshold switching rule:
$\pi^*(\text{H}_t) = \begin{cases} \pi_{slow} \text{ (CAPS)} & \text{if } H(\pi_\theta(\cdot|H_t)) > \gamma(\lambda) \\ \pi_{fast} \text{ (Greedy)} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
Here, $\gamma(\lambda)$ is an entropy threshold, implying that additional computation is allocated only when uncertainty exceeds a budget-determined tolerance limit.

##### 3.2.3. Block-Based Autoregressive MCMC (Intra-Inference Loop)

When System 2 is activated, CAPS employs a **Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm** to iteratively refine action trajectories. This process involves:

- **Proposal Step (Randomized Resampling):** A candidate trajectory $\tau_{new}$ is generated. This is done by keeping the prefix of the current action block unchanged and resampling the suffix from the base model's conditional generation distribution ($p_\theta$) with a temperature of $1/\alpha$. This step explores alternative paths within the local action manifold.
- **Acceptance Step (Global Sharpening Verification):** The decision to accept $\tau_{new}$ over $\tau_{old}$ is based on an acceptance rate $A(\tau_{old}, \tau_{new})$ that uses the sharpened power distribution:
 $A(\tau_{old}, \tau_{new}) = \min \left(1, \frac{p_\theta(\tau_{new}|I, H_t)^\alpha}{p_\theta(\tau_{old}|I, H_t)^\alpha} \cdot \frac{q(\tau_{old}|\tau_{new})}{q(\tau_{new}|\tau_{old})} \right)$.
 The term $p_\theta(\cdot)^\alpha$ acts as a strict verifier, prioritizing trajectories with higher global consistency. The second term is the Hastings Correction for the proposal distribution.
- **Iterative Refinement:** The proposal and acceptance steps are repeated $N_{MCMC}$ times, allowing the system to explore and verify candidate trajectories within its internal probability space before outputting an action.

#### 3.3. Chunk-Level Approximation and Theoretical Support

While the theoretical framework operates at the trajectory level, practical implementation involves block-based MCMC on finite action chunks, analogous to receding-horizon control. The SNR gating mechanism ensures that this local refinement is selectively applied at critical, low-SNR decision points that are most prone to long-range error accumulation. The base VLA model implicitly encodes long-horizon priors, meaning the acceptance step still selects local actions compatible with broader long-term consistency.

The methodology is supported by two theorems regarding **Effective Horizon Extension**:
- **Theorem 3.1 (Ideal Bound):** Under ideal sampling, global sharpening with $\alpha > 1$ extends the effective horizon from linear to polynomial order, approximately by a factor of $\epsilon^{1-\alpha}$ (where $\epsilon$ is the single-step drift probability).
- **Theorem 3.2 (Asymptotic Lower Bound):** Under MCMC sampling with finite steps $N$, the effective horizon extension is approximately $\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon^\alpha + O(\rho^N)}$, where $O(\rho^N)$ represents the residual sampling error due to incomplete MCMC convergence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates CAPS across multiple benchmarks, including simulated environments (RoboTwin, Simpler-WindowX, Libero-long) and real-world robotic setups, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving long-horizon robustness without retraining.

#### 4.1. RoboTwin 1.0 Benchmark: Bimanual Collaboration and Complex Manipulation

CAPS demonstrated performance improvements in tasks requiring precise bimanual coordination. Compared to the base policy ($\pi_0$), CAPS achieved a 15.2% increase in average success rate, reaching 47.4%. This also surpassed TACO, a baseline employing test-time scaling, by 6.1%. For a drift-prone task like "Dual Bottles Pick Hard," CAPS raised the success rate from 48.0% (base) to 61.0%, indicating the benefit of its iterative search mechanism.

#### 4.2. Simpler-WindowX: OOD Generalization

On Simpler-WindowX, which tests robustness against visual changes and low SNR scenarios, CAPS achieved an average success rate of 60.5% with $\pi_0$. This result notably outperformed specialized models like SpatialVLA (42.7%) and TACO (55.5%). In high-interference tasks such as "Carrot on Plate," CAPS improved the success rate by 19.0% over the base, which the authors attribute to the SNR gating mechanism effectively triggering resampling when visual noise lowered confidence.

#### 4.3. RoboTwin 2.0: Large-Scale Test-Time Scaling

Evaluations on the larger RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark further supported CAPS's scalability and performance. CAPS achieved an overall success rate of 66.2%, outperforming RDT (34.6%) and TACO (64.0%). In long-horizon tasks, such as "Lift Pot," CAPS showed a 14.0% improvement over $\pi_{0.5}$, suggesting that the global power distribution sampling effectively suppressed error accumulation. In simpler tasks, CAPS maintained performance comparable to baselines, indicating the efficiency of SNR gating in avoiding unnecessary computational overhead.

#### 4.4. Libero-long: Long-Horizon Tasks

For tasks specifically designed to test instruction consistency over long horizons, Libero-long, CAPS demonstrated a 97.6% average success rate. This significantly outperformed OpenVLA (49.8%), RoboMonkey (56.5%), and also slightly improved upon TACO (96.6%). For the "Moka Pots on Stove" task, CAPS increased the success rate from 68.0% to 91.0%, which the authors attribute to the MCMC search effectively avoiding local optima at "Negative Pivotal Windows."

#### 4.5. Real-World Evaluation

A comprehensive benchmark suite of 10 real-world long-horizon tasks on the XLeRobot platform demonstrated the sim-to-real transferability and generalized robustness of CAPS. With $\pi_0$ as the base model, CAPS achieved an average success rate of 71.0%, representing an 18.0% improvement over TACO (53.0%). Case studies, such as "Collaborative Storage" (context retention) and "Multi-stage Breakfast Preparation" (long-horizon memory), illustrated CAPS's ability to maintain task context and plan subsequent actions by detecting low SNR signals of uncompleted instructions.

#### 4.6. Ablation Study

A systematic ablation study on RoboTwin 1.0 investigated the contribution of each CAPS component:
- **Necessity of Power Distribution:** Setting $\alpha=1$ (no sharpening) resulted in a success rate of 38.5%, significantly lower than CAPS's 47.4%. This indicated that the raw probability landscape is too flat and noisy for effective search, necessitating global sharpening.
- **Iterative Search vs. Rejection Sampling:** Replacing MCMC with independent Best-of-N rejection sampling yielded 40.2% success, lagging behind CAPS. This suggested that MCMC's iterative local refinement is more efficient for high-dimensional action spaces compared to blind independent sampling.
- **Efficiency of Adaptive Compute:** An "Always-On" CAPS variant (without SNR gating) achieved a slightly higher success rate of 48.1% but incurred an 8.5x latency increase. In contrast, the full CAPS model, with its SNR-adaptive gating, achieved 47.4% success with only a 2.15x latency. This validated the "search-when-necessary" strategy, where CAPS selectively activated deliberation for approximately 15.3% of "Negative Pivotal Windows."
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The study found CAPS to be robust across a range of sharpening coefficients ($\alpha \in$) and MCMC iteration steps ($N_{MCMC} \in$). The Shannon entropy-based SNR metric proved superior for drift detection compared to random or minimum probability triggers.

Collectively, these results support the framework's core premise: that instruction drift can be mitigated by adaptively introducing computation and leveraging global power distributions for deliberative planning, leading to improved long-horizon robustness and efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a reconceptualization of instruction drift in long-horizon robotic planning, shifting the understanding from simple model forgetting to a systematic sampling error. By positing that local greedy sampling can lead to irreversible "Negative Pivotal Windows," the paper provides a new theoretical lens rooted in statistical physics for analyzing failures in embodied control.

The introduction of **Context-Aware Power Sampling (CAPS)** represents a significant contribution as a training-free, inference-time computation framework. Its key impacts and potential are:

1. **Enhanced Robustness in Long-Horizon Tasks:** CAPS substantially improves success rates across a diverse range of complex, long-horizon tasks on multiple benchmarks (RoboTwin, Simpler-WindowX, Libero-long, and real-world environments). This addresses a critical limitation of existing VLA models, making robots more reliable in multi-step, sequential, and coordinative manipulation.
2. **Adaptive and Efficient Computation:** The metacognitive control mechanism, based on Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and Shannon entropy, enables dynamic allocation of computational resources. This allows the system to transition between "fast intuition" (greedy execution) and "slow deliberation" (MCMC search) only when drift risk is detected. This "search-when-necessary" strategy optimizes inference-time compute, achieving significant robustness gains without incurring prohibitive latency, which is crucial for real-time robotic applications.
3. **Novel Application of Power Distributions:** Extending the theory of power distributions to continuous visuo-motor control provides a novel mathematical framework for sharpening global trajectory probabilities. This mechanism allows the model to "see through" locally appealing but globally detrimental actions, facilitating a lookahead search over the conditional generative trajectory distribution.
4. **Theoretical Underpinnings for Horizon Extension:** The theoretical analysis demonstrating that global sharpening can extend the robot's effective horizon from linear to polynomial order offers a quantitative explanation for the observed empirical improvements in long-horizon tasks. This provides valuable insights into how adaptive inference can fundamentally alter the capabilities of robotic policies.
5. **Practical Deployment Potential:** As a training-free method, CAPS can be readily integrated with existing VLA models (e.g., $\pi_0$, OpenVLA) without requiring extensive retraining or architectural modifications, accelerating its potential for real-world deployment. The real-world evaluation on the XLeRobot platform further validates its sim-to-real transferability and generalized robustness.

**Limitations and Future Work:** The paper acknowledges that the effectiveness of CAPS is constrained by the feasibility simplex of computational budget, MCMC mixing rate, and sharpening coefficient. Latency bottlenecks could arise in extremely high-frequency control scenarios or if the base model's energy landscape leads to a vanishing spectral gap, hindering MCMC convergence. Future work aims to address these limitations through reasoning distillation to transfer "slow thinking" capabilities into "fast execution" policies and by integrating multimodal physical verification to optimize sampling efficiency with physical feedback.

**Ethical Considerations:** The authors note that while global sharpening improves consistency, it could theoretically amplify inherent biases or unsafe priors present in the base VLA model. Therefore, rigorous safety guardrails would be necessary for real-world deployment.

Overall, this research offers a principled, efficient, and empirically validated approach to a central problem in embodied AI, contributing both theoretical insights into the nature of robotic drift and a practical framework for building more robust and reliable robotic systems.
