# Improving Multimodal Learning via Imbalanced Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China introduce a theoretical re-evaluation showing that optimal multimodal learning benefits from imbalanced modality contributions, inversely proportional to their prediction variances. Their Asymmetric Representation Learning (ARL) strategy implements this principle, achieving state-of-the-art performance with improvements up to 3.02% macro F1 on CREMA-D and consistent gains across diverse multimodal tasks and architectures.

## Problem

- Multimodal models frequently exhibit an "under-optimized problem," where individual modality encoders within a fused model perform below their unimodal potential.
- A prevailing hypothesis attributes this to "modality imbalance," suggesting a stronger modality suppresses weaker ones during joint training.
- Existing solutions largely focus on rebalancing learning across modalities, often through unimodal assistance, gradient modulation, or alternating learning.

## Method

- The authors theoretically demonstrate, via ==bias-variance decomposition==, that optimal multimodal decision dependency should be inversely proportional to each modality's prediction variance.
- They propose ==Asymmetric Representation Learning (ARL)==, which uses auxiliary regularizers to estimate ==modality variance (uncertainty)== without adding extra parameters.
- ARL then dynamically modulates gradients to each modality encoder based on their ==inverse variance ratio== and incorporates ==unimodal bias regularization== to minimize individual modality biases.

## Results

- ARL consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance across various multimodal tasks and datasets, including a **3.02%** macro F1 improvement on CREMA-D, **2.28%** on AVE, and **3.01%** on Kinetics-Sounds.
- The method demonstrates strong generalization capabilities, performing effectively with more than two modalities (MOSI), on large datasets (UCF-101), and when integrated with diverse architectures like CNN-based and Transformer-based models.
- Ablation studies confirm that both Unimodal Bias Regularization and Asymmetric Learning components, along with the Gradient Residual, synergistically contribute to the observed performance gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal multimodal learning is achieved not through balanced contributions, but by an imbalanced dependency where modalities with lower prediction variance contribute more to the final decision.
> - The 'under-optimized problem' can be effectively addressed by simultaneously minimizing individual modality biases and the variance of the fused multimodal prediction.
> - A residual connection in gradient modulation is crucial to prevent training stagnation in modalities with very small modulation factors, ensuring continuous optimization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal learning, which involves integrating information from diverse data sources like audio, visual, and text, has garnered substantial attention due to its potential to enhance model robustness and performance across applications such as action recognition, object detection, and speech recognition. Despite this promise, existing multimodal methods frequently encounter an "under-optimized problem," where the performance of individual modality encoders within a fused model falls short of their unimodal potential, sometimes even yielding results inferior to single-modality models.

The prevailing hypothesis in the research community attributes this under-optimization to "modality imbalance." This theory posits that a stronger, "dominant" modality can suppress the learning process of weaker modalities during joint training, hindering the full utilization of multimodal data. Consequently, current research efforts have largely focused on strategies to rebalance the learning across modalities. These strategies broadly fall into several categories:
- **Unimodal Assistance:** Employing additional unimodal classifiers, pre-trained models, or knowledge distillation to boost weaker modalities.
- **Gradient Modulation:** Directly adjusting the gradients of different modalities within the multimodal model to reduce the influence of dominant modalities or accelerate weaker ones.
- **Alternating Learning:** Transforming joint multimodal training into alternating unimodal learning processes to minimize inter-modality interference.

This paper critically re-evaluates the foundational premise of "modality imbalance" and the subsequent push for "balanced learning." It argues that "balanced learning is not the optimal setting for multimodal learning." This assertion directly challenges the core assumption underpinning a significant body of existing work. By providing theoretical proof derived from bias-variance analysis, the authors reposition the problem, proposing that optimal performance is achieved not by balancing, but by an *imbalanced dependency* on each modality that is inversely proportional to their respective prediction variances. This theoretical shift marks a potential paradigm change, moving from an intuitive notion of fairness across modalities to a more data-driven, statistically optimal distribution of influence. This work therefore represents a significant contribution by questioning established beliefs and offering a new theoretical framework that could guide future research in multimodal optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to fundamentally re-examine and improve the sub-optimal performance often observed in multimodal learning models. The authors are driven by a crucial insight: the conventional wisdom that multimodal learning should aim for "balanced learning" across modalities might be flawed.

The motivation for this challenge stems from an empirical observation. The authors demonstrate, using the CREMA-D dataset, that increasing the gradient of a *stronger* modality (e.g., audio) does not necessarily decrease performance, as would be expected by balanced learning theories, but can actually *improve* it (Figure 1). This counter-intuitive finding prompted a deeper theoretical investigation into the true bottleneck limiting multimodal performance.

Specifically, the key objectives are:
1. **Challenge the "Balanced Learning" Paradigm:** To demonstrate, both empirically and theoretically, that balanced learning is not always optimal for multimodal models.
2. **Derive Optimal Modality Dependency:** To theoretically prove, using bias-variance decomposition, that the optimal decision dependency ratio on each modality should be inversely proportional to their prediction variances. This implies that modalities with lower variance (i.e., higher certainty) should ideally contribute more to the final decision.
3. **Propose a Novel Optimization Strategy:** To develop a practical and versatile method, called Asymmetric Representation Learning (ARL), that can effectively implement this theory by modulating modality contributions according to their variances, thereby minimizing generalization error.
4. **Validate Effectiveness and Versatility:** To extensively test ARL across diverse multimodal tasks, datasets, architectural backbones, and numbers of modalities to confirm its superiority and generalizability.

In essence, the paper aims to shift the multimodal optimization strategy from an intuitive goal of "fairness" (balanced contribution) to a theoretically grounded objective of "optimality" (variance-weighted contribution).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, Asymmetric Representation Learning (ARL), is built upon a theoretical re-analysis of the under-optimized problem in multimodal learning, stemming from a bias-variance decomposition.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Foundation: Re-analysis of Under-optimized Problem

The authors reframe the joint multimodal learning process as an ensembling of multiple unimodal processes. By applying the bias-variance decomposition framework, typically used in regression, they analyze the generalization error of a multimodal model.
- **Bias Term:** They show that minimizing the bias of the fused multimodal prediction requires minimizing the bias of *each individual unimodal prediction*. They argue that directly combining unimodal biases with fixed weights (w0, w1) does not lead to a meaningful optimal solution for minimizing the overall bias, thus necessitating an approach to minimize individual modality biases.
- **Variance Term:** For the variance of the multimodal prediction, they derive that to minimize this term, the contribution weights (w0, w1) of each modality should be inversely proportional to their respective prediction variances (Equation 14-16). This is the cornerstone of their argument: `w0 / w1 = Var(s_m1) / Var(s_m0)`, meaning the modality with lower variance should have a greater contribution. This directly contradicts the balanced learning assumption.

#### 3.2. Asymmetric Representation Learning (ARL) Strategy

Based on this theoretical foundation, ARL introduces three key components designed to implement the derived optimal dependency and minimize bias.

1. **Modality Analysis:**
 - **Objective:** To accurately measure the "variance" (or uncertainty) of each modality's prediction.
 - **Approach:** ARL introduces auxiliary regularizers for each modality encoder. These regularizers calculate the logit output for each modality (p_m0, p_m1) independently of the main fusion module. The variance for classification tasks is approximated using the inverse of self-information entropy derived from the softmax output (Equation 17). A crucial design choice is that these auxiliary regularizers share parameters with the multimodal model and rely solely on modality representations, introducing *no extra parameters* and maintaining independence from the specific multimodal architecture or fusion method.

2. **Asymmetric Learning:**
 - **Objective:** To adjust the optimization dependency of each modality such that it aligns with the inverse of their respective variance ratio.
 - **Approach:** ARL calculates modulation coefficients (a_m0, a_m1) using a softmax function that compares the desired inverse variance ratio (q_m0/q_m1) with the current optimization dependency ratio (d_m0/d_m1) (Equation 18). A temperature coefficient `T` controls the intensity of modulation. These coefficients then modulate the gradients (g_s) passed back to each modality encoder (Equation 19-20). A key detail is the use of a *residual connection*, where the original gradient is added to the modulated gradient. This prevents the training process from stalling if a modulation factor becomes extremely small. This ensures continuous, albeit modulated, optimization.

3. **Unimodal Bias Regularization:**
 - **Objective:** To reduce the prediction bias of each individual modality, which the theoretical analysis showed is critical for minimizing overall generalization error.
 - **Approach:** ARL incorporates a cross-entropy loss for each unimodal prediction (u_m0, u_m1) directly into the total loss function (Equation 21-22). This explicitly optimizes the unimodal branches to reduce their inherent prediction biases, complementing the variance reduction achieved through asymmetric learning.

The total loss `L_ARL` is a combination of the standard multimodal cross-entropy loss and a weighted sum of the unimodal bias regularizers. The entire ARL strategy is designed as a flexible plug-in, requiring no changes to the multimodal model's architecture or fusion method, thus making it widely applicable.

#### 3.3. Comparison with Existing Methods

The authors highlight that existing methods primarily focus on "balanced learning" or "gradient balancing" to make modalities contribute equally. In contrast, ARL's core distinction lies in advocating for and implementing "imbalanced learning" where contributions are optimally weighted by the inverse of modality variances.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimental evaluation of ARL demonstrates its effectiveness and versatility across various multimodal tasks and datasets, consistently outperforming existing state-of-the-art methods.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison on Multimodal Tasks

- **Superior Performance:** ARL achieves the best performance across all tested datasets (CREMA-D, AVE, Kinetics-Sounds, MOSI, UCF-101) for tasks like emotion recognition, audio-visual event localization, and sentiment analysis.
 - On CREMA-D, ARL shows a significant improvement of 3.02% (macro F1) over the second-best method.
 - On AVE, it improves by 2.28%, and on Kinetics-Sounds by 3.01%.
- **Addressing Limitations of Balanced Methods:** Crucially, while existing balance-based methods (OGM-GE, AGM, PMR) sometimes improve performance, they can also *decrease* it on certain datasets (e.g., AVE), suggesting their approach can hinder dominant modalities. ARL, by contrast, consistently improves performance, including on AVE (5.75% improvement over concatenation baseline), validating its principle of optimizing for variance-weighted contributions rather than simple balance.

#### 4.2. Generalization Capabilities

- **More-than-Two Modalities:** ARL effectively handles scenarios with more than two modalities, demonstrated on the MOSI dataset (audio, visual, textual), where it again achieves superior performance compared to extended versions of other methods. This highlights its flexibility beyond typical two-modality settings.
- **Large Datasets:** On the more complex UCF-101 dataset with 101 action categories, ARL maintains its leading performance, verifying its generalizability to larger-scale and more challenging scenarios.
- **Different Architectures:** ARL's plug-in nature is validated by its ability to significantly boost performance when integrated with different multimodal architectures, including CNN-based (MMTM) and Transformer-based (mmFormer) models, even when these models employ intermediate fusion methods.
- **Larger Backbone Networks:** When evaluated with a larger backbone network like Swin-Base, ARL continues to provide performance gains, demonstrating its scalability and effectiveness with more powerful feature extractors.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

The ablation study on CREMA-D confirms the contribution of each component within ARL:
- **Unimodal Bias Regularization (UR):** Significantly improves performance, confirming its role in reducing decision bias.
- **Asymmetric Learning (AL):** Further boosts performance, validating its role in reducing decision variance by aligning optimization dependency with variance ratios.
- **Gradient Residual (GR):** Contributes to performance by ensuring that the gradients for suppressed modalities do not become too small, preventing training stagnation.
The cumulative effect of all components leads to the highest performance, underscoring their synergistic design.

#### 4.4. Visualization of Mechanism

A visualization on the AVE dataset provides intuitive evidence for ARL's operation. It shows that in the early training stages, the optimization dependency often diverges from the ideal inverse modality variance ratio. ARL dynamically adjusts modulation coefficients to bridge this gap, accelerating the optimization of the audio modality (m0) when its dependency is lower than desired. Importantly, the visualization reveals that the optimal ratio of optimization dependency is not 1 (balanced) but approximately 3, directly supporting the paper's central argument for imbalanced, variance-aware learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work offers both significant theoretical advancements and practical implications for the field of multimodal machine learning.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Significance

The most profound contribution is the theoretical refutation of the widely held belief that "balanced learning" is optimal for multimodal models. By leveraging bias-variance decomposition, the authors rigorously demonstrate that optimal performance is achieved when modality contributions are *imbalanced*, specifically inversely proportional to their prediction variances. This provides a fresh, statistically grounded perspective on the "under-optimized problem," shifting the focus from simply rebalancing modalities to strategically weighting them based on their inherent certainty or uncertainty. This new theoretical framework has the potential to guide future research directions, moving beyond ad-hoc balancing techniques to more principled optimization strategies.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact

The proposed Asymmetric Representation Learning (ARL) strategy translates this theoretical insight into a highly effective and versatile algorithm:
- **Performance Boost:** ARL consistently achieves state-of-the-art results across various multimodal tasks, datasets, and complex scenarios, demonstrating its direct utility in improving current multimodal models.
- **Versatility and Generalizability:** Its parameter-free nature, independence from specific model architectures (CNN, Transformer), fusion methods, and ability to handle multiple modalities make it a "plug-and-play" solution. This means researchers and practitioners can readily integrate ARL into existing or new multimodal systems without extensive modifications, significantly lowering the barrier to adoption.
- **Efficiency:** The method introduces no extra parameters, making it computationally efficient and avoiding the increased model complexity often associated with other improvement techniques.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Work

The authors acknowledge a key limitation in the current method: the estimation of modality variance using self-information entropy, while computationally efficient, may not be perfectly accurate as it relies on a single inference. They suggest that more sophisticated uncertainty estimation methods could lead to even more precise modulation coefficients. However, they also highlight the challenge of balancing this increased accuracy with computational complexity. This area presents a clear avenue for future research, where exploring advanced Bayesian methods or ensemble-based uncertainty quantification could further refine the ARL strategy.

Overall, this paper makes a critical contribution by challenging a fundamental assumption in multimodal learning and providing a robust, theoretically-backed solution. Its findings are likely to influence how researchers approach the design and optimization of multimodal models, potentially leading to more robust and higher-performing AI systems that leverage complex real-world data more effectively.
