# SAIL: Test-Time Scaling for In-Context Imitation Learning with VLM

> [!summary] Summary
> SAIL reframes in-context robot imitation as trajectory-level test-time search. Monte Carlo Tree Search treats each node as a complete end-effector trajectory and each edge as a refinement, guided by LPIPS retrieval from a growing archive of successful rollouts and a vision-language scorer supplying both node values and step-level progress annotations. Average success rises from 25% one-shot to 73%, reaching 95% on the hardest handover task.

## Problem

- One-shot VLM trajectory prediction caps success at the initial guess, and small pose or localization errors cascade with no external evaluation loop to correct them.
- Prior fixes such as hindsight trajectory sketches and demonstration retrieval only improve the quality of a single prediction, never systematically exploring the continuous motion space.
- Existing LLM search and validator loops scale test-time compute at the symbolic task-planning level, and binary success signals are too sparse to guide continuous refinement.

## Method

- ==Trajectory-level MCTS== where each node is a full end-effector trajectory (position, quaternion, gripper) and each edge a refinement, selected by ==prior-weighted PUCB== with branching factor 3.
- **Archive Retrieval** keeps a shared set of (initial image, successful trajectory) pairs seeded from demos and grown on every success, picking in-context references by ==LPIPS== nearest neighbors.
- **VLM Trajectory Scoring** decomposes the task into ordered subtasks from one demo and averages per-frame completion over 50 frames; those scores align to waypoints as step-level feedback.

## Results

- Across six ALOHA simulation tasks at 20 seeds each, average success grows from **25%** at one rollout to **73%** at 45 nodes, with HandOverBanana at **95%**.
- At a fixed 15-node budget MCTS reaches **65%** versus **51%** breadth-first and **37%** depth-first; similarity retrieval beats fixed **45%** and random **50%** baselines.
- Step-level feedback scores **65%** against **45-48%** for unscored dense history and **49%** for a final scalar; real SO-101 trials hit **5/6** for both search and distilled policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Relevance beats quantity in in-context demos: one visually similar retrieved trajectory outperforms three fixed or randomly drawn ones under the same context budget.
> - Refinement feedback must be score-aligned with the evaluator's own objective; raw rollout frames, action text, or a single final scalar fail to localize where trajectories break.
> - Test-time search doubles as an automated data engine: distilling its rollouts into a behavior-cloned policy preserves success while cutting execution time roughly ninefold.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot has learned a manipulation from a few demonstrations. It then faces a new object arrangement. In this setting, small errors in state estimation or object location can derail a motion, and a one-shot prediction has no evaluation loop to correct it.

SAIL addresses this by letting the robot try, inspect, and revise whole motions before carrying one out on the physical robot. It searches among complete candidate trajectories in simulation, scores how far each gets, and uses the score to improve later attempts. Across the simulated tasks, success rises as the search budget grows.

Success rates, shown as fractions over 20 simulated seeds per task, for single rollout, breadth-search, depth-search, and SAIL at 6, 15, 30, and 45 nodes. At 15 nodes, SAIL's average is highest, but breadth-search is higher on LC (.60 vs .50).

### 2. Searching complete motions

SAIL uses Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), which chooses which candidate to explore next. It balances promising branches against less-tested ones. In SAIL, each node is a complete trajectory, and an edge represents a proposed refinement. A policy VLM proposes a trajectory. An inverse-kinematics controller executes its waypoints in simulation. A scoring VLM then evaluates the resulting rollout. The search returns the best trajectory found, or it stops early if it finds a successful one.

Feedback enters the search in three steps. First, a shared archive starts with successful demonstrations and adds successful trajectories found during search. For each new scene, SAIL retrieves visually similar examples using LPIPS image distance. Second, the scoring VLM breaks one demonstration into ordered subtasks. It estimates completion at uniformly sampled frames, 50 per rollout in the experiments, and the system averages that progress into a scalar node score. Third, SAIL aligns those sampled scores to waypoints. The policy is prompted to preserve high-scoring trajectory segments and modify low-scoring ones.

This VLM progress score is a search signal. It is not the simulator's final ground-truth success measure.

Left: multiple initial scenes feed a trajectory-search tree, a selected trajectory, and an archive of successful demonstrations. Right: a demonstration yields a subtask list. Sampled rollout frames are scored for progress, and the progress feeds a trajectory score. The scores are then aligned with waypoints and supplied to the next refinement. The example values are illustrative labels in the schematic.

### 3. What the comparisons show

The evaluation covers six simulated tasks. For each task, the success rate is the percentage of 20 evaluation seeds where a successful trajectory is verified by the simulator's ground-truth function. Averaged across the six task rates, success rises from 25% with one rollout to 73% with a 45-node SAIL search. These results come from increasing MCTS-node budgets. They are not evidence of fewer generated tokens, fewer attempts, or shorter elapsed time.

**Search strategy.** At 15 nodes, average success is 65% for SAIL, 51% for breadth-search, and 37% for depth-search. Breadth-search expands candidates in parallel without a feedback loop, while depth-search refines one candidate at a time. SAIL is best on average but not on every task. Breadth-search beats it on LaptopClose, which the authors associate with that task's fine positional demands.

**Feedback.** Under a fixed 15-node budget, SAIL's step-level scores give a 65% average. Trajectory-only, image-only, and image-plus-trajectory histories give 45–48%, and final-score-only feedback averages 49%. These comparisons support the value of score-aligned feedback under this setup. They do not show that a VLM pinpoints the true causal location of every failure.

### 4. A bounded step toward deployment

The authors also tested SAIL on a physical robot with BlockIntoBowl. The team reconstructed a trial-specific simulation from RGB-D observations and searched there with at most 15 nodes, stopping early on success. They then transferred the selected trajectory to a LeRobot SO-101 arm. MCTS refinement succeeded in 5 of 6 trials. The authors attribute the failure to pose-estimation error and unmodeled contact dynamics.

SAIL's established contribution is searching and refining complete trajectories, guided by VLM progress scores, rather than relying on one guess. Its main boundary is clear: the scaling evidence is simulated, and the physical check covers one task using trial-specific digital twins. That physical result does not establish broad real-world generalization.
