# Validate the Dream Before You Trust Its Verdict: Admissibility for World-Model Simulators

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces a principled framework, an "admissibility ladder," for evaluating the trustworthiness of generative World Models (WMs) when used as closed-loop simulators for policy evaluation. Empirical results show that visual generation quality does not guarantee a WM's action-robustness, highlighting the necessity of structured accreditation for reliable policy testing.

## Problem

- Generative World Models (WMs) are increasingly used as test oracles for robotics, including safety-critical systems, but lack principled criteria for trusting their policy evaluation verdicts.
- Existing WM evaluation metrics primarily focus on visual fidelity, which does not ensure physical plausibility or correct responses to an agent's actions, particularly for off-policy scenarios.
- Traditional safety-critical simulator accreditation frameworks, designed for physics-based models, are not applicable to learned generative WMs, which synthesize novel futures without a direct ground truth for validation.

## Method

- A five-level **"admissibility ladder"** (L0-L4) is proposed, adapting principles from classical ==Verification, Validation & Accreditation (VV&A)== and ==Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF)== to generative World Models (WMs).
- The ladder defines progressive levels of trustworthiness, from basic generation quality (L0) to ==action-robustness== (L1), declared operating limits (L2), failure attribution (L3), and empirical ==verdict-transfer validation== (L4).
- The lower rungs of the ladder are empirically instantiated on two autonomous driving WMs to demonstrate the framework's operationalization and its ability to differentiate models based on ==action-conditioned fidelity==.

## Results

- Empirical evaluation of Vista and Epona, two autonomous driving WMs, showed a reversal in ranking: Vista achieved better visual fidelity (L0), while Epona demonstrated superior action-robustness (L1) across all metrics.
- Epona exhibited an Instruction-Execution Consistency (IEC) of **0.54** and an admissible action-following horizon of **3.2 seconds**, significantly outperforming Vista's IEC of **0.33** and **1.6-second** horizon.
- The observed decoupling empirically confirms that visual generation quality is insufficient to accredit a generative WM as a trustworthy test oracle for closed-loop policy evaluation, validating the need for the proposed ladder.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - There is a "trust inversion" where learned generative WMs, unlike trusted physics-based simulators, become the unverified components when providing verdicts on policies.
> - High visual generation quality alone does not predict a WM's action-robustness or its ability to correctly respond to a policy's actions, demonstrating a critical decoupling between these properties.
> - The "action-coverage gap" means WM verdicts are unreliable for actions outside their training distribution, necessitating explicit declaration of operating limits and out-of-distribution detection for trusted use.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of World Models (WMs) into robotics and autonomous systems development represents a significant shift, particularly in the evaluation of action policies. WMs, as generative models capable of learning environment dynamics, predict how a world responds to an agent's actions, increasingly serving as closed-loop simulators. This allows for the evaluation of policy consequences in an imagined world, yielding verdicts on success or safety. This practice is evident across various robotic embodiments, including manipulation tasks, legged robot planning, and autonomous driving (AD) systems, where WMs act as test oracles.

However, the proliferation of WMs as evaluative tools has outpaced the establishment of principled criteria for their trustworthiness. Traditional simulation-based safety validation relies on trusted, physics-based simulators that are engineered and empirically validated against real-world physics or data. These classical simulators produce verdicts about policies under test, with a measurable "sim-to-real" gap. In contrast, generative WMs are *learned artifacts*; they synthesize novel futures from data without an explicit ground-truth physics model against which to validate their internal dynamics. This creates a "trust inversion" where the simulator itself, rather than solely the policy, becomes the unverified component.

Existing evaluation metrics for generative WMs primarily focus on visual fidelity and distributional realism, such as Fréchet Video Distance (FVD). Research has increasingly demonstrated that high visual quality does not necessarily translate to accurate physical plausibility or correct reactions to an agent's actions, particularly for actions outside the training distribution. This disconnect, termed the "action-coverage gap" or "off-policy evaluation problem," means that a visually convincing WM may still render incorrect judgments on a policy's performance.

Furthermore, established methodologies for accrediting safety-critical simulators—including Verification, Validation & Accreditation (VV&A), Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF), and scenario-based testing standards—were developed for classical, physics-based simulators. These methods fundamentally presuppose an independent ground truth for validation and have not been adapted to address the unique challenges posed by learned generative WMs. Consequently, there remains a critical absence of principled criteria to determine when the verdicts produced by generative WMs can be reliably trusted as assurance evidence for safety-critical applications.

This paper directly addresses this gap by proposing an "admissibility ladder." It aims to adapt and extend existing certification frameworks to provide a structured, evidence-based approach for evaluating and accrediting generative WMs, thereby enabling their safe and verifiable use as test oracles. The work distinguishes itself from other related research that might focus on deterministic WMs or physical grounding without specifying comprehensive admissibility criteria for generative models used in closed-loop policy evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a principled framework for assessing the trustworthiness and admissibility of verdicts generated by World Models (WMs) when used as closed-loop simulators for evaluating action policies. This framework is specifically designed for generative WMs, which, unlike classical physics-based simulators, are learned artifacts without inherent, verifiable physics.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations:

1. **Unverified Nature of Generative WMs**: World Models are increasingly deployed as test oracles in robotics, including safety-critical domains like autonomous driving (AD). They predict the consequences of actions in an imagined world and return verdicts on policy success or safety. However, these WMs are themselves learned from data, making them unverified components. This contrasts with traditional simulation, where the simulator is typically trusted due to its engineering and validation against physical principles. The paper identifies this as a "trust inversion," where the reliability of the simulator itself is in question.

2. **Limitations of Existing Fidelity Metrics**: Current metrics for evaluating generative WMs, such as Fréchet Video Distance (FVD), primarily assess visual and temporal realism. These metrics focus on the plausibility of generated observations but do not account for the world's *correct* response to a policy's actions, especially those actions not well-represented in the training data. A model can generate visually convincing footage yet still provide an incorrect assessment of an action's outcome, leading to potentially unsafe policy development. This highlights a critical disconnect between perceptual quality and functional validity for policy evaluation.

3. **The Action-Coverage Gap**: Generative WMs are often trained on logged interaction data collected by a "behavior policy" (e.g., a human driver). Consequently, the model's dynamics are most reliable for action-observation pairs seen during training. When a policy under test explores actions outside this training distribution—actions rarely or never taken by the behavior policy—the WM's predictions are subject to extrapolation error. This "off-policy evaluation problem," termed the "action-coverage gap," renders WM verdicts unreliable in crucial untested regimes.

4. **Inapplicability of Traditional Validation Frameworks**: Established methodologies for certifying safety-critical simulators, such as Verification, Validation & Accreditation (VV&A), Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF), and scenario-based testing standards, were developed for physics-based simulators. These frameworks presuppose an independent ground truth against which the simulator can be validated. They lack the mechanisms to accredit a generative WM, which by nature synthesizes novel futures for which no direct real-world recording exists for validation. This leaves no principled criterion for when a generative WM's verdicts can be legally or ethically trusted as assurance evidence.

To address these motivations, the paper sets forth four main contributions:

- **Characterize Trust Inversion**: Precisely define why generative WMs are unverified artifacts, unlike classical simulators, and explain why existing simulator-validation methods are insufficient for their accreditation.
- **Formalize Action-Coverage Gap**: Define the action-coverage gap as an off-policy evaluation problem where WM verdicts become unsupported due to actions outside the training behavior policy's distribution.
- **Construct Admissibility Standard**: Develop a five-level "admissibility ladder" (L0–L4) by adapting principles from VV&A, SOTIF, and scenario-based testing to the generative setting. This standard specifies the evidence a WM must provide to have its closed-loop verdicts accepted as assurance evidence.
- **Instantiate and Demonstrate Decoupling**: Empirically instantiate the lower rungs of the ladder on two autonomous driving WMs to demonstrate that visual generation quality and action-robustness are distinct and can decouple, confirming that visual fidelity alone does not predict action-robustness.

Ultimately, the goal is to provide a structured, auditable vocabulary for discussing and certifying the trustworthiness of generative WMs, thereby enabling their reliable use in the development and safety validation of robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this research involves adapting established credibility practices from safety-critical simulation and engineering to develop a novel admissibility standard for generative World Models (WMs). This standard is organized as a hierarchical "admissibility ladder" (L0–L4), which a WM must ascend to for its closed-loop verdicts to be considered assurance evidence.

**A. Adaptation of Existing Certification Frameworks:**

The paper adapts two fundamental principles from classical accreditation procedures to the generative WM context:

1. **Fidelity-Sufficiency**: Traditional VV&A certifies a simulator based on whether its fidelity is *sufficient* for a *declared intended use*, rather than maximizing realism. For policy evaluation with generative WMs, this principle is reinterpreted. The certification object shifts from general visual realism to *action-conditioned fidelity*: whether the imagined world evolves realistically in response to the *specific actions* selected by the policy under test. Since a generative WM does not have a single real-world reference for validation, sufficiency must be established through a sequence of progressively stronger, verifiable claims, as defined by the ladder's rungs.

2. **Explicit Declaration of Operating Limits**: SOTIF and scenario-based testing standards require systems to declare the conditions under which their behavior is claimed. For generative WMs, this principle is adapted to the *statistical boundary* of the WM's *training envelope* (the distribution of conditions it was exposed to during training). The framework requires the WM to explicitly declare this envelope, detect departures from it, and refuse inputs that fall outside its boundaries, treating such violations as recognized sources of risk.

**B. The Admissibility Ladder (L0–L4):**

The proposed ladder consists of five levels, each representing a progressively stronger claim of trustworthiness and requiring specific evidence:

- **L0: Generation Quality**: This baseline level assesses the WM solely on visual and temporal fidelity, typically using metrics like Fréchet Video Distance (FVD), Content-Debiased FVD (CD-FVD), and Fréchet Trajectory Distance (FTD). It serves as a minimum-viability gate; a model failing here cannot host a policy test. However, passing L0 alone does not support any claim about the world's response to actions.

- **L1: Action-Robust**: At this level, the imagined world must demonstrate systematic responsiveness to the policy's actions, meaning different actions produce correspondingly different rollouts. This ensures the simulator is not merely replaying a pre-determined future. Instruments include action-controllability benchmarks that measure Instruction-Execution Consistency (IEC), Average Displacement Error (ADE), Dynamic Time Warping (DTW), and Success Rate (SR) between commanded and executed actions/trajectories. L1 certifies differentiation but not real-world validity.

- **L2: Envelope-Declared**: This level introduces the requirement for validity within a *declared training envelope* and *rollout horizon*. Within this bounded region, the simulator's reactions must be verifiable against real-world behavior or physical dynamics. Critically, the WM must declare its training envelope, detect out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs, and refuse to provide verdicts for inputs outside this declared region. Mechanisms for OOD detection include conformal prediction and ensemble-rollout disagreement. The rollout horizon specifies the time duration over which the WM's action-following remains accurate.

- **L3: Failure-Attributable**: At this stage, the framework requires that failures occurring at or beyond the declared envelope boundary be detectable and attributable. The system must distinguish between failures caused by the simulator itself and those attributable to the policy under test. This necessitates a set of OOD failure signatures and an attribution protocol, calibrated against real-world failure data (e.g., field disengagement records). Verdicts outside the envelope remain inadmissible but are transparently explained.

- **L4: Verdict-Transfer-Validated**: The highest level, L4, demands empirical evidence of a correlation between in-simulation and real-world outcomes *within the declared envelope*. This demonstrates that "good-in-sim" predicts "good-in-reality," allowing the WM's verdicts to count as assurance evidence. Outside the envelope, L3's attribution capabilities ensure transparency. The required evidence is a measured real-to-simulation policy-performance correlation.

**C. Empirical Instantiation (Lower Rungs):**

To demonstrate the operationalization of the ladder, the paper instantiates the lower rungs (L0, L1, and the horizon component of L2) on two open-weight, action-conditioned generative WMs for autonomous driving: Vista (2024) and Epona (2025).

- **Data and Tools**: The evaluation utilizes the nuScenes-based ACT-Bench split, which provides action templates and a pre-trained ACT-Estimator for inferring executed maneuvers and trajectories.
- **L0 Measurement**: FVD, CD-FVD, and FTD are computed by comparing generated rollouts against held-out real nuScenes clips.
- **L1 Measurement**: For each commanded action template, a rollout is generated, and the ACT-Estimator recovers the executed maneuver and trajectory. Metrics (IEC, ADE, DTW, SR) quantify the consistency between commanded and executed actions. The pipeline is validated against published scores for Vista.
- **L2 (Horizon) Measurement**: The ADE is computed for varying rollout horizons (h). The "admissible horizon" (h*) is defined as the maximum `h` for which the mean ADE remains within a threshold (1.8 meters, half a US lane width).

The approach is designed to be embodiment-agnostic, with AD serving as the initial rich domain for instantiation due to its mature assurance machinery. The empirical section focuses on illustrating the ladder's functionality and its ability to discriminate between models, rather than providing a comprehensive benchmark.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical instantiation of the lower rungs of the admissibility ladder (L0, L1, and the horizon component of L2) on two autonomous driving World Models, Vista (2024) and Epona (2025), yielded several key findings:

**1. L0: Generation Quality**
Both Vista and Epona successfully cleared the L0 threshold, indicating their ability to generate visually and temporally coherent videos. However, even at this foundational level, a distinction emerged:
- **Vista** achieved better scores on pixel- and motion-level fidelity metrics (FVD of 151.3 and CD-FVD of 51.6).
- **Epona** demonstrated a better trajectory-distribution score (FTD of 2.59).
This early divergence suggested that even within L0, different aspects of fidelity (visual vs. trajectory distribution) do not necessarily correlate perfectly, hinting at the broader decoupling observed at higher levels.

**2. L1: Action-Robustness**
Both models were found to be action-robust, clearing L1 by demonstrating that their Instruction-Execution Consistency (IEC) was significantly above the chance level of 1/8. This indicated that the generated rollouts systematically responded to commanded actions rather than ignoring them. However, Epona exhibited substantially higher action-robustness compared to Vista:
- **Epona** achieved an IEC of 0.54, an Average Displacement Error (ADE) of 2.35m, a Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) score of 41.5, and a Success Rate of 0.28.
- **Vista** achieved a lower IEC of 0.33, a higher ADE of 4.56m, a DTW of 97.3, and a Success Rate of 0.08.
Epona consistently outperformed Vista across all L1 metrics, showing a more accurate and consistent response to commanded maneuvers. A per-category breakdown further revealed Epona's near-perfect IEC on curve and constant-speed categories, while both models struggled with start and stop transitions.

**3. L2: Action-Following Horizon**
The measurement of the action-following horizon (h*), representing the duration over which the average displacement error (ADE) remained within half a standard US lane width (1.8 meters), provided further differentiation:
- **Epona** demonstrated a significantly longer admissible rollout horizon of **3.2 seconds**, with an ADE drift rate of approximately 0.53 m/s.
- **Vista** had a shorter admissible horizon of **1.6 seconds**, with a higher ADE drift rate of about 1.06 m/s.
This indicates that Epona's action-following capabilities maintain accuracy over approximately twice the duration compared to Vista, thereby supporting action-following claims for a substantially longer window.

**4. Decoupling of Generation Quality and Action-Robustness**
The most critical finding was the empirical demonstration of a **reversal in ranking** between generation quality (L0) and action-robustness (L1-L2).
- **Vista** ranked higher on visual generation quality (FVD and CD-FVD at L0).
- **Epona** ranked higher on every action-robustness metric at L1 and sustained a longer action-following horizon at L2.
This observed decoupling directly supports the paper's central premise: a model's visual fidelity (L0) does not predict its action-following capabilities (L1-L2), which are essential for the validity of closed-loop verdicts. Therefore, visual generation quality alone is insufficient to accredit a generative WM as a trustworthy test oracle. This single clear divergence highlights the necessity of separating the ladder's rungs and evaluating beyond mere visual plausibility.

It is important to note that the empirical instantiation was limited to L0, L1, and the horizon component of L2. The higher levels (full L2, L3, and L4) were beyond the scope of this initial empirical step, requiring more complex mechanisms like out-of-distribution detection, real-world failure data, and explicit sim-to-real correlation measurements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotics and autonomous systems by addressing a critical, currently unaddressed challenge: the principled accreditation of generative World Models (WMs) used as closed-loop test oracles. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several dimensions:

**1. Establishing Trust and Accountability for Generative WMs:**
The most direct impact is providing a structured framework—the admissibility ladder (L0-L4)—to assess when the verdicts from generative WMs can be trusted as assurance evidence. As WMs are increasingly adopted in safety-critical applications like autonomous driving, a lack of such criteria poses substantial risks. This work moves beyond qualitative assessments or sole reliance on visual fidelity, offering a systematic, evidence-based process for establishing confidence in these learned systems. This is crucial for regulatory compliance and public acceptance of AI-driven autonomous technologies.

**2. Shifting the Paradigm of WM Evaluation:**
The paper critically re-evaluates the prevalent practice of using visual and distributional realism metrics (e.g., FVD) as primary indicators of WM quality for policy evaluation. By demonstrating the decoupling between visual fidelity (L0) and action-robustness (L1-L2), it highlights that a visually plausible world model does not necessarily mean a functionally correct one. This research advocates for a shift towards evaluating *action-conditioned fidelity* and the declared operating limits, which are directly relevant to closed-loop policy performance. This paradigm shift will guide future WM development and benchmarking efforts, focusing on properties critical for reliable policy testing.

**3. Adapting and Extending Established Safety-Critical Methodologies:**
By adapting principles from Verification, Validation & Accreditation (VV&A), Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF), and scenario-based testing, the work bridges the gap between traditional engineering safety practices and emerging AI technologies. This adaptation demonstrates how robust, long-standing methodologies can be extended to address the unique characteristics of learned, generative systems, providing a familiar and rigorous framework for assessment in a new domain.

**4. Guiding Development and Research Directions:**
The admissibility ladder serves as a prescriptive standard, offering clear guidance to WM developers on what evidence is required at each level of trustworthiness. This provides a roadmap for improving WM capabilities, encouraging focus on areas like action-conditioned prediction, out-of-distribution detection, and transparent failure attribution, rather than solely on generation quality. Furthermore, by explicitly identifying the challenges at higher levels (L2-L4), such as the need for embodiment-agnostic OOD detection protocols, calibrated failure datasets, and empirical sim-to-real correlation, the paper outlines concrete directions for future research.

**5. Enhancing Safety and Robustness of Autonomous Systems:**
Ultimately, the framework contributes to the safer development and validation of autonomous systems. By ensuring that WM verdicts are only trusted within their validated envelopes and with clear understanding of their limitations, developers can make more informed decisions about policy performance. This mitigates risks associated with deploying policies trained or validated in unreliable simulated environments, thereby enhancing the overall robustness and safety of autonomous agents.

In summary, this research provides a foundational framework for the responsible development and deployment of generative World Models in safety-critical domains. It transforms the implicit "trust" in WM verdicts into an explicit, auditable process, fostering greater accountability and ultimately contributing to the advancement of reliable and safe autonomous technologies.
