# CSAR: Containerized System Architecture for Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> CSAR is a containerized system architecture that provides an explicit multi-user operational model for shared robotics infrastructure, leveraging LXC/LXD containers to enable efficient resource utilization and simplified software integration. It demonstrates robust performance for distributed robotic applications across heterogeneous networks, supporting reproducible and collaborative experimentation in laboratory settings.

## Problem

- Modern robotics faces challenges in software development, integration, deployment, and operation due to increasing complexity, distributed infrastructures, and collaborative team environments.
- Existing solutions like ROS 2 and DDS lack explicit models for computation allocation and shared infrastructure management, while cloud-native orchestrators like Kubernetes often struggle with real-time robotic requirements.
- Robotics laboratories suffer from hardware underutilization (especially GPUs), reproducibility fragility, and dependency conflicts, lacking a unified multi-user operating model for shared infrastructure.

## Method

- **CSAR** introduces a ==three-layered architectural framework== (Infrastructure Core, Platform & Multi-User Orchestration, Compute & Acceleration) for managing robotics resources across edge infrastructure.
- It utilizes ==LXC/LXD system containers== to provide persistent, isolated, and hardware-affine execution environments for multiple users, offering near-native performance.
- The framework includes a ==topology-aware container networking== scheme explicitly designed for ROS 2 and DDS, facilitating communication across segmented domains and heterogeneous networks.

## Results

- CSAR was deployed in the MAPIR laboratory, supporting **250 registered users** and up to **35 concurrent containers**, demonstrating practical utility in a multi-user research setting.
- The system achieved improved utilization of shared computational resources, particularly GPUs, and simplified software integration by eliminating dependency conflicts across diverse user projects.
- It successfully sustained distributed robotic workloads, such as edge-offloaded 3D SLAM over 5G and distributed GPU-accelerated semantic mapping, validating its performance over heterogeneous networks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A clear layered architecture effectively separates stable infrastructure from dynamic research workloads, improving failure containment and system stability for multi-user environments.
> - LXC/LXD system containers offer a balance of strong isolation, direct hardware access (e.g., GPUs), and near-native performance, making them suitable for resource-intensive robotic applications.
> - Explicitly managing ROS 2/DDS communication through DDS Discovery Servers and Routers is essential for reliable distributed operations across segmented and heterogeneous network topologies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Modern robotic applications are increasingly complex, relying on distributed computational infrastructures that span embedded devices, edge servers, and cloud resources. This evolution, coupled with the inherently collaborative nature of robotics projects, introduces significant challenges in the development, integration, deployment, and long-term operation of robotic systems. Multi-user robotics software teams frequently encounter issues related to dependency isolation, software compatibility, reproducibility of experiments, efficient sharing of specialized hardware, and deployment across diverse, heterogeneous environments.

Existing robotic middleware, such as Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) and Data Distribution Service (DDS), provide standards for communication between nodes but do not specify how computation should be allocated to hardware or how shared infrastructure should be managed. While the robotics community has adopted DevOps-inspired practices like containerization (e.g., Docker), continuous integration, and automated testing, these primarily address the engineering of software artifacts. They typically do not define an explicit multi-user operational model for shared computational infrastructure, especially for embodied, time-sensitive systems interacting with the physical world. Cloud-native orchestration platforms like Kubernetes, while prevalent in enterprise IT, are noted for struggling with the real-time and mixed-criticality requirements of robotic software due to their network abstraction layers and conflicts with ROS 2's internal scheduling and DDS's multicast discovery mechanisms.

Within this landscape, the efficient sharing of specialized hardware, particularly GPUs crucial for high-bandwidth sensory processing and AI models, remains a challenge. Traditional setups often tie high-end GPUs to individual workstations, leading to underutilization. While some research explores transparent GPU sharing in container clouds, implementing dynamic GPU multiplexing in real-time robotic contexts often requires complex middleware or specialized scheduling.

This work positions CSAR as a framework designed to bridge these gaps. It offers a coherent operational model for shared robotics infrastructure, integrating software engineering practices with the management of the underlying computational and physical resources for embodied systems. By leveraging system containerization (LXC/LXD) over application containerization (Docker), CSAR aims to provide persistent, isolated workspaces with near-native performance, which is a key distinction from approaches focused solely on stateless microservices. It also addresses distributed robotics and edge-cloud architectures by defining an explicit network topology that integrates DDS routers, aiming to overcome the limitations of generic cloud networking models for real-time ROS 2/DDS communication.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing CSAR stems from the escalating complexity and distributed nature of contemporary robotic applications, alongside the collaborative working model of robotics research teams. The authors identify several structural tensions and persistent challenges in current robotics laboratories:
- **Infrastructure–Deployment Incoherence:** Robotic applications are often developed in isolated personal environments but must later be integrated and executed on shared, heterogeneous infrastructure, leading to integration difficulties.
- **Hardware Underutilization:** Expensive computational resources, particularly GPUs, frequently remain idle or are tightly coupled to specific users, resulting in inefficient use of capital.
- **Reproducibility Fragility:** Software environments are implicitly linked to personal configurations, hindering long-term maintenance, reliable reproduction of experiments, and seamless integration of code from different team members.
- **Dependency and Version Friction:** Heterogeneous software requirements across different projects and users lead to fragile integrations and recurrent compatibility conflicts, consuming significant development time.
- **Absence of a Unified Multi-User Operating Model:** Robotics laboratories often lack an explicit architectural framework that unifies software development, integration, deployment, and execution under consistent principles, making system management ad hoc.
- **Limitations of Existing Tools:** While ROS 2 and DDS standardize communication, they do not define where computation runs or how it binds to hardware. DevOps practices, primarily conceived for cloud services, do not fully address the specific requirements of embodied, time-sensitive robotic systems interacting with the physical world.

In response to these motivations, the key objectives of CSAR are:
- To introduce **CSAR (Containerized System Architecture for Robotics)**, a container-centric architectural framework specifically tailored for robotics teams and the edge–cloud continuum.
- To provide an **explicit multi-user operating model** for shared robotics infrastructure that restores coherence between robotic system design principles and the practical infrastructure on which these systems are developed and executed. This involves treating containers as persistent, hardware-affine execution environments rather than ephemeral artifacts, and explicitly considering placement, hardware affinity, and network topology.
- To offer a **layered architectural decomposition** (Infrastructure Core, Platform & Multi-User Orchestration, Compute & Acceleration) that clearly separates long-lived system stability concerns from the dynamic and volatile nature of experimental research workloads, facilitating failure containment and reproducibility.
- To implement a **topology-aware container networking scheme** that is aligned with ROS 2 and DDS semantics, ensuring broadcast domains, discovery mechanisms, and cross-host communication are explicitly structured and managed.
- Ultimately, the framework aims to **simplify software integration**, **improve the utilization of shared computational resources**, and **facilitate safe prototyping**, as well as **reproducible and collaborative experimentation** within robotics teams.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CSAR is implemented as a three-layered architectural framework designed to manage computational resources and robotic workloads across an edge infrastructure, primarily utilizing Linux system containers (LXC/LXD) and ROS 2/DDS communication.

**Architectural Layers:**
1. **Layer 0 — Infrastructure Core:** This foundational layer provides the stable substrate for the entire system. It manages physical hardware resources, including CPUs, GPUs, and network interfaces, exposing them to upper layers. Key responsibilities include:
 - **Compute & Kernel Management:** Host Linux operating systems and kernel services.
 - **Storage & Data Persistence:** ZFS pools for resilient storage, segregating high-speed NVMe drives for active container execution from high-capacity SATA drives for backups and large datasets.
 - **Networking:** Core L2/L3 switching, VPN/WAN gateways, and ZeroTier-based secure network overlays (SNO) for encrypted tunnels to remote physical robots or researchers.
 - **Foundational Services:** Time synchronization (NTP), logging, monitoring, and DNS/naming services.
 This layer is designed for longevity and predictability, with human intervention limited to administration.
2. **Layer 1 — Platform & Multi-User Orchestration:** Built upon the Infrastructure Core, this layer constitutes the execution fabric for containerized environments. It employs LXC/LXD system containers, which encapsulate complete Linux distributions while sharing the host kernel, aiming for near-native performance without traditional hypervisor overhead. Its functions include:
 - **Cluster & Host Management:** LXD host cluster for container scheduling and inventory.
 - **Multi-User Tenant Isolation:** Provisioning independent, persistent LXC/LXD system containers for each user, allowing administrative privileges and customized software environments (Linux distributions, library versions, dependencies). Firewall policies enforce isolation.
 - **ROS 2/DDS-Aware Connectivity:** Isolated L2 network segments per user prevent interference. Cross-domain or cross-site ROS 2 communication is facilitated by explicit DDS Discovery Servers and DDS Routers, bridging selected ROS 2 interfaces across segmented domains. This approach manages multicast discovery challenges in complex network topologies.
3. **Layer 2 — Compute & Acceleration:** This is the dynamic, user-facing layer where robotic workloads are executed. It is designed to be disposable and volatile, allowing researchers to run complex robotic pipelines with direct hardware access.
 - **Dynamic Robotics Workloads:** Hosts ROS 2 nodes, AI inferences, perception pipelines, real-time control nodes, localization and SLAM, planning, and task-level coordination.
 - **GPU Acceleration & Near-Native Execution:** Leverages LXC profiles to grant containers direct, low-latency passthrough access to host GPUs, enabling CUDA-based perception and deep learning tasks with minimal virtualization overhead.
 - **Robotics Semantic Subsystems:** Containers are envisioned as functional components that can cooperate across distributed deployments, with consistent communication managed by Layer 1's ROS 2/DDS integration.
 - **Auxiliary Services & Tools:** Supports experiment runners, evaluation scripts, and batch jobs.

**Cross-Layer Interaction and Failure Containment:** The architecture promotes a clear bottom-up flow of capabilities: Layer 0 exposes raw resources, Layer 1 organizes them into isolated domains, and Layer 2 consumes these domains for workloads. This separation aims to contain failures, ensuring that issues within a Layer 2 container (e.g., dependency conflicts, crashes) do not affect the stability of the underlying infrastructure or other users.

**Deployment in MAPIR Laboratory:** The CSAR framework has been deployed in the Machine Perception and Intelligent Robotics (MAPIR) laboratory at the University of Málaga.
- **Physical Infrastructure:** Two servers, "edge" and "uedge," form a heterogeneous edge-computing cluster. "Edge" is a general-purpose node (Intel Core i7, 128 GB RAM, NVIDIA RTX 3060 Ti, TITAN X GPUs, NVMe/SATA storage, 2.5 Gb Ethernet). "Uedge" is a high-end server for intensive workloads (AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO, 512 GB RAM, three NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPUs, NVMe/SATA storage, 10 Gb Ethernet). A MikroTik router manages hybrid networking (Ethernet, Wi-Fi 6).
- **Multi-User Operation:** Supports 250 registered users, with 15-35 concurrent containers observed. Each user is assigned a dedicated IPv4 subnet and containers are registered in an internal DNS using FQDNs.
- **Monitoring and Observability:** A Prometheus-based monitoring stack with Grafana dashboards provides real-time visibility into host and container-level resource utilization (CPU, memory, GPU, network). A command-line interface simplifies operational tasks.

**System Demonstration via Use Cases:**
1. **Edge-Offloaded 3D SLAM over 5G:**
 - **Objective:** Demonstrate distributed SLAM across heterogeneous networks (5G to edge server).
 - **Setup:** A mobile robot dataset (from an AgileX Hunter 2.0 with LiDAR, RTK-GPS, cameras) is replayed from a remote laptop connected via 5G.
 - **Containers:** 1) Sensor playback on remote laptop, 2) FAST_LIO SLAM processing on `uedge` server, 3) RViz visualization on `uedge` server.
 - **Communication:** Secure overlay networking (ZeroTier) and DDS Discovery Server/DDS Router bridge the 5G connection and internal network.
2. **Distributed GPU-Accelerated Semantic Mapping:**
 - **Objective:** Illustrate execution of distributed ROS 2 applications with heterogeneous software/hardware requirements and GPU sharing.
 - **Setup:** Indoor 3D semantic mapping (localization, object detection, map fusion).
 - **Containers:** 1) Data acquisition and pose estimation on the robot (CPU-only), 2) Object detection using Detectron2 on an `edge` server (GPU-accelerated), 3) Semantic map management and visualization using Voxeland on an `edge` server (CPU/GPU).
 - **Communication:** Native ROS 2 topics and services managed by Layer 1.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The deployment of CSAR in the MAPIR laboratory and its evaluation through representative use cases yielded several findings regarding its capabilities and practical utility:

- **Architectural Coherence and Layered Separation:** The three-layered architecture (Infrastructure Core, Platform & Multi-User Orchestration, Compute & Acceleration) effectively separated stable infrastructure concerns from volatile research workloads. This organization facilitates failure containment, ensuring that errors in experimental software within Layer 2 containers do not compromise the stability of the core infrastructure or other users' ongoing work.
- **Simplified Software Integration and Dependency Management:** The use of LXC/LXD system containers for persistent, isolated execution environments allowed individual robotics team members to customize their software stacks, including Linux distributions, library versions, and middleware configurations (e.g., CUDA versions for GPU tasks). This approach demonstrably eliminated compatibility conflicts and dependency friction commonly encountered in shared development environments.
- **Improved Utilization of Shared Computational Resources:** CSAR enabled the controlled sharing of specialized hardware, particularly GPUs, among multiple users. Through LXC profiles, containers were granted direct, low-latency passthrough access to physical NVIDIA GPUs on host servers, facilitating the simultaneous execution of hardware-accelerated tasks (e.g., CUDA-based perception pipelines) with near bare-metal performance. This addressed the issue of hardware underutilization.
- **Facilitation of Safe Prototyping and Reproducible Experimentation:** The isolated nature of user containers, combined with the ability to take immediate snapshots, provided a stable framework for iterative algorithmic design and parameter tuning. Researchers could perform risky software changes, test new libraries (e.g., LLM dependencies, novel audio processing tools), or experiment with different ROS 2 distributions within their dedicated environments without impacting the host system or other users. This capability enhanced reproducibility and supported the long-term evolution of complex experiments.
- **Effective Distributed Workload Management over Heterogeneous Networks:**
 - **Use Case 1 (Edge-offloaded 3D SLAM over 5G):** Demonstrated that CSAR can sustain a distributed robotic workload where sensing, processing, and visualization are separated into distinct containers across physically remote locations connected by a 5G network. The secure overlay networking (ZeroTier) and DDS Discovery Server/DDS Router successfully managed ROS 2/DDS traffic across non-local links and segmented broadcast domains, enabling transparent communication between nodes operating under practical wide-area communication constraints.
 - **Use Case 2 (Distributed GPU-Accelerated Semantic Mapping):** Illustrated CSAR's ability to host distributed ROS 2 applications with diverse software and hardware requirements. The application, spanning CPU-only robot-side processing, GPU-accelerated object detection on an edge server, and semantic map management, operated cohesively. This validated the framework's capability to manage heterogeneous dependencies and allocate GPU resources efficiently for complex robotic tasks.
- **Active Laboratory Adoption:** The deployment in the MAPIR laboratory supports 250 registered users, with observed concurrency of 15-35 running containers during peak usage. This indicates that CSAR is not merely a conceptual framework but an actively utilized multi-user environment that supports daily research operations.
- **Enhanced Operational Awareness:** The integrated monitoring framework (Prometheus and Grafana) provided real-time visibility into system resources and container workloads, which is important for diagnosing performance issues in robotic systems where CPU load, GPU memory, and network latency are critical factors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CSAR architectural framework addresses a fundamental set of challenges in modern robotics development and research, particularly within multi-user, resource-constrained environments like academic laboratories. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Addressing the Infrastructure Gap in Robotics:** CSAR provides a structured and explicit multi-user operating model for robotics infrastructure, a gap often filled by ad hoc solutions. It bridges the divide between theoretical robotic system design and the practical realities of managing heterogeneous computational resources for embodied, time-sensitive applications. This can lead to more robust and scalable robotic deployments.
- **Fostering Collaboration and Efficiency:** By offering isolated, persistent, and hardware-affine execution environments, CSAR enables multiple researchers to work concurrently on diverse projects without encountering dependency conflicts or system instability. This promotes a more collaborative research environment and significantly improves the utilization rate of expensive specialized hardware, such as GPUs, in research laboratories and development teams.
- **Enhancing Reproducibility and Scientific Rigor:** The containerization approach, combined with the ability to snapshot and restore environments, directly contributes to the reproducibility of robotic experiments. This is crucial for validating research findings, facilitating long-term maintenance of software stacks, and ensuring the integrity of scientific work, a persistent challenge in complex software-driven fields.
- **Simplifying Development and Deployment:** The framework simplifies the process of integrating complex software stacks and deploying distributed robotic applications across the edge-cloud continuum. By abstracting away much of the underlying infrastructure complexity and providing explicit mechanisms for network topology and resource allocation, researchers can focus more on algorithmic development rather than system administration.
- **Enabling Advanced Robotic Applications:** CSAR's ability to seamlessly integrate high-performance computing (e.g., GPU acceleration for AI and perception) with real-time robotic control and wide-area communication is critical for the development of next-generation robotic systems. This includes applications leveraging large language models, advanced semantic mapping, and distributed multi-robot coordination.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays the groundwork for future advancements in distributed robotics, specifically by proposing extensions into policy-governed federation of ROS 2 domains and the creation of reproducible data planes for robotics artifacts. These future directions aim to further enhance scalability, robustness, and data management in complex robotic ecosystems.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The public availability of the CSAR implementation, including deployment templates, configuration files, and documentation, lowers the barrier to adoption for other institutions and development teams. This open-source approach has the potential to accelerate the dissemination and evolution of these best practices across the broader robotics community.
