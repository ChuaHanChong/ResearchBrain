# HGDiffuser: Efficient Task-Oriented Grasp Generation via Human-Guided Grasp Diffusion Models

> [!summary] Summary
> HGDiffuser, developed by researchers at Southern University of Science and Technology, presents a framework for efficiently generating 6-DoF parallel-jaw task-oriented grasps for robots by integrating human demonstration constraints directly into a diffusion model's sampling process. This approach achieves an 81.21% success rate in simulated task-oriented grasping, reducing inference time by 81.26% compared to prior two-stage methods.

## Problem

- Existing demonstration-based task-oriented grasping (TOG) methods use a two-stage process: extensive task-agnostic grasp sampling followed by filtering with human-derived constraints.
- This two-stage approach suffers from significant inefficiencies due to the vast 6-DoF grasp space requiring exhaustive sampling, leading to prolonged inference times.
- There is a risk of sampling failure if desired task-compliant grasps are sparsely represented or missed in the initial candidate pool.

## Method

- **HGDiffuser** develops a single-stage task-oriented grasp generation framework by directly integrating human-derived constraints into a diffusion model's guided sampling process.
- It utilizes a DiT (==Diffusion Transformer==) block-based architecture as the feature backbone for its Noise Conditional Score Network (NCSN), enhancing feature fusion of object point clouds, grasp representations, and noise steps.
- Explicit task-oriented constraints (hand-object contact regions and human wrist orientations) are incorporated via a non-network-based, differentiable loss function that guides the diffusion model during inference without requiring task-specific training data.

## Results

- HGDiffuser achieved an **81.21%** task-oriented grasping success rate in simulation, which is **9.74%** higher than the best two-stage baseline (RTAGrasp-MV's **71.47%**).
- The method significantly reduced inference time to **0.191** seconds for one grasp sample, representing an **81.26%** reduction compared to RTAGrasp-MV's **1.019** seconds.
- An ablation study demonstrated that DiT blocks improved task-agnostic grasp success by **9.3%** (from **71.35%** to **80.65%**) compared to an MLP-based backbone, while maintaining comparable inference speed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating task-oriented constraints directly into a diffusion model's sampling process, rather than as a post-filtering step, drastically improves efficiency and robustness for task-oriented grasp generation.
> - Diffusion Transformer (DiT) blocks effectively enhance feature fusion in diffusion models for complex geometric tasks, leading to higher quality grasp predictions compared to MLP-based backbones.
> - Using a differentiable, non-network-based loss function for guidance allows for flexible and interpretable integration of human demonstration constraints without needing large-scale, costly task-oriented training datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Task-oriented grasping (TOG) is a fundamental capability for robots performing manipulation tasks, where the grasp must not only be stable but also align with the subsequent task requirements. Humans naturally demonstrate task-oriented grasps, making human demonstrations a valuable source of information for robotic TOG. The research landscape for task-oriented grasp generation for parallel-jaw grippers can be broadly categorized into three approaches based on data type:

1. **Methods based on human demonstration data:** These methods leverage human grasp demonstrations (e.g., static images, videos) to infer robotic grasps. Early solutions employed direct mapping using manual rules or simple MLP networks. However, these often struggled with the diversity of human grasps and complex mapping relationships, frequently resulting in unstable grasps. More recent advancements in this category utilize a two-stage approach: first generating task-agnostic stable grasp candidates (e.g., using samplers like Contact-GraspNet), and then applying task-oriented constraints derived from human demonstrations (such as contact regions and wrist orientations) to filter these candidates. While this two-stage approach often achieves high-quality grasps, it is characterized by inefficiency due to the need for extensive sampling in the vast 6-Degree-of-Freedom (6-DoF) grasp space to ensure sufficient candidate diversity, and it carries a potential risk of sampling failure if the desired grasps are not adequately represented in the initial candidates.

2. **Methods based on manually annotated task-oriented grasp data:** This category relies on large-scale datasets specifically curated with manual annotations for TOG. While these methods, such as TaskGrasp and subsequent approaches like GraspGPT, show improved generalization through semantic knowledge and integration with large language/vision-language models, they are constrained by the high cost and labor intensity of manual annotation. Their generalization capabilities are typically limited to objects and tasks similar to those present in the training datasets.

3. **Methods based on large-scale internet data:** Recent studies explore zero-shot TOG generation by leveraging vision-language models (VLMs) pre-trained on massive web data. These approaches face limitations due to the absence of fine-grained object understanding data pertinent to TOG in their pre-training datasets and are often restricted to predicting task-related grasp regions rather than precise 6-DoF grasps. This generally leads to suboptimal performance for specific manipulation tasks.

This work, HGDiffuser, primarily fits within the first category, focusing on human demonstration-based methods. It addresses the identified limitations of current two-stage approaches—specifically, their inefficiency and potential for sampling failure. By integrating explicit task-oriented constraints directly into a diffusion-based generative model's sampling process, HGDiffuser aims to provide a single-stage, more efficient, and robust solution for transferring human task-oriented grasps to robotic parallel-jaw grippers, thereby improving upon the state of the art in demonstration-based TOG.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and effective framework for generating 6-DoF parallel-jaw task-oriented grasps for robotic systems, directly leveraging human grasp demonstrations.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and observations in the field of robotic manipulation:

1. **Cruciality of Task-Oriented Grasping:** Task-oriented grasping (TOG) is recognized as a critical initial step for robots to successfully perform complex manipulation tasks. A grasp that is merely stable but not aligned with the task can impede subsequent actions, potentially leading to task failure or inefficiency. For example, grasping a knife by its blade rather than its handle for a handover task is unsafe and impractical.

2. **Human Efficiency in Grasping:** Humans naturally perform task-oriented grasps, implicitly incorporating stability and task-specific constraints (e.g., avoiding collisions, facilitating subsequent actions). This inherent human capability provides a valuable source of information for training robotic systems.

3. **Limitations of Existing Demonstration-Based Methods:** Current state-of-the-art methods for transferring human task-oriented grasps to robots typically employ a two-stage process. The first stage involves extensive, task-agnostic sampling of stable grasp candidates across the vast 6-DoF space. The second stage filters these candidates by applying explicit task-oriented constraints derived from human demonstrations (such as contact regions and wrist orientations). While this approach can yield high-quality grasps, it suffers from significant inefficiencies. The requirement for exhaustive sampling to generate a sufficiently diverse set of candidates that can meet both stability and task-oriented requirements leads to prolonged inference times and a potential risk of failure if the desired task-compliant grasps are sparsely represented in the initial sampling space.

To address these motivations and limitations, the key objectives of HGDiffuser are:

- **Develop a Single-Stage Grasp Generation Framework:** The primary objective is to move beyond the inefficient two-stage paradigm by directly integrating task-oriented constraints into the grasp sampling process. This aims to eliminate the need for exhaustive task-agnostic sampling, leading to a more streamlined and efficient generation process.
- **Leverage Diffusion Models for Guided Sampling:** Utilize the inherent guided sampling mechanism of diffusion models to incorporate explicit task-oriented constraints (hand-object contact regions, wrist orientations) extracted from human demonstrations. This guidance mechanism should steer the generation process directly towards stable, task-compliant 6-DoF parallel-jaw grasps.
- **Enhance Grasp Quality with Advanced Architecture:** Improve the quality of generated grasps by incorporating Diffusion Transformer (DiT) blocks as the feature backbone of the diffusion model. This architectural choice is intended to leverage the attention mechanism of transformers for more effective feature fusion compared to traditional MLP or UNet-based backbones.
- **Improve Efficiency and Effectiveness:** Achieve a significant reduction in inference time while maintaining or improving the quality of task-oriented grasp generation, thereby enabling more practical and effective transfer of human grasping strategies to robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HGDiffuser framework is designed for generating 6-DoF parallel-jaw task-oriented grasps for robots by leveraging human grasp demonstrations. The system operates in two main phases: a transfer phase and an inference phase, with the core contribution being the HGDiffuser module responsible for transforming human grasps into robotic ones.

**Problem Formulation:**
Given a single-view RGB-D image of a human demonstration ($I_{demo}$) and the 3D mesh ($M_o$) of the target object ($o$), the goal is to reproduce the same task-oriented grasp on $o$ using a parallel-jaw robotic gripper, represented as $H \in SE(3)$.
In the transfer phase, vision foundation models estimate the object's point cloud ($X_o$) and the human hand's MANO parametric model ($X_h = \{\theta, \beta\}$). HGDiffuser then transforms this human grasp into the parallel-jaw gripper grasp $H$. During inference, $H$ is registered to the current object pose.

**Core Idea: Guided Diffusion for Conditional Grasp Generation**
HGDiffuser learns a conditional distribution $\rho(H | X_o, X_h)$. It achieves this by training a diffusion-based generative model on task-agnostic data to learn $\rho(H | X_o)$. Subsequently, during inference, it employs a guided sampling mechanism to integrate explicit task-oriented constraints $\rho(X_h | H)$ derived from human demonstrations, effectively sampling from the desired $\rho(H | X_o, X_h)$. This design enables task-oriented grasp generation without requiring large-scale task-oriented training data.

**A. DiT-based Diffusion Model for Task-Agnostic Grasp Generation ($\rho(H | X_o)$)**

1. **Training Procedure:**
 - The model employs Denoising Score Matching (DSM) to train a Noise Conditional Score Network (NCSN) $s_\theta(H, k, X_o)$. This NCSN is designed to estimate the score function $\nabla_H \log \rho_{\sigma_k}(H | X_o)$, where $k$ represents a noise scale level $\sigma_k$.
 - The training objective is to minimize a score matching loss function that quantifies the difference between the estimated score and the true score.

2. **Inference Procedure:**
 - Grasps are generated using annealed Langevin Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling.
 - Sampling starts with a noise sample $H_L$ from a standard normal distribution.
 - The process iteratively refines the grasp from $H_k$ to $H_{k-1}$ by adding a step proportional to the estimated score $s_\theta(H, k, X_o)$ and a Gaussian noise term. The step-dependent coefficient $\epsilon_k$ decreases as the noise level $k$ decreases.

3. **Network Architecture:**
 - The NCSN $s_\theta(H, k, X_o)$ takes the object point cloud ($X_o$), current noise step ($k$), and current grasp ($H_k$) as inputs, outputting the score $H_s \in SE(3)$.
 - **Object Point Cloud Encoding:** $X_o$ is encoded using VN-PointNet, an SO(3)-equivariant point cloud feature encoder, producing feature vector $f_o \in \mathbb{R}^d$.
 - **Grasp Encoding:** $H_k \in SE(3)$ is first mapped to a set of gripper points $X_g \in \mathbb{R}^{g \times 3}$. These gripper points are then processed by an MLP to generate the feature vector $f_g \in \mathbb{R}^{g \times d}$. This direct encoding of gripper points in Cartesian space facilitates integration with object point cloud features.
 - **Noise Step Encoding:** The noise step $k$ is encoded using transformer sinusoidal position embedding, resulting in feature vector $f_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$.
 - **DiT-based Feature Backbone:** To fuse the features $\{f_g, f_o, f_t\}$, HGDiffuser employs a backbone comprising $D$ Diffusion Transformer (DiT) blocks.
 - Each DiT block uses an attention mechanism.
 - The gripper points feature $f_g$ serves as the input tokens, treating each gripper point feature as an individual token, similar to image patches in image transformers.
 - The fused object point cloud feature $f_o$ and noise step feature $f_t$ are summed to form the condition feature input $f_c = f_t + f_o$. This condition feature is integrated into the block's adaptive layer normalization, which is considered an efficient and effective fusion method.

**B. Guidance-based Inference for Task-Oriented Grasp Generation ($\rho(H | X_o, X_h)$)**

1. **Inference Procedure:**
 - The problem is transformed into estimating the score function $\nabla_H \log \rho(H | X_o, X_h)$.
 - Using Bayes' theorem, this score function is decomposed into: $\nabla_H \log \rho(H | X_o) + \nabla_H \log \rho(X_h | H, X_o)$.
 - The first term is provided by the trained task-agnostic NCSN, $s_\theta(H, k, X_o)$.
 - The second term, $\nabla_H \log \rho(X_h | H, X_o)$, represents the guidance derived from human grasp demonstrations. Instead of a classifier, a non-network-based, differentiable loss function $L(X_h, H, X_o)$ is introduced. This loss evaluates the probability of a parallel-jaw grasp $H$ meeting task-oriented requirements based on human demonstration $X_h$. The lower the loss, the higher the probability.
 - The guided score function is expressed as: $\nabla_H \log \rho(H | X_o, X_h) = s_\theta(H, k, X_o) - \alpha\nabla_H L(X_h, H, X_o)$, where $\alpha$ is a scaling parameter.
 - The annealed Langevin MCMC update rule is modified to incorporate this guidance: $H_{k-1} = H_k + \epsilon_k [s_\theta(H, k, X_o) - \alpha\nabla_H L(X_h, H, X_o)] + \sqrt{2\epsilon}z$.

2. **Explicit Task-Oriented Constraint Extraction:**
 - Two key constraints are extracted from human grasp demonstrations ($X_h$) to compute the loss function $L$:
 - **Task-oriented grasp region constraint ($L_{region}$):** The contact points between the human hand ($X_h$) and the object ($X_o$) are computed to derive a center point $P_{region}$. $L_{region}$ is formulated as a RELU function of the Euclidean distance between the translational component of the robotic grasp $H$ (Trans(H)) and $P_{region}$, with a threshold $\tau_{region}$. It penalizes grasps whose centers are too far from the human contact region.
 - **Task-oriented grasp orientation constraint ($L_{direct}$):** The human wrist orientation ($D_{direct} \in SO(3)$) is computed from $X_h$. $L_{direct}$ is formulated as a RELU function of the geodesic distance between the rotational component of $H$ (Rot(H)) and $D_{direct}$, with an angular threshold $\tau_{direct}$. It encourages the gripper's orientation to align with the human wrist's orientation.
 - The final loss function is a weighted sum: $L = \beta L_{region} + L_{direct}$, where $\beta$ is a scaling parameter.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of HGDiffuser involved quantitative comparisons with existing methods, an ablation study on the DiT backbone, and real-world experiments. All experiments were conducted on a desktop PC with an Nvidia RTX 3090 GPU.

**A. Quantitative Comparison**
- **Baselines:**
 - **DemoGrasp:** A rule-based direct conversion method.
 - **RTAGrasp:** Represents state-of-the-art two-stage methods using Contact-GraspNet for candidate generation and human-derived constraints for filtering.
 - **RTAGrasp-MultiView (RTAGrasp-MV):** An enhanced version of RTAGrasp that aggregates grasps from four different viewpoints for more comprehensive object coverage, ensuring a fair comparison with HGDiffuser which uses complete object point clouds.
 - **Ours-Two-stage (Ours-TS):** A two-stage variant of HGDiffuser's grasp sampler (without human guidance), followed by constraint-based filtering, to enable a direct comparison of single-stage vs. two-stage approaches using the same core sampler.
- **Dataset:** OakInk dataset (subset of 340 object instances across 33 object categories), providing object meshes and human grasp demonstrations in MANO representation.
- **Metrics:** Task-oriented grasping success rate (evaluated in NVIDIA Isaac Gym simulation and manually assessed for task relevance), and inference time.

**Results:**
- **Success Rate and Efficiency (Figure 5, Table I):**
 - DemoGrasp achieved a low success rate of 27.85%, attributed to the limitations of manual rules in handling diverse human grasps.
 - RTAGrasp (single-view) had a success rate of 59.06%, often failing due to incomplete object coverage.
 - RTAGrasp-MV improved success to 71.47% by using multi-view inputs, but its inference time increased to 1.019s.
 - Ours-TS, with its sampler and two-stage filtering, achieved 78.12% success but required the longest inference time of 3.428s (for 500 samples).
 - **HGDiffuser (Ours) achieved the highest success rate of 81.21% with a significantly reduced inference time of 0.191s (sampling just one grasp).** This represents an 81.26% reduction in inference time compared to RTAGrasp-MV while achieving higher accuracy.
- **Impact of Sampling Quantity (Table I):**
 - For Ours-TS, increasing the number of sampled grasps from 100 to 500 improved the success rate from 71.18% to 78.12%, but inference time increased proportionally (0.671s to 3.428s).
 - **HGDiffuser's performance was largely unaffected by the number of grasp samples, achieving high success even with a single sample, demonstrating its efficiency advantage.**

**B. Ablation Study**
- **Objective:** To assess the impact of the DiT blocks as the feature backbone.
- **Baselines:**
 - **GraspLDM:** A state-of-the-art diffusion-based task-agnostic grasp sampler.
 - **Ours w/o DiT:** A variant of HGDiffuser without DiT blocks, using an MLP-based feature backbone (similar to GraspDiff).
- **Metric:** Task-agnostic grasp success rate (stability only, no task constraints).

**Results (Table II):**
- Ours w/o DiT achieved a success rate of 71.35%, which was 2.97% lower than GraspLDM (74.32%).
- **The full HGDiffuser model (Ours), incorporating DiT blocks, achieved a success rate of 80.65%, surpassing GraspLDM by 6.33% while maintaining a comparable inference time (0.167s vs. 0.183s).**
- This indicates that DiT blocks enhance feature fusion through their attention mechanism, leading to a significant improvement (9.3% increase from 71.35% to 80.65%) in grasp generation quality.

**C. Real-world Experiments**
- **Setup:** Franka Research 3 arm with a Franka hand and a wrist-mounted RealSense D435i camera.
- **Tasks:** Evaluated on 30 object-task pairs (10 objects, 8 tasks), each with one human demonstration.
- **Metrics:** Success rates across three stages: perception (object and human pose estimation), planning (grasp generation and trajectory planning), and action (physical execution).

**Results (Table III):**
- Perception stage: 86.67% success.
- Planning stage: 73.33% success.
- Action stage: 66.67% success.
- **Identified Challenges:**
 - Limitations of the task-agnostic grasp sampler in generating stable grasps for specific object regions (e.g., headphone headbands, scissor handles), leading to unstable grasps even with human guidance.
 - Inaccuracies in object pose estimation due to reconstructed mesh errors or partial occlusions, resulting in imprecise grasp execution—a common issue in demonstration-based methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HGDiffuser framework represents a notable advancement in the field of task-oriented robotic grasping, particularly in its approach to leveraging human demonstrations.

### 6. Significance

- **Addressing Efficiency and Stability in TOG:** The core significance lies in resolving the inefficiencies and potential failures associated with traditional two-stage demonstration-based TOG methods. By integrating task-oriented constraints directly into a single-stage diffusion-based sampling process, HGDiffuser eliminates the need for extensive, task-agnostic candidate generation, which has been a major bottleneck.
- **Novel Application of Guided Diffusion:** This work demonstrates an effective application of the guided sampling mechanism in diffusion models to a complex robotics problem. Utilizing non-network-based, differentiable loss functions for guidance provides a flexible and interpretable way to incorporate human-derived constraints without requiring large-scale task-specific training datasets.
- **Architectural Enhancement for Grasp Generation:** The incorporation of Diffusion Transformer (DiT) blocks as the feature backbone is a significant architectural contribution. The ablation study confirms that DiT blocks, through their attention mechanisms, enable superior feature fusion compared to MLP-based alternatives, directly leading to improved grasp generation quality.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Robot Learning and Deployment:** The substantial reduction in inference time (e.g., 81.26% compared to prior SOTA two-stage methods) achieved by HGDiffuser can significantly accelerate the process of transferring human manipulation skills to robots. This efficiency is critical for real-time applications and rapid deployment of robotic systems in various domains, from manufacturing to service robotics.
- **More Robust and Adaptable Robotic Systems:** By generating grasps that are inherently task-oriented and stable, HGDiffuser contributes to the development of more robust robotic manipulation capabilities. Robots equipped with this technology could perform tasks with greater precision and reliability, especially in environments requiring human-like dexterity and understanding of object functionalities.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** The ability to effectively transfer human grasping strategies could facilitate more intuitive and natural human-robot interaction. Robots could better anticipate and execute tasks in a manner aligned with human intent, leading to more seamless collaboration.
- **Advancements in Foundation Models for Robotics:** The successful integration of transformer-based architectures (DiT) in a diffusion model for complex geometric tasks like grasping provides insights for future development of foundation models in robotics. It suggests pathways for leveraging advanced neural network architectures to handle multi-modal inputs (point clouds, grasp representations, temporal information) more effectively.
- **Reduced Data Dependency for Task-Specific Learning:** By enabling task-oriented grasping without requiring vast, manually annotated task-specific datasets, HGDiffuser lowers the data barrier for deploying robotic manipulation skills. This could make it more feasible for researchers and practitioners to train and adapt robots for novel tasks with fewer demonstrations.
