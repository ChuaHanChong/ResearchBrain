# Scaling Laws for Native Multimodal Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Apple conducted a comprehensive study on scaling laws for native multimodal models, demonstrating that early-fusion architectures trained from scratch can achieve comparable performance to late-fusion approaches while offering superior training efficiency and lower deployment costs. Integrating Mixture of Experts into these early-fusion designs further enhances performance and enables implicit specialization to different modalities.

## Problem

- The dominant paradigm for multimodal AI relies on 'late-fusion' architectures, integrating separately pre-trained unimodal components, whose inherent optimality for general multimodal perception is questioned.
- A lack of comprehensive scaling laws for Native Multimodal Models (NMMs) limits understanding of their performance scaling with compute, parameters, and data, unlike well-established laws for unimodal LLMs.
- Current late-fusion approaches involve managing multiple complex components, which can hinder overall scaling efficiency, memory usage, and practical deployment.

## Method

- Conducted an extensive empirical study involving **457** trained models to systematically compare ==early-fusion== and ==late-fusion== Native Multimodal Models (NMMs) trained entirely from scratch on diverse image-text data.
- Extended and applied the ==scaling laws== framework to NMMs, analyzing how validation loss scales with computational resources (FLOPs), model parameters, and training tokens across different architectural designs.
- Integrated sparse ==Mixture of Experts (MoEs)== into early-fusion NMMs, exploring both modality-agnostic and modality-aware routing to investigate their impact on performance, efficiency, and implicit modality specialization.

## Results

- Early-fusion NMMs consistently matched late-fusion NMMs in validation loss curves, exhibiting nearly identical compute scaling exponents (e.g., c = **-0.0492** vs. c = **-0.0494**), but with better efficiency, especially at lower compute budgets.
- Native multimodal models follow scaling laws similar to LLMs, with compute-optimal scaling achieved by roughly balancing increases in parameters and training tokens (N* ∝ C**^0.526**, D* ∝ C**^0.473**).
- Sparse MoE early-fusion NMMs significantly outperformed dense models on validation loss and downstream tasks, with modality-agnostic routing proving more effective than modality-aware routing, and observed emergent modality specialization in network layers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Early-fusion architectures trained from scratch yield similar validation loss scaling exponents compared to late-fusion architectures, suggesting no inherent performance advantage for late-fusion in a from-scratch training regime.
> - Native Multimodal Models, particularly early-fusion variants, demonstrate superior training efficiency (faster training, less memory) and more favorable parameter-to-data trade-offs for compute-optimal scaling, leading to lower inference costs.
> - Mixture of Experts (MoEs) significantly improve the performance of early-fusion NMMs at the same inference cost, and their experts implicitly learn to specialize in different modalities (image vs. text) without explicit modality-aware routing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a fundamental architectural question in the rapidly evolving field of multimodal artificial intelligence, particularly concerning the perception and understanding of the world through combined signals like images and text.

The current mainstream approach for building powerful multimodal models, often referred to as "late-fusion," involves integrating separately pre-trained unimodal components. This typically means connecting a vision encoder (like CLIP or DINOv2) to a pre-trained Large Language Model (LLM), followed by multimodal fine-tuning (e.g., Flamingo, LLaVA, Gemini). This strategy has shown remarkable success and sample efficiency, leveraging the extensive pre-training on vast unimodal datasets.

This paper, however, challenges the *inherent superiority* of such late-fusion architectures. It revisits "Native Multimodal Models (NMMs)," defined as models trained from the ground up on all modalities simultaneously, without relying on pre-trained unimodal components or converting images into discrete tokens. While some previous works have explored NMMs, they often still employ pre-trained image tokenizers, which the authors explicitly avoid, opting for raw multimodal inputs. This work pushes back against the prevailing wisdom, exploring whether a more integrated, "from-scratch" approach can be equally, if not more, effective.

Furthermore, the research deeply integrates with the established framework of **scaling laws** in deep learning. Pioneered by works on Large Language Models (LLMs) such as Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. (e.g., GPT-3, Chinchilla), scaling laws predict how model performance (loss) scales with increases in computational resources (FLOPs), model parameters (N), and training data (D). This paper extends this crucial analytical framework to the multimodal domain, comparing different architectural choices (early vs. late fusion) and even sparse models (Mixture of Experts, MoEs) within this lens. By doing so, it provides quantitative insights into resource allocation for multimodal model development, a topic that has been less explored for native multimodal systems compared to their unimodal counterparts.

Finally, the paper contributes to the growing interest in **Mixture of Experts (MoE)** architectures. MoEs have demonstrated significant gains in efficiency and capacity for LLMs by sparsely activating parameters. This work investigates their application within native multimodal models, exploring how they can facilitate "modality-specific specialization" within a unified, early-fusion architecture. This is a novel angle, especially compared to prior MoE research in multimodal settings that might employ modality-aware routing or late-fusion designs. By showing that modality-agnostic MoEs can implicitly learn specialization, the paper offers a sophisticated approach to handling the inherent heterogeneity of multimodal data.

In essence, this research positions itself at a critical juncture, questioning current architectural biases, extending fundamental scaling principles to a complex domain, and exploring advanced sparse architectures to pave the way for more efficient and effective general-purpose multimodal AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a comprehensive scaling laws study of **Native Multimodal Models (NMMs)**, which are defined as models trained from the ground up on all modalities simultaneously, without relying on separately pre-trained unimodal components. This fundamental investigation aims to provide a deeper understanding of how these integrated models perform and scale with increasing computational resources.

This overarching objective is driven by several key motivations and specific research questions:

1. **Revisiting Architectural Design:** The dominant paradigm in multimodal AI involves "late-fusion" architectures, where separately pre-trained vision encoders are connected to LLMs. While successful, the authors question whether such approaches are *inherently optimal* for perceiving the world through multimodal signals. Their motivation is to systematically compare this prevalent strategy with "early-fusion" architectures, which process raw multimodal inputs from the very beginning with minimal modality-specific parameters. They aim to determine if late-fusion holds any intrinsic advantage when models are trained from scratch.

2. **Exploring Efficiency and Deployability:** The authors are motivated by the practical considerations of scaling large AI systems. Current late-fusion approaches involve managing multiple components, each with its own hyperparameters, pre-training data, and scaling properties. This complexity can hinder scaling efforts. The paper seeks architectures that are more flexible, easier to train, more efficient in terms of memory and speed, and simpler to deploy, especially with abundant multimodal data now available.

3. **Deriving Scaling Laws for NMMs:** A core motivation is to extend the established scaling laws framework (which has been highly successful for LLMs) to native multimodal models. This involves:
 - Predicting the final performance (validation loss) of NMMs as a function of compute budget (FLOPs).
 - Determining the *compute-optimal* configurations, i.e., the ideal trade-off between the number of model parameters (N) and the amount of training tokens (D) for a given compute budget.
 - Analyzing how these scaling properties vary across different multimodal architectural choices (early vs. late fusion) and training data mixtures.

4. **Investigating Modality Specialization within Unified Architectures:** Recognizing that multimodal data is inherently heterogeneous, the researchers are motivated to explore how a unified early-fusion model can dynamically allocate its capacity across modalities. This leads to the objective of investigating **Mixture of Experts (MoEs)**, a technique that allows models to learn modality-specific weights. They aim to demonstrate if MoEs can significantly benefit performance and converge faster, and whether they can implicitly learn specialization without explicit modality-aware routing.

In summary, the paper's key objectives are to systematically evaluate and compare early-fusion and late-fusion native multimodal models through extensive scaling studies, establish their scaling laws, and explore the benefits of integrating Mixture of Experts for achieving efficient modality specialization. The overarching motivation is to find more effective, efficient, and scalable architectural designs for general-purpose multimodal perception.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors undertake an extensive experimental study involving **457 trained models** to systematically investigate the scaling properties of Native Multimodal Models (NMMs). Their methodology is rooted in the established scaling laws framework and involves comparing different architectural designs and training strategies.

#### 3.1. Model Architectures

- **Native Multimodal Models (NMMs):** The core focus is on models trained "from scratch" on multimodal data (image and text) without relying on pre-trained unimodal encoders or tokenizers. The models are based on the **autoregressive transformer architecture** with SwiGLU FFNs and QK-Norm.
- **Early Fusion:** These models enable multimodal interaction from the beginning. They use a single transformer that processes raw multimodal input—tokenized text and continuous image patches—with minimal modality-specific components (e.g., only a linear layer to patchify images). Image patches are linearly projected to match text token dimensions. Causal attention is used for text, and bidirectional attention for image tokens.
- **Late Fusion:** These models delay multimodal interaction, typically after separate unimodal components have processed each modality independently. The vision encoder follows a CLIP-like architecture connected to a decoder.
- **Mixture of Experts (MoEs):** For exploring sparsity and specialization, the authors incorporate MoEs into the early-fusion NMM architecture. They use a dropless-MoE implementation, employing a **top-k expert-choice routing mechanism** with `k=1` and `E=8` available experts. An auxiliary load-balancing loss (weight 0.01) ensures balanced expert utilization. They compare **modality-agnostic routing** (learned router module) with **modality-aware routing** (pre-defined rules based on modality type).

#### 3.2. Scaling Laws Framework

The study rigorously applies the scaling laws framework, adapting it from prior work on LLMs.

- **Loss Prediction:** The primary performance metric is validation loss (L), measured as the average over interleaved image-text, image-caption, and text-only data mixtures. A power-law relationship is assumed for loss based on model size (N) and training tokens (D): `L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β`. Parameters (E, A, B, α, β) are fitted using the L-BFGS algorithm with Huber loss.
- **Compute (FLOPs) Estimation:**
 - For early-fusion models: `C = 6ND` (where N is decoder parameters, D is total multimodal tokens).
 - For late-fusion models: `C = 6(N_v D_v + ND)` (where N_v and D_v refer to vision encoder parameters and vision-only tokens, respectively).
- **Compute-Optimal Configurations:** Based on the fitted power laws, the study determines the optimal number of parameters (N*) and training tokens (D*) for a given compute budget (C), expressed as `N* ∝ C^a`, `D* ∝ C^b`, and `L ∝ C^c`. These relationships guide efficient resource allocation.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup and Training

- **Model Sizes:** Models range from 0.3B to 4B active parameters. Model width is scaled while keeping the depth constant (24 layers).
- **Training Data:** A diverse mixture of public and private multimodal datasets is used: DCLM, Obelics, DFN, COYO, and a private collection of High-Quality Image-Text Pairs (HQITP). The default mixture is 45% image-caption, 45% interleaved, and 10% text-only data, with variations explored.
- **Input Processing:** Images are resized to 224x224 resolution with a 14x14 patch size. Text and image patches form a context length of 1k for multimodal sequences.
- **Training Efficiency:** Training is performed using `bfloat16`, Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP), activation checkpointing, and gradient accumulation. Sequence packing is used for image captioning.
- **Optimization:** Decoupled AdamW optimizer with specific momentum values and weight decay. Learning rates vary with model size. A constant learning rate schedule with a cooldown phase (inverse square root scheduler) is used.
- **Image Augmentations:** Include Random Resized Crop and Random Horizontal Flip.

#### 3.4. Evaluation

- **Primary Evaluation:** Validation loss on held-out subsets of interleaved (Obelics), image-caption (HQITP), and text-only data (DCLM) is the main metric for deriving scaling laws.
- **Downstream Task Validation:** To ensure correlation with practical performance, a subset of models is instruction-tuned (SFT) on the LLaVA mixture and evaluated on various VQA and captioning tasks (e.g., VQAv2, COCO, TextCaps), reporting accuracy and CIDEr scores.
- **MoE Specialization Analysis:** To quantify modality specialization in MoE models, a specialization score is computed as `1 - H(p)`, where `H` is the binary entropy of each expert's text/image token distribution, averaged across experts within a layer. This visualizes which experts handle which modalities and how this changes across layers.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a robust comparative analysis of different NMM architectures, providing quantitative insights into their scaling behavior, efficiency, and suitability for multimodal learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive scaling laws study, encompassing 457 models, yielded several significant findings that challenge conventional wisdom in multimodal AI development:

#### 4.1. Early Fusion vs. Late Fusion Architectures

- **No Inherent Advantage for Late Fusion:** The study found no inherent advantage to late-fusion architectures over early-fusion ones when trained from scratch. Both architectures exhibit remarkably similar validation loss when trained with the same compute budget (FLOPs). The loss scaling exponent `L ∝ C^c` was nearly identical (`c = -0.0492` for early fusion vs. `c = -0.0494` for late fusion).
- **Early Fusion Outperforms at Lower Compute:** Early-fusion models showed stronger performance at lower parameter counts and compute budgets (Figure 3). While the performance gap decreased at larger scales, early fusion consistently maintained a slight edge across various training FLOPs.
- **Enhanced Training Efficiency for Early Fusion:** Early-fusion models were significantly more efficient to train, consuming less memory and training faster under the same compute budget (Figure 4). This advantage became more pronounced with increasing compute, highlighting their superior operational efficiency.
- **Parameter-to-Data Trade-off:** Compute-optimal late-fusion models required a higher parameters-to-data ratio compared to early-fusion models. This implies that for a fixed compute budget, late-fusion benefits from more parameters, while early-fusion benefits more from longer training (more tokens) (Figure 1, bottom). This reduces inference costs for early-fusion models, making them easier to deploy.
- **Resolution and Mixture Robustness:** Early-fusion consistently outperformed late-fusion across varying image resolutions, with the gap widening at higher resolutions (Figure 17). Additionally, early-fusion models attained favorable performance when increasing the proportion of interleaved documents and text-only data in the training mixture (Figure 7, Figure 15, Figure 16).

#### 4.2. Scaling Laws of Native Multimodal Models (NMMs)

- **Similarity to LLMs:** NMMs follow scaling laws akin to those observed for text-only LLMs. The performance scaling with compute (`L ∝ C^c`) is very close to that of models like GPT-3 (`L ∝ C^-0.048` vs. `L ∝ C^-0.049` for NMMs).
- **Balanced Scaling of Parameters and Data:** The optimal scaling exponents for model parameters (α = 0.301) and training tokens (β = 0.335) in `L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β` are similar to LLMs. For compute-optimal scaling, the number of training tokens and model parameters should be scaled roughly equally (`N* ∝ C^0.526`, `D* ∝ C^0.473`). However, NMMs benefit slightly more from larger models (higher 'a' exponent) and slightly less from longer training (lower 'b' exponent) compared to LLMs.
- **Data Type Variations:** While overall trends are similar, scaling exponents vary slightly depending on the target data type (e.g., image-caption, interleaved, text) and training mixture (Table 2, Table 3). Image-caption data generally shows a higher rate of improvement with increasing compute.

#### 4.3. Native Pre-training vs. Continual Training

- Native multimodal models, when trained for longer, can close the performance gap with models initialized from pre-trained LLMs (Figure 8). While image-captioning data converged faster, interleaved and text data required more tokens (up to 1T) for native models to catch up. This suggests native training can be a more efficient path to comparable performance when considering the full pre-training cost of initialized components.

#### 4.4. Advantages of Sparse Early-Fusion NMMs (Mixture of Experts)

- **Significant Performance Improvement:** Sparse NMMs (MoEs) significantly outperformed their dense counterparts at the same inference cost (number of active parameters) (Figure 10). This gap was more pronounced for smaller model sizes. MoEs also achieved lower overall loss (Figure 9, Figure 26).
- **Unbalanced Scaling for MoEs:** Scaling laws for MoEs indicate that scaling the number of *training tokens* is more important than the number of *active parameters* (`b` significantly higher than `a`). This differs from dense models, reflecting the higher total (inactive) parameters in sparse architectures.
- **Modality-Agnostic Routing Superiority:** Modality-agnostic routing (learned router) consistently outperformed modality-aware routing (hard-coded experts for specific modalities) (Figure 11).
- **Emergent Modality Specialization:** MoE experts implicitly learned to specialize in different modalities, with this specialization being particularly prominent in the early and last layers of the network (Figure 12, Figure 13, Figure 25). Mid-layers showed more cross-modality sharing, suggesting a hierarchical processing of multimodal features.

#### 4.5. Downstream Task Validation

- Evaluation on downstream tasks (VQA and captioning) confirmed the rankings observed from validation loss, with early-fusion outperforming late-fusion, and MoEs outperforming dense models (Table 4).

In essence, the findings suggest that the perceived advantages of late-fusion models, largely driven by the reuse of pre-trained unimodal components, do not hold when comparing against native, from-scratch training. Early-fusion, especially when combined with Mixture of Experts, offers a more efficient, scalable, and powerful approach to multimodal perception.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries substantial significance and potential impact for the future development and deployment of multimodal artificial intelligence, challenging deeply ingrained assumptions and offering new directions for architectural design.

#### 5.1. Re-evaluation of Multimodal Architectural Paradigms

The most profound impact of this work is its direct challenge to the prevailing "late-fusion" paradigm, which has dominated multimodal AI by integrating separately pre-trained unimodal models. By demonstrating that native, early-fusion models trained from scratch can perform on par with, or even better than, late-fusion counterparts (especially at lower compute and with superior efficiency), the paper necessitates a re-evaluation of current architectural choices. This could lead to a shift towards more integrated, holistic multimodal learning from the ground up, moving away from relying solely on grafting pre-trained components.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Efficiency and Reduced Resource Consumption

The finding that early-fusion models are "more efficient to train" (faster and consume less memory) is critically important in an era where training large AI models incurs astronomical computational and environmental costs. This efficiency translates directly into reduced development timelines, lower infrastructure expenses, and a smaller carbon footprint. For organizations with finite resources, or those aiming for more sustainable AI, this offers a compelling alternative.

#### 5.3. Simplified Deployment and Operational Overhead

Early-fusion models, with their unified architecture that processes raw multimodal inputs, are inherently simpler to design, manage, and deploy compared to late-fusion systems involving multiple, independently-scaled components. This simplicity can streamline the entire machine learning lifecycle, from research to production, reducing operational overhead and accelerating the adoption of multimodal capabilities in real-world applications.

#### 5.4. Practical Guidelines for Compute-Optimal Scaling

By extending the robust framework of scaling laws to native multimodal models and Mixture of Experts, this research provides invaluable practical guidelines for researchers and engineers. The derived power-law relationships (e.g., optimal parameter-to-data ratios, loss reduction rates with compute) enable more informed decisions about resource allocation. This means that future multimodal models can be designed and trained more efficiently, ensuring that computational budgets are spent optimally to achieve desired performance levels.

#### 5.5. Advancing Mixture of Experts for Multimodality

The successful integration of Mixture of Experts (MoEs) into early-fusion NMMs is a significant advancement. Demonstrating that MoEs can implicitly learn modality-specific specialization, particularly with modality-agnostic routing, opens new avenues for building highly capable and scalable multimodal models that can dynamically adapt to heterogeneous inputs. This approach offers a powerful mechanism to increase model capacity without proportionally increasing inference costs, a key advantage for resource-constrained environments or applications requiring very large models.

#### 5.6. Implications for Industry and On-Device AI

For companies like Apple, where many authors are affiliated, these findings are particularly relevant. The emphasis on efficiency, deployability, and strong performance at lower parameter counts (a characteristic of early-fusion models) aligns perfectly with the demands of on-device AI for consumer products (e.g., smartphones, smart home devices) where computational resources are constrained. This research could directly inform the next generation of multimodal AI capabilities in such devices, enabling richer and more responsive user experiences.

In conclusion, "Scaling Laws for Native Multimodal Models" is more than just an empirical study; it's a foundational piece of research that challenges existing paradigms, provides quantitative insights into resource optimization, and paves the way for a new generation of efficient, scalable, and inherently multimodal AI systems.
