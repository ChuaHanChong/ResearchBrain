# OPG-Policy: Occluded Push-Grasp Policy Learning with Amodal Segmentation

> [!summary] Summary
> OPG-Policy from Sun Yat-Sen University presents a learning framework for robots to retrieve occluded target objects in dense clutter by integrating amodal segmentation with deep reinforcement learning. This approach enables more efficient push-grasp actions, demonstrating higher success rates (e.g., 82.66% average in simulation) and fewer manipulation attempts compared to existing methods, with robust transfer from simulation to real-world environments.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation in dense clutter often requires retrieving specific, partially or fully occluded target objects.
- Current goal-oriented grasping methods rely solely on visible object portions, leading to significant uncertainty about the target's full extent and interaction with surrounding clutter.
- This partial observability results in sub-optimal, inefficient motion sequences with many unnecessary attempts to clear obstacles or grasp.

## Method

- Introduces **OPG-Policy**, a deep reinforcement learning framework that leverages amodal segmentation to perceive both visible and occluded parts of target objects.
- Utilizes a Deep Q-Network (DQN) that takes RGB-D and amodal mask heightmaps as input to learn optimal push and grasp actions.
- Incorporates a coordinator module, an MLP binary classifier, which uses amodal mask-derived features and Q-values to intelligently decide whether to push or grasp.

## Results

- In simulation, OPG-Policy achieved an **82.66%** average success rate and **2.61** average total attempts, outperforming baselines and demonstrating superior efficiency in highly occluded scenarios (e.g., **68%** success vs **57%** for GE-GRASP in hard 30-object tasks).
- The framework consistently reduced the average number of grasp/push attempts, particularly in severely occluded cases (**4.75** attempts vs **5.74** for GE-GRASP), indicating improved motion efficiency.
- Real-world experiments showed robust sim-to-real transfer, with the simulation-trained model achieving an **85%** success rate in challenging real-world tests and **90%** success on unfamiliar objects without fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Providing complete amodal masks of occluded target objects significantly improves a robot's ability to plan efficient manipulation sequences.
> - A dedicated coordinator module that leverages both learned Q-values and amodal mask-derived domain knowledge (e.g., occlusion rate, border occupancy) is essential for effective push-grasp action selection.
> - Rewarding pushing actions based on their impact on future grasping opportunities, through a dynamically adaptive threshold, guides the policy towards optimal clearing strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly grasping, has been a central challenge in robotics for decades. This work specifically addresses **goal-oriented grasping in dense clutter**, a more complex variant of the general grasping problem.

Traditionally, robotic grasping research began with **generic grasping**, focusing on identifying stable force-closure grasp positions, often relying on precise 3D object models. While successful in sparse environments, these methods falter in cluttered scenarios due to difficulty in identifying viable grasp points for specific targets. Recent advancements have shifted towards **data-driven strategies** leveraging deep neural networks with RGB or RGB-D imagery. However, even these approaches struggle with dense clutter, where the target object is often partially or fully occluded by other objects.

This limitation led to the emergence of **goal-oriented grasping**, which integrates non-prehensile actions like pushing to clear obstacles and create space for grasping. Previous works in this domain, such as those by Yang et al., Xu et al., and Kiatos et al., employ various learning strategies, including DQN-based frameworks, Bayesian exploration, and Monte Carlo Tree Search, to coordinate pushing and grasping actions. A common theme among these methods is their reliance on **partially observable segments** of the occluded target. This incomplete information often leads to inefficiencies, as the policy struggles to generate optimal motions without knowledge of the invisible portions of the target.

This is where the paper introduces a crucial innovation: the integration of **amodal segmentation**. Amodal segmentation, a task actively studied in computer vision, reveals *both* visible and hidden (occluded) parts of objects. While amodal segmentation has shown promise in generic grasping to infer object layering for grasp prioritization and in active perception tasks, its potential in the context of goal-oriented grasping in dense clutter has remained largely "untapped," as the authors state.

OPG-Policy positions itself as the **first general learning framework for occluded goal-oriented grasping that explicitly uses amodal segmentation masks as complementary inputs**. By providing a comprehensive understanding of the target object's full extent, even when occluded, this work aims to overcome the "motion inefficiency" problem that plagues previous methods. It bridges the gap between advanced perception (amodal segmentation) and complex manipulation strategies (push-grasp policies) to enable more efficient and robust robot performance in highly cluttered, real-world scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an **adaptive push-grasp policy** that can efficiently retrieve **occluded target objects in dense clutter**.

The core motivation stems from a critical limitation of existing goal-oriented grasping methods:
1. **Uncertainty from Partial Observation:** Previous methods typically learn policies based solely on the *visible* portions of occluded targets. This partial observability leads to significant uncertainties regarding the full shape, size, and true location of the target object, as well as its interaction with surrounding clutter.
2. **Low Motion Efficiency:** Consequently, without complete information, these policies often generate sub-optimal or inefficient motion sequences. Robots may execute unnecessary pushes, attempt grasps prematurely, or fail to identify the most effective path to the target, resulting in a high number of manipulation attempts and a lower success rate.
3. **Mimicking Human Capability:** Humans exhibit remarkable ability to navigate dense clutter and retrieve specific items efficiently, largely because they can infer the complete shape and presence of occluded objects. The motivation is to imbue robots with a similar "comprehensive understanding" of the scene.

To address these issues, the authors propose leveraging **amodal segmentation** to predict the occluded portions of the target. By incorporating this rich, complete object information, OPG-Policy aims to enable the robot to generate **optimal motions** that lead to higher success rates and significantly improved motion efficiency in challenging cluttered environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OPG-Policy is a novel framework that integrates amodal segmentation with deep reinforcement learning to achieve efficient push-grasp policies for occluded targets.

#### 3.1. System Overview

The system operates by capturing RGB-D images of the workspace with a fixed-mount camera. These images are fed into a pre-trained **amodal segmentation module** to generate amodal masks of the target object. The RGB, depth, and target amodal mask images are then orthographically projected into color, depth, and amodal mask heightmaps, forming the state representation (I, D, A). To encode rotational information, these heightmaps are rotated at 16 different angles (22.5° step size). These rotated heightmaps are processed by a **DenseNet-based feature extraction module**, which feeds into separate **Push Q Net** and **Grasp Q Net**. These Q-networks predict pixel-wise Q maps, where each pixel's value represents the expected future return of executing a corresponding push or grasp action at that location and orientation. Finally, a **Coordinator module**, leveraging domain knowledge derived from the amodal mask and the highest Q values, determines the optimal action type (push or grasp) to be executed by the robot.

#### 3.2. Amodal Segmentation Module

The framework employs **UOAIS** as its amodal segmentation module. UOAIS is a hierarchical feature fusion method capable of generating amodal masks, which reveal both visible and occluded parts of objects. To train this module, the authors developed a novel data collection method in simulation. They use a general segmentation method (e.g., Segment Anything Model (SAM)) to segment visible object parts. To obtain amodal mask annotations, a single object is placed in a "comparison area" with the same pose as an occluded object in the workspace. SAM segments both the workspace and comparison area images to produce visible and full masks, respectively. Custom filtering and matching algorithms then synchronize these masks to compute the amodal mask annotations for training the UOAIS module.

#### 3.3. Deep Q-Network (DQN)

The goal-oriented grasping problem is modeled as a discrete Markov Decision Process (MDP).
- **State Representation:** The state `(I, D, A)` consists of the color heightmap (`I`), depth heightmap (`D`), and target amodal mask heightmap (`A`). Rotational invariance is achieved by rotating these heightmaps at 16 discrete orientations.
- **Network Architecture:** The feature extraction module is an encoder comprising two convolutional layers followed by a DenseNet121 pre-trained on ImageNet. The Push Q Net and Grasp Q Net share the same structure: three convolutional layers followed by bilinear upsampling, mapping features to pixel-wise Q maps. The pixel with the highest Q value in the respective Q map indicates the optimal pushing or grasping action (position and orientation).

#### 3.4. Coordinator

A critical component of OPG-Policy is the coordinator, designed as a three-layer MLP binary classifier that decides between a push or grasp action. Its decision is based on a set of carefully selected domain knowledge features:
- `q_p` and `q_g`: The best push Q value and best grasp Q value, representing the future expected return of these actions.
- `o`: The target object's occlusion rate (ratio of occluded portion to its complete amodal mask).
- `a_b` and `a_n`: Indicators of target surroundings. `a_b` is the ratio of the occluded target border to the full target border. `a_n` is the ratio of the occluded target border to the full target mask. These metrics quantify how occluded and crowded the target's border is.
- `f_c`: Grasp fail count, reflecting the history of failed grasp attempts for the current target.
The coordinator is trained using a binary cross-entropy loss, with supervision derived from the observable outcome of grasping actions (1 for success, 0 for failure). This specific design leverages the comprehensive information provided by the amodal mask to make informed decisions about action types.

#### 3.5. Reward Function Design

The reward functions are tailored for each action type to promote efficiency and goal achievement:
- **Grasping Reward:**
 - `R = 0.25`: If the grasping position falls within the target's amodal mask, encouraging accurate target identification.
 - `R = 1`: If the target object is successfully grasped out, marking task completion.
 - `R = 0`: Otherwise.
- **Pushing Reward:**
 - `R = 0.5`: If a pushing action reduces the target object's occluded rate by more than 0.1, encouraging obstacle clearing.
 - `R = 1`: If a pushing action raises the next best grasp Q value beyond a **dynamically adaptive threshold (T_g)**. This is a key innovation, as `T_g` adapts using exponentially weighted updates (`T_g = β*T_g + (1-β)*Q_g`) based on the evolving grasp Q net during training. This ensures that pushes are rewarded for creating better grasping opportunities, not just for reducing occlusion.
 - `R = 0`: Otherwise.

#### 3.6. Training Details

The training employs a **staged adaptive method** to progressively increase scene complexity:
- **Phase 1 (0-1000 iterations):** Low clutter (n=7 target objects, m=3 obstructive objects). Uses ε-greedy policy for action selection, as the agent's grasping skills are nascent.
- **Phase 2 (1000-3000 iterations):** Moderate clutter (m increases from 3 to 8). Switches to using the coordinator for action type selection.
- **Phase 3 (3000-5000 iterations):** High clutter (m=13).
- **Phase 4 (Beyond 5000 iterations):** Very high clutter (m=18).
Target object selection also adapts: random selection up to 3000 iterations, then preference for the *most occluded* object to enhance proficiency in demanding scenarios. The agent retrieves all potential targets before a scene reset. The DQN model minimizes temporal difference error using Huber loss, and the coordinator is trained with binary cross-entropy loss.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of OPG-Policy was rigorously evaluated through extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world environments.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

The simulation tests compared OPG-Policy against GTI, GTI-amodal* (GTI with OPG-Policy's amodal segmentation), GE-GRASP, and OPG-Policy-no-coordinator* (OPG-Policy without the coordinator). Performance metrics included success rate and total grasp/push attempts.

- **Random Case:**
 - In scenarios with 15 and 30 objects, and a "30 objects (hard)" scenario (heavily occluded target), OPG-Policy consistently outperformed baselines.
 - For 15 objects, GE-GRASP had a slight edge, but OPG-Policy pulled ahead significantly in more cluttered "30 objects" (84% vs 81% success rate) and "30 objects (hard)" (68% vs 57% success rate) scenarios.
 - OPG-Policy achieved the highest average success rate (82.66%) and lowest average total attempts (2.61).
 - Notably, GTI-amodal* performed *worse* than vanilla GTI, indicating that simply integrating amodal masks without a dedicated policy learning framework (like OPG-Policy's rewards and coordinator) can degrade performance.
 - OPG-Policy-no-coordinator* showed that while amodal segmentation helps, the coordinator is crucial for optimal performance, improving both success rate and efficiency.

- **Occluded Case:**
 - Tests were designed with varying occlusion ratios (0.2-0.4, 0.4-0.6, 0.6-0.8).
 - OPG-Policy demonstrated superior motion efficiency, particularly in severely occluded cases (0.6-0.8 ratio), reducing average grasp/push attempts by nearly one compared to GE-GRASP (4.75 vs 5.74 attempts), while maintaining a competitive success rate (90% vs 91%). This highlights OPG-Policy's ability to devise more effective action strategies due to its comprehensive target understanding via amodal segmentation.

- **Challenging Case:**
 - Six highly structured and challenging scenarios with obstacles placed above the target were used.
 - OPG-Policy achieved a remarkable 99% average success rate and 4.19 average movements, significantly outperforming GTI (88% success, 5.85 movements) and GE-GRASP (98% success, 4.98 movements). This validates the robustness and effectiveness of OPG-Policy in complex, structured occlusion scenarios.

#### 4.2. Real World Experiments

The real-world experiments used a UR10 robotic arm, ROBOTIQ gripper, and Intel RealSense D435i camera. Crucially, the model was trained *exclusively in simulation* and deployed without any real-world fine-tuning. Baselines (GTI, GE-GRASP) were augmented with UOAIS for fair comparison. Target objects were limited to the YCB dataset.

- **Challenging Test (C-Test):** Four distinct scenarios with occluded targets.
 - OPG-Policy achieved 85% success rate and 7.0 total attempts, outperforming GE-GRASP (82.5% success, 7.62 attempts) and GTI (65% success, 8.42 attempts).

- **Generalization Test (G-Test):** Twelve unfamiliar household objects were used to test adaptability.
 - OPG-Policy demonstrated strong generalization with a 90% success rate and 6.65 total attempts, significantly better than GE-GRASP (85% success, 7.3 attempts) and GTI (80% success, 7.75 attempts).

These real-world results underscore OPG-Policy's exceptional ability to transfer from simulation to reality without fine-tuning, its robustness in challenging cluttered settings, and its strong generalization capabilities to novel objects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The OPG-Policy framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for goal-oriented grasping in complex, cluttered environments. Its primary contributions and potential impacts are:

1. **Pioneering Amodal Segmentation in Goal-Oriented Grasping:** OPG-Policy is presented as the first general learning framework to explicitly introduce and effectively leverage amodal masks as complementary inputs for push-grasp policy learning. This is a crucial conceptual leap, moving beyond reliance on partial observations to a comprehensive understanding of occluded objects, which fundamentally improves the robot's "perception" of the scene.

2. **Enhanced Motion Efficiency and Success Rate:** By providing complete object information, OPG-Policy enables the policy to generate more optimal and efficient action sequences. The experimental results consistently demonstrate superior performance in terms of both higher success rates and fewer grasp/push attempts across diverse and increasingly challenging cluttered scenarios. This directly addresses the "low motion efficiency" problem identified in previous works.

3. **Innovative Policy Learning Framework:** The work introduces several methodological innovations:
 - A **staged adaptive training method** that progressively increases scene complexity, allowing the agent to learn from simpler scenarios before tackling more difficult ones.
 - A **dynamically adaptive reward** for pushing actions, which aligns push objectives with the evolving grasp Q-net, ensuring pushes strategically improve subsequent grasping opportunities.
 - A **dedicated coordinator** that intelligently integrates best Q-values with rich domain knowledge (occlusion rate, border occupancy, grasp failure history) derived from the amodal mask to make informed decisions between pushing and grasping, a key factor in its superior performance.

4. **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful deployment of the simulation-trained model to real-world environments without any fine-tuning is a testament to its robustness and generalizability. This is highly significant, as it reduces the time and cost associated with training physical robots, making the approach more practical for real-world applications.

5. **Broad Applicability:** The enhanced capability for goal-oriented grasping in dense clutter has wide-ranging potential impacts:
 - **Industrial Automation:** More robust and efficient bin picking, part handling, and assembly in manufacturing.
 - **Logistics and Warehousing:** Faster and more accurate item retrieval from densely packed shelves or containers.
 - **Household Robotics:** Improved performance for domestic robots in organizing spaces, clearing clutter, and performing everyday tasks in dynamic and unconstrained environments.
 - **Service Robotics:** Applications in retail, hospitality, and healthcare where robots need to interact with diverse objects in complex settings.

In conclusion, OPG-Policy pushes the boundaries of robotic manipulation by integrating advanced visual perception with intelligent action planning. Its ability to "see" and reason about occluded objects, combined with its efficient policy learning, paves the way for more capable and autonomous robots in a variety of real-world scenarios.
