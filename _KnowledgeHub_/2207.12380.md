# Task-Relevant Failure Detection for Trajectory Predictors in Autonomous Vehicles

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduce a probabilistic run-time monitor for autonomous vehicles that detects "task-relevant" prediction failures by assessing their impact on the planning cost. This p-quantile anomaly detector offers statistical guarantees on false-positive and false-negative rates, demonstrating efficient real-time operation and improved safety and reliability in driving scenarios.

## Problem

- Autonomous vehicles (AVs) frequently encounter mispredictions from trajectory modules due to inherent aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties.
- These mispredictions can lead downstream motion planners to make unsafe decisions, such as collisions, posing a critical safety concern.
- Existing failure detection methods often lack task-relevance, triggering on benign errors, or do not provide quantifiable performance guarantees (FPR/FNR), resulting in overly conservative AV behavior or unquantifiable reliability.

## Method

- The authors define a ==p-quantile anomaly== as an event where the observed AV planning cost falls into the top 'p'-quantile of the predicted cost distribution, directly linking prediction errors to their safety impact.
- They propose a ==p-Quantile Anomaly Detector (QAD)== algorithm that employs a ==sampling-based method== to compare observed planning costs against predicted cost distributions, triggering a detection when the observed cost is unexpectedly high.
- The **QAD** provides ==analytical bounds on False-Positive Rate (FPR) and False-Negative Rate (FNR)==, enabling data-free calibration and quantitative assessment of the detector's reliability.

## Results

- The QAD achieved a mean **AUROC of 0.946 ± 0.002**, demonstrating superior performance in identifying task-relevant prediction failures compared to baseline detectors.
- When empirically calibrated, QAD exhibited a **FPR of 8.4% ± 0.4%** and a **FNR of 7.9% ± 0.7%**, striking a balance between safety and minimizing unnecessary interventions.
- Data-free calibration, when bounded for FPR, successfully limited the empirical FPR to **3.4% ± 0.3%** (for a target **5%** bound), confirming the detector's ability to satisfy theoretical guarantees for this metric.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Quantifying prediction failures as "p-quantile anomalies" based on their direct impact on the AV's planning cost provides a task-relevant and objective metric for safety monitoring.
> - A sampling-based approach enables computationally efficient, real-time detection without requiring analytical expressions for complex cost distributions, making the detector practical for integration into existing AV stacks.
> - Deriving analytical bounds for FPR and FNR allows for data-free calibration, which enhances trustworthiness and simplifies deployment by enabling parameter tuning without extensive labeled failure datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous vehicles (AVs) rely heavily on prediction modules to forecast the future trajectories of other agents (e.g., vehicles, pedestrians, bicyclists) in their environment. The accuracy of these predictions is critical for downstream motion planners to ensure the AV operates safely and avoids collisions. However, trajectory forecasting is inherently challenging due to significant uncertainties. These uncertainties are broadly categorized as aleatoric, stemming from the stochastic nature of human behavior, and epistemic, arising from unavoidable learning errors in prediction models trained on finite datasets. Consequently, prediction networks frequently produce mispredictions, which can lead the motion planner to make suboptimal or unsafe decisions.

The broader research landscape in AV safety has explored various approaches to address these challenges. One common strategy involves computing "inevitable collision sets" (ICS), often using techniques like Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis. In these approaches, the AV identifies situations where it is close to entering an ICS and executes evasive actions. A primary limitation of many such methods is the assumption of adversarial behavior from other agents, which can lead to overly conservative safety interventions that compromise the AV's performance or functionality.

Another related area is anomaly detection. This field aims to identify events that deviate significantly from a reference input. While widely applied in signal processing and to high-dimensional inputs like images, many anomaly detection methods are not "task-relevant," meaning they do not discriminate between benign deviations and those that are critical to the system's operational goals, such as safety for an AV. Some existing methods provide guarantees on detection rates but often come with caveats such as specific architectural requirements, retrospective guarantees, or reliance on test data being statistically similar to training data.

More recently, there has been increasing interest in "planning-aware prediction," where prediction modules are more tightly integrated with other components of the autonomy stack, particularly planning. This integration can involve architectural changes or the development of metrics that evaluate perception and prediction in a planning-aware manner (e.g., weighting errors that could cause collisions). However, these approaches often focus on improving the prediction model itself or evaluating its outputs, rather than providing an online, independent monitoring mechanism with performance guarantees for detecting harmful failures.

This work contributes to the landscape by addressing the gap between prediction module performance and AV safety. It proposes a run-time monitor that specifically detects prediction failures deemed "task-relevant" – those that would detrimentally affect the ego vehicle's planning cost and thus its safety. Unlike many prior anomaly detection methods, this approach provides statistical guarantees on false-positive and false-negative rates. Furthermore, it operates as an independent module, allowing the prediction network to optimize solely for prediction accuracy without modification for detectability, and it offers data-free calibration capabilities, distinguishing it from purely data-driven monitoring methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a probabilistic run-time monitor capable of detecting "harmful" prediction failures in autonomous vehicle (AV) trajectory predictors. This is specifically termed a "task-relevant failure detector," designed to enhance AV safety without compromising operational performance.

The motivation for this work arises from several critical factors:
1. **Safety Imperative:** Prediction modules are fundamental for AVs to navigate environments with other mobile agents. However, inherent uncertainties in trajectory forecasting, stemming from both the stochasticity of human behavior (aleatoric uncertainty) and unavoidable learning errors in prediction networks (epistemic uncertainty), lead to frequent mispredictions. These mispredictions can mislead the downstream motion planner, potentially causing the AV to make unsafe decisions, such as collisions or near-misses. The paramount importance of safety in AV operation necessitates a robust mechanism to identify and respond to such failures.
2. **Minimizing Unnecessary Interventions:** While ensuring safety is critical, a detector that triggers too frequently, even for benign prediction errors, can lead to overly conservative AV behavior. Such unnecessary interventions can degrade the AV's performance, efficiency, and passenger comfort. Therefore, a key motivation is to ensure the detector is "task-relevant," meaning it only triggers when a prediction failure is genuinely "harmful" to the AV's safety or operational goals. The paper illustrates this with an example: if a non-ego vehicle is predicted to stay in its lane but turns into the AV's lane, the cost for the planner increases, warranting a trigger. Conversely, if the non-ego vehicle turns away from the AV's lane, even if the prediction was inaccurate, the planning cost decreases, and no intervention is needed.
3. **Independence and Modularity:** Integrating failure detection directly into the prediction module could force the prediction network to optimize for failure detectability in addition to prediction accuracy, potentially hindering its primary performance. The researchers are motivated to create an independent monitoring module that does not modify the prediction module itself. This allows for easier integration into existing AV stacks and enables each module to specialize in its respective task without trade-offs.
4. **Interpretability and Trustworthiness:** For safety-critical systems like AVs, decisions must be interpretable and trustworthy. Many existing anomaly detection methods lack clear metrics for their performance quality. A significant motivation is to equip the detector with explicit performance measures, specifically guarantees on false-positive rate (FPR) and false-negative rate (FNR). Such metrics facilitate understanding the detector's reliability and enable systematic calibration.

In summary, the key objectives are to formalize a notion of anomaly that captures task-relevance, develop a fast and efficient detector with guaranteed FPR and FNR, enable data-free calibration, and demonstrate its efficacy in real-world driving scenarios. These objectives collectively aim to create a practical and trustworthy safety mechanism for AV prediction modules.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on defining and detecting a "p-quantile anomaly" by analyzing the impact of trajectory prediction errors on the AV's planning cost.

#### 3.1. Problem Definition and Cost Formulation

The AV's environment and dynamics are modeled as a Partially-Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The state space `X` includes the ego agent's state, non-ego agents' states, and environmental maps. The core of the approach relies on a cost function, `c_t: X × U → [0, ∞)`, where higher values indicate worse or less safe outcomes for the AV. This framework is agnostic to the specific choice of cost function. The prediction module, given a history of state estimates, provides a distribution `ψ` over future non-ego agent trajectories. This distribution, in turn, induces a sequence of distributions `φ` on the predicted cost `ĉ_t+τ` for each future time step `τ`. The "observed cost," `c_?_t+τ`, represents the actual cost estimated by the AV at time `t+τ` based on perceived states and control inputs.

#### 3.2. p-Quantile Anomaly

The central concept is the "p-quantile anomaly," defined as follows: for any time step `t+τ`, if the observed cost `c_?_t+τ` lies within the top `p`-quantile of the predicted cost distribution `φ_t+τ(ĉ_t+τ | x_0:t)`, then a p-quantile anomaly exists. This definition inherently captures "task-relevance" because it directly relates prediction errors to their impact on a cost function that reflects AV safety and performance. If the observed cost is unexpectedly high relative to the predicted distribution, it signals a potentially harmful misprediction.

#### 3.3. p-Quantile Anomaly Detector (QAD) Algorithm

Since an analytical expression for `φ` (the predicted cost distribution) is generally unavailable due to non-linear cost functions and complex prediction models, a sampling-based approach is used.
- **Sampling:** At each time step `t+τ`, `M` predicted costs `S_t+τ = {ĉ^1_t+τ..., ĉ^M_t+τ}` are sampled independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) from `φ_t+τ`. These samples are then arranged in increasing order.
- **Detection Criterion:** The detector `D` triggers a positive detection if the observed cost `c_?_t+τ` is greater than or equal to at least `M-n` of the `M` sampled predicted costs, i.e., `D(n, S):= c_?_t+τ ≥ ĉ^M-n_t+τ`. The parameter `n` is an integer in `{0..., M-1}`. Intuitively, this checks if the observed cost ranks unexpectedly high among the predicted costs.
- **Operational Flow (Algorithm 1):** The QAD is executed at each planning cycle. It reuses trajectory samples already drawn by the prediction network for planning purposes, minimizing computational overhead. For each non-ego agent and each time step `τ` in the prediction horizon, the observed cost `c_?_w` is computed, and if it exceeds the `(M-n)`-th ranked predicted cost `ĉ^M-n_w`, the detector returns `True` along with the ID of the responsible agent. The detection computation itself is reported to take less than 0.1 milliseconds.

#### 3.4. Bounds on False-positive and False-negative Rates

To provide guarantees and interpretability, the paper defines and derives bounds for the False-Positive Rate (FPR) and False-Negative Rate (FNR):
- **FPR:** The probability of detecting a p-quantile anomaly when one does not truly occur (`c_?_t+τ < c_a_t+τ(p)`, where `c_a_t+τ(p)` is the `p`-quantile cost). The bound `FPR ≤ Σ(i=0 to n) (M choose i) * p^i * (1-p)^(M-i)` is derived.
- **FNR:** The probability of *not* detecting a p-quantile anomaly when one truly occurs (`c_?_t+τ ≥ c_a_t+τ(p)`). The bound `FNR ≤ Σ(i=n+1 to M) (M choose i) * p^i * (1-p)^(M-i)` is derived.
These bounds are based on the assumption that whether a sample from `φ` falls into the top `p`-quantile is a Bernoulli trial. This theorem provides a quantitative measure of the detector's reliability.

#### 3.5. Data-free Detector Calibration

The analytical bounds for FPR and FNR allow for "data-free calibration." A user can specify desired upper bounds for FPR or FNR, and then `M` and `n` can be chosen accordingly without requiring a large dataset for empirical calibration. Acknowledged limitation of the analysis is that strong bounds cannot be simultaneously provided for both FPR and FNR (the sum of the bounds equals 1). However, empirical results suggest that the detector can achieve low FPR and FNR concurrently in practice.

#### 3.6. Planner and Cost Functions

- **Planner:** A motion planner based on a motion primitive tree is used. It generates discrete control actions, creating a tree of possible trajectories. The planner selects the trajectory that minimizes a weighted sum of multiple cost functions, utilizing trajectory forecasts from Trajectron++ for other agents.
- **Cost Terms:** The cost function `c_t+τ` is a weighted sum of seven terms:
 1. `c_ttc`: Time-to-collision cost.
 2. `c_d2a`: Momentum-shaped distance to other agents.
 3. `c_d2g`: Distance to goal.
 4. `c_d2r`: Distance to reference trajectory.
 5. `c_velocity`: Speed limit cost.
 6. `c_comfort`: Ego comfort cost (acceleration, jerk, rotational velocity/acceleration).
 7. `c_reverse`: Penalty for reversing.
These cost terms are computed for both predicted and observed states.

#### 3.7. Trajectory Prediction Module

Trajectron++, a state-of-the-art multi-agent trajectory forecasting model based on a graph-structured recurrent neural network and a conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE), is employed to predict non-ego agent motions.

#### 3.8. Comparison Methods

The QAD is benchmarked against four other detection approaches:
1. **Likelihood detection:** Triggers when the likelihood of observed states under the predicted Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is below a threshold.
2. **Uniform and Partial Cost Degradation Tests (UDT and PDT):** Use `p`-values to detect shifts in the cost distribution, comparing a weighted mean of reference signals to the online signal.
3. **Time-to-Collision (TTC) detector:** Triggers when TTC drops below a threshold.
4. **Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis:** Triggers if a collision is possible under adversarial non-ego agent behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed p-quantile Anomaly Detector (QAD) was evaluated through experiments on the nuScenes and nuPlan teaser datasets, comparing its performance against several baseline methods.

#### 4.1. Task-relevant Detection (Run-time Monitoring)

- **Computational Efficiency:** The anomaly detection computation was found to take less than 0.1 milliseconds (ms), demonstrating its suitability for real-time monitoring within typical AV planning cycles, where cost evaluations for planning take approximately 1 ± 0.5 ms.
- **Dataset and Labeling:** Experiments were conducted on 260 nuPlan driving scenarios, totaling 2568 prediction horizons. Scenarios were hand-labeled for task-relevant prediction errors by visual inspection, ensuring independence from the detection methods. The dataset exhibited a significant imbalance, with only 3.5% of scenarios labeled as positive for task-relevant failures.
- **ROC Curve Analysis:** The Receiver Operator Characteristic (ROC) curve (Fig. 3) indicated that QAD performed closest to an ideal detector, achieving the highest Area Under the ROC (AUROC) curve. The mean AUROC for QAD was 0.946 ± 0.002.
- **Optimally Calibrated Detector Performance (Table 1):** When calibrated to their best empirical operating points (farthest from the y=x line on the ROC curve), QAD demonstrated a False Positive Rate (FPR) of 8.4% ± 0.4% and a False Negative Rate (FNR) of 7.9% ± 0.7%. This constituted the lowest FPR among all compared methods, and the third-lowest FNR (slightly higher than PDT and HJ Reachability, which had higher FPRs). In contrast, the Likelihood baseline performed poorly (AUROC 0.693), and the HJ Reachability detector was notably conservative (FPR 23.7%).
- **Data-free Calibration Performance (Table 2):**
 - Using Theorem 1 for data-free calibration, with a target anomaly `p`-quantile of 5% (a common statistical threshold), two versions of QAD were tested: one calibrated to bound FPR below 5% and another to bound FNR below 5%.
 - The FPR-bounded QAD successfully satisfied its theoretical bound, achieving an empirical FPR of 3.4% ± 0.3%.
 - The FNR-bounded QAD, however, violated its theoretical bound, showing an empirical FNR of 13.9% ± 1.1% (for a 5% FNR bound). This disagreement between theoretical bounds and empirical performance for FNR was attributed to a potential mismatch between the theoretical `p`-quantile anomaly criterion and the human perception of an anomaly during labeling, as well as the dataset's imbalance.
 - Despite this, the FNR-bounded QAD performed best among all data-free calibrated detectors in terms of distance from the y=x line (favoring FNR). This suggests that even with an uninformed quantile choice and without training data, the detector can be calibrated to perform effectively.
 - Ensemble detectors, combining QAD with UDT, PDT, and HJ Reachability, also showed competitive performance, with an FNR ensemble detector having a favorable balance (FPR 15.2%, FNR 8.7%).

#### 4.2. Filtering Expert Driving Logs

- **Task and Dataset:** The detector's ability to filter "interesting" scenarios from unlabeled human-driven driving logs (for robustifying prediction networks or diagnostic evaluations) was evaluated using 375 nuPlan and 152 nuScenes scenarios (3890 prediction horizons), all without collisions. A proxy cost function based on momentum-shaped distance and time-to-collision was used, as the human driver's true cost function was unavailable.
- **Detection Rates (Table 3):** Likelihood detection (58.5% detected) and TTC (17.4% detected) showed substantially higher detection rates than other methods, suggesting they are more prone to false positives in benign scenarios. QAD (FPR) and UDT detected similar percentages (5.4%), while QAD (FNR) detected 6.4%.
- **Qualitative Assessment (Fig. 4):** Qualitatively, QAD, UDT, and PDT effectively identified interesting scenarios, such as unexpected pedestrian movements or close-proximity interactions that might be overlooked by simpler metrics. In contrast, HJ Reachability and Likelihood detection frequently flagged scenarios as anomalous even when predictions were accurate and no inherent unsafety was present (e.g., standard car-following or turning scenarios). This indicated that QAD, along with UDT and PDT, exhibited a better alignment with human intuition regarding "interesting" or potentially problematic situations, avoiding the over-conservatism of some baselines.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous vehicles by proposing a novel, task-relevant failure detection mechanism for trajectory predictors that is both theoretically grounded and practically efficient. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced AV Safety:** The primary impact is the direct improvement of AV safety. By specifically identifying "harmful" prediction failures—those that propagate into increased planning costs and potentially unsafe AV actions—the detector enables timely interventions. This capability is crucial for mitigating risks associated with the inherent uncertainties in forecasting the behavior of other road users.
- **Improved AV Performance and Reliability:** The task-relevant nature of the detector prevents unnecessary interventions. Unlike overly conservative safety mechanisms that trigger on benign prediction errors, this detector only acts when the observed cost is significantly worse than predicted. This balance ensures that AV functionality and efficiency are not unduly compromised, leading to smoother, more natural, and more reliable autonomous driving experiences.
- **Increased Trustworthiness and Interpretability:** Providing analytical guarantees on False-Positive Rate (FPR) and False-Negative Rate (FNR) is a key advancement. These guarantees allow AV developers and regulators to quantify the detector's reliability, fostering greater trust in the system. The ability for data-free calibration, though with some analytical limitations, further simplifies its deployment and parameter tuning, making it more practical for real-world applications where extensive labeled failure data might be scarce.
- **Modular and Efficient Integration:** The design of the detector as an independent module is significant. It avoids the need to modify existing, often complex, prediction networks, which can then continue to optimize solely for prediction accuracy. Furthermore, its computational efficiency, achieved by reusing trajectory samples already generated for planning, minimizes added computational overhead, making it suitable for real-time AV stacks.
- **Facilitating Diagnostic and Development Processes:** The detector's ability to filter "interesting" scenarios from unlabeled driving logs provides a valuable tool for AV development. It can help engineers quickly identify specific situations where prediction models struggle or where the AV's planning might be challenged, thereby guiding efforts to robustify prediction networks, improve planning algorithms, or conduct targeted diagnostic evaluations.
- **Potential for Adaptive Systems:** The research suggests a future impact in enabling adaptive planning frequencies. By monitoring the "health" of predictions, the detector could signal when a re-plan is genuinely necessary, freeing up computational resources for other AV stack modules and potentially lowering energy consumption. This could lead to more resource-efficient AV operation.
- **Foundation for Causal Explanations:** While not fully explored in this paper, the work lays a foundation for providing causal explanations for detections (e.g., "the pedestrian was anticipated to stand still but moved quickly into our path"). Such explanations could further enhance an AV's ability to react intelligently to unexpected events.

In conclusion, this research presents a practical, theoretically sound, and computationally efficient solution for a critical problem in autonomous driving, addressing both safety and performance concerns with a focus on interpretability and modularity.
