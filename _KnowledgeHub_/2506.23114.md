# Minimizing Acoustic Noise: Enhancing Quiet Locomotion for Quadruped Robots in Indoor Applications

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University developed MUTE, a deep reinforcement learning framework that optimizes quadruped robot gait and control to significantly reduce acoustic noise during indoor locomotion. The method achieved an average noise reduction of 8 dBA, corresponding to a 2.5-fold decrease in sound pressure, on various surfaces without requiring hardware modifications, and allows for a dynamic trade-off between speed and quietness.

## Problem

- Current quadruped robots generate disruptive acoustic noise, limiting their practical deployment in noise-sensitive indoor environments like hospitals and offices.
- High noise levels negatively impact human well-being, productivity, and the perception of robots in human-robot interaction contexts.
- Existing locomotion research primarily focused on motion control and terrain adaptability, often overlooking the significant noise generated by repetitive foot-ground impacts.

## Method

- Introduced MUTE, a deep reinforcement learning framework that optimizes gait design and control strategies to minimize the foot's velocity at the moment of ground contact, thereby reducing impact noise.
- Utilized an Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) architecture with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), integrating a continuous phase variable and a forward prediction model for accurate foot-ground contact estimation.
- Incorporated a scalar 'quiet factor' (β) into the policy and reward function, allowing the robot to dynamically balance the trade-off between locomotion speed and acoustic noise reduction.

## Results

- MUTE with β=1 achieved an average Mean Noise Level (MNL) of 64.80 dBA and Peak Noise Level (PNL) of 72.97 dBA, demonstrating an average noise reduction of approximately 8 dBA compared to baseline methods.
- The quiet factor (β) successfully enabled a tunable trade-off: increasing β reduced MNL from 77.85 dBA (β=0) to 73.18 dBA (β=1), albeit with a minor increase in velocity tracking error.
- During a 91.7-meter long-distance test on mixed indoor surfaces, the robot maintained an MNL of 68.25 dBA, remaining below the 70 dBA threshold considered safe for human hearing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Acoustic noise in quadruped robots can be effectively mitigated through software-based motion control algorithms, specifically by reducing foot-ground impact velocity, without needing hardware modifications.
> - Integrating a continuous phase estimation network within a deep reinforcement learning framework enables precise control over foot-ground contact dynamics for noise minimization.
> - A 'quiet factor' parameter allows for flexible, application-specific adjustments, enabling the robot to prioritize either quietness or speed based on environmental demands.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of quadruped robotics has made advancements in robot locomotion, particularly in the ability to traverse complex and unstructured outdoor environments. Research leveraging methods such as Reinforcement Learning has enabled quadruped robots to navigate challenging terrains, making them suitable for applications like search and rescue, exploration, and various service industries.

However, the integration of these robots into indoor, noise-sensitive human environments, such as hospitals, offices, and residential spaces, presents a specific challenge related to acoustic noise generation. While significant efforts have been directed towards motion control and terrain adaptability, the noise produced during operation has not been a primary focus in much of the existing research. This oversight can limit the practical deployment of quadruped robots in settings where maintaining low noise levels is critical.

Previous studies have indicated that noise pollution in indoor environments can negatively impact human well-being, productivity, and recovery in healthcare settings. Furthermore, in human-robot interaction (HRI) contexts, the acoustic characteristics of robots influence user perception, with quieter robots generally perceived as more competent and comfortable. The primary source of noise in quadruped robots during locomotion is often identified as the repetitive impact of their feet on the ground, especially on hard surfaces. Motor and joint mechanisms also contribute, though their impact can be less significant at typical indoor operating speeds.

This study addresses the gap in existing research by focusing specifically on the optimization of acoustic noise generated by quadruped robots. Unlike approaches that involve hardware modifications to reduce motor noise or damp foot impacts, this work concentrates on developing advanced motion control algorithms. By optimizing gait design and control strategies, the research aims to mitigate foot-ground impact noise through software-based solutions, thereby enhancing the suitability of existing quadruped robot platforms for quiet indoor operation without requiring physical redesign. This positions the work as a contribution to the field of robotic control and HRI, enabling broader and more seamless integration of quadruped robots into human-centric indoor environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to optimize the acoustic noise generated by quadruped robots during locomotion in indoor environments. This is driven by the recognition that while quadruped robots have demonstrated substantial capabilities in traversing diverse and complex terrains, their widespread adoption in noise-sensitive indoor settings is constrained by the noise they produce.

The specific motivations and objectives include:

- **Addressing the Limiting Factor of Noise in Indoor Deployment:** The paper highlights that current quadruped robots, despite their versatility, generate excessive noise that is often disruptive in human-centric indoor areas. Environments such as hospitals, offices, and residential spaces demand low noise levels for patient recovery, employee productivity, and resident comfort. The current noise profiles of these robots impede their practical deployment in such scenarios, making noise reduction a critical enabler for wider adoption.
- **Improving Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** Noise significantly impacts the user experience in HRI. Citing prior research, the authors note that quieter robots are perceived more positively, suggesting greater competence and eliciting less discomfort. Minimizing acoustic noise is therefore crucial for fostering more seamless and acceptable interactions between humans and robots.
- **Focusing on Foot-Ground Impact Noise:** The primary source of significant acoustic noise in quadruped robots during locomotion is identified as the repetitive impact of the robot’s feet striking the ground, especially on hard surfaces. While motor and joint mechanisms also contribute, their noise levels are generally lower at typical indoor operating speeds and are often addressed by hardware design. This study specifically targets the more dominant foot-ground impact noise through control algorithms, without altering the robot’s physical structure.
- **Developing Advanced Motion Control Algorithms for Noise Minimization:** The core objective is to propose a novel approach, termed Minimizing Acoustic Noise (MUTE), that achieves smoother and quieter movement through optimized gait design and tailored control strategies. This involves fine-tuning gait patterns, adjusting the timing and velocity of foot-ground contact, and dynamically controlling limb movements to reduce noise output while maintaining locomotion stability and efficiency.
- **Establishing a Comprehensive Evaluation Framework:** To quantitatively assess the effectiveness of the proposed method, the study aims to establish a framework for measuring robot noise levels. This includes defining metrics such as Mean Noise Level (MNL) and Peak Noise Level (PNL) in dBA and evaluating performance across diverse indoor surface materials and locomotion speeds.
- **Demonstrating Effectiveness in Real-World Scenarios:** A key objective is to experimentally validate the MUTE approach in real-world indoor environments. This involves comparing its noise reduction performance against baseline methods at equivalent locomotion speeds and assessing its ability to maintain low noise levels during prolonged usage, ultimately demonstrating its suitability for deployment in noise-sensitive settings without detrimental effects on human health.
- **Introducing a "Quiet Factor" for Adaptability:** The study aims to introduce a "quiet factor" (β) that allows the robot to dynamically balance the trade-off between locomotion speed and noise reduction. This factor provides flexibility, enabling the robot to prioritize quiet operation when necessary, while still allowing for faster movement when noise is less of a concern.

By pursuing these objectives, the research seeks to overcome a significant barrier to the widespread integration of quadruped robots into various indoor applications, enhancing their social acceptance and utility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on minimizing the acoustic noise generated by quadruped robots during locomotion, primarily by controlling the foot's velocity at the moment of ground contact. This approach is based on the principle that the amplitude of impact-generated sound is proportional to the square root of the impact energy, which in turn is related to the foot's kinetic energy (E = 1/2 mv^2). Therefore, minimizing the foot’s velocity just prior to contact is proposed as an indirect method to reduce noise.

The approach, termed MUTE (Minimizing acoUsTic noisE), integrates optimized gait design with tailored control strategies within a deep reinforcement learning framework.

**Problem Formulation:**
The problem is modeled as an infinite-horizon Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), represented by M = (S, A, T, R, Ω, O, γ). The agent's policy (π) selects an action (a_t) based on the current state (s_t), aiming to maximize the expected cumulative reward. Historical sequences of observations (o_H_t) are used to capture temporal dependencies, with their implicit encoding represented by a latent variable (z_t).

**Phase Estimation:**
Accurate estimation of foot-ground contact moments is crucial. A continuous phase variable (φ_t,i ∈) is introduced for each leg, where φ_t,i = 0 signifies leg lift-off and φ_t,i = 1 denotes ground contact. This variable cycles through the swing (0 to 1) and stance (1 to 0) phases.
An LSTM network processes the historical observation sequence (o_H_t) to estimate the terrain height map (ˆh_t) and body velocity (ˆv_t). A forward prediction model, following the Variational Autoencoder (VAE) structure, is trained to represent the latent variable (z_t) and reconstruct the next observation (ˆo_t+1). Finally, an MLP network takes the estimated ˆh_t, ˆv_t, and z_t as input to predict the phase (ˆφ_t).
The phase estimation network is trained using a total loss function (L) comprising:
- **Estimation Loss (L_est):** Mean Squared Error (MSE) between estimated and true velocities and terrain heights.
- **Forward Model Loss (L_fwd):** Combines MSE for observation reconstruction and Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence to regularize the latent variable.
- **Phase Estimation Loss (L_φ):** MSE between predicted and true phase values, where true phases are derived from simulation.

**Quiet Factor (β):**
To manage the trade-off between speed and noise reduction, a scalar quiet factor β ∈ is introduced. β = 0 prioritizes speed, while β = 1 emphasizes noise minimization. This factor is integrated into the policy network as part of the command input (c_t), alongside desired velocities. It modulates the reward function: r = βr_φ + (1 - kβ)r_v + r_o, where r_φ is a phase-related reward for gait optimization, r_v is a task-related reward for velocity tracking, and r_o includes other terms. The parameter k (set to 0.2) controls the degree of speed reduction for noise minimization.

**Training Framework:**
An Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) architecture is employed, trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.
- **Policy Network:** The actor generates optimal actions (a_t) based on observations (o_t), estimated terrain height (ˆh_t), body velocity (ˆv_t), and the latent variable (z_t). Observations (o_t) include angular velocity, gravity vector, joint angles and velocities, previous action, and command input (c_t, which incorporates β). Actions (a_t) represent desired joint position offsets that are tracked by a PD controller.
- **Value Network:** The critic estimates the value function using the full state information (s_t), which includes o_t, ˆh_t, ˆv_t, z_t, and privileged information (o_priv_t) from the simulation (e.g., true terrain height, foot contact, phase ground truth).

**Reward Terms Design:**
The reward function is designed to promote noise reduction while ensuring stability and efficiency. Key components include:
- **Task-related rewards:** For linear and angular velocity tracking.
- **Phase-related rewards (r_φ):** Penalizes high foot velocity when dropping (as phase nears 1) and rewards foot elevation (as phase nears 0). This directly targets impact velocity.
- **Smoothness penalties:** For linear velocity (z), angular velocity (xy), joint power, joint acceleration, and action rate.
- **Safety penalty:** For collisions.
- **Body constraints:** For maintaining desired body height and orientation.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Simulation Training:** Unitree Go1 EDU quadruped robot, PPO algorithm, Adam optimizer, 4096 parallel agents in Isaac Gym. Training for 6000 episodes (approx. 8 hours on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090Ti GPU). Curriculum learning gradually increases terrain complexity.
- **Real-World Transfer:** The trained policy is directly transferred to a real Unitree Go1 EDU robot without further tuning. The robot uses only proprioceptive sensors. The control policy runs at 50 Hz on an Intel Core i9-12900H CPU.
- **Noise Measurement:** A sound pressure sensor mounted 30 cm above the ground measures noise in A-weighted decibels (dBA) at 20 Hz during robot locomotion.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Mean Noise Level (MNL) for overall noise and Peak Noise Level (PNL) for maximum noise events. Both are calculated from Sound Pressure Level (SPL) in dBA.
- **Compared Methods:** DreamWaQ (state-of-the-art), Built-in MPC (default Unitree policy), MUTE w/ β=0, and MUTE w/ β=1.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the MUTE approach's effectiveness in reducing acoustic noise across various indoor surfaces and speeds, and analyzed the impact of the quiet factor (β).

**Noise Reduction Performance at Equivalent Speeds (0.5 m/s):**
- **Consistent Outperformance:** MUTE consistently generated lower noise levels compared to both DreamWaQ and the Built-in MPC across all tested indoor surfaces: wooden flooring, carpet, and porcelain tiles.
- **Impact of Quiet Factor:**
 - **MUTE w/ β = 1** (prioritizing noise reduction) achieved the lowest noise levels. Average MNL was 64.80 dBA and average PNL was 72.97 dBA across surfaces. This represents an average reduction of approximately 7.78 dBA in MNL compared to the Built-in MPC (79.01 dBA) and 7.78 dBA compared to DreamWaQ (72.58 dBA).
 - **MUTE w/ β = 0** (prioritizing speed) still demonstrated lower noise levels than baseline policies. Average MNL was 68.91 dBA and average PNL was 76.52 dBA. This indicates that the MUTE framework provides inherent noise reduction even when not actively prioritizing it.
- **Surface-Specific Reductions:** The most significant noise reductions were observed on hard surfaces like wood and tiles, which are typically more prone to noise generation from foot impacts.

**Noise Reduction Across Different Actual Speeds (Figure 4):**
- **Speed-Noise Relationship:** Noise levels generally increased with the robot’s actual locomotion speed, as expected.
- **MUTE's Effectiveness Across Speeds:** MUTE with β = 1 consistently achieved the lowest MNL and PNL across the entire speed range tested (0.4 m/s to 1.0 m/s) on wooden flooring.
- **Average Reduction:** On average, MUTE reduced noise levels by approximately 8 dBA compared to baseline methods at comparable locomotion speeds. This corresponds to a 2.5-fold decrease in sound pressure.
- **MUTE w/ β = 0:** Even without prioritizing noise, MUTE still produced lower noise levels than the baseline policies, demonstrating the inherent benefits of the proposed control strategies.

**Quiet Factor (β) Analysis (Figure 6):**
- **Noise-Speed Trade-off:** Experiments with β varying from 0 to 1 (at a commanded speed of 0.8 m/s on wooden flooring) showed a direct correlation between β and noise levels.
- **Noise Reduction with Increasing β:** As β increased, the MNL decreased from approximately 77.85 dBA (at β=0) to 73.18 dBA (at β=1). PNL also consistently remained lower at higher β values.
- **Velocity Tracking Compromise:** Concurrently, the velocity tracking squared error showed a minor increase as β approached 1. This indicates that prioritizing noise reduction (higher β) involved a slight compromise in the robot's ability to precisely track commanded speeds, which aligns with the design of the reward function.

**Long-Distance Walking on Mixed Surfaces (Figure 5):**
- **Real-World Scenario:** The robot traversed a 91.7-meter path over mixed indoor surfaces (wooden board, ceramic tile, carpet, concrete) at an average speed of 0.36 m/s, taking 256 seconds.
- **Low Noise Maintenance:** The Mean Noise Level (MNL) recorded during this long-distance test was 68.25 dBA, with a Peak Noise Level (PNL) of 76.8 dBA.
- **Safety Threshold:** The recorded MNL of 68.25 dBA falls below the 70 dBA threshold generally considered safe for human hearing, suggesting no significant risk of hearing damage. The paper also noted that the perceived noise level by humans would be significantly lower than the trunk-mounted sensor reading due to sound attenuation with distance.

These results collectively indicate that the MUTE approach effectively reduces acoustic noise in quadruped robots, demonstrating consistent performance across different surfaces and speeds, and offering a tunable balance between quietness and locomotion efficiency through the quiet factor β.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper introduces a method for significantly reducing the acoustic noise generated by quadruped robots during locomotion, particularly in indoor environments. This work holds several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Expanded Applicability in Noise-Sensitive Environments:** The primary significance lies in enhancing the suitability of quadruped robots for deployment in environments where noise is a critical concern. This includes healthcare facilities (hospitals, clinics), professional workspaces (offices), and residential areas. By achieving an average noise reduction of 8 dBA, which corresponds to a 2.5-fold decrease in sound pressure, the robots become less disruptive and more acceptable for operation in close proximity to humans.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** Quieter operation can lead to more comfortable and positive human-robot interactions. As demonstrated by prior research, robots that are perceived as less noisy are often viewed as more competent and less discomforting. This work contributes to fostering greater trust and acceptance of quadruped robots in service and assistive roles.
- **Software-Based Noise Mitigation without Hardware Changes:** A notable aspect of this research is its reliance on optimized motion control algorithms rather than hardware modifications. This provides a cost-effective and adaptable solution, as existing quadruped robot platforms can benefit from enhanced quietness through software updates without requiring physical redesign or specialized noise-dampening components. This increases the practical deployability of current robot models.
- **Dynamic Balance between Performance and Quietness:** The introduction of the "quiet factor" (β) is a significant contribution, offering flexibility in robot operation. This allows operators to dynamically adjust the robot's behavior to prioritize either speed or noise minimization based on the specific requirements of the environment or task. For instance, in a library, a higher β can ensure maximum quietness, while in an industrial setting where speed is paramount, a lower β can be chosen.
- **Contribution to Robotic Control and Gait Optimization:** The methodology, which involves phase estimation, a specialized reward function, and an asymmetric actor-critic framework, demonstrates a novel approach to integrate an environmental constraint (noise) directly into the robot's learning and control architecture. This advances the field of robust and adaptive robot locomotion.
- **Long-Term Integration into Society:** By making quadruped robots less intrusive, this research facilitates their broader integration into daily life. Whether assisting in domestic tasks, delivering supplies in hospitals, or performing surveillance in quiet zones, the reduced noise levels contribute to making robots more seamlessly part of human society. The experimental validation showing noise levels below human hearing safety thresholds for prolonged operation reinforces this potential.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a foundation for further research into multi-objective robot control, where noise reduction can be integrated with other performance metrics such as energy efficiency, robustness, and maneuverability. Future studies could explore more sophisticated acoustic modeling or adaptive β tuning based on real-time environmental noise conditions.

In conclusion, this study offers a pragmatic and effective solution to a significant limitation in quadruped robot deployment, enabling these advanced machines to operate quietly and acceptably within human-centric indoor environments, thereby expanding their practical utility and fostering their integration into various service and assistive roles.
