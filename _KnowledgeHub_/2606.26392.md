# MPC-Injection: Biasing Off-Policy Locomotion RL Toward Controller-Induced Behavior Basins

> [!summary] Summary
> MPC-Injection biases off-policy reinforcement learning for robot locomotion by strategically incorporating Model Predictive Control-generated trajectories into the replay buffer. This method guides RL agents toward stable, deployable gaits defined by an MPC, achieving locomotion quality comparable to reward shaping and adversarial imitation learning while substantially reducing engineering complexity, with a 25% injection ratio proving consistently effective.

## Problem

- Off-policy Reinforcement Learning for robot locomotion often produces high-reward policies that exhibit kinematically or dynamically undesirable behaviors, rendering them undeployable.
- Guiding RL towards designer-preferred behaviors typically involves high engineering overhead through extensive reward shaping or complex imitation learning pipelines.
- Locomotion tasks frequently offer multiple high-return behavior basins, making it challenging for designers to specify a desired qualitative behavior using only simple, underspecified reward functions.

## Method

- Introduce ==MPC==-generated trajectories, exhibiting a desired locomotion behavior, into the off-policy RL replay buffer.
- Recompute rewards for MPC transitions using the RL task reward function, and sample minibatches uniformly from the combined buffer.
- Train standard off-policy RL algorithms (e.g., SAC, TD3) on this augmented data, implicitly guiding the actor and critic towards the MPC-preferred state distribution without additional loss terms or networks.

## Results

- Successfully biased policies for a 2D walker and a quadruped toward stable, designer-preferred gaits (upright walking, trotting) using a **25%** MPC-injection ratio, while pure RL converged to undeployable behaviors.
- Achieved comparable locomotion quality to traditional reward shaping and Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) methods, but with substantially lower engineering effort required for behavior specification.
- Showed greater robustness in sim-to-real transfer on a Go2 quadruped and applicability to novel morphologies like a 3-legged half-cheetah, where alternative biasing data is scarce.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicitly biasing the replay buffer's state distribution with controller data effectively steers off-policy RL towards designer-preferred behavior basins, even under underspecified task rewards.
> - An optimal intermediate injection ratio (around 25%) reliably transfers the desired behavior, ensuring stability and convergence while higher ratios can lead to erratic policy learning.
> - Existing MPC controllers, designed for desired dynamics and kinematics, can serve as a low-cost, effective interface for specifying high-level behavioral preferences to an RL agent.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust and deployable locomotion policies for robots using reinforcement learning (RL) is a significant challenge in robotics. A recurring issue is that RL policies, particularly for legged locomotion, often converge to behaviors that maximize a given reward function but are kinematically or dynamically undesirable for real-world deployment (e.g., limb vibration, torso scooting). These undesirable behaviors represent "locally optimal but undeployable" solutions within distinct "behavior basins" in the state-visitation space. The core problem this paper addresses is how to effectively and efficiently guide RL toward a designer-preferred behavior basin without incurring prohibitive engineering costs.

The current landscape for biasing RL toward desired behaviors in locomotion primarily relies on two approaches:
1. **Reward Shaping:** This involves manually designing and tuning additional reward terms to encourage or penalize specific motions. While effective, it typically requires extensive trial-and-error, is specific to a particular behavior, and necessitates retraining for changes.
2. **Imitation Learning (e.g., Adversarial Motion Priors - AMP):** These methods reduce the burden of reward engineering by using reference motion data. However, they introduce their own complexities, such as discriminator networks, kinematic retargeting of motion capture data, and the need for large, curated datasets. Human demonstration-augmented replay methods also fall into this category, but are limited by the practicality of collecting human demonstrations for diverse robots and tasks.

MPC-Injection proposes a third approach that leverages existing Model Predictive Control (MPC) systems. Prior work has explored combining MPC and RL in various ways, such as using RL as a residual policy for MPC, MPC for generating reference trajectories, or MPC for bootstrapping RL in sparse-reward or manipulation tasks. This paper positions MPC-Injection distinctly by focusing on *behavior biasing*—specifically, selecting among *distinct high-return behavior basins* under an *intentionally underspecified locomotion reward* by altering the replay buffer's state distribution. Unlike previous uses of controller-generated replay for improving return or accelerating exploration, this work provides an empirical characterization and analysis of its role in shaping the qualitative *type* of locomotion learned when multiple high-reward solutions exist. It aims to achieve the benefits of reward shaping and adversarial imitation learning without their associated engineering overhead, by transferring the controller's preferred behavior implicitly through the replay buffer, rather than explicit imitation losses or reward modifications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objectives and motivations of this research are rooted in addressing the fundamental discrepancy between reward maximization in reinforcement learning and the generation of practically deployable robotic behaviors:

1. **Address the Undeployable Behavior Problem:** The primary motivation stems from the observation that off-policy RL for legged locomotion often converges to high-return policies that exhibit undesirable behaviors, such as dragging limbs, scooting, or excessive limb vibration. These behaviors, while technically optimal under simple reward functions (e.g., velocity tracking), are unusable in real-world applications. The paper aims to find a method to steer RL away from these local optima towards qualitatively superior, deployable gaits.

2. **Enable Behavior Biasing with Underspecified Rewards:** Many locomotion tasks naturally admit multiple high-return solutions, which the authors term "behavior basins." The goal is to provide a mechanism for a designer to select a preferred behavior basin from these options, even when the task reward function is intentionally simple and underspecified, making it uninformative about the desired kinematic or dynamic style.

3. **Reduce Engineering Overhead Compared to Existing Methods:**
 - **Critique of Reward Shaping:** Reward shaping, while effective, requires extensive manual tuning of numerous reward terms. This process is time-consuming, highly specific to a desired behavior, and typically necessitates retraining if the behavior is modified. The motivation is to avoid this "heavy specification cost."
 - **Critique of Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) / Trajectory Tracking:** AIL methods, like Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), alleviate reward engineering but introduce significant overhead. This includes training a discriminator network, performing kinematic retargeting of motion capture data, and curating reference motion datasets. The research aims to develop an alternative that sidesteps these complexities.

4. **Leverage Model Predictive Control (MPC) for Behavior Specification:** The paper is motivated by the idea that if an MPC controller already exists for a desired behavior, or if one is easier to design than an elaborate reward function or motion dataset, its inherent behavior specification could be transferred to an RL policy. MPC naturally encodes desired dynamics and kinematics through its cost function and prediction model.

5. **Develop a Low-Overhead and Architecturally Minimal Solution:** A key objective is to achieve behavior biasing with minimal modifications to the standard off-policy RL pipeline. MPC-Injection aims to avoid adding new networks (like discriminators), new loss terms (like behavior cloning or imitation losses), or auxiliary objectives. Instead, it seeks to transfer the desired behavior purely through passively altering the state distribution in the replay buffer.

6. **Analyze Replay-Distribution Biasing for Basin Selection:** Unlike previous work that used controller-generated data primarily for improving return or bootstrapping in sparse-reward settings, this research specifically aims to empirically characterize and analyze the mechanism of replay-distribution biasing as a means for *selecting among distinct high-return behavior basins* in locomotion under underspecified rewards. This represents a novel analytical focus for this type of mechanism.

In essence, the research seeks a practical, efficient, and robust method to guide RL toward desirable, deployable locomotion gaits by implicitly leveraging the behavioral preferences of an MPC controller, thereby offering a competitive alternative to existing high-cost behavior specification techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MPC-Injection is a low-overhead method designed to bias an off-policy Reinforcement Learning (RL) agent toward a designer-preferred behavior basin by modifying the composition of the RL replay buffer. The core idea is that by introducing transitions from a Model Predictive Controller (MPC) that exhibits the desired behavior, the actor and critic networks of the RL agent are trained on a state distribution that includes the controller's preferred states, thereby implicitly steering the learned policy.

The methodology involves three main components: data generation, replay buffer injection, and the underlying off-policy RL framework.

1. **Data Generation:**
 - **MPC Controller:** An MPC controller is deployed in the same simulation environment used for RL training. This MPC is designed with an objective function to produce the desired locomotion behavior (e.g., a specific gait like trotting). The burden of behavior specification is thus shifted from the RL reward function to the MPC cost function.
 - **Offline Trajectory Collection:** Unlike some prior work that generates controller data online, MPC trajectories are generated *offline*. This allows for precise control over the ratio of MPC-to-RL transitions in the replay buffer.
 - **Variety of MPC Methods:** The method is demonstrated to be agnostic to the specific MPC technique used. For the 2D walker, a sampling-based MPC was employed, while for the Go2 quadruped, a gradient-based MPC (MPX model predictive controller) was utilized.
 - **Reward Recomputation:** Rewards for the MPC transitions are recomputed at injection time using the RL task reward function, rather than being stored with the MPC trajectories. This enables evaluation of different RL reward functions against the same pre-collected MPC dataset without regeneration.
 - **Action Space Alignment (Quadruped):** For the quadruped, where MPC outputs joint torques and the RL policy outputs target joint position residuals, a conversion step is necessary. MPC torques are transformed into equivalent position targets via inverse PD calculations before injection into the replay buffer. This conversion is an approximation, providing "approximate off-policy samples" rather than exact ones, but it is treated as a modeling approximation within the standard injection pipeline.

2. **Replay Buffer Injection:**
 - **Injection Ratio (p):** A key parameter, `p`, defines the target fraction of replay buffer entries that should originate from the MPC.
 - **Injection Mechanism:** After each on-policy transition generated by the current RL policy is inserted into the replay buffer, MPC transitions are added to the buffer until the empirical fraction of MPC-generated data (`|D_MPC|/|D|`) reaches the target ratio `p`.
 - **Uniform Sampling:** Minibatches for training the actor and critic networks are sampled uniformly from the combined replay buffer. Consequently, the expected fraction of MPC samples per gradient update also approximates `p`.
 - **No Additional Loss or Weighting:** Crucially, MPC transitions enter the learning process solely through their presence in the replay buffer. There is no separate imitation loss, no sample weighting, and no auxiliary objective function introduced into the RL algorithm. The off-policy agent's training process (e.g., using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) or Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3)) remains otherwise standard.

3. **RL Framework:**
 - **Off-Policy Algorithms:** The method is evaluated using common off-policy actor-critic algorithms, specifically SAC and TD3. The off-policy nature of these algorithms is critical, as they are designed to learn from transitions generated by *any* policy, not just the current policy, making them compatible with mixed data sources.
 - **Actor-Critic Updates:** The critic network learns to estimate Q-values on the augmented state distribution, which remains valid because the (s, a, r, s') tuples are valid samples of the environment's dynamics and reward function. The actor's objective, which maximizes Q-values for actions produced by the current policy at sampled states, is implicitly biased. By seeing MPC-visited states, the actor is optimized to take high-Q actions in regions of the state space that its own rollouts might not yet explore, pulling it towards the controller's preferred basin. It's important to note that the actor learns to take *its own* actions, not to clone the MPC's actions.
 - **Injection Ratio's Impact:** The analysis suggests an optimal injection ratio: too low, and the effect is negligible; too high, and the actor is trained on states it cannot consistently reach, leading to unstable learning. An intermediate ratio (e.g., 25%) allows the policy to be pulled towards the MPC basin while still refining its behavior within that basin through its own exploration.

The methodology emphasizes simplicity and efficiency, leveraging existing MPC capabilities and the inherent flexibility of off-policy RL replay buffers to achieve targeted behavior biasing without complex architectural or objective function modifications.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research primarily evaluated two claims: (1) MPC-Injection's ability to select qualitatively distinct locomotion basins under identical task rewards, and (2) its performance comparison against reward shaping and adversarial motion priors (AMP) in terms of locomotion quality and engineering overhead.

#### 4.1. Replay Buffer Distribution Biasing Under an Identical Reward

Experiments were conducted on a 2D walker (from DeepMind Control Suite) and a Unitree Go2 quadruped in simulation, using both SAC and TD3 for the walker and SAC for the quadruped.

- **2D Walker Results:**
 - **Multiple High-Return Solutions:** The walker's simple torso velocity-tracking reward (Equation 1) was confirmed to admit multiple high-return solutions, as various MPC-Injection ratios (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) converged to similar episodic returns (Figure 3). This highlights that return alone is not indicative of the learned behavior basin.
 - **Qualitative Behavior Shift:**
 - **Pure RL (0% injection):** Consistently converged to a "grounded scooting strategy," where the walker dragged its torso along the ground (Figure 4b, blue walker). The state-distribution embeddings (UMAP) for this policy appeared diffuse (Figure 4a).
 - **25% MPC-Injection:** Reliably learned an upright, periodic walking gait that matched the MPC behavior (Figure 4b, orange walker). Its state-distribution embeddings were compact and structured, indicating a well-defined and consistent behavior (Figure 4a).
 - **Optimal Injection Ratio:** Higher injection ratios (50%, 75%, 100%) failed to produce regular gaits, resulting in erratic behaviors like jumping and falling. The 100% injection ratio also led to a drop in episodic return. The 25% injection ratio was identified as the most reliable for inducing the controller's behavior basin, consistent with prior work in non-locomotion tasks regarding ideal injection ratios.
 - **Early Basin Separation:** Analysis of torso height distributions during training showed that with 0% injection, the walker's torso height eventually collapsed to ground-scooting values, while with 25% MPC-Injection, the torso height remained in an upright range from early training, indicating an early divergence in behavior basins (Figure 8, Table 9).

- **Quadruped Results:**
 - The Go2 quadruped exhibited a similar pattern to the walker.
 - **Pure RL:** Learned to vibrate its joints, leading to chaotic and irregular footstep trajectories (Figure 5a), and an undeployable gait (Figure 1, left).
 - **25% MPC-Injection:** Successfully learned a structured trotting gait with periodic footstep patterns (Figure 5b), qualitatively matching the MPC controller's behavior (Figure 1, right).
 - **Torque Usage:** MPC-Injection resulted in lower-torque behavior (Figure 5c), consistent with the energy-cost objectives of the MPC controller, further indicating a bias toward the controller's preferred dynamics.
 - An injection-ratio sweep on the quadruped corroborated 25% as the most reliable operating regime.

#### 4.2. Sim-to-Real Comparisons to Reward Shaping and AMP

The policies trained with MPC-Injection were qualitatively compared against policies trained with traditional reward shaping and Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), including sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree Go2 quadruped.

- **Locomotion Quality:**
 - All three methods (reward shaping, AMP, and MPC-Injection) produced comparable locomotion quality, resulting in stable trotting gaits (Figure 6, supplemental videos).
 - Specific gait characteristics differed: Reward-shaped policies produced a robust, stable gait with relatively hard foot strikes. AMP policies mimicked the energetic trot from retargeted dog motion capture data. MPC-Injection policies converged to a stable trot similar to reward shaping, but more closely resembling the simulated MPC controller.
- **Engineering Overhead:**
 - **Reward Shaping:** Required extensive manual tuning of twenty-one reward terms.
 - **AMP:** Demanded training an additional discriminator network, kinematic retargeting of motion capture data, and curated reference datasets.
 - **MPC-Injection:** Required a pre-existing MPC controller solving the same MDP, but left the RL objective unchanged, resulting in substantially lower engineering cost for behavior specification compared to the baselines.
- **Robustness and Failure Modes:**
 - **Reward Shaping:** Occasionally converged to unwanted behaviors (e.g., walking on knees) depending on the random seed.
 - **AMP:** Exhibited instability, consistent drift, and required multiple real-world trials to achieve stable velocity tracking without falling. It was also highly sensitive to perturbations.
 - **MPC-Injection:** Demonstrated stability under torso perturbations, similar to reward shaping, and avoided the instability issues observed with AMP.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Case Study on Unusual Robot Morphologies

A case study on a 3-legged half-cheetah demonstrated MPC-Injection's advantage for morphologies lacking reference datasets or human demonstrations.

- **Pure RL:** Converged to an undesirable two-legged walking strategy, leaving the third leg vestigial.
- **25% MPC-Injection:** Enabled the policy to utilize all three legs in a coordinated gait that mirrored the optimized MPC behavior, illustrating its utility when alternative behavior data sources are scarce.

In summary, MPC-Injection successfully biases off-policy RL toward designer-preferred locomotion behaviors using simple rewards, achieving qualitative performance comparable to more complex methods while significantly reducing engineering overhead and exhibiting greater robustness than AMP in some cases. The 25% injection ratio was consistently found to be effective across different robots and RL algorithms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MPC-Injection represents a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for robotic locomotion, offering a practical and efficient method for behavior biasing. Its primary impact lies in bridging the gap between reward-maximizing RL policies and the generation of truly deployable, desirable robotic behaviors.

1. **Reduced Engineering Burden for Behavior Specification:** The most direct impact is the substantial reduction in the engineering effort typically required to guide RL. By leveraging existing or easily designable MPC controllers, MPC-Injection bypasses the extensive manual tuning of reward functions (as seen in reward shaping with twenty-one terms) and the complex machinery of adversarial imitation learning (discriminator networks, kinematic retargeting, and motion data curation). This translates to faster development cycles and lower costs for deploying RL-based locomotion policies.

2. **Enhanced Practicality of RL for Locomotion:** The method makes RL more practical for real-world robotic applications. By consistently steering policies towards stable, human-interpretable gaits (like a trot) rather than locally optimal but undeployable behaviors (like scooting or limb vibration), it increases the likelihood that an RL-trained policy will be suitable for hardware deployment. The qualitative sim-to-real transfer on the Unitree Go2 quadruped underscores this practicality.

3. **Mechanism for Targeted Behavior Selection:** MPC-Injection provides a novel and effective mechanism for *behavior biasing*—selecting a specific, designer-preferred behavior basin among multiple high-return solutions under an underspecified reward. This allows researchers and engineers to use simpler, more general reward functions for task completion while relying on the MPC to define the *style* or *type* of locomotion.

4. **Applicability to Novel and Unusual Robot Morphologies:** The case study on the 3-legged half-cheetah highlights a critical advantage: MPC-Injection's utility when human demonstrations or motion capture datasets are impractical or impossible to obtain. For robots with unconventional designs, an MPC can often be designed more readily than collecting diverse human-like motion data, making MPC-Injection a powerful tool for developing behaviors for such systems.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** While acknowledging limitations (e.g., potential for drift over extended training, current focus on simple tasks), the work lays a foundation for future investigations into replay-distribution biasing. It opens avenues for studying curriculum-based injection, basin-aware early stopping, or critic regularization to maintain desired behaviors. Furthermore, exploring its robustness across broader command spaces, diverse terrains, and contact-rich tasks will expand its applicability.

In conclusion, MPC-Injection offers a valuable alternative for behavior specification in RL for locomotion. It positions standard controller design as an effective interface for transferring desired behaviors, enabling the development of deployable gaits with significantly reduced overhead when a suitable MPC is available or can be efficiently developed. This advances the state of the art in making RL a more accessible and reliable tool for robotics.
