# ZONDA: Zero-shot Object Navigation with Dynamic Avoidance in Multi-floor Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> ZONDA is a zero-shot object navigation framework designed for multi-floor and dynamic environments, employing a novel heuristic multi-floor planner, multi-view target verification, and explicit pedestrian avoidance. The system achieved state-of-the-art success rates of 66.5% on HM3D and 48.2% on MP3D for static multi-floor navigation, and 48.8% on the HM3D-DYNA dynamic environment benchmark, while demonstrating successful real-world deployment.

## Problem

- Traditional ObjectNav methods are constrained by closed-set vocabularies, poor generalization across different robot hardware, and the requirement for extensive task-specific training data.
- Existing zero-shot ObjectNav frameworks struggle with complex real-world scenarios, particularly in multi-floor environments where low-level stair traversal often relies on hardware-specific learned policies.
- Reliability issues persist in target object confirmation due to single visual observations being prone to false positives from visual distractors or cluttered backgrounds, leading to task failures in dynamic settings.

## Method

- **ZONDA** integrates an ==Object-Semantic-Traversability Map== for comprehensive environment representation, enabling open-vocabulary understanding and robust multi-floor navigation.
- A ==Heuristic Multi-Floor Planner== facilitates efficient exploration and cross-floor transitions by clustering frontiers, scoring exploration blocks, and identifying stairways through geometric and semantic cues, avoiding platform-specific learned controllers.
- ==Multi-View Target Verification== aggregates high-quality observations of candidate objects for VLM-based cross-examination, complemented by a ==Dynamic Pedestrian Avoidance== module that tracks, predicts, and plans around moving obstacles using ==Kalman filters== and ==MPPI==.

## Results

- ZONDA established state-of-the-art performance vs. ASCENT in static multi-floor navigation (**66.5%** vs. **65.4%** SR on HM3D, **48.2%** vs. **44.5%** on MP3D) and in dynamic-obstacle robustness on HM3D-DYNA (**48.8%** vs. **30.9%** SR).
- Ablation studies confirmed the crucial role of each ZONDA component: multi-view verification alone improved HM3D SR from **41.5%** to **66.5%**, and the block-based exploration and cross-floor navigation modules enhanced efficiency and task completion rates.
- Successful real-world deployment on a TITA wheel-legged biped robot demonstrated ZONDA's transferability from simulation, enabling effective dynamic pedestrian avoidance and object localization in an office environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling multi-floor navigation from platform-specific learned policies through heuristic planning and geometric traversability significantly enhances hardware generalization for embodied agents.
> - Aggregating multiple high-quality views and applying joint VLM reasoning for target objects substantially improves the accuracy and robustness of zero-shot target confirmation by mitigating single-view ambiguity.
> - Explicitly incorporating dynamic obstacle tracking and trajectory prediction capabilities is critical for safe and efficient navigation in human-shared environments, addressing limitations of static mapping approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses challenges within the field of Object Goal Navigation (ObjectNav), a foundational capability for embodied AI agents. Traditional approaches to ObjectNav often rely on supervised learning methods, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), which have demonstrated performance in simulated environments. However, these methods typically exhibit several limitations:
1. **Closed-set vocabulary**: They are restricted to recognizing object categories seen during training, limiting their applicability to novel objects.
2. **Lack of generalization**: The learned policies are often specific to a particular robot's kinematics and physical configuration, necessitating costly retraining when deployed on different hardware platforms.
3. **Dependency on extensive data**: Training requires significant amounts of task-specific interaction data, which can be expensive to acquire.

In response to these limitations, recent research has shifted towards **zero-shot ObjectNav**. This paradigm leverages pre-trained foundation models, such as large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs), to enable open-vocabulary semantic reasoning. The goal is to allow robots to identify unseen object categories and navigate in novel environments without requiring task-specific fine-tuning.

Despite advancements in zero-shot ObjectNav, current state-of-the-art frameworks encounter specific challenges that restrict their deployment in complex real-world settings:
- **Multi-floor environments**: Most existing zero-shot methods simplify 3D environments into 2D grids for computational efficiency and to utilize established 2D planners. This dimensionality reduction renders them ineffective in multi-floor buildings. While some efforts, like ASCENT, have addressed multi-floor navigation using LLM-driven frameworks, their low-level execution for stair traversal typically relies on RL-based PointNav policies. These policies are hardware-specific, requiring retraining if the robot's physical characteristics change.
- **Target confirmation reliability**: Current zero-shot methods often confirm target objects based on a single visual observation. This single-view approach is susceptible to false positives due to visual similarities, shadows, or cluttered backgrounds, leading to premature task termination and failure.
- **Dynamic environments**: The majority of ObjectNav benchmarks, such as MP3D and HM3D, assume static environments. In practical applications, robots must navigate safely alongside dynamic elements, particularly moving pedestrians. Traditional static mapping pipelines treat dynamic agents as fixed obstacles, which can lead to navigation failures in real-world scenarios.

ZONDA positions itself by specifically targeting these three core limitations, aiming to develop a zero-shot ObjectNav framework that is robust in multi-floor, dynamic environments and features improved target verification without reliance on hardware-specific learned controllers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for developing the ZONDA framework are derived from the identified limitations of existing ObjectNav methods, particularly in the context of zero-shot learning and real-world deployment:

1. **To enable robust navigation in multi-floor environments without platform-specific learned controllers**:
 - **Motivation**: Existing multi-floor zero-shot methods often depend on reinforcement learning (RL) policies for low-level tasks like stair traversal. These RL policies are tightly coupled to a specific robot's kinematics and configuration (e.g., chassis size, camera height). This dependency necessitates costly retraining for each different robot platform, hindering scalability and real-world adoption. The objective is to decouple the navigation system from specific hardware constraints for increased generalizability.

2. **To enhance the accuracy and reliability of target object verification**:
 - **Motivation**: Current zero-shot ObjectNav systems frequently confirm a target object based on a single visual observation. This single-view approach is prone to errors, where visually similar distractors, lighting conditions, or cluttered backgrounds can lead to false positive identifications. Such misidentifications result in premature task termination and navigation failure. The objective is to mitigate this single-view confirmation bias to improve the success rate of object goal navigation tasks.

3. **To facilitate safe and anticipatory navigation in dynamic, human-shared environments**:
 - **Motivation**: Most ObjectNav benchmarks and existing methods assume static environments, where obstacles remain fixed. In real-world indoor settings, robots must operate alongside moving entities, especially pedestrians. Treating these dynamic agents as permanent obstacles, as done by static mapping pipelines, leads to blockages, collisions, and navigation failures. The objective is to incorporate an explicit mechanism for perceiving, tracking, and predicting the movement of dynamic obstacles, enabling anticipatory avoidance behaviors for safer and more efficient navigation.

By addressing these specific challenges, ZONDA aims to advance the practical applicability of zero-shot ObjectNav, making embodied agents more capable and reliable in complex, unconstrained real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ZONDA is structured as a zero-shot object navigation framework designed to operate in multi-floor environments with dynamic obstacles. It integrates three primary modules built upon a foundational Object-Semantic-Traversability Map.

#### 3.1. Object-Semantic-Traversability Map

The framework maintains three interconnected map representations, each at a 0.1 m grid resolution:

1. **Object Map**:
 - Constructed from RGB-D images. Object detection and segmentation identify instances, whose masks are back-projected to 3D using depth data.
 - Each detected object receives an incremental confidence score (δ), which is a clipped value influenced by the detector confidence score (S_det), a penalty for observations near image edges (f_FOV), and an attenuation for objects too close or too far (f_dist).
 - These scores are accumulated into a sparse 3D voxel grid. A 2D object map is derived by projecting the 3D grid onto a bird’s-eye view, where each cell's semantic label is determined by the class with the maximum aggregated confidence along the vertical z-axis.

2. **Semantic Map**:
 - An LLM first infers the likely room type for a given target object.
 - The object name and inferred room type are embedded into normalized text vectors (v_obj, v_room) using a vision-language model (VLM).
 - For each RGB frame, the VLM extracts a global image embedding (v_img). An image-level semantic response (sim) is computed as the maximum cosine similarity between the image embedding and both the room and object embeddings.
 - This score is projected onto map cells within the current field of view (FOV), weighted by a confidence factor (W_FOV) that decays towards the edges. The map is then updated via an exponential moving average (α = 0.9) for temporal smoothing.

3. **Traversable Map**:
 - A 2D grid storing estimated ground height for each cell. Overhead structures (e.g., ceilings) are removed from the point cloud.
 - The average height of remaining points in a grid cell (after filtering points above vertical gaps) defines its local ground height (h̄).
 - A height-difference map (∆h) is computed by comparing each cell's ground height with its 8-connected neighbors.
 - Cells with ∆h < H_agent (the robot's maximum traversable height, platform-dependent) are classified as traversable (e.g., ramps, stairs); others are marked as vertical obstacles.

#### 3.2. Heuristic Multi-Floor Planner

This module provides a unified planning strategy for both single-floor exploration and cross-floor transitions.

- **Single-Floor Exploration**:
 - Safe frontiers (boundaries between explored and unexplored regions) are clustered into blocks using DBSCAN based on geodesic distances.
 - Each block (b_m) is assigned a score (V(b_m)) that considers the maximum semantic score within the block (s^(m)_max), the geodesic distance from the robot to the block centroid (d^(m)_center), and a semantic reward (R_sem(b_m)) added if any frontier in the block exceeds a similarity threshold (τ_sem).
 - The block with the highest score (b*) is selected, and other blocks are queued.
 - Within the selected block, the visitation order of frontiers is determined by formulating it as a Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP), solved using a greedy nearest-neighbor heuristic.

- **Cross-Floor Navigation**:
 - Activated when the target is not found, and all frontiers on the current floor are explored.
 - Stairways are identified by querying the Object-Semantic-Traversable Map: a cell is a stair candidate if its semantic label is "stairs" (with confidence ≥ δ_stair) and it meets the traversability condition (∆h < H_agent).
 - The robot plans paths through these verified stair regions, restricting movement to traversable cells.
 - After traversing a stair segment, the robot performs a 360° rotation to capture a panoramic image for VLM-based scene reasoning, confirming stair landing or new floor arrival.
 - Upon confirming a new floor, the previous Object-Semantic-Traversable Map is archived, a new one is initialized, and the connection (including 3D egress and entry positions) is registered in an undirected topological graph (G_topo) to enable bidirectional traversal without re-exploration.

#### 3.3. Multi-View Target Verification

To reduce false positives from single-view ambiguity, ZONDA employs a multi-view verification module.
- An observation buffer stores high-quality views for each potential target instance. View quality (Q) is determined by raw detection confidence (S_raw), a factor favoring large, clear observations (f_area), and a penalty for objects touching image boundaries (f_edge).
- When the robot reaches a candidate target location, the buffer is queried for the highest-quality views across multiple scales (e.g., near and far fields).
- These aggregated views are simultaneously sent to the VLM for cross-examination. This joint reasoning, combining scene context with detailed appearance, allows the VLM to distinguish between the actual target and visually similar distractors, improving final confirmation accuracy.

#### 3.4. Dynamic Pedestrian Avoidance

This module runs in parallel with the static mapping process to handle moving pedestrians.
- Pedestrians are detected and segmented from RGB-D frames. Their 3D centroids are extracted by back-projecting depth points within instance masks.
- These centroids are tracked using a constant-velocity Kalman filter (state: x, y, v_x, v_y), with data association handled by the Hungarian algorithm based on Euclidean distance.
- Future pedestrian positions are extrapolated over a prediction horizon (T_pred = 3.0 s, at 0.5 s intervals) using the tracked velocities.
- Both detected pedestrians and their predicted trajectories are projected onto the occupancy grid, inflated with a 0.50 m safety radius, and treated as dynamic obstacles.
- The Heuristic Multi-Floor Planner selects the next waypoint, and A* search is used for global path planning around these dynamic obstacles.
- For execution, a dual strategy is adopted: discrete commands for discrete simulation environments, and an MPPI (Model Predictive Path Integral) local planner for continuous velocity commands in real-world scenarios, ensuring smooth path tracking and proactive avoidance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ZONDA was conducted across various scenarios, including static multi-floor environments, dynamic environments, ablation studies, and real-world deployment.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets**: Habitat simulator with HM3D (20 multi-floor scenes, 2,000 validation episodes, 6 COCO object categories) and MP3D (11 multi-floor scenes, 2,195 validation episodes, 21 open-vocabulary categories).
- **HM3D-DYNA**: A novel benchmark extending HM3D by adding a moving pedestrian to each episode, spawning 3-5m from the robot and moving towards the target at 0.5 m/s.
- **Action Space**: Discrete actions in simulation ({MOVE FORWARD (0.25 m), TURN LEFT (30°), TURN RIGHT (30°), STOP}). A* search converts waypoints into motion sequences.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL).
- **Implementation Details**: SegFormer and SAM2 for segmentation, RT-DETR (COCO), Grounding DINO (HM3D), and OWLv2 (MP3D) for object detection. Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B for semantic score map embeddings and Qwen3-VL-Flash for multi-view verification. Experiments utilized a single NVIDIA RTX 5060 GPU.

#### 4.2. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Algorithms in Static Multi-Floor Environments

ZONDA was compared against state-of-the-art learning-based and zero-shot ObjectNav algorithms on HM3D and MP3D benchmarks.
- **HM3D**: ZONDA achieved an SR of 66.5% and an SPL of 33.0%. It surpassed the strongest zero-shot baseline, ASCENT (SR 65.4%, SPL 33.5%), in SR, and the learning-based PIRLNav (SR 64.1%, SPL 27.1%). While ASCENT had a slightly higher SPL, ZONDA's improved SR indicates greater success in confirming targets.
- **MP3D**: ZONDA established new state-of-the-art performance with an SR of 48.2% and an SPL of 21.5%. This significantly outperformed ASCENT (SR 44.5%, SPL 15.5%), demonstrating navigational efficiency and reduced redundant exploration in expansive, open-vocabulary environments.

#### 4.3. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Algorithms in Dynamic Environments

To evaluate robustness against dynamic obstacles, ZONDA was compared with ASCENT on the HM3D-DYNA benchmark.
- **HM3D-DYNA**: ZONDA achieved an SR of 48.8% and an SPL of 24.7%. In contrast, ASCENT, which lacks a dedicated dynamic perception module, exhibited a significantly lower SR of 30.9% and SPL of 16.6%. This performance difference highlights the efficacy of ZONDA's explicit pedestrian tracking and prediction for safe navigation in human-shared spaces.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Ablation experiments quantified the contribution of three key ZONDA components:
- **Heuristic block-based exploration**: Without frontier clustering and block scoring, ZONDA's performance decreased. On HM3D, SR dropped from 66.5% to 62.6% and SPL from 33.0% to 26.8%. On MP3D, SR fell from 48.2% to 38.6% and SPL from 21.5% to 13.4%. This indicates the importance of the block-based strategy for efficient exploration.
- **Cross-floor navigation**: When restricted to a single floor, ZONDA's SR on HM3D decreased from 66.5% to 57.8% (SPL from 33.0% to 28.5%). On MP3D, SR dropped from 48.2% to 44.2% (SPL from 21.5% to 20.7%). This confirms the benefits of cross-floor transitions, though the impact was less pronounced on MP3D where more targets are on the starting floor.
- **Multi-view target verification**: Removing multi-view verification, making the robot rely on a single detection view, resulted in substantial performance drops. On HM3D, SR decreased from 66.5% to 41.5% (SPL from 33.0% to 23.4%). On MP3D, SR fell from 48.2% to 30.2% (SPL from 21.5% to 11.6%). This demonstrates that aggregating multiple discriminative views significantly improves target confirmation robustness.

#### 4.5. Real-World Deployment

ZONDA was deployed on a physical Direct Drive Tech TITA wheel-legged biped robot using an off-board computing architecture (Intel Core i7-14700KF CPU and NVIDIA RTX 5060 Ti GPU) and ROS 2. For continuous motion control in the real world, MPPI was used as a low-level local planner. In an indoor office scenario, the robot successfully avoided a moving pedestrian, localized a "trash can," and terminated the task. This deployment demonstrates the system's transferability from simulation to a physical robot, maintaining a semantic map for global exploration and an object map for localization while adapting platform-dependent parameters.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ZONDA framework contributes to the field of embodied AI and robotics by addressing several long-standing challenges in zero-shot Object Goal Navigation.

- **Enhanced Real-World Applicability**: By explicitly addressing multi-floor navigation, robust target verification, and dynamic pedestrian avoidance, ZONDA moves zero-shot ObjectNav closer to real-world deployment in complex indoor environments. This integration of capabilities is a step towards making embodied agents more versatile and reliable outside of controlled laboratory settings.

- **Hardware Generalization**: The proposed Heuristic Multi-Floor Planner, which enables stair traversal using platform-dependent geometric constraints rather than platform-specific RL policies, represents a shift towards improved hardware generalization. This approach could reduce the need for extensive and costly retraining when deploying the system on different robot platforms, potentially lowering the barrier to entry for various robotic applications.

- **Increased Robustness and Safety**: The Multi-View Target Verification module significantly reduces false positives by leveraging multi-scale observations and joint VLM reasoning. This contributes to higher task success rates and greater reliability. Furthermore, the Dynamic Pedestrian Avoidance pipeline, with its explicit tracking and trajectory prediction, enables anticipatory behaviors that enhance safety in human-robot co-existence spaces, which is a critical requirement for public deployment.

- **Advancement in Zero-Shot Capabilities**: ZONDA extends the capabilities of zero-shot navigation by maintaining open-vocabulary semantic reasoning while simultaneously handling complex spatial and dynamic challenges. This builds upon the strengths of foundation models and demonstrates their potential for addressing practical robotic tasks without requiring task-specific training data.

The successful real-world deployment on a biped robot, alongside strong performance in challenging simulations, validates the framework's practical utility and transferability. Future work to extend the framework to a wider range of dynamic obstacles and outdoor environments could further broaden its impact, moving towards more autonomous and capable robotic systems.
