# Steerable Vision-Language-Action Policies for Embodied Reasoning and Hierarchical Control

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces Steerable Policies, Vision-Language-Action models capable of interpreting diverse command abstractions including tasks, atomic motions, and pixel coordinates. The work demonstrates that this enhanced steerability improves task generalization and enables more effective utilization of high-level VLM reasoning for robotic control, yielding superior performance in real-world manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Generalist robotic policies struggle to interpret and execute a wide range of open-ended commands, particularly due to limitations in how high-level VLM reasoning translates to concrete robot behaviors.
- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often follow formulaic and imprecise commands, leading to poor generalization, largely because their training data lacks diverse and detailed linguistic supervision.
- The traditional natural language interface between high-level VLMs and low-level VLAs acts as a bottleneck, hindering the transfer of fine-grained VLM deductions, such as precise spatial coordinates, into actionable robot commands.

## Method

- The authors introduce "**Steerable Policies**": VLA models trained to interpret a mixture of ==six distinct command styles==, including high-level tasks, subtasks, atomic motions, gripper traces (pixel paths), specific points (pixel coordinates), and combinations thereof.
- An automated, scalable pipeline was developed to synthetically generate millions of these diverse steering commands from existing robot demonstration data by leveraging large language models for subtask decomposition and rephrasing.
- Two hierarchical control methods are proposed: fine-tuning VLMs to act as ==embodied reasoners== that generate ==chain-of-thought rationales== and steering commands, and enabling off-the-shelf VLMs to use ==in-context learning== to dynamically select the most appropriate steering abstraction for the low-level policy.

## Results

- Steerable Policies, when guided by a human oracle using all command styles, achieved near **100%** success rates on Bridge tasks, demonstrating their inherent capability and responsiveness to diverse commands.
- When controlled by fine-tuned high-level embodied reasoners, Steerable Policies consistently outperformed **five baselines**, including prior ECoT variants and standard OpenVLA, showing significant gains in motion and semantic generalization.
- Interfacing Steerable Policies with off-the-shelf VLMs using in-context learning universally outperformed both standard OpenVLA and a SayCan-like baseline, with the VLM effectively adapting its command strategy and making corrective actions by switching between different abstraction levels.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Different steering command styles offer complementary strengths; for instance, trace and point commands are more effective for semantic generalization (e.g., interacting with out-of-distribution objects), while atomic motion commands excel in spatial relation tasks.
> - Improving low-level policy steerability is critical for unlocking and effectively transferring the extensive semantic knowledge, common-sense reasoning, and in-context learning capabilities of pretrained VLMs to real-world robotic tasks.
> - VLMs can leverage in-context learning to dynamically reason about and select the most appropriate steering abstraction, allowing for adaptive, corrective steering and more robust task progression in multi-step, long-horizon scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robotic policies capable of interpreting and executing open-ended commands represents a significant objective in robotics research. A prevailing approach involves integrating pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which provide extensive semantic and perceptual knowledge, into robotic control systems. This often takes a hierarchical form, where a high-level VLM processes broad task instructions and subsequently issues sub-commands to a lower-level policy, frequently a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model.

A key challenge within this paradigm is effectively translating the abstract reasoning and common-sense knowledge of VLMs into concrete robot behaviors. Previous methods commonly employ natural language task instructions as the interface between high-level VLMs and low-level VLAs. However, this linguistic interface has been identified as a limiting factor, restricting the extent to which VLM reasoning can precisely guide the low-level policy's actions. Existing VLA models, often trained on datasets with limited linguistic diversity, tend to follow formulaic, homogeneous, and imprecise commands, which hinders their ability to generalize and perform novel tasks requiring nuanced control.

Prior research has attempted to enhance policy steerability through various means, such as generating more diverse, detailed, or composable synthetic language labels, or incorporating non-textual steering modalities like gripper traces or visual marks. This paper distinguishes itself by proposing a system that trains VLAs on a broad spectrum of input types, rather than a single modality, and notably, expresses all these diverse steering commands within a text-token format compatible with generative VLMs. This differentiates it from approaches that might use a single, specialized steering input or mix multiple non-text modalities in a way that is less conducive to VLM integration. The work positions itself as a critical step in overcoming the bottleneck of insufficient policy steerability, thereby aiming to unlock the full potential of VLM capabilities for robust and generalized robotic control within hierarchical architectures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the development of generalist robotic policies that can execute a wide array of tasks by following open-ended commands. The authors identify a fundamental limitation in current hierarchical robotic control systems: the bottleneck of insufficient policy steerability. While pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) possess extensive semantic and visual reasoning capabilities, their utility in robotics is constrained if the low-level robot policy (VLA) cannot execute the detailed behaviors inferred by the VLM.

The motivation stems from several observations:
- **Limited VLA Command Following**: Existing VLAs typically exhibit limited ability to interpret and execute diverse commands. This limitation is primarily attributed to the datasets used for their training, which often contain labels that are too formulaic, homogeneous, and imprecise to specify the full range of behaviors required for novel or complex tasks.
- **Ineffective VLM Knowledge Transfer**: If a VLM, for instance, deduces that an object needs to be grasped at a precise location, this reasoning remains impractical if the underlying VLA cannot comprehend commands specifying such specific spatial coordinates. The traditional interface of high-level natural language task instructions proves inadequate for transferring fine-grained VLM reasoning to low-level robotic actions.
- **Untapped Dataset Potential**: Robot datasets, despite sparse and succinct task labels, implicitly contain rich information about semantics, interactions, and behaviors. The authors propose that this untapped potential can be leveraged by augmenting these datasets with more descriptive and detailed synthetic language, enabling the policy to flexibly invoke learned skills in novel contexts.
- **Need for Grounded Features**: Beyond densifying task descriptions, the ability to prompt policies with grounded features, such as pixel coordinates, is crucial for specifying behaviors related to out-of-distribution objects or for resolving ambiguities. Modern VLMs are capable of outputting such grounded features, making them a suitable modality for enhancing robot control if the low-level policy is capable of interpreting them.

To address these motivations, the key objectives are:
1. **Introduce Steerable Policies**: Develop VLAs capable of robustly following a significantly broader range of commands, encompassing not only high-level tasks but also subtasks, atomic motions, and grounded pixel coordinates.
2. **Scalable Command Generation**: Establish an automated, scalable pipeline to synthetically generate these diverse steering commands from existing robot demonstration data, thereby overcoming the cost and labor intensity of manual annotation.
3. **Enhance Hierarchical Control**: Propose and evaluate novel methods for VLMs to effectively control these Steerable Policies. This includes fine-tuning VLMs into embodied reasoners that generate chain-of-thought rationales and steering commands, and enabling off-the-shelf VLMs to leverage in-context learning to dynamically select the most appropriate steering abstraction.
4. **Improve Generalization**: Demonstrate that by improving low-level controllability, Steerable Policies can better unlock the pretrained knowledge within VLMs, leading to improved task generalization across various axes (in-distribution, motion, spatial, semantic, and long-horizon tasks) in real-world robotic manipulation experiments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology introduces **Steerable Policies**, which are Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models trained to interpret and act upon a diverse range of command abstractions. This approach aims to expand the interface between high-level VLM reasoning and low-level robot behaviors, facilitating improved generalization.

**A. Styles of Steering Commands**
The Steerable Policies are trained on a mixture of six distinct command styles, designed to be versatile, conducive to various control sources (human, high-level reasoners, in-context learning VLMs), and scalable for synthetic generation:
1. **Tasks**: Standard, high-level instructions (e.g., "put the carrot in the pot"). These enable the Steerable Policy to perform tasks typically handled by conventional VLAs.
2. **Subtasks**: Intermediate steps in a task (e.g., "reach for the carrot"). These commands facilitate the composition of existing semantic skills for novel task contexts.
3. **Atomic Motions**: Granular, low-level movements that do not inherently reference semantic scene content (e.g., "move left", "open gripper"). These allow for fine-grained control over robot kinematics.
4. **Gripper Traces**: A sequence of 2D pixel coordinates defining a path for the robot's gripper (e.g., "move from [x1, y1] to [x2, y2]"). This provides direct spatial guidance for movement.
5. **Points**: Specific pixel coordinates indicating positions of objects or areas of interest (e.g., "lift above <pot position>", "grasp at <carrot position>"). This is distinct from traces by specifying target locations rather than paths.
6. **Combinations**: Hybrid commands integrating multiple modalities (e.g., "move right from [x, y] to put the mushroom on the plate at [x, y]"). These commands enhance specificity and flexibility.

**B. Generating Steering Commands at Scale**
To acquire a sufficiently large and diverse dataset of steering commands, the authors developed an automated pipeline:
1. **Embodied Feature Extraction**: The pipeline begins by extracting grounded features from existing robot datasets (specifically, the Bridge WidowX dataset). This involves:
 - Programmatically extracting motion language using methods similar to Zawalski et al..
 - Identifying task-relevant object names and mapping them to segmentation masks using Molmo. These masks are then propagated across video frames using SAM2 to ensure temporal consistency for open-vocabulary bounding boxes.
 - Extracting the robot's gripper traces using a DETR model fine-tuned on manually annotated images.
2. **Subtask Decomposition**: Using the extracted motions and objects, Gemini 2.0 is queried to segment each robotic episode into a sequence of semantic subtasks.
3. **Diverse Command Generation**: For each identified subtask, Gemini 2.0 is queried again. This time, it restates the subtask into various equivalent steering commands across all six styles described above. Grounded features (gripper traces, object centroids) are provided in the prompt to facilitate the generation of precise, context-aware commands.
This pipeline expanded the original Bridge dataset from 38,000 "standard" task-level labels to approximately 206,000 subtasks and nearly 2 million total steering commands. These synthetically generated commands are then used to train the Steerable Policies via behavioral cloning, where a randomly sampled steering command replaces the standard language label for each frame. The training leverages the OpenVLA codebase with a Prismatic VLM 7B architecture.

**C. Hierarchical Methods with Steerable Policies**
The research proposes two primary methods for high-level VLMs to control the low-level Steerable Policies:

1. **Training High-level Embodied Reasoners**:
 - A VLM is fine-tuned to act as a high-level policy. Given a task instruction and the current observation, this VLM generates a chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, followed by an appropriate steering command (Fig. 4a).
 - **Rationale Generation**: To provide supervision for reasoning, Gemini 2.0 is queried to produce post-hoc rationales explaining *why* each subtask and corresponding command is appropriate for a given starting frame (Fig. 3, Appendix A-B).
 - **Training**: The high-level policy is trained using next-token prediction to autoregressively generate both the reasoning and the steering command.
 - **Inference**: The reasoner is queried less frequently (every N=5 environment steps) to output a command that the Steerable Policy executes. This approach is expected to transfer the VLM's generalized representations and reduce spurious biases.

2. **In-context Reasoning for Choosing Steering Abstractions**:
 - This method leverages the zero-shot in-context learning capabilities of off-the-shelf, API-based VLMs (e.g., Gemini 3.0).
 - **Prompting**: The VLM receives the robot's current observation, the overall task, detailed descriptions of each steering command style (including strengths and weaknesses), and a history of past observations and executed commands (Fig. 4b, Appendix B).
 - **VLM Role**: The VLM is prompted to (1) analyze the current scene, (2) determine the next logical robot action, and crucially, (3) reason about the most appropriate level of abstraction for the steering command.
 - **Inference**: The VLM predicts a steering command, which the Steerable Policy executes for N=20 steps before the high-level VLM is re-queried. This enables the VLM to adapt its command choices over time, learn from past outcomes, and translate its scene understanding into precise, actionable corrections, a capability not present in prior single-modality VLM-robot interfaces.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments address three research questions regarding Steerable Policies and their integration with hierarchical VLM control, evaluated on real-world robotic manipulation tasks from the Bridge WidowX dataset.

**A. Diverse Steering Commands Induce Performant and Compositional Behaviors in Steerable Policies**
This section evaluated the inherent capabilities of the Steerable Policy by using a human operator as an "oracle" high-level policy, capable of issuing diverse steering commands.
- **Oracle Performance**: When given unrestricted access to all command styles, the human oracle achieved near 100% success rates on Bridge tasks, establishing a high upper bound for achievable performance.
- **Complementary Strengths of Command Styles**: Restricting the human operator to individual command styles revealed that no single style consistently outperformed others across all generalization axes, indicating complementary strengths. Each individual style, however, surpassed the performance of standard task-level language alone.
 - **Trace/Point commands** exhibited superior performance in semantic generalization tasks, particularly for interacting with out-of-distribution objects where naming might fail but pointing is effective.
 - **Atomic motion commands** were most effective in tasks involving spatial relations. The policy demonstrated a tendency to interpret underspecified motion commands within a "manifold of reasonable actions," meaning it would move in the commanded direction while also considering contextual scene information (e.g., moving "left" might implicitly mean "move left to grasp the object").
 - **Task/Subtask commands** were reliable for in-distribution task components but did not achieve the highest performance in generalization settings.
These findings suggest that a Steerable Policy is responsive to varied command styles and highly performant when provided with appropriate, context-dependent steering commands.

**B. Steerable Policies Enable VLMs to Effectively Use Embodied Reasoning Training**
This experiment evaluated the performance of Steerable Policies when controlled by fine-tuned high-level embodied reasoners, comparing against prior methods that incorporate reasoning data into VLAs.
- **Superior Performance**: The approach of controlling Steerable Policies with learned high-level embodied reasoning VLMs consistently outperformed five baselines: standard OpenVLA and π0.5, two ECoT-Lite variants, and the full Embodied Chain-of-Thought (ECoT) Reasoning VLA.
- **Generalization Benefits**: Performance gains were most notable in motion and semantic generalization tasks. This is attributed to the ability of atomic motion, trace, and pointing commands to enable the VLM reasoner to maintain reliable control even under distribution shifts.
- **VLA Architecture Agnostic**: The benefits of steerability and VLM reasoning were observed across different VLA architectures (OpenVLA and π0.5-based Steerable Policies), suggesting that the proposed paradigm is broadly applicable.
- **Value of Explicit Reasoning**: While hierarchical control with Steerable Policies yielded performance benefits even without explicit reasoning training in the high-level VLM, integrating embodied reasoning training further improved generalization and overall performance.

**C. Steerable Policies Unlock In-context Reasoning in VLMs**
This section investigated whether off-the-shelf VLMs can more effectively leverage their in-context reasoning capabilities when interfaced with Steerable Policies, particularly for multi-step, long-horizon tasks.
- **Outperformance of Baselines**: The approach of using Steerable Policies with an in-context learning VLM universally outperformed both a standard OpenVLA and a SayCan-like baseline (which restricted the VLM to subtask language prompts). This performance gap primarily highlights the benefit of allowing the VLM to dynamically select from a full spectrum of steering abstractions.
- **Impact of Abstraction Choice**: The ability for the VLM to reason about *which* abstraction to use for steering the low-level VLA proved critical for performance gains over the SayCan-like baseline.
- **Effectiveness without Explicit Reasoning**: The proposed method also outperformed a non-reasoning ablation (where the VLM did not generate explicit intermediate thoughts), suggesting that even without overt reasoning steps, in-context learning VLMs are adept at issuing effective steering commands.
- **Qualitative Advantages**:
 - **Corrective Steering**: The VLM demonstrated the ability to adapt its command strategy through in-context learning. If an initial command (e.g., pointing) failed, it could switch to a different abstraction (e.g., motion command) to correct behavior and progress the task (Fig. 9a, 9b).
 - **Enhanced VLM Understanding**: Steerability allowed the VLM to translate its nuanced scene understanding (e.g., recognizing a specific object confusion or needing clearance for movement) and past error history into precise, actionable corrections, which were often impossible to achieve with subtask-level commands alone (Fig. 9c).
 - **Dynamic Abstraction Selection**: The VLM could dynamically reason about and select the most appropriate steering abstraction based on the current state (e.g., using a pointing command for initial object approach in a cluttered scene, then switching to a high-level "grasp" command once aligned) (Fig. 9d).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Steerable Policies, a novel paradigm for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that significantly expands their ability to interpret and act upon a wide spectrum of command abstractions. By enabling VLAs to respond to detailed instructions such as subtasks, atomic motions, and grounded pixel coordinates, the work addresses a fundamental bottleneck in current hierarchical robotic control systems where high-level VLM reasoning often exceeds the low-level policy's capacity for precise execution.

The primary significance lies in demonstrating that improved policy steerability is critical for effectively transferring the robust semantic knowledge, common-sense reasoning, and in-context learning capabilities of pretrained VLMs to real-world robotic tasks. The proposed methods for VLM control – both fine-tuning high-level embodied reasoners and leveraging off-the-shelf VLMs with in-context learning – demonstrate substantial performance improvements across various generalization axes and challenging long-horizon tasks. This indicates a more effective grounding of VLM intelligence in robot behaviors, moving beyond the limitations of traditional task-level language interfaces.

The potential impact of Steerable Policies is multifold:
- **Enhanced Generalization**: By allowing VLMs to select the most appropriate command abstraction, robots can exhibit more robust and flexible behaviors when confronted with novel objects, spatial arrangements, or complex multi-step tasks, thereby improving generalization capabilities.
- **Increased Controllability**: The ability to issue fine-grained commands, including pixel-level guidance, offers a level of control that can be crucial for precision manipulation and for recovering from errors where high-level language is insufficient.
- **Scalable Data Generation**: The automated pipeline for synthetically generating diverse steering commands addresses a major hurdle in robotics data collection, making the training of highly steerable policies more practical and cost-effective.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction**: A richer command interface could lead to more intuitive and effective human-robot collaboration, as operators or higher-level AI systems can provide instructions at the most natural level of abstraction for a given context.
- **Unlocking VLM Potential**: The work provides a pathway for "disembodied" VLM intelligence to be more fully realized in the physical world by offering the necessary expressive interface for VLMs to translate their advanced reasoning into actionable robotic policies.

Future work could explore further refining VLMs' understanding of Steerable Policy affordances, potentially through reinforcement learning, to enable models to autonomously determine the optimal steering style for new situations and tasks. This could lead to cross-task in-context learning, where the VLM transfers its understanding of how to effectively steer the robot to entirely novel environments. As robotic datasets continue to scale in behavioral diversity, the compositional nature of steering commands is expected to become even more critical for tackling increasingly complex and open-world robotic challenges.
