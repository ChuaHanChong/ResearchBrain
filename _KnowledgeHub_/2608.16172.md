# SparkVLA: Stop-Aware Hierarchical VLA with Adaptive Action Chunking for Long-Horizon Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> SparkVLA is a hierarchical VLA that unifies subtask-stop and action-prefix-length decisions into one ranking over a shared candidate set, scored via full self-attention and trained on offline ordinal preferences. It eliminates threshold tuning and separate stop detectors, reaching 47.12% success on RoboCerebra, beating the hierarchical baseline by 30.57 points.

## Problem

- Hierarchical VLA systems treat subtask termination as an isolated binary decision, judging whether to stop without access to the upcoming action plan or execution horizon.
- Adaptive action-chunking methods select execution length via RL heads or entropy cues but provide no mechanism to signal subtask completion.
- Stopping point and execution length are mutually dependent, yet existing architectures evaluate them with separate modules that cannot exploit this coupling.

## Method

- **SparkVLA**'s ==unified candidate ranking== formulates Stop and every action-prefix length as competing options scored by one head, removing threshold tuning and requiring only offline ordinal supervision.
- **Anchor-Conditioned Context Encoding** builds a history-aware subtask anchor via self-attention over prior subtask anchors, guiding ==Grid-Sampler== visual-token pruning toward task-relevant regions.
- **Stop-Aware Action-Prefix Selection** head applies full self-attention across all candidates plus a stop-aware auxiliary loss contrasting the Stop score against continuation scores via log-sum-exp.

## Results

- On RoboCerebra, SparkVLA reaches **47.12%** average success rate, exceeding the official hierarchical baseline HPE by **30.57 points** and reproduced Mem-0 by **26.83 points**.
- Ablations show unifying Stop into the candidate set lifts Stop accuracy from 80.37% to **96.65%** and success rate from 42.53% to **47.12%** versus an independent stop head.
- Real-robot trials on three multi-step tasks give **69.3%** average success versus 48.0% for pi-0.5, with the largest gain of **30 points** on the 12-subtask Tea Preparation task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Stopping and horizon-length decisions carry mutual information that separate modules structurally cannot access; joint ranking recovers gains neither module can achieve alone.
> - Conditioning termination scores on the full proposed action chunk via self-attention lets a stop decision use content the executor already planned, not just current-state features.
> - Boundary-only inference invocation plus command caching cuts compute 2x-3x while improving accuracy, showing efficiency and precision need not trade off in hierarchical control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation, particularly their application to long-horizon tasks. Recent VLA models have demonstrated generalization capabilities but often struggle with composing multiple skills over extended periods due to training on short trajectory segments.

Hierarchical VLA architectures have emerged as a method to tackle multi-step, long-horizon manipulation by pairing a high-level VLM planner with a low-level VLA executor. These systems employ various mechanisms for interaction, such as passing latent features, subtask-level language commands, or multi-scale memory across the interface. Previous studies have identified the "interface mechanisms," specifically the termination condition for subtasks and the execution horizon for action chunks, as critical design choices for hierarchical VLA performance.

However, existing hierarchical VLA systems typically treat these two decisions—when to terminate a subtask and how long to execute a proposed action chunk—in isolation. Termination modules (e.g., binary detectors, VLM-predicted lengths, progress signals) decide to stop without considering the upcoming action plan, while adaptive action chunking methods (e.g., DEHP, ACH, AQC, AAC) select execution length but lack explicit signals for subtask completion. This isolation creates a structural information asymmetry, as the optimal stopping point is dependent on the executor's planned actions, and the optimal execution length is dependent on the subtask boundary.

SparkVLA positions itself by proposing a unified approach to these two mutually dependent decisions. It aims to bridge the gap between hierarchical planning and adaptive execution by allowing termination and action chunking to be evaluated jointly, thereby resolving the information asymmetry present in prior work.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to resolve the mutual dependency between subtask termination and action-chunk execution length decisions within hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems. The authors contend that existing hierarchical architectures evaluate these two critical interface decisions in isolation, leading to suboptimal performance in long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks.

The motivation for this objective stems from several observations regarding the limitations of current hierarchical VLA systems:
- **Mutual Dependency:** The optimal point to terminate a subtask is influenced by what the low-level executor plans to do next, while the optimal length to execute a proposed action chunk depends on where the current subtask boundary lies. These decisions are inherently intertwined.
- **Consequences of Isolation:** Treating these decisions independently leads to several issues:
 - **Premature termination:** Discards progress and requires unnecessary replanning.
 - **Late termination/overshooting:** Leads to actions beyond the subtask goal, potentially causing errors or inefficiencies.
 - **Over-commitment to action chunks:** Can lead to policy drift, especially in dynamic environments.
 - **Under-commitment to action chunks:** Wastes replanning budget by requiring more frequent re-observations and decisions than necessary.
- **Lack of Unified Context:** Current methods for termination, such as binary detectors or progress signals, operate without access to the content of the upcoming action plan. Similarly, adaptive action chunking methods determine execution length without an explicit signal for subtask completion. This absence of "mutual information" between the two decision-making processes prevents a holistic assessment.

To address these issues, SparkVLA proposes to formulate both subtask termination and action-prefix length selection as a single ranking problem. By unifying "Stop" and all possible action-prefix lengths into a single candidate set, the system can leverage a shared representation to evaluate how well each option serves the current subtask from the present state. This approach is motivated by the potential to eliminate heuristic threshold tuning and to rely solely on offline ordinal preferences for training, simplifying the design and enhancing decision accuracy at the critical interface between high-level planning and low-level execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SparkVLA is structured as a hierarchical VLA system comprising a high-level VLM planner and a low-level VLA executor. The architecture is built upon a shared, pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA model, from which two separately parameterized branches are derived. The high-level branch utilizes the VLM backbone of $\pi_{0.5}$ for autoregressive subtask generation and hosts the action-prefix selector. The low-level branch employs the complete $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA, including its action expert, to propose action chunks. The selector operates at designated "decision boundaries," which are re-observation points where new execution decisions are made.

The core methodological contribution is the formulation of subtask termination and execution-horizon selection as a single ranking problem over a unified candidate set $\mathcal{C} = \{0, 1, \dots, H\}$, where $0$ represents "Stop" and $n \geq 1$ represents an action-prefix of length $n$.

The system's closed-loop operation proceeds as follows:
1. At the beginning of subtask $k$, the planner generates a natural-language subtask command $c_k$ based on the global instruction, semantic history $H_k$, and current observations.
2. At each decision boundary $t$, conditioned on $c_k$, the executor proposes an action chunk $A_t = (a_t, \dots, a_{t+H-1})$.
3. The SparkVLA selector then evaluates $A_t$ along with two context representations, $\alpha_k$ (history-aware subtask anchor) and $m_t$ (high-level hidden feature), to score the candidates in $\mathcal{C}$.
4. If an action-prefix of length $n$ is selected, those $n$ actions are executed before re-observation and the next decision boundary.
5. If "Stop" is selected, subtask $c_k$ is considered complete, appended to the history $H_{k+1}$, and the planner generates the next subtask command $c_{k+1}$.

The methodology incorporates two key modules:

**A. Anchor-Conditioned Context Encoding:** This module constructs the context representations $\alpha_k$ and $m_t$ in three stages:
1. **History-Aware Subtask Anchor ($\alpha_k$):** Upon subtask command $c_k$ generation, a "raw anchor" $\hat{\alpha}_k$ is extracted from the penultimate transformer layer of the planner's VLM backbone. This raw anchor encodes goal semantics and onset-state memory. To incorporate cross-subtask context, $\hat{\alpha}_k$ is fused with a history of preceding raw anchors $A_k = \{\hat{\alpha}_1, \dots, \hat{\alpha}_{k-1}\}$ using self-attention, yielding the history-aware subtask anchor $\alpha_k$. This process is performed once per subtask and cached.
2. **Anchor-Guided Visual Pruning:** A frozen SigLIP visual encoder provides dense visual tokens. To focus on task-relevant regions, a differentiable Grid-Sampler, conditioned on $\alpha_k$, predicts $K$ normalized 2D coordinates. Bilinear interpolation retrieves visual features at these coordinates, effectively pruning the visual sequence from $N$ to $K$ tokens per camera.
3. **High-Level Hidden Feature Encoding ($m_t$):** The pruned visual tokens, proprioceptive state, and subtask command embedding are projected and concatenated. These are then processed through the first $\ell_m$ layers of the high-level VLM backbone, with LoRA adapters, to compute the high-level hidden feature $m_t$. This step is recomputed at each decision boundary.

**B. Stop-Aware Action-Prefix Selection:** This head receives $A_t$, $\alpha_k$, and $m_t$ and maps them to scores over the unified candidate set $\mathcal{C}$.
1. **Unified Candidate Scoring:** The scoring head is implemented as a full-attention transformer. The subtask anchor $\alpha_k$ and hidden feature $m_t$ are projected into context tokens. A learned embedding $e_0$ represents the "Stop" candidate. Each action-prefix candidate $e_n$ (for $n=1, \dots, H$) is initialized from the terminal action of its prefix ($a_{t+n-1}$) combined with a learned length embedding. All these tokens attend to one another within the transformer, allowing each candidate to be contextualized by the complete proposed chunk and the subtask goals. A scoring layer then maps each candidate's output to a scalar score $s_{t,i}$. The highest-scoring option $\hat{n}_t = \text{arg max}_{i \in \mathcal{C}} s_{t,i}$ is selected.
2. **Stop-Aware Ranking Objective:** The selector is optimized using two complementary losses applied to the same candidate scores:
 - **Pairwise Ranking Loss ($L_{rank}$):** This loss enforces relative ordering based on offline ordinal priorities $\rho_i$. Priorities are assigned based on successful demonstrations: "Stop" ranks highest after subtask completion ($t \geq \tau'$); before completion, covering prefixes ($n \geq n^*$) outrank non-covering ones, and among covering prefixes, those closest to the boundary are preferred. For unsuccessful rollouts, all action-prefixes tie above "Stop" to encourage continued exploration. A uniform random perturbation $\Delta$ is applied to the annotated completion boundary $\tau$ ($\tau' = \tau + \Delta$) to enhance robustness to annotation noise. The loss uses Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) on pairwise score differences.
 - **Stop-Aware Auxiliary Loss ($L_{stop}$):** This loss provides a stronger gradient signal for the termination decision, especially at subtask boundaries. It uses BCE to contrast the "Stop" score $s_{t,0}$ against a log-sum-exp aggregation of continuation scores. This term sharpens the termination boundary without introducing a separate inference pathway.
 - The total loss is $L = L_{rank} + \lambda_{stop} L_{stop}$.

**Training Details:** Both the high-level planner and low-level executor are independently fine-tuned from pre-trained $\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoints. The high-level branch is then trained in two phases: first, the planner backbone is supervised for subtask command prediction; second, both base branches are frozen, and the selector modules are trained to optimize stop and dynamic chunk selection using the unified ranking objective.

**Inference Scheduling:** To improve efficiency, the subtask command is cached at subtask onset, and the selector is invoked only at chunk boundaries, not at every single step. This scheduling maintains closed-loop observation after each selected prefix while amortizing high-level computation. A short confirmation window filters transient score fluctuations before committing a "Stop" decision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from simulation experiments on RoboCerebra and LIBERO benchmarks, ablation studies, and real-world robot experiments.

**Simulation Experiments on RoboCerebra:**
- **Overall Success Rate:** SparkVLA achieved an average success rate (SR) of 47.12% on the RoboCerebra benchmark. This represented a substantial improvement over baselines:
 - Outperformed the official hierarchical baseline, HPE, by 30.57 percentage points (SR 16.55%).
 - Surpassed the independently reproduced Mem-0, a recent open-source hierarchical VLA, by 26.83 percentage points (SR 20.29%).
 - Outperformed the standalone $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA by 34.83 percentage points (SR 12.29%).
- **Performance by Condition:** SparkVLA showed significant gains in conditions requiring robust adaptation and memory:
 - Dynamic Observations: +34.78 percentage points (SR 33.13%) over $\pi_{0.5}$.
 - Memory Exploration: +47.69 percentage points (SR 43.87%) over $\pi_{0.5}$.
 - These gains are attributed to adaptive shorter prefixes enabling more frequent re-observation in changing scenes, and the history-enriched anchor preventing revisits to completed targets.
- **Executor Generalization:** The SparkVLA high-level system demonstrated generalization across different low-level executors, yielding improvements over their standalone versions:
 - SparkVLA + OpenVLA-OFT achieved 24.15% SR, compared to standalone OpenVLA-OFT's 1.68%.
 - SparkVLA + SmolVLA achieved 25.32% SR, compared to standalone SmolVLA's 2.80%.

**Simulation Experiments on LIBERO:**
- SparkVLA achieved an average task-level success rate of 98.5% across the LIBERO suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long).
- The clearest performance difference was observed on LIBERO-Long tasks, where SparkVLA achieved 98.0% SR, representing a 5.6 percentage point improvement over $\pi_{0.5}$ (92.4%). This highlights the benefit of subtask decomposition and the proposed interface design for multi-step sequences.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Chunk Selection and Stop Integration:**
 - Upgrading chunk-selection methods (from fixed to RLPD, AQC, and pairwise ranking) with an independent stop head incrementally improved SR from 34.26% to 42.53%, while Stop Accuracy remained around 80%.
 - Unifying "Stop" into the same candidate set with pairwise ranking significantly improved Stop Accuracy from 80.37% to 96.65% (+16.28 percentage points) and SR from 42.53% to 47.12%. This indicates a bidirectional benefit where stop decisions leverage action-prefix context, and prefix selection benefits from an accurate termination signal.
- **Context Encoding and Selector Placement:**
 - Progressive improvements in SR were observed with enhanced visual token processing: no pruning (40.03%), spatial pruning (43.46%), raw anchor (45.18%), and history-aware anchor (47.12%). The history-aware anchor contributed a 1.94 percentage point gain.
 - Placing the selector on the high-level planner backbone resulted in 47.12% SR, a 9.84 percentage point improvement over placement on the low-level executor backbone (37.28%). This suggests the planner's richer context of task progress and subtask history is beneficial.
- **Inference Scheduling:**
 - Caching the subtask command led to a 2.91x speedup without accuracy loss.
 - Invoking the selector only at chunk boundaries (vs. every step) provided a 2.10x speed increase and improved Stop Accuracy from 93.10% to 96.65% (+3.55 percentage points).
 - With both optimizations, SparkVLA maintained 88% throughput compared to standalone $\pi_{0.5}$ (4.14 Hz vs. 4.68 Hz).

**Real-World Experiments:**
- SparkVLA was deployed on an AirbotPlay dual-arm platform for three multi-step tasks: Shelf Arrangement (6 subtasks), Drawer Restocking (6 subtasks), and Tea Preparation (12 subtasks).
- Achieved an average success rate of 69.3% across these tasks, outperforming $\pi_{0.5}$ by +21.3 percentage points (SR 48.0%).
- The largest performance gain was on Tea Preparation (+30.0 percentage points, SR 56.0%), attributed to the extended horizon requiring explicit subtask memory and stop-aware termination.
- The qualitative behavior of adaptive prefixes (shorter for contact, longer for free-space transport) transferred from simulation to physical hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of SparkVLA represents a methodical advancement in the field of hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems for robotic manipulation. Its primary significance lies in addressing a fundamental architectural challenge: the isolated treatment of subtask termination and action-chunk execution length decisions. By formulating these as a unified ranking problem, SparkVLA offers a principled solution that resolves their mutual dependency.

The potential impacts of this work include:

- **Improved Long-Horizon Manipulation:** The demonstrated substantial improvements in success rates on complex, multi-step tasks in both simulation (RoboCerebra, LIBERO) and real-world environments validate the efficacy of the unified decision-making framework. This can lead to more robust and capable robots for tasks requiring sequences of precise actions.
- **Enhanced Interface Design:** The unified ranking approach simplifies the design of hierarchical VLA interfaces by eliminating the need for separate detectors, heuristic threshold tuning, or online reward signals. This could streamline the development and training of future hierarchical robotic policies.
- **Increased Decision Accuracy and Efficiency:** The integration of "Stop" into the action-prefix selection process allows termination decisions to leverage knowledge of the proposed action plan, while action chunking benefits from an accurate subtask completion signal. This bidirectional information flow contributes to higher accuracy in identifying subtask boundaries and selecting appropriate execution lengths, leading to fewer errors and more efficient execution.
- **Generalizability Across Executors:** The findings that SparkVLA's high-level system generalizes and provides consistent gains when paired with different low-level VLA executors (OpenVLA-OFT, SmolVLA) suggest that its benefits are derived from its fundamental interface design rather than being specific to a particular model pairing. This indicates a broadly applicable architectural improvement.
- **Memory and Context Awareness:** The Anchor-Conditioned Context Encoding module, particularly the history-aware subtask anchor, enhances the system's ability to maintain persistent memory of the environment and task progress. This is crucial for complex tasks involving sequential manipulation and state changes, where agents need to recall prior actions and goals.
- **Real-World Applicability:** The successful deployment on a physical robot platform, showcasing improved performance on multi-step tasks like tea preparation and drawer restocking, indicates the practical utility and robustness of SparkVLA's design in physical dynamics.

Future research directions, such as extending SparkVLA to open-world settings, incorporating online adaptation, and exploring a broader range of planner-executor combinations, hold promise for further expanding its capabilities and applicability in advanced robotics. This work establishes a foundation for more integrated and intelligent decision-making at the planning-execution interface of hierarchical robotic systems.
