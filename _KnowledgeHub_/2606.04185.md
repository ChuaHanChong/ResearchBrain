# Distribution-Free Risk-Aware Planning and Control Under Uncertainty Using Conformal Spectral Risk Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Michigan developed Conformal Spectral Risk Control (CSRC) and integrated it into Model Predictive Control (CSRC-MPC) to provide distribution-free statistical safety guarantees for autonomous systems operating under uncertainty. This framework achieved a 100% success rate in simulated vehicle obstacle avoidance, significantly outperforming a baseline by reducing constraint violations from 52.9% to 6.0% and halving solve times, even when the underlying uncertainty models were misspecified.

## Problem

- Autonomous systems require robust planning and control under uncertainty, but precise characterization of uncertainty distributions is often difficult in real-world dynamic environments.
- Traditional Risk-Aware Model Predictive Control (RA-MPC) relies on accurate uncertainty distribution models, leading to potentially unsafe operations if these models are misspecified.
- Existing distribution-free conformal methods provide statistical guarantees but do not directly extend to controlling general spectral risk measures, which offer flexible and nuanced risk assessment.

## Method

- Developed Conformal Spectral Risk Control (CSRC) to extend existing conformal methods to general spectral risk measures by reformulating them as weighted expectations, enabling distribution-free risk quantification.
- Introduced offline calibration and conservative approximations (e.g., truncating unbounded weighting functions, bounding online weights) to address computational challenges and unknown loss function CDFs, making the method practical for online applications.
- Integrated CSRC-generated prediction sets into a ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) framework (CSRC-MPC), reformulating spectral risk constraints using Lipschitz continuity to provide statistical safety guarantees with respect to true, unknown uncertainty.

## Results

- The CSRC framework empirically controlled the risk of loss functions below user-specified thresholds for CVaR and Wang risk measures in both stationary and dynamic obstacle avoidance scenarios, confirming its statistical guarantees.
- CSRC-MPC achieved a **100%** success rate in obstacle avoidance and reduced constraint violation rates from **52.9%** (SAA-MPC baseline) to **6.0%** under uncertainty misspecification, demonstrating superior robustness.
- The proposed CSRC-MPC framework exhibited improved computational efficiency, with an average solve time of **49.9** ms per control step, nearly halving the **104.9** ms of the SAA-MPC baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spectral risk measures can be reformulated as weighted expectations, allowing their integration into weighted conformal risk control frameworks for distribution-free risk quantification.
> - Conservative approximations and offline calibration enable practical, computationally efficient application of conformal methods to control general spectral risk measures in real-time systems.
> - Leveraging the Lipschitz continuity of constraint functions, distribution-free prediction sets can be integrated into MPC to provide robust statistical safety guarantees, even when the true uncertainty distribution is unknown or misspecified.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of safe planning and control for autonomous systems operating in dynamic and uncertain environments is a significant area of research. A primary challenge in these settings is the inherent presence of uncertainties, which necessitate robust methods for evaluating and mitigating associated risks.

Traditional approaches to handling uncertainty in Model Predictive Control (MPC), a widely used framework for optimal trajectory and control input computation, include Robust MPC (RMPC) and Stochastic MPC (SMPC). RMPC typically assumes bounded uncertainty and optimizes for the worst-case scenario, providing strong safety guarantees but often leading to conservative decisions. SMPC aims to reduce this conservatism by enforcing probabilistic safety constraints. However, SMPC's effectiveness is contingent on the accurate characterization of the underlying uncertainty distribution, and it may not adequately address rare, high-consequence ("tail") events that fall outside a chosen probability threshold.

More recently, Risk-Aware MPC (RA-MPC) has emerged, utilizing advanced risk measures such as Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) to incorporate tail events into safety constraints. While RA-MPC represents an advancement over RMPC and SMPC in managing extreme risks, it generally continues to rely on the assumption that the uncertainty distribution is known or can be accurately estimated from data. In real-world applications, such precise characterization is often difficult to achieve due to limited data or inherent complexity, meaning that risk satisfaction guarantees with respect to the true uncertainty may not hold if the assumed distribution is inaccurate.

Concurrently, Conformal Prediction (CP) and its generalization, Conformal Risk Control (CRC), have gained traction as distribution-free frameworks for uncertainty quantification. CP generates prediction sets with statistical coverage guarantees without requiring distributional assumptions. CRC extends this by controlling the expected value of bounded, monotone loss functions below a user-specified threshold. These conformal methods have been applied in various planning and control contexts, including quantifying uncertainty in obstacle trajectory predictions and learned dynamics models for MPC, and in designing control barrier functions. However, existing conformal applications in planning and control are typically limited to probabilistic or expectation-based constraints, rather than the more general spectral risk measures.

This work addresses a research gap identified between RA-MPC and conformal methods. Existing RA-MPC can account for tail events but often relies on potentially inaccurate uncertainty models. Conversely, existing conformal methods offer distribution-free guarantees but do not directly extend to the control of general spectral risk measures, which provide a flexible framework for risk assessment. The paper thus aims to bridge this gap by developing a distribution-free RA-MPC framework that can enforce general spectral risk constraints while maintaining robustness to uncertainty misspecification.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical challenges associated with accurately characterizing uncertainty distributions in real-world autonomous system operations. In dynamic and uncertain environments, such as urban settings or off-road terrain, data limitations or imperfect information often preclude the precise estimation of true uncertainty. An incorrect understanding or misspecification of the underlying uncertainty distribution can lead to decisions that appear safe under assumed models but may be hazardous with respect to the true, unknown uncertainty. Current risk-aware control strategies typically depend on these potentially inaccurate distributional assumptions, thereby lacking guarantees regarding actual risk constraint satisfaction.

To address this critical limitation, the research paper outlines three key objectives:

1. **Develop a distribution-free risk quantification method for spectral risk measures:** The first objective is to create a framework capable of generating prediction sets that can directly control spectral risks below a user-defined threshold. This framework is designed to operate without requiring explicit assumptions about the underlying uncertainty distribution. This involves extending the existing Conformal Risk Control (CRC) methodology to accommodate general spectral risk measures, which offer a broader and more flexible approach to risk assessment than simple probabilistic or expectation-based measures.

2. **Integrate distribution-free risk quantification into a Model Predictive Control framework:** The second objective is to incorporate the prediction sets, generated by the newly developed distribution-free risk quantification method, into a Risk-Aware Model Predictive Control (RA-MPC) framework. The aim is to establish a statistically guaranteed safety mechanism. This integration is intended to ensure satisfaction of spectral risk constraints with respect to the true, unknown uncertainty distribution, even when the controller's internal model of uncertainty is misspecified.

3. **Validate the proposed framework in simulated autonomous navigation scenarios:** The third objective is to empirically validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed RA-MPC framework through simulation studies. Specifically, the validation focuses on demonstrating improved safety performance and reduced computational solve times in vehicle obstacle avoidance scenarios, particularly under conditions of uncertainty misspecification, when compared to a baseline RA-MPC approach that relies on distributional assumptions.

These objectives collectively aim to enhance the safety and reliability of autonomous systems by providing a principled method for risk management that is robust to the inherent ambiguities of real-world uncertainty.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a novel framework termed Conformal Spectral Risk Control (CSRC) and integrates it into a Model Predictive Control (MPC) structure, referred to as CSRC-MPC. The approach is designed to provide distribution-free statistical safety guarantees under uncertainty.

#### 3.1. Conformal Spectral Risk Control (CSRC)

The core of the methodology lies in extending Conformal Risk Control (CRC) to general spectral risk measures.
- **Reformulation of Spectral Risk Measures:** Spectral risk measures are typically defined by an integral involving the quantile function of a random variable Z, ρ(Z) = ∫₀¹ φ(v)F⁻¹_Z(v)dv. To make this compatible with CRC, the authors reformulate it as a weighted expectation: ρ(Z) = E[Zφ(F_Z(Z))], where φ is a weighting function and F_Z is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of Z. This reformulation allows the application of weighted expectation control techniques.
- **Application of Weighted Conformal Risk Control (W-CRC):** The W-CRC framework, which controls E[w(ˆY)L(λ)] ≤ θ for bounded weights w(ˆY) and loss function L(λ), is adopted. In this context, the weights are defined as w_i := φ(F_L(L_i(λ))), where L_i(λ) is the loss function evaluated on calibration data points.
- **Challenges and Solutions:**
 - **Unknown CDF of the Loss Function:** The weights w_i depend on F_L, the CDF of the loss function, which is generally unknown. To address this, the authors propose constructing an estimator, ˆF_L, using an independent, exchangeable estimation dataset (D_est). The empirical cumulative distribution function (eCDF) is utilized as this estimator.
 - **Online Computation and Unbounded Weights:** Standard W-CRC requires solving an optimization problem (Eq. 11) that involves the weight of the current test data point (w_n+1), making it computationally intensive for online applications. Furthermore, the weighting function φ for spectral risk measures can be unbounded. To mitigate these issues, a conservative approximation is introduced:
 - w_n+1 is replaced by a finite upper bound w_max.
 - For unbounded φ, the weighting function is truncated at v = 1-ǫ (denoted as ˆφ(v)). To account for this truncation and maintain the original risk guarantee, a correction factor ζ is applied to the user-specified risk threshold θ. This ensures that controlling the truncated risk measure ρ_t below θ-ζ is sufficient to control the original, untruncated risk measure ρ below θ (Theorem 2).
- **Offline Calibration:** These modifications allow for an offline calibration procedure where an optimized prediction set parameter ˆλ is determined by solving an optimization problem (Eq. 19) using a calibration dataset (D_cal), D_est, and the conservative approximations. This procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1, referred to as Conformal Spectral Risk Control (CSRC).

#### 3.2. Distribution-Free RA-MPC with CSRC (CSRC-MPC)

The generated prediction sets are then integrated into an MPC framework to enforce spectral risk constraints.
- **Optimal Control Problem Formulation:** A standard optimal control problem (OCP) is defined (Eq. 20) with state ξ_k, control u_k, uncertain observation Y_k, stage cost r, system dynamics f, and a constraint function h subject to a spectral risk constraint ρ(h(ξ_k, Y_k)) ≤ 0.
- **Statistical Safety Guarantees:** The core idea is to reformulate the spectral risk constraint in a way that provides guarantees with respect to the true uncertainty, even if Y_k's distribution is unknown.
 - **Assumption:** The constraint function h(ξ_k, Y_k) is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous with respect to Y_k (Assumption 1).
 - **Reformulated Constraint:** By leveraging the Lipschitz continuity and the CSRC-generated prediction set C_ˆλ(ˆY_k|t) (which provides a bound on the discrepancy between the prediction ˆY_k|t and the true observation Y_k), the original risk constraint ρ(h(ξ_k, Y_k)) ≤ 0 can be conservatively enforced by an equivalent constraint h(ξ_k, ˆY_k|t) ≤ -K(θ + ˆλ_k|t) (Theorem 3). Here, K is the Lipschitz constant, θ is the user-specified risk threshold, and ˆλ_k|t is the prediction set parameter from CSRC. This reformulated constraint ensures that if the new constraint is satisfied, the original spectral risk constraint with respect to the true uncertainty is also satisfied.
- **Implementation with MPPI:** The OCP is solved using Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, a sampling-based MPC algorithm. The constraints (including the reformulated risk constraint) are incorporated into the cost function as penalties, allowing the MPPI algorithm to find optimal trajectories that satisfy the safety requirements.

This methodology delineates a systematic approach to robust, risk-aware control that bypasses the need for explicit distributional assumptions, providing a more reliable foundation for autonomous system safety.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed CSRC framework and the integrated CSRC-MPC in simulated vehicle obstacle avoidance scenarios.

#### 4.1. CSRC Framework Validation

- **Risk Control Performance:** The CSRC framework was validated for two spectral risk measures: Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) with a risk parameter α = 0.9 and the Wang risk measure with a risk parameter γ = 1.5. In both stationary and dynamic obstacle avoidance scenarios, the framework successfully generated prediction sets where the empirical risk of the loss function, evaluated on an independent test dataset (D_test), was controlled below the user-specified threshold of θ = 0. This empirical validation confirms the statistical guarantees provided by CSRC.
- **Data Efficiency for CDF Estimation:** The study indicated that using a relatively small estimation dataset (D_est) of 100 samples for constructing the CDF estimator (ˆF_L) was sufficient. Increasing the size of D_est to 1000 samples did not lead to significant improvements in risk control performance, suggesting that the framework can be practical even with limited data for CDF estimation.

#### 4.2. Stationary Obstacle Avoidance Scenario

- **Scenario Description:** The vehicle navigated to avoid two stationary circular obstacles. The key challenge was a misspecification in the obstacle location estimation model: the vehicle assumed estimation errors (δˆx, δˆy) followed N(1, 0.3²) distribution, while the true errors (δx, δy) followed N(0, 0.3²). This introduced a systematic bias in the vehicle's perception of obstacle locations.
- **CSRC-MPC Performance:** The proposed CSRC-MPC framework, utilizing prediction sets calibrated by CSRC for both CVaR and Wang risk measures, demonstrated successful obstacle avoidance. Vehicle trajectories remained clear of obstacles despite the misspecified estimation model (Figure 3). The optimized prediction set parameters (ˆλ) were 2.25 for CVaR and 2.36 for the Wang risk measure, effectively buffering against the uncertainty.
- **Baseline SAA-MPC Performance:** A baseline RA-MPC using Sample Average Approximation (SAA-MPC) with the same CVaR risk level (α = 0.9) failed to avoid the obstacles. This failure was attributed to the SAA-MPC's reliance on the misspecified estimation model, leading to an inaccurate approximation of the true risk constraint (Figure 3).
- **Conclusion:** CSRC-MPC exhibited robustness to uncertainty misspecification, maintaining safety guarantees where the baseline approach failed.

#### 4.3. Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance Scenario

- **Scenario Description:** The vehicle avoided a dynamic obstacle moving in a straight line with constant velocity. The vehicle employed a linear predictor for obstacle trajectories, but this model was intentionally misspecified by introducing an artificial bias of 0.5 m to the predicted x and y positions. The true observation error followed N(0, 0.05²).
- **CSRC Validation for Dynamic Scenarios:** Box plots of the risk of the loss function over prediction timesteps confirmed that the CSRC framework, using CVaR (α = 0.9), maintained the average risk below the θ = 0 threshold at every prediction step (Figure 4), empirically validating its statistical guarantees for dynamic predictions.
- **Comparative Performance (Table I):**
 - **Obstacle Constraint Violation Rate:** CSRC-MPC achieved a significantly lower obstacle constraint violation rate of 6.0%, compared to 52.9% for the SAA-MPC baseline. This metric was evaluated using 10,000 Monte Carlo samples from the true observation model.
 - **Success Rate:** CSRC-MPC achieved a 100% success rate in avoiding obstacles, whereas SAA-MPC had a success rate of 67.5%.
 - **Average Solve Time:** CSRC-MPC demonstrated a lower average solve time of 49.9 ms per control step, compared to 104.9 ms for SAA-MPC. This computational efficiency is attributed to the offline calibration of prediction sets by CSRC, reducing online computational burden.
- **Visual Comparison:** Representative vehicle trajectories illustrated that CSRC-MPC consistently planned safe paths, adapting to the biased obstacle trajectory predictions. In contrast, SAA-MPC, relying solely on online sampling from the biased model, frequently resulted in collisions (Figure 5).
- **Marginal Guarantees Note (Remark 2):** While CSRC-MPC drastically reduced constraint violations, it did not entirely eliminate them in every simulation when evaluated by Monte Carlo samples. This is explained by the marginal nature of CSRC's guarantees over all possible obstacle trajectories, as opposed to stronger conditional guarantees. Nevertheless, the substantial improvement over the baseline indicates enhanced safety.

The findings collectively demonstrate that CSRC-MPC effectively addresses the challenge of uncertainty misspecification, leading to improved safety and computational efficiency in risk-aware planning and control tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous systems by introducing a distribution-free approach to risk-aware planning and control, addressing a fundamental challenge in real-world deployment. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

1. **Enhanced Safety and Robustness in Uncertain Environments:** The primary impact is the provision of statistical safety guarantees for autonomous systems, even when the true underlying uncertainty distribution is unknown or misspecified. This capability is crucial for deploying autonomous vehicles in complex, dynamic environments where accurate characterization of all uncertainties is often impractical. By demonstrating improved obstacle avoidance and reduced constraint violations under misspecification, the framework offers a more reliable foundation for safe operation.

2. **Generality of Risk Assessment:** The extension of Conformal Risk Control (CRC) to general spectral risk measures (CSRC) is a notable technical advancement. This allows autonomous systems to employ a broader and more flexible class of risk measures, including CVaR and Wang risk measures, which can account for tail events and express varying degrees of risk aversion beyond simple probabilistic or expectation-based constraints. This enhanced flexibility enables more nuanced and sophisticated risk management strategies.

3. **Computational Efficiency for Real-time Applications:** The methodology's design, which leverages offline calibration of prediction sets, leads to a reduction in online computational burden. The reported decrease in average solve time compared to baseline methods like SAA-MPC indicates that the proposed CSRC-MPC framework is feasible for real-time planning and control applications. This efficiency is critical for autonomous systems that require rapid decision-making.

4. **Reduced Reliance on Distributional Assumptions:** By operating in a distribution-free manner, the framework mitigates the risks associated with modeling errors and uncertainty misspecification. This eliminates the need for potentially inaccurate empirical estimations or strong distributional assumptions, which often compromise safety guarantees in traditional risk-aware control methods. This characteristic makes the approach more practical and robust for real-world scenarios where precise uncertainty models are difficult to obtain.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a robust framework that opens several avenues for future research. This includes exploring adaptive conformal prediction techniques to maintain guarantees under distribution shifts, investigating stronger conditional risk guarantees for highly safety-critical applications, extending the framework to handle multiple, potentially interacting sources of risk, and validating the approach through physical hardware experiments. These directions could further enhance the applicability and rigor of distribution-free risk management in robotics.

In summary, this research provides a principled, robust, and computationally viable method for risk-aware planning and control, offering a significant step towards the safer and more reliable deployment of autonomous systems in complex and uncertain environments.
