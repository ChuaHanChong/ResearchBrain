# Adversarial Flow Matching for Imperceptible Attacks on End-to-End Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed Adversarial Flow Matching (AFM), a gray-box attack that generates visually imperceptible perturbations to disrupt end-to-end autonomous driving systems by exploiting vulnerabilities in Transformer backbones. AFM achieved attack success rates up to 88.24% while maintaining superior visual imperceptibility, and induced high-risk "active hijacking" behaviors like a 40.06% off-road rate in closed-loop simulations.

## Problem

- Modern end-to-end autonomous driving (AD) systems, particularly those relying on Transformer backbones, are susceptible to adversarial attacks that can induce hazardous driving maneuvers.
- Existing adversarial attack methods struggle with real-world applicability; white-box attacks require impractical full model access, while black-box attacks are either too slow (query-based) or lack sufficient transferability.
- Current adversarial examples often lack visual imperceptibility, producing noticeable artifacts, and state-of-the-art generative attacks incur high computational costs due to iterative generation processes.

## Method

- A gray-box adversarial attack framework called Adversarial Flow Matching (AFM) is introduced, leveraging partial knowledge of the target AD model's Transformer backbone.
- AFM employs a Flow Matching-guided generative mechanism to enable efficient one-step (1-NFE) synthesis of adversarial examples in the latent space, addressing the computational overhead of iterative methods.
- The method uses attention-guided multi-objective optimization, combining latent space and neural velocity field perturbations, to maximize feature distortion in road-centric and high-saliency regions while ensuring visual imperceptibility.

## Results

- AFM achieved high attack success rates (up to 88.24% on TransFuser and 87.14% on SimLingo) while maintaining state-of-the-art visual imperceptibility, exemplified by an LPIPS as low as 0.074.
- The method demonstrated robust cross-model transferability between different AD architectures and significantly improved computational efficiency, generating adversarial samples in approximately 6.7 seconds per instance.
- In closed-loop simulations, AFM induced "active hijacking" behaviors, resulting in high-risk scenarios such as a 40.06% off-road rate and 20% route deviation, unlike other generative methods that often caused passive "static freezing."

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transformer backbones, a common component in both monolithic VLA and specialized modular end-to-end autonomous driving (AD) architectures, present a shared structural vulnerability exploitable by gray-box adversarial attacks.
> - Flow Matching offers an efficient, one-step generative mechanism for creating adversarial examples, addressing the computational limitations of prior iterative generative methods while preserving visual imperceptibility.
> - Effective and imperceptible attacks can be achieved by synergistically perturbing the generative latent space and a neural average velocity field, guided by the AD model's internal attention mechanisms, to subtly manipulate driving decisions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of autonomous driving (AD) is experiencing a transition from traditional modular designs to end-to-end (E2E) architectures. This transition is characterized by two primary paradigms: monolithic Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and specialized modular architectures. A common characteristic across both paradigms is the increasing reliance on Transformer backbones for complex reasoning. This shared architectural component introduces a potential vulnerability: the susceptibility of these models to adversarial attacks, where visually imperceptible perturbations can induce hazardous driving maneuvers.

Existing adversarial attack research on AD systems typically falls into white-box or black-box categories. White-box attacks, while effective, require full model transparency (access to internal gradients and structures), limiting their real-world applicability. Black-box attacks are further divided into query-based methods, which suffer from prohibitive latency, and transfer-based methods, which often experience performance degradation due to policy misalignment between surrogate and target models. The area of gray-box attacks, which leverage limited but vital structural information (e.g., knowledge of a Transformer backbone), remains relatively underexplored in the AD domain.

Furthermore, traditional adversarial attacks predominantly rely on pixel-wise additive perturbations, which can lead to noticeable visual artifacts. Recent advancements have shifted towards generative adversarial paradigms, utilizing models like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Diffusion Models to produce more diverse and visually naturalistic perturbations. However, these generative approaches often incur substantial computational overhead due to iterative sampling processes.

This research addresses these gaps by proposing a novel gray-box attack framework that specifically targets the structural vulnerabilities of Transformer backbones within E2E AD models. It aims to achieve an improved balance between attack effectiveness, visual imperceptibility, and computational efficiency, thereby providing insights into the robustness of contemporary AD systems against a more realistic threat model.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to proactively identify and expose the inherent vulnerabilities of modern AD agents, particularly the emerging monolithic models exemplified by VLA, through a novel adversarial attack framework.

The key motivations and more specific objectives are:

- **Addressing Shared Vulnerabilities:** Both monolithic VLA and specialized modular AD architectures increasingly integrate Transformer backbones. This work aims to investigate if this shared architectural component introduces a common vulnerability to visually imperceptible perturbations that can manipulate AD models into hazardous maneuvers.
- **Bridging the Attack Paradigm Gap:** Current white-box attacks are impractical for real-world deployment due to the assumption of full model transparency. Black-box attacks, while more practical, often face challenges with prohibitive query latency or limited transferability. This research is motivated by the need for a gray-box attack that leverages limited but crucial structural knowledge (e.g., the presence of a Transformer backbone) to strike a balance between efficacy, efficiency, and deployability.
- **Enhancing Imperceptibility and Naturalness:** Traditional pixel-wise additive perturbations often result in visually conspicuous adversarial examples. The motivation is to generate adversarial examples that are both effective in disrupting AD models and visually imperceptible to human observers, by operating within a generative framework.
- **Improving Computational Efficiency for Generative Attacks:** While generative adversarial paradigms offer improved imperceptibility, they typically suffer from high sampling latency due to iterative processes (e.g., in diffusion models). A key objective is to introduce a generative mechanism, specifically Flow Matching, that enables efficient one-step generation of adversarial examples, thereby reducing computational overhead.
- **Targeting Latent Space and Velocity Fields:** The research aims to achieve effective and imperceptible attacks by synergistically perturbing the generative latent space and a neural average velocity field. This dual perturbation strategy is designed to manipulate the latent semantic features of Transformer-based AD agents while strictly preserving visual fidelity.
- **Diagnostic Insights for Robust System Development:** Beyond merely demonstrating an attack, the underlying motivation is to provide critical diagnostic insights into model vulnerabilities. By exposing these weaknesses, the research aims to facilitate the development of next-generation, inherently robust AD systems, particularly those built on VLA-driven paradigms.
- **Investigating Cross-Model Transferability:** A further objective is to examine if adversarial examples generated by this gray-box method exhibit robust cross-model transferability, indicating that the attack exploits fundamental architectural weaknesses rather than model-specific quirks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Adversarial Flow Matching (AFM), operates as a gray-box adversarial attack framework. It is designed to exploit the structural vulnerabilities of Transformer backbones within end-to-end autonomous driving agents.

#### 3.1. Problem Definition and Threat Model

- **AD Agent Model:** An end-to-end AD agent $M$ maps sensory inputs $x$ to planning trajectories $y$. It is modeled as $y = \varphi(\Phi(x), c)$, where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the Transformer backbone extracting feature embeddings $F$, and $\varphi(\cdot)$ is the downstream reasoning module generating trajectories based on $F$ and auxiliary information $c$.
- **Adversary Objective:** To craft an adversarial example $x_{adv}$ such that the resulting trajectory $y'$ significantly deviates from the clean trajectory $y$, while $x_{adv}$ remains visually imperceptible.
- **Threat Model (Gray-box):** The adversary has access to the gradients of the Transformer backbone $\Phi(\cdot)$ but not to the full internal parameters of the downstream reasoning module $\varphi(\cdot)$, specific model weights, or exact cost functions.
- **Optimization Objective:**
 1. **Feature Distortion:** Maximize the deviation of latent semantic features $F = \Phi(x)$ in task-critical regions to induce erroneous predictions.
 2. **Visual Fidelity Preservation:** Ensure $x_{adv}$ maintains strict visual imperceptibility and statistical consistency in the latent space.
- **Attack Formulation:** The attack is formulated as a constrained optimization problem involving dual perturbations: a latent perturbation $\delta_z$ and a neural average velocity field perturbation $\delta_u$. These perturbations are applied within a Flow Matching generative process.

#### 3.2. Adversarial Flow Matching (AFM) Framework

The AFM framework comprises two main components: a Flow Matching-guided generative mechanism and an attention-guided multi-objective optimization.

#### 3.2.1. Flow Matching-guided Generative Mechanism

- **Efficient One-Step Generation:** AFM leverages Flow Matching to enable efficient one-step (1-NFE) adversarial generation directly in the latent space. This bypasses the iterative numerical integration common in diffusion models.
- **Neural Average Velocity Field:** The method utilizes a neural average velocity field $u(z_t, r, t)$ that captures the global direction of the probability path, allowing for direct state transitions.
- **Latent Space Perturbation:**
 1. A clean sample $x_{clean}$ is encoded into a latent representation $z_{clean}$ using a pre-trained VAE encoder $E(\cdot)$.
 2. The Flow Matching's reversibility property is used to invert $z_{clean}$ to a noise-level latent anchor $z_{mid} = S_{0 \rightarrow 1}(z_{clean})$ in a single step.
 3. Dual perturbations are introduced:
 - $\delta_z$: A latent perturbation that shifts the anchor state, resulting in $z_{start} = z_{mid} + \delta_z$.
 - $\delta_u$: A velocity perturbation that explicitly warps the generative flow, applied to the pre-trained Flow Matching network $S_\theta(\cdot)$ to form $u_{attack} = S_\theta(z_{start}, t_{start}, t_{end}) + \delta_u$.
 4. The adversarial latent code $z_{adv}$ is synthesized via a differentiable single-step linear ODE update: $z_{adv} = z_{start} - (t_{start} - t_{end}) \cdot u_{attack}$ (with $t_{start}=1$ and $t_{end}=0$).
 5. Finally, the adversarial image $x_{adv}$ is reconstructed using a pre-trained VAE decoder $D(\cdot)$.

#### 3.2.2. Attention-Guided Multi-objective Optimization

The optimization aims to maximize disruption to driving decisions while maintaining visual imperceptibility. All network parameters are fixed during the attack; gradients are used solely to update $\delta_z$ and $\delta_u$. The total loss $L_{total}$ is a weighted sum of three components:

- **Road-Focused Feature Loss ($L_{feat}$):** Maximizes the deviation of feature embeddings $F$ from the Transformer backbone, primarily in road-centric regions. A spatial mask $M$ is applied, assigning higher weights to the lower (road) regions of the feature map, reflecting their importance for driving decisions.
 - $L_{feat} = \frac{1}{|F|} \sum_{i,j} M_{i,j} \|F^{(i,j)}_{adv} - F^{(i,j)}_{clean}\|^2_2$
- **Attention-Weighted Loss ($L_{attn}$):** Exploits Transformer-specific inductive biases by leveraging internal attention maps. The global token saliency map $A_{clean}$ from the Transformer backbone is used to compute an attention-weighted deviation, targeting high-saliency regions with temperature scaling ($\tau$).
 - $L_{attn} = \sum_{k=1}^N \text{Softmax}(A^{(k)}_{clean}/\tau) \|F^{(k)}_{adv} - F^{(k)}_{clean}\|^2_2$
- **Latent Anchor Constraint ($L_{constraint}$):** Regularizes the adversarial latent code to ensure visual realism and prevent distribution collapse. This is achieved by minimizing the difference in standard deviation between the adversarial and clean latent variables.
 - $L_{constraint} = |\sigma(z_{adv}) - \sigma(z_{clean})|$
- **Total Loss:** $L_{total} = -\lambda_f L_{feat} - \lambda_a L_{attn} + \lambda_c L_{constraint}$, where $\lambda$ are weighting coefficients.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Target Models:** Two representative E2E AD agents are selected: TransFuser (specialized modular, processing RGB and LiDAR BEV inputs with intermediate Transformer fusion) and SimLingo (monolithic VLA, built on InternVL2 with InternViT for visual encoding and Qwen2 for reasoning). Both incorporate Transformer modules, which is the structural prior for the gray-box attack.
- **Datasets:** Official datasets for TransFuser (228k frames from CARLA, stratified into Complex and Common scenarios) and SimLingo (3.1M frames, stratified into Daytime and Nighttime scenarios) are used.
- **Implementation:** Dual perturbations are optimized for 50 iterations using Adam. Specific learning rates, clipping bounds, and weighting coefficients for the loss terms are defined.
- **Baselines:** Standard gradient-based attacks (FGSM, PGD) and generative/perceptual attacks (DiffAttack, PerC-AL, NCF) are used for comparison.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Attack Effectiveness (Open-Loop):** Mean Trajectory Shift (SHIFT) and Attack Success Rate (SR, based on safety violations like collision, off-road, lane deviation).
 - **Computational Efficiency:** Average Generation Time (TIME).
 - **Visual Imperceptibility:** Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS), and Fréchet Inception Distance (FID).
 - **Closed-Loop Performance:** Route Completion (RC) and granular breakdown of physical failure modes (Off-Road, Collisions, Route Deviation, Blocked, Timeouts) using the Bench2Drive benchmark.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted demonstrate the performance of AFM across various dimensions: attack effectiveness, visual imperceptibility, computational efficiency, cross-model transferability, and closed-loop driving impact.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Comparison of Attack Performance (Open-Loop)

- **Visual Imperceptibility:** AFM consistently achieved the strongest visual imperceptibility across all evaluated methods and scenarios. On TransFuser (Common scenarios), it yielded an LPIPS of 0.141, SSIM of 0.881, and FID of 23.180, outperforming all baselines. Similarly, on SimLingo (Daytime scenarios), AFM achieved the lowest LPIPS (0.074), FID (8.875), and highest SSIM (0.961). Qualitative comparisons (Fig. 3) show AFM producing smoother, more spatially coherent perturbations compared to the noticeable artifacts of other methods.
- **Attack Effectiveness:** AFM substantially degraded the performance of both TransFuser and SimLingo.
 - On **TransFuser**, AFM achieved an SR of 88.24% in Common scenarios and 69.35% in Complex scenarios. While PerC-AL achieved higher SRs (e.g., 99.28%), this came at the cost of severe visual degradation (FID > 190, SSIM ~0.2). AFM maintained high image quality while inducing strong trajectory deviations, demonstrating a superior trade-off.
 - On **SimLingo**, AFM recorded an SR of 87.14% in Daytime and 85.98% in Nighttime scenarios, outperforming DiffAttack (59.98%) and PerC-AL (71.42%) in terms of SR under Daytime conditions, while simultaneously maintaining superior imperceptibility.
- **Computational Efficiency:** AFM demonstrated a generation time of approximately 6.7 seconds per sample, significantly faster than other state-of-the-art generative attacks like DiffAttack (10-11 seconds) and PerC-AL (10-11 seconds). This efficiency is attributed to its one-step (1-NFE) generation through Flow Matching, a marked improvement over iterative denoising methods. Gradient-based attacks (FGSM, PGD) were faster (0.1-1.8s) but produced severely perceptible perturbations.

#### 4.2. Cross-Model Transferability Analysis

AFM exhibited robust cross-model transferability in a strict gray-box setting, where attacks generated on one architecture were evaluated on another without access to the target model's gradients.
- **SimLingo → TransFuser (SL → TF):** AFM achieved the highest SR (12.82%) and SHIFT (0.506 m) among all methods, alongside the best perceptual quality (LPIPS 0.022, SSIM 0.995).
- **TransFuser → SimLingo (TF → SL):** AFM achieved an SR of 46.68% and SHIFT of 1.192 m, comparable to the best baselines, while still providing the strongest visual imperceptibility (LPIPS 0.148, SSIM 0.871).
These results suggest that AFM exploits fundamental structural vulnerabilities within Transformer backbones, indicating a widespread threat to diverse AD agents incorporating these modules.

#### 4.3. Closed-Loop Evaluation and Temporal Dynamics

Closed-loop simulations on SimLingo using the Bench2Drive benchmark assessed practical hazards in dynamic environments, with attacks intermittently injected.
- **Reconciling Efficacy and Imperceptibility:** AFM successfully generated meaningful disruption while maintaining state-of-the-art imperceptibility (LPIPS: 0.075, SSIM: 0.956), unlike gradient-based methods or PerC-AL, which introduced noticeable visual artifacts (LPIPS $\geq$ 0.237).
- **Active Hijacking vs. Static Freezing:** A granular analysis of failure modes (Table IV, Fig. 9) revealed that AFM induced "active hijacking" by forcing high-risk maneuvers, as evidenced by the highest average Off-Road rate (40.06%), significant Route Deviation (20%), and Collisions (1.5). This contrasts with generative baselines like NCF and DiffAttack, which primarily caused "static freezing" (high Blocked rates, minimal Off-Road/Collisions), essentially paralyzing the agent rather than misdirecting it.
- **Sustained Temporal Disruption:** The temporal evolution of trajectory deviations (Fig. 10) showed AFM inducing a continuous, sustained accumulation of errors, driving the vehicle's trajectory significantly beyond safety thresholds, effectively hijacking the continuous control policy. Other generative baselines showed bounded deviations or early termination.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies on the perturbation budget $\epsilon$ confirmed an inherent trade-off: increasing $\epsilon$ improved attack effectiveness (higher SR) but degraded visual fidelity (lower SSIM, higher LPIPS). The chosen default budget represented a balanced operating point.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Adversarial Flow Matching (AFM), representing a significant contribution to the understanding and mitigation of adversarial threats in autonomous driving systems.

**Novel Attack Paradigm:** AFM is presented as the first attempt to leverage Flow Matching for adversarial example generation against AD agents. This approach yields efficient one-step (1-NFE) generation, substantially mitigating the computational overhead and discretization errors associated with iterative generative attack frameworks (e.g., diffusion models).

**Exposure of Shared Architectural Vulnerabilities:** The study demonstrates that AFM effectively exploits critical structural vulnerabilities inherent to the Transformer backbones of E2E AD agents. This is significant because it applies to both monolithic VLA models and specialized modular architectures, highlighting a pervasive weakness across modern AD paradigms.

**Superior Trade-off Between Efficacy and Imperceptibility:** AFM achieves a notable balance, delivering substantial degradation in AD performance while simultaneously maintaining state-of-the-art visual imperceptibility. This represents an advancement over previous methods that often compromise one aspect for the other, making AFM-generated attacks more challenging to detect.

**Practical Applicability and Transferability:** The robust cross-model transferability exhibited by AFM is a key finding. It indicates that effective and visually imperceptible attacks can be executed with only the prior knowledge that the target AD model incorporates a Transformer-based module, effectively approximating a black-box attack in utility. This suggests a widespread and generalizable threat.
Furthermore, while generation latency exists, the paper outlines practical deployment strategies such as intermittent digital injection at critical timestamps or offline generation for physical attack proxies (e.g., adversarial patches). This broadens the perceived real-world threat, as continuous real-time perturbation is not always necessary for effective disruption.

**Diagnostic Insights for Robust System Design:** By systematically investigating and exposing these vulnerabilities, the research provides critical diagnostic insights for the AD community. This information is invaluable for guiding the development of more robust and inherently secure next-generation AD systems, particularly those relying on Transformer-based architectures and VLA-driven paradigms. The findings underscore the necessity for defense mechanisms tailored to these specific attack vectors.

**Future Research Directions:** The paper also outlines future work, including enhancing attack effectiveness to achieve white-box potency without compromising imperceptibility and migrating these techniques to the physical domain for comprehensive real-world validation. This indicates the potential for further advancements in understanding and countering such threats.
