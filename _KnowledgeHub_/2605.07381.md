# Escaping the Diversity Trap in Robotic Manipulation via Anchor-Centric Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> Robotic manipulation adaptation often suffers from a "diversity trap" when fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models with limited data, where spreading samples too thinly across conditions destabilizes policies. Anchor-Centric Adaptation (ACA) addresses this by first stabilizing core behaviors with repeated demonstrations at specific anchors, then selectively expanding to high-risk boundary conditions, achieving a 40.8% absolute improvement in mean success rate over diversity-first baselines at a budget of N=100.

## Problem

- Deploying pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on specific physical robots creates distribution gaps due to embodiment mismatch and environmental variations.
- Real-robot adaptation is constrained by tight data budgets, typically tens to hundreds of trajectories, making extensive data collection resource-intensive and expensive.
- The common heuristic of maximizing condition diversity in data collection can be a "diversity trap" under low budgets, leading to increased estimation noise and destabilized policies.

## Method

- The paper formalizes the problem as a Coverage–Density Trade-off, decomposing policy error into estimation error (sample density) and extrapolation bias (coverage).
- **Anchor-Centric** Adaptation (ACA) is proposed as a two-stage framework for budget-efficient VLA adaptation.
- Stage 1 focuses on Anchor-Centric Stabilization, where a "policy skeleton" is established by repeatedly demonstrating a minimal set of core anchors to reduce estimation variance, followed by Stage 2 which uses error-driven boundary mining and constrained residual updates for targeted expansion to high-risk areas.

## Results

- ACA consistently outperformed diversity-first baselines, achieving a **40.8%** absolute mean success rate improvement (**72.5%** vs. **31.7%**) at a budget of N=100 and maintaining higher stability across varying workspace regions.
- The framework demonstrated sample efficiency, with ACA at N=50 achieving performance comparable to the baseline at N=150, effectively reducing data requirements by up to three times.
- Ablation studies confirmed the inverted-U performance trend with respect to anchor quantity, validating the Coverage–Density Trade-off, and showed the synergistic benefits of error-driven boundary mining and constrained residual adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Under stringent data budgets, there exists a Coverage–Density Trade-off, where indiscriminately increasing task coverage by sampling many distinct conditions leads to reduced sample density per condition, amplifying estimation noise.
> - The relationship between the number of distinct anchors and overall task success exhibits a non-monotonic, inverted-U shape, indicating an optimal level of diversity for a given data budget.
> - Stabilizing the policy with dense sampling at a few core anchor conditions (creating a robust "policy skeleton") is critical before attempting to expand to more diverse or boundary conditions.
