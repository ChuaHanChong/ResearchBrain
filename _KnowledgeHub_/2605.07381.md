# Escaping the Diversity Trap in Robotic Manipulation via Anchor-Centric Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> Robotic manipulation adaptation often suffers from a "diversity trap" when fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models with limited data, where spreading samples too thinly across conditions destabilizes policies. Anchor-Centric Adaptation (ACA) addresses this by first stabilizing core behaviors with repeated demonstrations at specific anchors, then selectively expanding to high-risk boundary conditions, achieving a 40.8% absolute improvement in mean success rate over diversity-first baselines at a budget of N=100.

## Problem

- Deploying pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on specific physical robots creates distribution gaps due to embodiment mismatch and environmental variations.
- Real-robot adaptation is constrained by tight data budgets, typically tens to hundreds of trajectories, making extensive data collection resource-intensive and expensive.
- The common heuristic of maximizing condition diversity in data collection can be a "diversity trap" under low budgets, leading to increased estimation noise and destabilized policies.

## Method

- The paper formalizes the problem as a Coverage–Density Trade-off, decomposing policy error into estimation error (sample density) and extrapolation bias (coverage).
- **Anchor-Centric** Adaptation (ACA) is proposed as a two-stage framework for budget-efficient VLA adaptation.
- Stage 1 focuses on Anchor-Centric Stabilization, where a "policy skeleton" is established by repeatedly demonstrating a minimal set of core anchors to reduce estimation variance, followed by Stage 2 which uses error-driven boundary mining and constrained residual updates for targeted expansion to high-risk areas.

## Results

- ACA consistently outperformed diversity-first baselines, achieving a **40.8%** absolute mean success rate improvement (**72.5%** vs. **31.7%**) at a budget of N=100 and maintaining higher stability across varying workspace regions.
- The framework demonstrated sample efficiency, with ACA at N=50 achieving performance comparable to the baseline at N=150, effectively reducing data requirements by up to three times.
- Ablation studies confirmed the inverted-U performance trend with respect to anchor quantity, validating the Coverage–Density Trade-off, and showed the synergistic benefits of error-driven boundary mining and constrained residual adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Under stringent data budgets, there exists a Coverage–Density Trade-off, where indiscriminately increasing task coverage by sampling many distinct conditions leads to reduced sample density per condition, amplifying estimation noise.
> - The relationship between the number of distinct anchors and overall task success exhibits a non-monotonic, inverted-U shape, indicating an optimal level of diversity for a given data budget.
> - Stabilizing the policy with dense sampling at a few core anchor conditions (creating a robust "policy skeleton") is critical before attempting to expand to more diverse or boundary conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represents a significant advancement in robotics, offering broad general capabilities through the unification of perception, instruction following, and control. These models leverage large-scale vision-language pretraining and action prediction, leading to strong semantic generalization and transfer abilities in various robotic settings (e.g., Zitkovich et al., 2023; Black et al., 2025). A notable trend involves treating actions as tokens in autoregressive multimodal models or generating continuous action trajectories using diffusion- or flow-based policies for improved smoothness and temporal fidelity (Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025). The availability of large cross-embodiment datasets and open-source generalist policies further facilitates the scaling and adaptation of VLAs (O’Neill et al., 2024; Team et al., 2025).

However, deploying these pretrained VLA models on specific physical platforms introduces challenges due to the "embodiment mismatch" and subtle environmental variations, which create distribution gaps between pretraining and deployment environments (Kawaharazuka et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). Consequently, real-robot post-training or adaptation is often a prerequisite for achieving reliable performance (Xiang et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025). This adaptation process is frequently constrained by tight data budgets, as collecting real-world robot demonstrations is resource-intensive and expensive, typically limiting available data to tens or hundreds of trajectories (Kim et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025a).

Within this context of data-efficient adaptation, a common heuristic involves maximizing condition diversity, aiming to collect demonstrations across the widest possible range of task conditions to enhance generalization (Li et al., 2025b; Hou et al., 2025). While this strategy aligns with data-scaling principles in other domains, the present work identifies it as a potential "diversity trap" in low-budget robotic adaptation regimes. The existing literature on VLA adaptation explores various techniques such as parameter-efficient updates, modular architectures, and constrained refinement to improve robustness (Zhang et al., 2025e; Ni et al., 2025). Active or error-driven data collection methods have also been investigated to prioritize demonstrations that offer high marginal value under limited budgets (Zhu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025).

This research specifically addresses the fundamental question of optimal data collection strategy under tight budgets. It argues that the "maximize coverage" heuristic can be self-defeating due to increased estimation noise when samples are spread too thinly. By formalizing this phenomenon as a Coverage–Density Trade-off, the paper bridges a gap in current adaptation strategies by proposing a structured data allocation framework that prioritzes policy stability before expanding coverage to high-risk areas.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the challenge of adapting advanced Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for reliable deployment on real robot hardware, especially when faced with strict data collection budgets. Despite the broad capabilities demonstrated by pretrained VLA models, a significant "embodiment gap" and environmental discrepancies necessitate real-world post-training. The high cost and time investment associated with collecting robot demonstrations mean that adaptation processes must operate under severe data limitations, typically involving only a few dozen to a few hundred trajectories.

The primary objective of this work is to identify and address a critical issue termed the "diversity trap." This trap arises from the common heuristic of collecting demonstrations across a wide array of distinct task conditions to maximize coverage and promote generalization. The authors hypothesize that under stringent data budgets, this "maximal diversity" approach can be counterproductive. By thinly distributing a limited number of samples across many unique conditions, each condition receives insufficient representation. This scarcity of data per condition leads to inflated estimation variance and consequently destabilizes the learned action vector field, undermining the reliability of the resulting policy.

To formalize this observed phenomenon, the research aims to characterize real-robot post-training as a "Coverage–Density Trade-off." This involves decomposing the policy error into two competing components: an estimation error (related to sample density at specific conditions) and an extrapolation bias (related to the coverage of the entire task space). The hypothesis is that increasing the number of distinct conditions (coverage) improves the ability to extrapolate to unseen regimes but simultaneously reduces the local sample density, thereby increasing estimation noise. The goal is to mathematically demonstrate the existence of an interior optimal allocation of unique conditions for a fixed budget, thereby challenging the assumption that uniform diverse sampling is always optimal under data scarcity. Empirical observations, showing an inverted-U relationship between task success and the number of distinct anchors, further motivate this formalization.

Guided by this theoretical analysis, a key objective is to propose a practical, two-stage adaptation framework called Anchor-Centric Adaptation (ACA). This framework is designed to navigate the Coverage–Density Trade-off effectively. It aims to first stabilize a robust "policy skeleton" by repeatedly demonstrating a minimal set of "core anchors," thereby concentrating the budget to reduce estimation noise. Subsequently, it seeks to selectively expand the policy's competence to "high-risk boundaries" by identifying areas where the initial policy performs poorly through a teacher-forced error mining process. The final objective is to integrate this boundary-specific knowledge via constrained residual updates, which are designed to improve performance in new areas without degrading the stability established at the core anchors. Ultimately, the research aims to validate this framework through real-robot experiments, demonstrating that ACA can significantly improve task reliability and success rates compared to conventional diverse sampling strategies under identical data budgets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research comprises a theoretical framework to formalize the Coverage–Density Trade-off, followed by the development and experimental validation of a two-stage adaptation framework called Anchor-Centric Adaptation (ACA).

#### 3.1. Theoretical Framework: Coverage–Density Trade-off

The theoretical analysis begins by formulating the real-robot adaptation process as learning a time-dependent conditional vector field, $f^*(z, p, t)$, where $p$ represents geometric variations (e.g., object pose), $t$ is flow time, and $z$ is the action-state. The adaptation is performed under a fixed, small budget of $N$ samples (trajectories) collected at $K$ distinct conditions, with $n_i$ repeats for each condition $p_i$, such that $\sum_{i=1}^K n_i = N$. The underlying regression target is modeled as $y = f^*(z, p, t) + \epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is sub-Gaussian noise representing real-world stochasticity.

To make the allocation trade-off explicit, the analysis employs three key assumptions:
- **A1 (Smoothness in Condition)**: The true vector field $f^*(z, \cdot, t)$ is $L$-Lipschitz with respect to the condition parameter $p$.
- **A2 (Effective Local Estimation)**: The effective estimation error at an observed condition $p_i$ decreases with its repeat count, specifically bounded by $C\sigma/\sqrt{n_i}$. This reflects variance reduction from repeated supervision.
- **A3 (Surrogate Generalization)**: An idealized local predictor queries the nearest observed condition, i.e., $\hat{f}(z, p, t) := \hat{f}(z, p_{i(p)}, t)$, where $p_{i(p)}$ is the nearest anchor to $p$. This assumption simplifies the analysis by making the coverage term explicit, particularly for worst-case dependence on fill distance $h$.

Based on these assumptions, Proposition 3.1 derives a **Coverage–Density Bound**: the worst-case expected field error is bounded by the sum of the maximum anchor estimation error (density term) and an extrapolation bias term ($Lh$, where $h$ is the fill distance, the maximum distance from any point in the task space to its nearest anchor).

To obtain a concrete scaling law, Lemma 3.2 considers a quasi-uniform anchor placement (where $h \propto K^{-1/d}$) and uniform repeats ($n_i = N/K$). This yields an error function $E(K) \propto C\sigma \sqrt{K/N} + LcK^{-1/d}$. Corollary 3.3 demonstrates that for non-negligible noise $\sigma > 0$, this error function is minimized at an interior optimal $K^* < N$, specifically $K^* \propto (L^2 N / \sigma^2)^{d/(d+2)}$. This theoretical result indicates that fully diverse sampling (where $K=N$ and $n_i=1$) is suboptimal, as its leading estimation noise term ($C\sigma$) remains constant, leading to the "diversity trap."

The theoretical framework further connects vector-field accuracy to task behavior using Grönwall’s inequality, suggesting that field approximation errors can amplify along trajectories. This motivates a two-stage allocation strategy: first, stabilize learning via repeated anchors (reducing density error), then selectively expand to boundary conditions (reducing coverage error) and update the policy with a parameter-efficient residual correction.

#### 3.2. Anchor-Centric Adaptation (ACA) Framework

ACA is a two-stage framework operationalizing the theoretical insights for budget-efficient VLA adaptation, partitioning the total budget $N$ into $N_A$ (anchoring), $N_{probe}$ (boundary identification), and $N_{bd}$ (boundary expansion). ACA builds upon flow-based VLA architectures (e.g., Black et al., 2024; 2025), decoupling perception (pretrained VLM) from action (task-specific Action Expert).

**Stage 1: Anchor-Centric Stabilization (Densification)**
- **Anchoring Strategy**: Core anchors $\{p_i\}$ are selected to form a coarse, quasi-uniform cover of the reachable workspace.
- **Data Collection**: Repeated demonstrations ($n_i > 1$) are collected at each anchor, forming dataset $\mathcal{D}_A$.
- **Training**: A stable core policy, $\theta_0$, is trained for the Action Expert using a flow-matching objective on $\mathcal{D}_A$, while the VLM remains frozen. This stage aims to reduce estimation variance in core regions.

**Boundary Mining via Teacher-Forced Deviation**
- **Probe Collection**: A set of probe trajectories ($N_{probe}$) is collected via teleoperation at conditions sampled across the workspace, forming a candidate set $\mathcal{P}_{cand}$.
- **Deviation Score**: For each $p \in \mathcal{P}_{cand}$, a teacher-forced deviation score, $e(p)$, is computed. This involves decoding actions from the Stage-1 policy on the *demonstration observation sequence* and comparing them to the *demonstrated actions* using an $L_1$ norm average. Teacher forcing prevents compounding errors, providing a cleaner proxy for action prediction error.
- **Selection and Expansion**: The $k$ candidates with the highest deviation scores are selected as boundary locations, $\mathcal{P}_{bd}$. These $k$ probe trajectories are reused. Additional trajectories are collected by sampling nearby conditions around these selected boundary locations, forming an expanded boundary dataset $\mathcal{D}_{bd}$.

**Stage 2: Constrained Residual Adaptation (Expansion)**
- **Boundary Training**: This stage trains on $\mathcal{D}_{bd}$, which includes the reused high-deviation probes and newly collected local-expansion trajectories.
- **Residual Architecture**: The Stage-1 policy $\theta_0$ is frozen. A small, trainable residual branch, $\Delta_\phi$, is introduced within the Action Expert. The final action prediction is $f_{final}(z, p, \tau) = f_{\theta_0}(z, p, \tau) + \Delta_\phi(z, p, \tau)$. The residual branch $\Delta_\phi$ is parameterized using low-rank adapters (LoRA) inserted into selected linear layers of the Action Expert and modulated by the flow-time embedding.
- **Optimization**: Only the parameters $\phi$ of the residual branch are optimized on $\mathcal{D}_{bd}$ using the flow-matching objective, with $\theta_0$ fixed. The residual branch is zero-initialized to start exactly from the Stage-1 policy, facilitating localized capacity for boundary regimes while minimizing global drift.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

Experiments are conducted on a 7-DoF Franka Panda arm with a multi-view camera system (RealSense D435 and D405). Demonstrations are collected via leader-follower teleoperation. Four tabletop manipulation tasks are used: Block Stacking, Cup Placement, Table Cleaning, and Toy Tidying. Performance is evaluated using **Region-Level Success Rates (S@i)**, which categorize the workspace into nested regions of increasing difficulty: S@1 (core, 25% area), S@2 (medium, 50% area), and S@3 (near-boundary, 90% area). Success rates are reported over 20 evaluation trajectories per task. The VLA policies $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ (Black et al., 2024; 2025) are used as backbones. Training details include Adam optimizer, batch size 64, 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs, and specific learning rate schedules for both stages, with LoRA rank and $\alpha$ of 32 for residual adaptation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to validate the ACA framework in mitigating the diversity trap, assess sensitivity to anchor properties, and quantify the contributions of boundary mining and residual adaptation.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison with Baseline

ACA was compared against a baseline using standard VLA fine-tuning with maximal-diversity sampling (every demonstration collected at a distinct, non-repeated condition).

- **Consistent Performance Gains**: ACA consistently outperformed the diversity-first baseline across all tested data budgets (N=50, 100, 150) and all region-level success metrics (S@1, S@2, S@3). For instance, with a budget of N=100, ACA achieved a 72.5% mean success rate, which represents a 40.8% absolute improvement over the baseline's 31.7%.
- **Alleviating the Diversity Trap**: The baseline frequently exhibited a collapse in success rates in near-boundary regions (S@3), often dropping to zero, indicating insufficient per-condition density. ACA, by contrast, maintained higher stability across the entire workspace. For example, in Block Stacking at N=150, ACA achieved an 80% success rate (16/20 trials) in S@3, compared to the baseline's 20% (4/20 trials).
- **Sample Efficiency**: ACA demonstrated notable sample efficiency. At the lowest budget of N=50, ACA's mean success rate of 46.3% was comparable to the baseline's performance at N=150 (52.9%), suggesting that ACA can achieve similar or better performance with one-third of the data.
- **Robustness to Distribution Shifts**: As task conditions shifted from the central core (S@1) to the boundaries (S@3), baseline performance deteriorated significantly. ACA maintained more stable performance across these regions, supporting the claim that anchor-based consolidation provides a robust foundation.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

**Anchor Spatial Distribution (N=100, K=6 anchors):**
- **Necessity of Anchoring**: All tested anchor configurations consistently outperformed the baseline, even spatially biased layouts like "Top-Left." This highlights that establishing a stable "policy skeleton" through repeated anchoring is more impactful than uniform spatial coverage initially.
- **Centralization and Reach**: Centralized anchor arrangements (e.g., "Center Rect," "Circular") yielded the highest success rates, particularly in S@3 (boundary regions). This indicates that minimizing the maximum extrapolation distance from any point in the workspace to an anchor (achieved by centralized anchors) is effective in balancing local sampling density with global stability.
- **Geometric Pattern Invariance**: The performance differences between rectangular and circular anchor arrangements were marginal, suggesting that the benefits of ACA are largely robust to the specific geometric pattern of anchors, provided they maintain a sufficiently centralized distribution.

**Impact of Anchor Quantity (K) on Success Rate:**
- **Inverted-U Trend**: Across all budgets (N=50, 100, 150), task success exhibited a non-monotonic, inverted-U relationship with the number of anchors (K). This directly validated the Coverage–Density Trade-off and the existence of an optimal K, where increasing diversity beyond this point leads to a decline in performance.
- **Diversity Trap Validation**: As K increased past the optimal point, success rates tended to decrease, supporting the hypothesis that excessive diversity under a fixed budget dilutes per-condition sampling density, leading to policy destabilization by estimation noise.
- **Budget-Dependent Optimum**: The optimal K appeared to scale with the total interaction budget, with the performance peak broadening as N increased from 50 to 150. This aligns with the theoretical prediction that larger budgets can support greater diversity without sacrificing stability.

**Ablation on Boundary Mining and Residual Adaptation (N=100, 6 anchors):**
- **Synergy**: The combination of error-driven boundary mining and constrained residual updates yielded the highest mean success rate (72.5%), demonstrating that both identifying critical data gaps and having a stable update mechanism are crucial.
- **Effectiveness of Error-Driven Mining**: The addition of the boundary mining phase improved performance in boundary regions (S@3). Compared to a random-sampling baseline of equivalent size, error-driven mining significantly raised S@3 success, confirming that teacher-forced deviation effectively identifies extrapolation risks.
- **Stability via Residual Updates**: Using the residual adaptation pathway preserved stability in the core regions (S@1 and S@2) while effectively improving performance in under-supported boundary areas. Full-parameter fine-tuning for boundary expansion, while possible, resulted in performance degradation in the consolidated core, which the residual approach avoided.

**Ablation on Anchor Budget ($N_A$) Ratio (N=100 total budget):**
- **Stabilization as Prerequisite**: Reducing the anchor budget ($N_A$) to 50 (from an optimal of 80 or 100) led to a global performance decline, with S@1 success dropping from 80.0% to 65.0%. This validated that an insufficient initial anchor budget results in a fragile policy skeleton, hindering subsequent boundary adaptation.
- **Optimal Budget Partitioning**: While an $N_A$ of 100 (out of 100 total budget) maximized core success (82.5%), allocating $N_A=80$ trajectories (leaving 20 for boundary mining and expansion) yielded the highest overall mean success (72.5%) and superior boundary performance (65.0% in S@3 compared to 55.0% with $N_A=100$). This indicates that, beyond a certain consolidation level, dedicating a portion of the budget to targeted Stage 2 expansion provides greater marginal gains than further anchor repetition.

**Performance Gains Across Different VLAs:**
- **Architecture-Agnostic Efficacy**: ACA was tested on both $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ backbones and demonstrated consistent performance improvements for both models across all tasks. This suggests that the anchor-centric principle is a general data-allocation strategy applicable to flow-based VLAs.
- **Synergy with Base Capabilities**: While ACA enhanced both models, the stronger $\pi_{0.5}$ backbone achieved larger absolute gains, indicating that higher initial representation quality in the base VLA model facilitates more effective anchor-based refinement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in the context of adapting advanced Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for real-world deployment. The key significance lies in its principled approach to data-efficient adaptation under stringent budgetary constraints, a common and critical challenge in practical robotics.

Firstly, the work formally characterizes and empirically validates the **Coverage–Density Trade-off** and identifies the **diversity trap** in VLA adaptation. By demonstrating that indiscriminately maximizing coverage with sparse, single-shot demonstrations can destabilize learning due to non-vanishing estimation noise, the research provides a theoretical foundation for understanding suboptimal data collection strategies. The observed inverted-U performance trend with respect to the number of distinct task conditions offers a clear empirical signal of this trade-off, guiding practitioners to a more informed approach to data allocation.

Secondly, the proposed **Anchor-Centric Adaptation (ACA)** framework offers a practical and effective solution to navigate this trade-off. By initially focusing on repeated demonstrations at a minimal set of "core anchors" to stabilize a policy skeleton, ACA addresses the estimation noise problem. Subsequently, its error-driven boundary mining and constrained residual adaptation stages allow for targeted and parameter-efficient expansion to high-risk areas without compromising the stability of the learned core behaviors. This two-stage approach leads to significantly improved task reliability and success rates under the same data budget compared to conventional diverse sampling.

The potential impact of this work is multifaceted:
- **Enhanced Reliability and Safety**: By ensuring more stable policy skeletons and mitigating unpredictable behaviors caused by data scarcity, ACA directly contributes to the development of more reliable and safer embodied agents for real-world applications.
- **Data-Efficient Robotics**: The framework's ability to achieve robust performance with limited human demonstrations is crucial for lowering the operational costs and time associated with deploying sophisticated robotic systems. This is particularly relevant in specialized or hazardous environments where data collection is inherently difficult and expensive.
- **Democratization of Robotics**: By reducing the data requirements for adapting high-performance VLA foundation models, ACA can lower the barrier to entry for researchers and industries in deploying advanced robotics. This could accelerate the adoption of robotic solutions in various domains, from manufacturing to service industries, where tailored adaptation with minimal new data is essential.
- **Guidance for Future Research**: The theoretical formalization provides a strong basis for further research into optimal experimental design for robotic learning. Future work, as suggested by the authors, could explore extending the anchoring principle to long-horizon tasks via hierarchical decomposition and developing autonomous methods for anchor discovery, further optimizing resource utilization. This research offers a foundational step towards more intelligent and resource-aware robotic learning systems.
