# Enhancing Continual Learning of Vision-Language Models via Dynamic Prefix Weighting

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Yonsei University developed Dynamic Prefix Weighting (DPW), a framework that enhances continual learning for Vision-Language Models by applying dynamic, token-level weighting to prefixes and adapters. DPW achieved state-of-the-art performance on the Multi-domain Task Incremental Learning benchmark, outperforming existing methods by 2.0% (Avg) and 2.3% (Last) while maintaining parameter efficiency.

## Problem

- Existing Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods for Vision-Language Model (VLM) continual learning apply task-specific information uniformly at the sample level, ignoring varying token relevance.
- Conventional attention mechanisms used in prefix-tuning can blur distinctions between task-relevant and task-irrelevant tokens, hindering fine-grained weighting and reducing task separability.
- Fixed normalization of prefix weights limits the dynamic adjustment of the total amount of task-specific information injected per token, potentially leading to under or over-adaptation.

## Method

- **DPW** introduces a gating module with ==Reparameterized Prefix Attention (RePA)== to replace conventional attention, creating task-specific projections for prefixes that directly capture token relevance.
- A ==Conditional Activation (CondAct)== module processes RePA scores, providing dynamic normalization and filtering of prefix weights to control the total amount and selection of information per token.
- A ==Residual Weighting Mechanism (RWM)== adaptively scales the contribution of an adapter (==LoRA==) based on residual prefix weights, ensuring adapters complement prefixes only when additional adaptation is needed.

## Results

- DPW achieved state-of-the-art performance on the MTIL (Order I) benchmark, with 'Ours' variant (**30.8M** parameters) outperforming GIFT by **2.0%** (Avg. score) and **2.3%** (Last score).
- On the challenging ODCL-CIL benchmark, DPW recorded a **78.6%** Avg. score and **86.6%** Last score, surpassing the next best method DPeCLIP by **2.5%** and **2.0%** respectively.
- Ablation studies confirmed that all components (RePA, CondAct, RWM) contribute to improved performance, with RWM providing the most significant gains across Transfer, Avg., and Last scores.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pretrained attention query projections can reduce the distinction between task-relevant and task-irrelevant tokens, leading to increased cosine similarity and diminished task separability in feature space.
> - Dynamically adjusting the total information injected via prefixes per token (CondAct) is crucial for preserving zero-shot performance and mitigating forward forgetting in continual learning.
> - Adapters, when guided by a residual weighting mechanism, can effectively complement prefixes by addressing tokens that require further adaptation beyond what prefixes alone provide, enabling orthogonal contributions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges within the field of Continual Learning (CL), specifically as applied to Vision-Language Models (VLMs) such as CLIP. Continual learning aims to enable models to adapt to sequential data streams while mitigating catastrophic forgetting, a phenomenon where new knowledge acquisition leads to the loss of previously learned information. This capability is particularly relevant for VLMs, which, despite their zero-shot generalization abilities, often require adaptation to specific downstream tasks.

Traditional approaches to CL for VLMs have involved fine-tuning the entire model or employing knowledge distillation techniques. However, the large scale of VLMs presents significant computational and data storage challenges for full model fine-tuning or memory-based replay. This has led to the exploration of Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods, which freeze the backbone model and introduce a small number of learnable parameters. Common PEFT techniques include adapters, which insert lightweight modules into the network, and prefix-tuning, which appends learnable prompt vectors to input tokens. These methods enable adaptation to new tasks with reduced computational cost and parameter overhead.

Existing PEFT approaches in CL for VLMs have primarily focused on adaptively weighting newly introduced parameters at the sample level. However, a limitation in these methods is their uniform treatment of all tokens within a sample, assigning them equivalent amounts of task-specific information. Research indicates that dynamically adjusting the information injection at a token-level based on task relevance can improve performance. Additionally, current prefix-tuning methods often rely on attention mechanisms to assign prefix weights. These mechanisms, designed to capture global contextual relationships, can inadvertently blur token-wise distinctions, making fine-grained weighting difficult. This blurring affects not only individual samples but also hinders the disentanglement of token embeddings across tasks, which can reduce knowledge transfer effectiveness and increase task interference in CL settings. The paper identifies these limitations as fundamental issues hindering effective continual learning in VLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the continual learning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) by enabling more precise and dynamic adaptation to sequential tasks while preserving previously acquired knowledge and improving zero-shot generalization.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods for VLM continual learning:

- **Lack of Token-Level Dynamic Weighting:** Existing PEFT methods, such as those employing adapters or prefix-tuning, typically apply task-specific information uniformly across all input tokens within a given sample. This sample-level weighting approach disregards the varying relevance of individual tokens to a specific task. For example, some tokens (e.g., those corresponding to foreground objects) might require more task-specific adaptation than others (e.g., background tokens). The authors identify that previous studies have shown the benefits of dynamic, token-dependent adjustment of injected information.
- **Limitations of Conventional Attention Mechanisms in Prefix-Tuning:** In prefix-tuning, attention mechanisms are commonly used to assign weights to prefixes based on their interaction with input tokens. However, the authors observe that the pretrained attention projections, designed for general contextual understanding, tend to project task-relevant and task-irrelevant tokens closer together in the feature space. This behavior obscures the distinct characteristics of tokens, making it difficult for the model to accurately capture task relevance and assign fine-grained prefix weights. This phenomenon is not limited to within-sample distinctions but also extends to reducing the separability of token embeddings across different tasks, leading to increased task interference and less effective knowledge transfer.
- **Fixed Normalization of Prefix Weights:** Many existing prefix-tuning approaches normalize prefix weights (e.g., using a softmax function), often forcing their sum to one. While this can provide stability, it limits the dynamic adjustment of the total amount of additional information injected per token. Such a fixed sum can prevent tokens that require substantial adaptation from receiving adequate task-specific guidance, or conversely, over-inject information into less relevant tokens, potentially disrupting pretrained knowledge.
- **Ineffective Synergy Between Prefixes and Adapters:** While both prefixes and adapters are widely used PEFT techniques, their combined utilization in CL often lacks a mechanism to dynamically balance their contributions. An effective continual learning framework would ideally leverage the strengths of both: prefixes for direct task-specific conditioning and adapters for more generalized or subtle adjustments. Current methods either use one predominantly or combine them without a refined token-level coordination strategy.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a unified framework, Dynamic Prefix Weighting (DPW), designed to provide fine-grained, token-level modulation of task-specific information by dynamically assigning weights to both prefixes and adapters.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Dynamic Prefix Weighting (DPW), is a framework designed to assign refined weights to prefixes and adapters based on the task relevance of individual input tokens, fostering effective synergy between these parameter-efficient fine-tuning components. DPW consists of two main modules: a gating module and a residual weighting mechanism.

#### 3.1. Gating Module

The gating module is introduced to replace the conventional attention mechanism typically used in prefix-tuning, aiming to provide more accurate and dynamically adjustable prefix weights. It comprises two sub-components:

- **Reparameterized Prefix Attention (RePA):** Instead of relying on the original attention's query-key dot product to compute prefix scores (S_XP = Q * K_P^T), RePA simplifies this process into a single affine transformation (S_XP = X * W_G + B_G). This reparameterization effectively unifies the original attention projection parameters (W_Q, W_K, b_Q, b_K) and the learnable prefix key (P_K) into new composite learnable matrices (W_G, B_G). By training these composite parameters, RePA aims to capture task relevance more directly and precisely from input tokens (X) without disrupting the pretrained knowledge encoded in the original projection matrices. This design allows for learning task-specific projections exclusively for prefixes, leading to prefix scores that better reflect token relevance for the current task.

- **Conditional Activation (CondAct):** This module processes the prefix scores obtained from RePA, transforming them into dynamically adjusted weights. It replaces the softmax activation used in conventional prefix-tuning. CondAct operates in two steps:
 - **Conditional Normalization:** After applying a sigmoid function (σ) to the raw prefix scores (s_ijk), the weights (g_ijk) are normalized. If the sum of sigmoid-activated scores for a given token (Σ_k σ(s_ijk)) is greater than or equal to one, the weights are normalized such that their sum equals one. Otherwise, they remain as the sigmoid-activated scores. This mechanism allows the total sum of prefix weights for each token to dynamically range up to one, preventing task-specific information from becoming overly dominant, which helps preserve zero-shot performance and mitigate forward forgetting.
 - **Conditional Filtering:** This step refines the normalized weights (g_ijk) by selectively suppressing less relevant prefixes. It introduces a token-wise dynamic cutoff value (cutoff_ijk) based on the likelihood of the prefix attention score for the `[CLS]` token under a Gaussian distribution learned from training data. Prefixes with weights below this dynamic cutoff are effectively zeroed out. This adaptive filtering, conditioned on task-specific likelihoods, allows the model to leverage only the most informative prefixes for each token, further enhancing knowledge transfer and adaptation. The final prefix output (O_prefix) is then computed by multiplying these filtered weights (e_G) with the prefix value vectors (P_V).

#### 3.2. Residual Weighting Mechanism (RWM)

RWM addresses tokens that may require additional adaptation beyond what prefixes provide. It leverages the "residual" prefix weights to dynamically control the contribution of an adapter. For parameter efficiency, LoRA is employed as the adapter, using a shared down-projection matrix (D) and task-specific up-projection matrices (U_t). The shared down-projection matrix D is initialized with the top-k left singular vectors of the CLIP model's value projection matrix (W_V) and frozen during CL. This constrains the adapter's fine-tuning to a principal subspace, effectively leveraging pretrained knowledge.

The adapter's output (E_t(X)) is scaled by an adaptive factor (Δ_i). This factor is calculated as the maximum of zero and the sum of sigmoid-activated prefix scores minus one (max(0, Σ_k σ(s_ijk) - 1)). If the sum of prefix weights for a token does not exceed one, the corresponding adapter weight becomes zero, meaning the adapter does not contribute to that token. This mechanism ensures that the adapter selectively applies information only to tokens where prefixes alone are insufficient for adaptation. This selective application allows for a balance between the aggressive modifications introduced by prefixes and the more conservative updates from adapters, achieving complementary adaptation.

The final output of the DPW module (O_R) for each attention head is the element-wise sum of the prefix output (O_prefix) and the weighted adapter output (O_adapter).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates DPW across two domain-class incremental learning benchmarks: Multi-domain Task Incremental Learning (MTIL) and Open-Domain Continual Learning (ODCL-CIL), using standard metrics: Transfer (zero-shot generalization), Avg. (average accuracy across all tasks and stages), and Last (final performance across all tasks).

#### 4.1. Performance on MTIL Benchmark (Order I)

On the MTIL Order I benchmark, DPW consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance across all evaluation metrics. The "Ours" variant (30.8M parameters) outperforms the second-best approach, GIFT, which employs full fine-tuning with additional synthetic data, by 2.0% in Avg. score and 2.3% in Last score. Compared to other PEFT methods like MoE-Adapter and DIKI, DPW demonstrates superior performance with a comparable or lower number of trainable parameters. The high Transfer scores (70.4% for "Ours") indicate that DPW effectively adapts to new tasks while preserving previously learned knowledge, mitigating forward forgetting. A parameter-efficient variant, "Ours†" (4.6M parameters), also consistently outperforms all competing methods.

#### 4.2. Performance on ODCL-CIL Benchmark

In the more challenging ODCL-CIL scenario, where task IDs are unavailable during inference, DPW again achieves state-of-the-art performance. The "Ours" variant records a 78.6% Avg. score and 86.6% Last score, surpassing the next best method, DPeCLIP, by 2.5% and 2.0% respectively. This indicates DPW's robustness in class-incremental settings without explicit task information.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study on MTIL demonstrates the contribution of each DPW component:
- Replacing the traditional query-key dot product with **RePA** improved the Last score, indicating refined prefix scores.
- Replacing softmax with **CondAct** further improved overall performance by enabling dynamic control over the amount of information added per token.
- Combining RePA and CondAct yielded additional gains by better capturing task-specific characteristics.
- Integrating the **Residual Weighting Mechanism (RWM)** with both RePA and CondAct provided the most significant improvements across Transfer, Avg., and Last scores, demonstrating its effectiveness in allowing adapters to complement prefixes for tokens requiring further adaptation.

#### 4.4. Analysis of Pretrained Attention Limitations

The paper provides an analysis illustrating that pretrained query projections can reduce the distinction between task-relevant and task-irrelevant tokens. Cosine similarity analysis showed that after query projection, the similarity between task-relevant and task-irrelevant tokens increased substantially, blurring distinctions. UMAP visualizations further indicated that token embeddings from different tasks, initially well-separated, collapsed into overlapping regions after projection, leading to diminished task separability and increased interference. This finding supports the motivation for RePA.

#### 4.5. Analysis of RePA and CondAct Components

- **RePA Analysis:** Experiments showed that RePA is an effective and efficient alternative to conventional attention mechanisms for prefix scoring. Re-training attention projection matrices, while improving performance, introduced a significantly higher parameter count (228.0M) compared to RePA (30.8M), with RePA still achieving better performance.
- **CondAct Analysis:** Incorporating a sigmoid activation into RePA initially improved performance. However, conditional normalization in CondAct mitigated the issue of overly large total weights from sigmoid, leading to substantial improvement in Transfer accuracy by maintaining prefix information within a stable range. Conditional filtering further enhanced Transfer scores by enabling dynamic and token-wise selection of informative prefixes based on learned score distributions.

#### 4.6. Analysis of RWM and Weight Distribution

- **RWM Effectiveness:** RWM demonstrated superior performance compared to simply increasing prefix length or using an adapter with a learnable router. The results suggest that RWM enables adapters to operate orthogonally to prefixes, complementing them by extending beyond the subspace spanned by prefix vectors, thereby addressing tokens not sufficiently adapted by prefixes alone.
- **Adaptive Weight Distribution:** An analysis of weight distribution revealed that trained tasks relied more heavily on prefix information, while zero-shot tasks depended primarily on the adapter. This adaptive weighting mechanism suggests that the model can dynamically adjust its reliance on task-specific (prefixes) versus generalizable (adapters) knowledge depending on whether it encounters learned or novel distributions.

#### 4.7. Parameter Efficiency and Computational Costs

DPW consistently outperforms a baseline method (DIKI) across various configurations of learnable parameters, demonstrating higher performance even with fewer parameters. Furthermore, DPW exhibits lower inference times compared to other PEFT approaches, attributed to the simplicity of RePA's affine transformations and the removal of explicit router operations in RWM.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, Dynamic Prefix Weighting (DPW), that introduces a novel mechanism for token-level dynamic weighting of prefixes and adapters in continual learning scenarios for Vision-Language Models. The work addresses several identified limitations in existing parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, particularly the uniform application of task-specific information across tokens and the constraints imposed by conventional attention mechanisms.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to achieve state-of-the-art performance on challenging domain-class incremental learning benchmarks (MTIL and ODCL-CIL). This includes outperforming methods that utilize significantly more parameters or additional data, while maintaining parameter efficiency. The observed improvements in "Transfer" scores indicate enhanced zero-shot generalization and reduced forward forgetting, which are critical for practical continual learning systems. The empirical findings regarding the limitations of pretrained attention mechanisms in maintaining token and task separability also contribute to a deeper understanding of challenges in adapting large pretrained models.

The potential impact of DPW extends to:
- **Improving VLM Adaptation:** By enabling more precise and adaptive injection of task-specific information at the token level, DPW could facilitate more effective adaptation of VLMs to diverse, evolving real-world tasks.
- **Enhanced Continual Learning Systems:** The framework’s ability to dynamically balance prefix and adapter contributions, coupled with its token-level conditioning, offers a robust approach to mitigating catastrophic forgetting and improving knowledge transfer across sequential tasks.
- **Broader PEFT Applications:** The core principles of dynamic token-level weighting, reparameterized prefix attention, and residual weighting could be generalized to improve the effectiveness of prefix-tuning and adapter-based methods in various other downstream tasks beyond continual learning, potentially leading to more efficient and adaptable large language and vision models.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The proposed design, which streamlines operations and avoids complex routing mechanisms, also demonstrates benefits in inference time, making it potentially suitable for resource-constrained environments or applications requiring rapid adaptation.

Overall, this work provides a method for enhancing the robustness and adaptability of VLMs in continual learning settings, with implications for the broader field of parameter-efficient model adaptation.
