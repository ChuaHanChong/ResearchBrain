# Verify Before You Distill: Prompt-Level Teacher Gating for On-Policy Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Teacher-Gated On-Policy Distillation (TGOPD) audits a frozen teacher per prompt before letting it supervise. The teacher draws three probe rollouts; a verifier scores them, and a two-of-three pass rate opens a gate routing the prompt exclusively to dense on-policy distillation, else to verifier-grounded GRPO. It beats vanilla OPD in all six domain-scale settings and lifts teacher-node GPU utilization from 9.8% to 78.9%.

## Problem

- Vanilla on-policy distillation admits dense reverse-KL teacher supervision on every prompt without checking correctness, so a confidently wrong teacher drives a strong misleading update.
- Distributional proxies such as teacher entropy or teacher-student likelihood agreement measure uncertainty, not outcome correctness; teacher confidence separates reliable from unreliable code prompts at only 0.51 AUROC.
- Outcome-aware methods gate individual trajectories or tokens, and domain routers never audit the routed teacher, while asynchronous teacher nodes sit idle 57-78% of the time.

## Method

- ==Teacher reliability probe==: the frozen teacher draws three fresh rollouts per prompt, the same verifier scores them, and their pass rate is an unbiased ==Binomial estimate== of expected teacher reward.
- ==Hard gate== opens when the probe pass rate reaches tau = 2/3, selecting the dense ==reverse-KL OPD== advantage; otherwise it withholds the teacher and applies un-normalized ==verifier-grounded GRPO==, never blending the two.
- Probes are issued concurrently with student decoding to fill the teacher node's structurally idle window in the ==asynchronous slime loop==; teacher scoring stays unconditional on every prompt.

## Results

- Beats vanilla OPD in all six domain-scale settings, largest on code (**+3.0 average** at 4B, **+2.9** at 35B); at 35B it is the only method with positive LiveCodeBench transfer, **+3.0** over base.
- Multi-domain training raises the seven-benchmark average from **53.40** to **54.54** at 4B and **60.99** to **61.94** at 35B; teacher utilization rises **9.8%** to **78.9%** for a **5.9%** step-time increase.
- Threshold sweep with five probes is inverted-U, peaking near majority vote (3/5); masking gated-off prompts instead of the GRPO fallback still recovers about **90%** of the gain (**+1.08** vs **+1.20**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Verify outcomes rather than trusting confidence: model-internal uncertainty can be nearly uninformative about correctness in domains like code, where errors look exactly like successes.
> - Withdraw supervision selectively instead of weakening it everywhere; uniformly shrinking the teacher's role removes useful signal together with harmful signal and can undercut the base model.
> - Gains come mainly from blocking bad supervision, not from replacing it; but the audit needs an automatic verifier, leaving open-ended tasks uncovered.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of language model post-training, specifically focusing on On-Policy Distillation (OPD). OPD is recognized as a compute-efficient approach that accelerates post-training by enabling a student model to learn from dense, token-level supervision provided by a stronger, frozen teacher model on the student's own generated rollouts. This method has been shown to achieve teacher-level accuracy significantly faster than Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) by transforming sparse outcome supervision into rich, per-token guidance.

Despite its efficiency, vanilla OPD faces two primary challenges. First, in asynchronous training setups, the teacher model's computational resources are often underutilized, remaining idle for substantial periods while awaiting student rollouts. Second, vanilla OPD applies teacher supervision uniformly across all prompts without a prompt-level reliability check. This unconditional application of a mode-seeking reverse KL objective can be problematic when the teacher is confidently incorrect on certain prompts, leading to strong but misleading updates and potentially inducing negative transfer into the student model. Existing distributional proxies, such as entropy or teacher-student likelihood agreement, primarily measure uncertainty or compatibility and do not directly verify the correctness of the teacher's output.

The broader landscape of reliability-aware distillation has explored two main directions. One direction uses distributional evidence, with methods like EOPD introducing forward KL at high-entropy teacher tokens, TrOPD defining trust regions based on teacher-student decoding agreement, and REOPOLD clipping token rewards using likelihood ratios and student entropy. These methods aim to mitigate uncertainty or optimize risk but do not directly assess outcome correctness. The second direction incorporates outcome evidence, as seen in RG-OPD, which retains trajectory-level distillation when verifier feedback aligns with the teacher-student likelihood gap, or RLSD, which uses environmental correctness to determine update direction. SPOT evaluates teacher-proposed branches via verifier-scored student continuations. While these methods use outcome feedback, they typically operate at the trajectory or token-branch level, or are applied in specific self-distillation contexts.

TGOPD distinguishes itself within this landscape by introducing a mechanism that utilizes otherwise-idle teacher capacity to perform repeated, verifier-scored teacher probes at the *prompt level*. This provides direct outcome evidence for teacher reliability, allowing for a strategic, prompt-level decision to either admit dense OPD supervision or revert to a verifier-grounded GRPO signal. This approach addresses both the computational inefficiency and the reliability concerns of vanilla OPD in a novel, integrated manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness and computational efficiency of On-Policy Distillation (OPD) for post-training large language models. This overarching goal is driven by two key motivations:

The first motivation stems from a fundamental weakness observed in vanilla OPD: the unconditional admission of teacher supervision without a prompt-level reliability check. The authors identify that a teacher's reliability can vary significantly from one prompt to another. When a teacher provides a confidently incorrect response, the mode-seeking nature of the reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence objective used in OPD can amplify these errors, leading to strong yet misleading updates that can negatively impact student performance. Existing methods that attempt to account for teacher reliability often rely on distributional proxies like entropy or teacher-student likelihood agreement. While these proxies can indicate uncertainty or compatibility, they do not directly verify the correctness of the teacher's output. Figure 2 in the paper illustrates this disconnect, showing that teacher self-confidence on code generation tasks can be a poor indicator of actual outcome correctness, with high-confidence responses still being wrong a high percentage of the time in low-reliability regimes. Therefore, a key objective is to develop a mechanism that can directly and accurately verify teacher reliability at the individual prompt level using outcome evidence, thereby enabling a selective application of dense teacher supervision.

The second motivation addresses a significant inefficiency in the typical asynchronous deployment of OPD. In such setups, the student model generates rollouts on dedicated inference nodes, while a stronger, often domain-specialized, frozen teacher model scores these completed rollouts on a separate node. The teacher's scoring pass, being a forward evaluation over already-generated tokens, is computationally less intensive than the student's autoregressive decoding. Crucially, the teacher's scoring cannot commence until a batch of student rollouts is ready. This inherent dependency leads to periods where the teacher's accelerators remain idle between scoring batches. Figure 1 quantifies this inefficiency, showing that a teacher node in a 4B run averages only 9.8% GPU utilization, remaining below 5% for 59% of the measured hour, while other nodes are busy. This observed underutilization of teacher-side compute capacity presents an opportunity. The objective is to leverage this otherwise-idle capacity to perform the prompt-level reliability checks, thereby improving the training signal without incurring substantial additional wall-clock overhead.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
1. **Develop a prompt-level teacher reliability verification mechanism:** This mechanism should directly assess the correctness of teacher responses using outcome feedback, rather than relying on confidence scores or distributional measures, to prevent negative transfer from unreliable teacher signals.
2. **Integrate this mechanism efficiently into the OPD pipeline:** The reliability checks should primarily utilize the teacher's idle computational capacity in asynchronous settings, transforming wasted resources into a source of improved training signal.
3. **Implement a dynamic supervision routing strategy:** Based on the reliability assessment, the system should adaptively choose between dense OPD supervision (when the teacher is reliable) and a verifier-grounded fallback mechanism (when the teacher is unreliable), to ensure that the student always receives an appropriate and beneficial learning signal.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Teacher-Gated On-Policy Distillation (TGOPD) is designed around the principle of verifying teacher reliability at the prompt level before admitting dense supervision. The methodology involves a novel approach to reliability estimation, a hard gating mechanism, and adaptive supervision routing, all integrated into an asynchronous training pipeline to leverage idle compute capacity.

**Teacher Reliability Probe and Gate:**
The core of TGOPD is its method for estimating teacher reliability. For each prompt `x`, the teacher's reliability, `R_T(x)`, is defined as the expected verifier reward of its generated rollouts. To estimate this, the teacher independently generates a small number of `K_T` probe rollouts, denoted `{hat_y_k}`, for the same prompt `x` using its own policy `π_T(·|x)`. These probe rollouts are then scored by the same binary verifier `r(x, y)` used for student rollouts, which evaluates correctness against a ground-truth outcome (e.g., unit tests for code, rule-based judge for math). The empirical pass rate of these probes, `q_T(x) = (1/K_T) * sum(r(x, hat_y_k))`, serves as an unbiased estimator of the teacher's prompt-level reliability. This approach directly uses task outcomes, offering a more direct assessment of correctness than model-internal confidence or entropy measures. In the main experiments, `K_T` is set to 3.

A hard gate, `g(x)`, then determines the supervision strategy based on this reliability estimate. The gate opens (i.e., `g(x) = 1`) if `q_T(x)` meets or exceeds a predefined threshold `τ`; otherwise, it closes (`g(x) = 0`). For `K_T = 3`, `τ = 2/3` is typically used, meaning at least two of the three probes must pass for the gate to open. This binary decision ensures exclusive routing of supervision signals.

**Gate-Conditioned Supervision Routing:**
For each prompt, TGOPD routes the supervision to one of two distinct signals:
1. **Dense On-Policy Distillation (OPD):** If the gate opens (`g(x) = 1`, i.e., the teacher is deemed reliable), the student receives dense, token-level supervision from the teacher. This signal is derived from the mode-seeking reverse KL objective, specifically the teacher-student log-likelihood ratio (`hat_A_OPD_i,t`), as in vanilla OPD (Equation 1). This ensures that when the teacher is reliable, the student efficiently concentrates on the teacher's high-probability behavior.
2. **Verifier-Grounded GRPO (Generalized Advantage Estimation for Policy Optimization):** If the gate closes (`g(x) = 0`, i.e., the teacher is deemed unreliable), teacher supervision is withheld. Instead, the student learns from verifier-grounded GRPO. This provides a trajectory-level, scalar advantage (`hat_A_GRPO_i,t`, Equation 2), which is the student's verifier reward `r(x, y_i)` centered around the mean reward of its rollout group. This signal is verifier-grounded but coarser, reinforcing relatively better attempts within the student's own rollouts. If all rollouts in a group yield identical rewards, the centered GRPO advantage is zero, producing no update for that prompt.

The final per-token advantage for the student update, `hat_A_TGOPD_i,t`, is a linear combination controlled by `g(x)` (Equation 6), acting as a selector: `g(x) * hat_A_OPD_i,t + (1 - g(x)) * hat_A_GRPO_i,t`. This ensures that OPD and GRPO never jointly supervise the same prompt. The student then optimizes a standard PPO-clipped policy-gradient surrogate objective, `L_TGOPD(θ)` (Equation 7), using this advantage.

**Algorithm and System Realization:**
TGOPD is integrated into an asynchronous rollout-update cycle (Algorithm 1). During each cycle:
- Student rollouts are generated on dedicated rollout nodes.
- Concurrently, the teacher generates `K_T` probe rollouts on the teacher node. This crucial step is designed to primarily occupy the teacher's otherwise-idle time, as it typically waits for student rollouts to be ready for scoring.
- Both the teacher's log-probabilities for student rollouts and the verifier scores for student rollouts are computed unconditionally. This is because teacher scoring is cheap and verifier scores are needed for the GRPO fallback.
- The reliability `q_T(x)` is calculated from the teacher probes, the gate `g(x)` is applied, and the appropriate advantage (`hat_A_OPD` or `hat_A_GRPO`) is selected for each prompt.
- Finally, the student's parameters are updated using the selected advantages.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted at two scales: a 4B dense student (Qwen3.5-4B) and a 35B mixture-of-experts student (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B). For each of three domains (mathematics, code, instruction following), a dedicated domain-specialist teacher, trained using GRPO, is used and frozen. The training uses the "slime" asynchronous framework, with PPO-clipped surrogate updates and IcePop for train-inference mismatch control. Baselines include Vanilla OPD, TrOPD, RG-OPD, and an RLSD-style variant. Training data are sourced from DAPO-Math-17K, CodeI/O, and Nemotron-Cascade 2. Evaluation is performed using standard in-domain benchmarks such as AIME, HMMT-Feb for math; LiveCodeBench, OJBench for code; and IFBench, IFEval for instruction following.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TGOPD demonstrates its effectiveness in improving student performance and resource utilization across various configurations.

**Single-Domain Performance (Table 1):**
TGOPD consistently outperformed Vanilla OPD in all six single-domain settings, covering 4B and 35B student models across mathematics, code, and instruction following (IF). The most significant gains were observed in the code domain (+3.0 average at 4B, +2.9 at 35B), which aligns with the diagnostic finding that teacher confidence is least reliable for assessing correctness in this domain (Figure 2). TGOPD achieved the highest scores among all distillation methods in 7 out of 14 in-domain benchmark columns and surpassed the performance of the domain teacher in 6 of these columns. Notably, at the 35B scale for code, other distillation methods resulted in negative transfer, performing below the untrained base model on LiveCodeBench. In contrast, TGOPD achieved positive transfer (+3.0 over the base model) and even surpassed the teacher (+1.3 LCB, +1.1 OJBench). This outcome supports the method's ability to selectively remove unreliable teacher signals, allowing the GRPO fallback to reinforce beneficial student attempts. The RLSD-style baseline, which uniformly applies a reduced teacher role, exhibited performance below the untrained base model in 8 of 14 columns, suggesting that non-selective reduction of teacher influence can remove valuable supervision.

**Multi-Domain Performance (Table 2):**
When extended to multi-domain OPD (MOPD), where a single student is trained on prompts from math, code, and IF simultaneously, TGOPD also showed improvements. It raised the seven-benchmark average at both scales (4B: +1.14 points; 35B: +0.95 points) and won 6 of 7 columns at 4B and 5 of 7 columns at 35B. Significant gains were observed on AIME 2026 (+3.85 at 4B), IFBench (+2.94 at 4B), and OJBench (+4.31 at 35B), further corroborating the single-domain findings regarding code reliability. Minor regressions were observed to be within the expected variance of early-training checkpoints.

**Compute Efficiency and Resource Utilization (Table 3, Figure 4, Figure 6):**
The analysis of GPU utilization revealed that vanilla OPD teacher nodes exhibited low utilization (5.0% to 9.8% average) and substantial idle periods (57-78% of 15-second samples below 5%). This pattern was consistent across all four configurations (single- and multi-domain, 4B and 35B scales). TGOPD effectively utilized this idle capacity for probe generation, increasing teacher-node GPU utilization significantly to between 57.7% and 82.8% and reducing idle time to 0-2%. This indicates that probes largely filled the existing idle windows without adding substantial load during active teacher periods. Rollout-node utilization remained stable, suggesting no sustained resource contention on student-side compute. Cluster-wide GPU utilization improved by +8.6 to +18.0 percentage points, with the majority of this gain directly attributable to reclaimed teacher capacity. A matched timing comparison for a 35B code run indicated that TGOPD increased mean step time by 5.9%, implying that while most probe work overlaps with idle time, the audit is not entirely without wall-clock overhead.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Gate Threshold (Figure 5):** An ablation study using `K_T = 5` probes in the math domain showed an inverted-U relationship between gate strictness (`τ`) and downstream accuracy. Performance peaked around `τ = 3/5` (60% pass rate), close to the default `τ = 2/3`. Setting the threshold too low admitted unreliable signals, while setting it too high rejected useful teacher signals, sometimes leading to performance below vanilla OPD. This suggests a broad optimum around a majority-vote criterion for reliability.
- **Closed-Gate Policy (Table 4, Table 5):** A comparison between the GRPO fallback (TGOPD's default) and simply masking (disabling the update for prompts that fail the reliability audit) revealed that blocking the unreliable teacher signal accounts for approximately 90% of TGOPD's observed benefits. The GRPO fallback provided a smaller, additional benefit, generally outperforming masking, particularly in single-domain math and code (OJBench). This indicates that while removing harmful signals is the primary driver of improvement, providing a verifier-grounded alternative is often preferable to simply omitting updates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Teacher-Gated On-Policy Distillation (TGOPD) represents a significant advancement in the post-training of large language models, offering improvements in both model performance and computational efficiency.

**Enhanced Model Robustness and Performance:** The core contribution of TGOPD is its ability to judiciously apply teacher supervision based on prompt-level reliability verified through direct outcome evidence. By withholding dense, mode-seeking supervision from unreliable teachers, TGOPD effectively mitigates the risk of negative transfer and amplification of confident errors, which are inherent weaknesses in vanilla OPD. This leads to more robust and accurate student models, as evidenced by consistent performance gains over vanilla OPD across various scales and domains. The ability of TGOPD to enable student models to surpass both the base model and even the teacher in challenging domains like code generation (e.g., on LiveCodeBench at 35B scale) highlights its potential to unlock higher levels of capability during distillation.

**Improved Computational Efficiency:** TGOPD addresses the long-standing issue of underutilized teacher compute capacity in asynchronous OPD setups. By repurposing otherwise-idle teacher GPU cycles for reliability estimation, the method drastically increases teacher-node utilization (e.g., from ~10% to ~79% in a 4B single-domain run). This intelligent resource allocation translates into substantial cluster-wide GPU utilization gains without causing significant resource contention on student nodes or imposing large wall-clock overheads. This makes the overall OPD training process more economically viable and sustainable, especially for large-scale language models.

**Generalizability and Scalability:** The effectiveness of TGOPD across different model scales (4B and 35B) and diverse, specialized domains (mathematics, code, instruction following) demonstrates its broad applicability. Furthermore, its seamless integration into multi-domain OPD frameworks, where it continues to yield performance improvements, underscores its versatility for complex, multi-task learning scenarios. This generalizability suggests that TGOPD can be a valuable component in a wide range of LLM post-training pipelines.

**Paradigm Shift in Teacher-Student Interaction:** TGOPD introduces a more sophisticated and adaptive form of teacher-student interaction. Rather than a blanket application of teacher knowledge, it advocates for a dynamic, context-aware gating mechanism that ensures the student receives high-quality, relevant supervision on a per-prompt basis. This moves beyond relying solely on proxies for uncertainty or compatibility, favoring direct empirical evidence of correctness. Such a paradigm shift could inspire future research into more nuanced and intelligent teacher guidance systems in complex AI learning environments.

**Directions for Future Research:** The paper identifies avenues for further exploration, such as extending outcome-grounded reliability estimation to open-ended tasks where automatic verifiers are less straightforward to implement. Additionally, the current binary routing decision could evolve into more uncertainty-aware or graded gating mechanisms, potentially allowing for a finer control over the blend or strength of supervision signals. These extensions could further enhance the adaptability and performance of distillation techniques.
