# Learning from Algorithm Feedback: One-Shot SAT Solver Guidance with GNNs

> [!summary] Summary
> The RLAF paradigm enables a Graph Neural Network (GNN) to learn one-shot guidance for SAT solvers by optimizing directly on computational cost. This approach accelerates established SAT solvers by up to 69% on certain problem types, generalizing to larger instances and outperforming supervised learning methods.

## Problem

- Hand-crafted branching heuristics in SAT solvers are rigid, require extensive expert knowledge, and often do not adapt well across diverse problem instances.
- Existing machine learning approaches either rely on costly expert-defined labels (supervised learning) or incur prohibitive computational overhead due to per-decision inference (reinforcement learning).
- Suboptimal heuristic design limits the full efficiency potential of complete search algorithms for NP-complete problems like SAT.

## Method

- Propose the Reinforcement Learning from ==Algorithm Feedback== (**RLAF**) paradigm, directly optimizing a GNN policy to minimize SAT solver computational cost.
- Design a "one-shot" GNN that generates a full set of variable weights and polarities for a SAT instance in a single forward pass before solver execution.
- Integrate these learned weights and polarities into existing solver branching heuristics (e.g., VSIDS, look-ahead) as a multiplicative factor and direct polarity choice.

## Results

- RLAF-guided Glucose solver achieved mean runtime reductions of up to **69%** on satisfiable 3SAT(400) problems and consistently accelerated solvers across 3-coloring and cryptographic instances.
- The approach consistently outperformed supervised learning baselines for guiding SAT solvers, demonstrating the efficacy of direct optimization on solver cost.
- Learned policies generalized from relatively "easy" training instances to significantly harder and larger problems, with GNN inference time being negligible compared to solver runtime.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct optimization on solver performance via RLAF can yield more effective guidance than supervised learning based on proxy labels.
> - A single GNN inference at the beginning of the solver run is sufficient to provide effective, global guidance and overcome the computational bottleneck of per-decision deep learning.
> - Policies trained on simpler problem instances generalize effectively to larger and more complex problems, demonstrating the transferability of learned strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) is a foundational NP-complete problem in computer science with broad applications in areas like hardware verification, software testing, artificial intelligence planning, and cryptography. Modern SAT solvers, primarily based on the Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland (DPLL) algorithm and its advanced variant Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL), have achieved remarkable performance on large and complex instances. However, a critical factor determining their efficiency is the effectiveness of **hand-crafted heuristics**, particularly branching heuristics that decide which variable to assign next during the search. These heuristics are often rigid, require extensive expert knowledge to develop, and may not generalize well across diverse problem distributions.

The broader research landscape is witnessing a significant shift towards augmenting or replacing these hand-crafted heuristics with **machine learning (ML)** techniques. This movement aims to create adaptive, data-driven solvers that can learn optimal strategies from experience.

Within this landscape, several approaches have emerged:
- **Supervised Learning for Heuristics:** One common direction involves training ML models to predict properties of variables (e.g., whether a variable is part of an UNSAT core or a backbone) and then using these predictions to guide existing heuristics. Examples include Selsam and Bjørner (2019) who predict UNSAT cores to adjust VSIDS scores, and Wang et al. (2024) who predict backbone literals for polarity guidance. This approach requires ground-truth labels, which can be expensive or impossible to obtain for all relevant properties across all problem types. Moreover, as this paper demonstrates, the effectiveness of such supervised signals can be limited compared to end-to-end learning.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) for Heuristics:** A more ambitious approach is to directly train solvers using RL, where the solver's performance (e.g., runtime or number of decisions) serves as a reward signal, thus eliminating the need for explicit expert-defined labels. Previous RL-based efforts, such as Kurin et al. (2020) and Cameron et al. (2024), train GNNs to make branching decisions. A major drawback of these methods is their **computational overhead**: they require a GNN forward pass for *every single* branching decision during the SAT solver's execution. Given that GNNs are computationally intensive and SAT solvers make millions of decisions, this significantly slows down the solver.

This work introduces **Reinforcement Learning from Algorithm Feedback (RLAF)**, a novel paradigm that distinctively positions itself within this landscape. It addresses the efficiency challenge of prior RL approaches by proposing a **"one-shot guidance"** mechanism. Instead of influencing every single decision dynamically, the GNN performs a *single forward pass* at the beginning of the solver's execution. This pass generates a set of variable-wise weights and polarities that are then injected into the existing branching heuristic, modulating it throughout the entire search without requiring further GNN computations. This "one-shot" nature significantly reduces the computational overhead, making the learned guidance practically viable. By directly optimizing the solver's computational cost through RL, RLAF bypasses the limitations of handcrafted heuristics and the need for predefined expert labels characteristic of supervised learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the inherent limitations of hand-crafted heuristics in complete search algorithms like SAT solvers. While these heuristics have driven significant performance improvements, they are:
1. **Rigid and Difficult to Adapt:** They are often fixed and do not easily adapt to specific instance distributions or evolving problem characteristics without extensive manual tuning and expert knowledge.
2. **Suboptimal:** Their design relies on human intuition and domain expertise, which may not capture the most effective strategies for navigating vast and complex search spaces.

Against this backdrop, the authors aim to leverage the power of machine learning, specifically Reinforcement Learning and Graph Neural Networks, to create adaptable and data-driven SAT solver guidance.

The **key objectives** of this work are:

1. **Develop a Novel Integration Mechanism:** Introduce a generic and flexible method to inject machine-learned variable weights and polarities into the branching heuristics of *existing* SAT solvers, ensuring compatibility without requiring fundamental redesign of the solver's core logic.
2. **Propose a One-Shot GNN Guidance Policy:** Design a Graph Neural Network (GNN) policy that, in a *single forward pass*, assigns weights and polarities to all variables of a given SAT instance. This "one-shot" approach aims to overcome the prohibitive computational cost associated with per-decision GNN inferences in prior RL-based SAT solving methods.
3. **Formulate as an RL Problem with Algorithm Feedback:** Cast the task of inferring optimal weights and polarities for cost reduction as a reinforcement learning problem. Crucially, the reward signal is derived *directly from the solver's computational cost* (e.g., number of decisions), eliminating the need for any expert supervision or predefined labels. This is the essence of their "Reinforcement Learning from Algorithm Feedback (RLAF)" paradigm.
4. **Empirical Validation:** Demonstrate that modern policy-gradient RL techniques (specifically GRPO) can effectively train these GNN-based RLAF policies. This involves showing that the learned policies significantly reduce solver runtimes, generalize effectively to larger and harder problems than those used for training, and consistently outperform supervised learning baselines that rely on predefined variable properties.
5. **Gain Insights into Learned Policies:** Investigate whether the learned variable weight policies capture known structural properties of SAT problems and whether they are solver-agnostic or solver-specific.

In essence, the overarching goal is to achieve a practical and effective way to infuse deep learning intelligence into combinatorial optimization algorithms, specifically SAT solvers, to enable them to adapt and perform better without relying on cumbersome manual heuristic engineering.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work lies in the novel **Reinforcement Learning from Algorithm Feedback (RLAF)** paradigm, which involves training a GNN to provide one-shot guidance to existing SAT solvers.

#### 3.1. Guided Branching Heuristics

The authors propose a generic modification to DPLL-derived SAT solvers' branching heuristics. Most such solvers select a branching variable `x` by maximizing a `SCORE(x)` function (e.g., VSIDS in CDCL solvers or look-ahead scores in March). The proposed modification introduces a learned variable weight `w(x) ∈ R>0` which scales the base solver's score: `x_hat = argmax_x w(x) * SCORE(x)`. Additionally, a learned polarity `p(x) ∈ {0,1}` determines the initial assignment of the chosen variable. This ensures that the learned guidance acts as a modulation rather than a complete replacement of the solver's sophisticated existing heuristics, allowing for injection of prior knowledge without sacrificing the solver's original strengths. Specific modifications for Glucose (VSIDS) and March (look-ahead) solvers are detailed.

#### 3.2. Graph Representation and GNN Architecture

To enable the GNN to process SAT instances, each Boolean formula `ϕ` is first converted into a **"Literal-Clause Graph"** `G(ϕ)`. This graph has two types of nodes: literals (variables and their negations) and clauses. Edges connect literals to clauses they appear in, and also connect each literal to its negation. This standard representation allows the GNN to capture the structural dependencies within the SAT problem.

A trainable **Message-Passing Neural Network (MPNN)** `N_θ` processes this graph. The GNN consists of `L` stacked layers. Each layer performs message passing: clauses aggregate information from their literals, and then literals aggregate information from clauses they belong to, as well as from their opposing literal. Initial node embeddings are derived from the logarithm of node degrees, and message passing functions are implemented using 2-layer MLPs. The final output of `N_θ` for each variable `x` is a 2-dimensional real vector `y(x) = [μ(x), ρ(x)]`.

#### 3.3. Guidance Policy (One-Shot)

The crucial aspect is the "one-shot" generation of guidance. The GNN's output `y(x)` for each variable `x` is mapped to parameters of probability distributions:
- **Variable Weights `w(x)`:** The `μ(x)` output is used as the log-mean for a **Log-Normal distribution** `π_w_θ(x) = LogNormal(μ(x), σ_w)`. The Log-Normal distribution ensures positive weights and was chosen after preliminary experiments.
- **Variable Polarities `p(x)`:** The `ρ(x)` output is passed through a sigmoid function to parameterize a **Bernoulli distribution** `π_p_θ(x) = Bernoulli(Sigmoid(ρ(x)))`.
The complete policy `π_θ(ϕ)` is the joint distribution over all variable-wise weights and polarities, allowing for independent sampling for each variable. At test time, instead of sampling, the deterministic mode of these distributions is used (most probable weight and polarity). This single GNN forward pass produces the complete set of parameters `W = (w, p)` for the entire SAT instance, which are then injected into the solver at the beginning of its run.

#### 3.4. Policy Optimization (RLAF with GRPO)

The objective is to minimize the expected computational cost (number of decisions) of the guided SAT solver. This is framed as a **single-step Markov Decision Process (MDP)**: the SAT instance `ϕ` is the state, choosing a variable parameterization `W` is the action, and the reward `R(ϕ, W)` is the negative of the solver's cost `(-Cost(ϕ, W))`.

The authors employ **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, a variant of PPO, to train the GNN. The training process proceeds iteratively:
1. **Sample Instances and Parameterizations:** In each iteration, a batch of `N` training instances `ϕ_i` is sampled. For each `ϕ_i`, `M` variable parameterizations `W_i,j` are sampled independently from the current policy `π_θ(ϕ_i)`.
2. **Collect Algorithm Feedback:** The SAT solver is run `M` times for each instance `ϕ_i`, each time guided by a different sampled `W_i,j`. The computational cost (number of decisions) is recorded.
3. **Compute Advantages:** For each `(ϕ_i, W_i,j)` pair, a group-relative advantage `A_hat_i,j` is calculated based on the reward `R(ϕ_i, W_i,j)` relative to the mean and standard deviation of rewards collected for the *same instance* `ϕ_i`. This normalization helps in stabilizing training.
4. **Optimize Policy:** The GNN model weights `θ` are updated via stochastic gradient ascent to maximize a clipped policy update objective (similar to PPO), which aims to increase the probability of actions (parameterizations) that yielded high advantages. A KL divergence penalty term is added to stabilize training and prevent excessive policy shifts.

#### 3.5. Training Setup

Due to the "solver-in-the-loop" nature of RLAF, training requires `N * M` SAT solver calls per GRPO iteration. To keep training feasible on single-GPU, multi-core CPU machines (24-48 hours total), the authors constrain the training data to relatively "easy" problems that can be solved quickly (within a fraction of a second). They emphasize that this is not a limitation, as their experiments demonstrate generalization to much harder problems at test time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted using two distinct base SAT solvers, Glucose (CDCL, VSIDS) and March (DPLL, look-ahead), across three diverse SAT problem distributions: random 3SAT, Graph Coloring (3COL), and Cryptographic instances.

#### 4.1. Performance Improvement and Generalization

- **Consistent Speedups:** RLAF-trained policies consistently and substantially accelerated the base solvers. For instance, on 3SAT(400) problems, Glucose's mean runtime was reduced by 69% for satisfiable instances and 41% for unsatisfiable instances. Similar significant improvements were observed for satisfiable 3-coloring problems and cryptographic instances.
- **Generalization to Harder Problems:** A key finding is that policies trained on relatively "easy" problems successfully generalized to significantly larger and harder instances during testing. This demonstrates that the approach is practical, as extensive training on industrially difficult problems (which would be computationally prohibitive for the `solver-in-the-loop` training) is not required for effective guidance.
- **Solver-Specific Performance:** The magnitude of improvement varied. While Glucose saw substantial gains across the board, March's improvement was smaller on unsatisfiable 3SAT instances. In some cases for March+RLAF on unsatisfiable 3SAT, even with fewer decisions, the runtime was slightly worse due to the overhead of the GNN forward pass, suggesting the base March solver is already highly optimized for these specific random instances. However, March also saw substantial acceleration on more structured problems like 3COL and CRYPTO.
- **Negligible GNN Overhead:** The wall-clock time for the GNN forward pass (0.02 to 0.1 seconds) was negligible compared to the solver runtimes on non-trivial instances, validating the efficiency of the "one-shot" guidance approach.

#### 4.2. Comparison to Supervised Approaches

- **Outperformance of RLAF:** RLAF-trained policies consistently outperformed supervised learning approaches that predict handcrafted variable properties like UNSAT core membership (Selsam & Bjørner, 2019) or backbone membership (Wang et al., 2024). This was true for both Glucose and March base solvers across most problem distributions.
- **Limitations of Supervised Signals:** The supervised baselines sometimes struggled (e.g., backbone-guided heuristic performing worse on unsatisfiable 3SAT problems) or were not applicable (e.g., empty backbones for 3COL, trivial UNSAT cores for 3SAT), highlighting the limitations of relying on pre-defined, possibly incomplete, expert-derived signals for training. RLAF's direct optimization on solver cost overcomes these issues.

#### 4.3. Exploring Learned Variable Weights

- **Correlation Across Solvers:** The variable weights learned by policies trained with different base solvers (Glucose vs. March) showed a strong positive correlation (Pearson correlation coefficient between 0.73 and 0.85). This suggests that RLAF learns inherent structural properties of SAT instances that are beneficial across different algorithmic strategies, rather than being specific to one solver's internal mechanics.
- **Correlation with Handcrafted Heuristics:**
 - For 3COL and CRYPTO instances, variables assigned high weights by the RLAF policies predominantly belonged to the UNSAT core, indicating that RLAF "self-discovered" a weighting strategy akin to this known, effective heuristic.
 - For 3SAT instances, no clear correlation was observed between learned weights and backbone membership, implying that RLAF found different, yet effective, guidance strategies for this problem type.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact:

#### 5.1. Novel Training Paradigm (RLAF) and One-Shot Guidance

The introduction of Reinforcement Learning from Algorithm Feedback (RLAF) as a paradigm is a major novelty. By directly optimizing against the algorithm's computational cost as the sole reward signal, RLAF bypasses the limitations of supervised learning which requires costly and often incomplete human-defined labels. The "one-shot" GNN guidance, providing all variable weights and polarities in a single forward pass, is a critical technical innovation. It effectively resolves the prohibitive computational overhead that plagued previous RL-based approaches that required GNN inference at every branching decision. This makes learned guidance practical for high-performance SAT solvers.

#### 5.2. Enhanced SAT Solver Performance and Generalization

The empirical results demonstrate RLAF's ability to significantly accelerate established SAT solvers (Glucose and March) across diverse and challenging problem distributions (random, structured, cryptographic). The consistent outperformance of RLAF-trained policies over expert-supervised methods highlights the power of end-to-end learning from raw algorithm feedback. Furthermore, the demonstrated generalization to larger and harder instances after training on relatively easier ones is crucial for practical applicability, as it means the models learn transferable knowledge without requiring unfeasible training on industrial-scale problems.

#### 5.3. Data-Driven Heuristic Design

This work offers a promising path towards truly data-driven heuristic design in combinatorial optimization. It moves beyond manually engineering heuristics, which is an arduous and expert-intensive process, towards automatically learning effective strategies directly from computational experience. The discovery that RLAF policies can either align with (e.g., UNSAT core) or diverge from (e.g., backbone for 3SAT) handcrafted heuristics while still outperforming them underscores the potential for ML to find novel, non-intuitive, and highly effective algorithmic strategies.

#### 5.4. Broader Applicability and Future Research Directions

The proposed RLAF methodology is not inherently limited to SAT solvers. The authors explicitly point out its potential applicability to other combinatorial optimization problems like Mixed-Integer Programming (MIPs) and Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs), where branching or selection heuristics are critical components. Any algorithm that relies on selecting an element (e.g., a variable, an edge, a node) based on a score function could potentially benefit from this "external multiplicative weights" approach, where a neural network learns to generate these weights to guide the heuristic. This opens up a vast avenue for future research across various algorithmic domains.

#### 5.5. Acknowledged Limitations and Future Work

The authors candidly discuss current limitations, particularly GNN scalability for very large, industrial-scale instances and the boundedness of standard message-passing GNNs by color refinement (limiting their expressive power for highly symmetric patterns). This sets the stage for future research into more compact graph encodings, advanced GNN architectures, and distributed computing resources to handle even larger training datasets and more complex problems.

In conclusion, "Learning from Algorithm Feedback: One-Shot SAT Solver Guidance with GNNs" presents a significant advancement in the field of AI for combinatorial optimization. By introducing the RLAF paradigm and efficient one-shot guidance, it paves the way for the practical integration of deep reinforcement learning into complex algorithms, promising a future where algorithmic heuristics are dynamically learned and continuously improved through direct interaction with the computational environment.
