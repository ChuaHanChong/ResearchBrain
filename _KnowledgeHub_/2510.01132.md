# A Practitioner's Guide to Multi-turn Agentic Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UCSD and NVIDIA present a systematic guide for training large language models as agents in multi-turn interactive environments. The work outlines a practical recipe leveraging curriculum learning, stabilized policy optimization, and verified dense rewards, demonstrating improved skill acquisition and generalization across diverse tasks like text adventures and software engineering.

## Problem

- The field of multi-turn Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLM agents lacks systematic understanding and consistent definitions, leading to fragmented approaches.
- Existing RL methods are primarily optimized for single-turn scenarios, struggling with sparse terminal rewards and imprecise turn-level credit assignment in multi-turn interactions.
- A lack of comprehensive analysis on effective design choices hinders practitioners from developing robust and generalizable LLM agents for complex interactive environments.

## Method

- Frames multi-turn agentic tasks as a ==Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)== with a key innovation of ==token-level credit assignment== for rewards.
- Systematically deconstructs the complex problem into ==three core, interdependent pillars: Environment, Reward, and Policy==, for structured analysis.
- Conducts extensive empirical investigations across diverse benchmarks (TextWorld, ALFWorld, SWE-Gym) to derive a practical, empirically-backed ==training recipe== for LLM agents.

## Results

- The proposed recipe, emphasizing Curriculum + Stabilized Policy + Verified Dense Reward, substantially enhances LLM agent performance and generalization across various task complexities and types.
- PPO demonstrated superior sample efficiency and robustness compared to other RL algorithms (RLOO, Reinforce++, GRPO) in TextWorld, with GRPO proving effective for SWE-Gym.
- Dense, turn-level rewards significantly accelerated training, and fine-grained verifier rewards boosted SWE-Gym success rates from **4.2%** (sparse binary) to **22%**, highlighting the necessity of execution-based feedback.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multi-turn RL for LLM agents requires careful co-design across environment setup, policy initialization, and reward engineering, rather than a simple extension of single-turn methods.
> - A strong Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) prior dramatically accelerates RL efficiency, with an optimal SFT-to-RL ratio crucial for in-domain success and generalization.
> - Dense, turn-level rewards and fine-grained, execution-based verified rewards are essential for effective learning in complex tasks, significantly outperforming sparse or model-based proxy rewards.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has seen explosive growth, with a recent focus shifting from static, single-turn query-response systems to dynamic, multi-turn agentic behaviors. This transition is crucial for enabling LLMs to act as autonomous agents that can navigate complex environments, plan across extended horizons, and perform sequential decision-making. However, the current landscape of multi-turn Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLM agents is characterized by fragmentation, inconsistent definitions, and a lack of systematic analysis of effective design choices.

Existing RL methods for LLMs, such as PPO, RLOO, GRPO, and DAPO, have primarily been optimized for single-turn scenarios, where rewards directly follow individual actions. Adapting these methods to multi-turn agentic scenarios presents significant challenges because outcomes are often revealed only after a sequence of interactions, breaking the direct action-reward coupling that single-turn methods rely on. The paper highlights several shortcomings in existing "multi-turn" approaches:
- Some refer to tool-augmented single queries as multi-turn, which are not truly interactive or sequential in the RL sense.
- Many rely on model-based assumptions, which may not hold in open-ended or partially observable environments.
- Approaches working on true interactive environments often suffer from sparse terminal rewards or imprecise turn-level credit assignment, leading to unstable or inefficient learning.

This paper directly addresses these gaps by providing a systematic formulation and empirical analysis of multi-turn RL for LLM agents. It attempts to standardize understanding and practice in this rapidly evolving domain. By breaking down the design space into three fundamental and interdependent pillars—environment, reward, and policy—the authors offer a structured approach to analyzing what truly works.

The research's relevance is underscored by its use of popular and challenging benchmarks:
- **TextWorld** and **ALFWorld** represent situated embodied reasoning, where agents must understand and interact with textual descriptions of physical environments.
- **SWE-Gym** focuses on real-world software engineering tasks, pushing LLM agents towards practical problem-solving in complex, code-based domains.
These benchmarks are critical for evaluating the generalizability and robustness of agentic LLMs, ensuring the proposed "recipe" is applicable across diverse and demanding applications. The work contributes significantly by moving beyond ad-hoc solutions to a more principled understanding of multi-turn agentic learning, which is essential for developing robust and general-purpose AI agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observed fragmentation and lack of systematic understanding in the nascent field of multi-turn Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Model (LLM) agents. Despite the rapid advancements in LLM capabilities and their application in agentic settings, there is no standardized framework or comprehensive analysis to guide practitioners on effective design choices. This has led to inconsistent results across different studies and a general confusion about what constitutes "true" multi-turn learning versus adaptations of single-turn methods.

The overarching objective of this paper is to **facilitate research efforts on the open research question: What factors are practically important in making multi-turn RL for LLM agent learning work.** To achieve this, the authors embark on a systematic investigation, aiming to:

1. **Deconstruct the Design Space:** Break down the complex problem of multi-turn RL for LLM agents into three core, interdependent pillars: Environment, Reward, and Policy. This decomposition provides a structured approach for analysis, allowing researchers to isolate and study the impact of various design choices within each pillar.
2. **Empirically Derive a Practical Recipe:** Through extensive experimentation across diverse situated textual domains (TextWorld, ALFWorld, SWE-Gym), identify and consolidate effective strategies and guidelines that constitute a robust "recipe" for training LLM agents.
3. **Analyze Environment Impact:** Investigate how factors like task complexity (state and action space sizes, optimal solution length) and task diversity influence agent performance and generalization capabilities.
4. **Evaluate Reward Signals:** Understand the effects of reward sparsity versus density, and the comparative efficacy of verified execution-based rewards versus model-based, synthesized rewards in complex tasks.
5. **Examine Policy Design:** Explore the crucial interplay between Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) as a prior policy and subsequent online RL training. Additionally, compare the stability and sample efficiency of various RL algorithms (biased vs. unbiased policy gradients) in multi-turn settings.
6. **Provide Reproducible Insights:** Commit to releasing an open-source framework, training scripts, and model weights to ensure the reproducibility of their findings and enable future research to build upon their contributions.

Ultimately, the goal is to move beyond disparate, heuristic-driven approaches and establish a principled, empirically-backed understanding that can guide the development of more capable and generalizable LLM agents in interactive environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors frame multi-turn agentic tasks as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), defined by (S, A, T, R, Ω, O, γ), where agents interact with environments through natural language observations and actions. A key methodological innovation is the **token-level credit assignment** for rewards: scalar rewards ($r_t$) are assigned only to the `<eos>` token marking the end of an action command, with all other action tokens receiving zero reward. State tokens are masked out from the loss calculation, ensuring only action generation is optimized.

The experimental methodology systematically investigates the three pillars: Environment, Policy, and Reward.

**Environments and Task Setup:**
- **TextWorld:** A text-adventure game generator. Complexity is controlled along three dimensions: world size (w), number of objects (o), and quest length (q). E.g., "w2-o3-q4" (2 rooms, 3 objects, 4-step quest). Tasks are procedurally generated with unique seeds.
- **ALFWorld:** An embodied household environment, used in its text-only variant across six task categories. Training on "train" split, evaluation on "valid unseen."
- **SWE-Gym:** Real-world software engineering tasks (bug fixes, feature implementation). Five task types (getmoto, pydantic, mypy, pandas, iterative dvc) are used, with tasks categorized by solution complexity (lines of code) and verification complexity (test suite size).

Agents must generate executable natural language commands from observations without action hints, mimicking real-world interaction challenges. The framework is built upon `veRL` for efficient RL training.

**Base Models and Algorithms:**
- **Base LLMs:** Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and Qwen3-8B.
- **RL Algorithms:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Generalized Policy Gradients (GRPO), Reinforce++, and Reinforcement Learning with Optimal Objectives (RLOO). PPO's clipped surrogate objective is explicitly defined with token-level advantage estimation using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE).

**Training and Evaluation:**
- **Metrics:** Task success rate (percentage of objectives achieved within exploration budget) and test suite passing ratios (for SWE-Gym).
- **Hyperparameter Tuning:** A systematic sweep on TextWorld (Qwen-1.5B, w2-o3-q4, PPO) identified optimal settings (e.g., KL coefficient 0.01, temperature 0.7, specific learning rates, gamma 1.0) which were then applied consistently.
- **Exploration Budget:** Varied maximum steps (TextWorld) and allowed tool calls/context window (SWE-Gym) to understand impact.

**Experimental Conditions for each Pillar:**

1. **Environment Analysis (§5):**
 - **Complexity Scaling:** Compared base configuration (w2-o3-q4) with variations increasing spatial (w8-o3-q4), object (w2-o12-q4), both (w8-o12-q4), and proportional scaling (w4-o6-q8) in TextWorld. SWE-Gym tasks were categorized by solution and verification complexity.
 - **Generalization:** Trained on simpler TextWorld/SWE-Gym environments and evaluated on more complex ones.
 - **Task Diversity:** Compared single-type task training with mixed-type training in ALFWorld (e.g., pick & place vs. all 6 types) and SWE-Gym (getmoto vs. all 5 types), ensuring constant total data size.

2. **Policy Analysis (§6):**
 - **Prior Model Policy:** Used TextWorld gold solutions for Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT). Investigated performance with varying SFT data (0 to 100 demonstrations) followed by RL, and determined an optimal SFT:RL ratio under a fixed budget (SFT costing 10x RL episode). Explored cross-domain SFT transfer.
 - **RL Algorithm Comparison:** Benchmarked PPO, RLOO, Reinforce++, and GRPO on TextWorld (w2-o3-q4, w4-o6-q8) across different model sizes to isolate algorithmic contributions.

3. **Reward Analysis (§7):**
 - **Reward Density:** Compared sparse (task completion only) vs. dense (intermediate steps) rewards in TextWorld using built-in functions.
 - **Reward Source/Granularity:** For SWE-Gym, compared sparse binary verified rewards (+1 for full test suite pass) with dense ratio-based verified rewards (percentage of unit tests passed). Also, evaluated model-based rewards from CodeRM-8B and GPT-4.1 judges as surrogates for verifiers.

The entire methodology emphasizes empirical rigor, systematic ablation studies, and a focus on practical insights that can inform the design of future LLM agent systems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several critical insights across the three pillars of environment, policy, and reward, culminating in a practical recipe for multi-turn agentic RL:

**Environment:**
- **Complexity Scaling:** Multi-turn RL performance, while generally improved, scales negatively with environment complexity. The *improvements* from RL diminish as complexity increases (e.g., PPO improvement drops from 88% on base TextWorld to 51% on most complex). Object complexity was found to be more challenging than spatial complexity in TextWorld. Larger models (7B) demonstrated superior handling of increased state spaces in complex environments.
- **Exploration Horizon:** For simple TextWorld tasks, performance saturated beyond 2x the optimal solution length (around 8 exploration steps). However, complex coding tasks (SWE-Gym) benefited significantly from extended horizons (context windows and tool calls), indicating sustained exploration is crucial for iterative debugging and refinement.
- **Generalization from Simple to Complex:** Agents trained on simpler environments (e.g., w2-o3-q4 in TextWorld or "Easy" tasks in SWE-Gym) showed substantial generalization to more complex ones, acquiring reusable skills like spatial exploration, object manipulation, error correction, and state tracking.
- **Generalization Across Task Types:** Multi-turn RL successfully applied to ALFWorld and SWE-Gym. Agents trained on single task types showed decent generalization to unseen task types. Surprisingly, multi-task training (e.g., mixture of 4 or 6 types in ALFWorld) enhanced performance even on single task types and provided better overall generalization.

**Policy:**
- **Impact of Prior Policy (SFT):** A strong SFT prior dramatically accelerated RL efficiency. A minimal amount of SFT (60 demonstrations) followed by 400 RL episodes achieved performance nearly comparable to 5000 pure RL episodes.
- **Optimal SFT:RL Ratio:** Under a fixed budget, a hybrid approach of SFT followed by RL (e.g., 60 SFT demos + 400 RL episodes) provided the best balance between in-domain success and generalization to harder tasks, outperforming pure SFT or pure RL. Cross-domain SFT priors were found to actively *harm* multi-turn RL training, leading to policy collapse.
- **RL Algorithm Comparison:** Both PPO and RLOO showed improvements over base models, confirming that performance gains stem from the multi-turn formulation itself, not solely PPO-specific heuristics. However, PPO was found to be significantly more sample-efficient and robust than RLOO, Reinforce++, and GRPO in TextWorld, especially on harder tasks. GRPO, while underperforming PPO on TextWorld, proved computationally advantageous and effective for SWE-Gym tasks with sparse rewards.

**Reward:**
- **Reward Density:** Dense turn-level rewards significantly accelerated multi-turn RL training. PPO benefited substantially from more frequent feedback, while RLOO, with its unbiased gradient estimates, showed less sensitivity to reward density. Optimal reward density requires calibration based on the specific RL algorithm.
- **Verified vs. Model-Based Rewards:** For complex tasks like SWE-Gym, fine-grained *verifier* rewards (e.g., unit test pass rates) were essential, boosting success rates from 4.2% (sparse binary reward) to 22%. Model-based judges (CodeRM-8B, GPT-4.1), while offering some improvement over base models, significantly underperformed verified rewards (7.2% and 9.3% respectively vs. 22%). This highlights the necessity of execution-based unit tests over model-based proxies for robust learning in complex domains.

In essence, the findings underscore that multi-turn RL for LLMs is not a straightforward extension of single-turn optimization but requires a carefully co-designed system across environment setup, policy initialization, and reward engineering.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a significant contribution to the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM) agents by addressing a critical need for standardization and systematic understanding in multi-turn reinforcement learning (RL). Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Practical Guidance for Practitioners:** The most direct impact is the provision of a concrete, empirically-derived "recipe" for multi-turn agentic RL: **Curriculum + Stabilized Policy + Verified Dense Reward**. This recipe offers invaluable practical guidelines for researchers and developers struggling with the fragmented and often heuristic-driven approaches currently prevalent. By outlining specific recommendations across environment design (start simple, build curriculum), policy choices (prioritize stable, biased algorithms like PPO/GRPO, leverage SFT priors optimally), and reward engineering (granular, verified execution feedback over model-based proxies), the paper fills a crucial gap in practical implementation knowledge.

2. **Advancing Agentic AI Capabilities:** By demonstrating that multi-turn RL enables LLMs to acquire transferable skills across varying complexities and task types (TextWorld, ALFWorld, SWE-Gym), the research pushes the boundaries of autonomous agent capabilities. This includes skills like state tracking, spatial exploration, object manipulation, and iterative debugging, which are fundamental for agents operating in real-world, interactive environments. The findings lay a foundation for developing more robust, adaptive, and general-purpose LLM agents.

3. **Optimizing Resource Allocation:** The detailed analysis of SFT-to-RL ratios under fixed budgets provides practical insights into efficient resource allocation. Demonstrating that a small amount of carefully curated SFT data can dramatically accelerate RL convergence, while cross-domain SFT can be harmful, helps practitioners make informed decisions about data collection and training strategies, potentially saving significant computational and human annotation costs.

4. **Standardization and Reproducibility:** The paper's systematic breakdown of the problem into environment, policy, and reward pillars, coupled with a strong commitment to open-sourcing its framework (built on veRL), training scripts, model weights, and detailed hyperparameters, will foster greater standardization and reproducibility in the field. This commitment allows other researchers to validate findings, build upon the presented recipe, and accelerate collective progress, addressing the current fragmentation in methodologies.

5. **Refining RL for LLMs:** The comparative analysis of different RL algorithms (PPO, RLOO, GRPO, Reinforce++) provides valuable insights into their suitability for multi-turn LLM agent training. By distinguishing between gains from the multi-turn formulation itself and specific algorithmic heuristics, the paper contributes to a more nuanced understanding of RL algorithm performance in this domain. The emphasis on stable, biased algorithms (PPO, GRPO) for practical scenarios, despite the theoretical appeal of unbiased estimators like RLOO, is a key takeaway for implementation.

6. **Impact on Real-World Applications:** The evaluation on SWE-Gym, a benchmark for software engineering tasks, directly highlights the potential for LLM agents to tackle complex, long-horizon real-world problems. The finding that fine-grained, execution-based rewards are superior to model-based proxies for such tasks has immediate implications for building reliable coding assistants and automated bug-fixing systems.

In conclusion, this paper provides not just theoretical insights but actionable recommendations that will empower practitioners to design, train, and deploy more effective and generalized LLM agents, thereby accelerating the transition of LLMs from static tools to dynamic, autonomous problem-solvers in a variety of interactive domains.
