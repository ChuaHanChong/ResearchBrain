# Embedding Morphology into Transformers for Cross-Robot Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories developed an embodiment-aware transformer policy that integrates robot morphology into Vision-Language-Action models using kinematic tokens, a Mix-Mask topology-aware attention bias, and joint-attribute conditioning. This approach consistently enhanced task success rates by up to 2.3-fold on single robots and demonstrated superior multi-robot generalization compared to baseline methods.

## Problem

- Existing transformer-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies are largely embodiment-agnostic, leading to reduced robustness and generalization across diverse robot hardware.
- Prior methods for embedding robot morphology into policies lacked a direct joint-level interface within VLA models, making it difficult to apply kinematic biases effectively.
- There was a challenge in balancing local kinematic message passing with global task coordination in topology-aware attention, and in incorporating rich per-joint semantic attributes beyond basic connectivity.

## Method

- Introduced ==Kinematic Tokens (KT)== to create a joint-wise action representation, factorizing action sequences across individual joints into temporal chunks and using auxiliary tokens to increase capacity.
- Developed a ==Mix-Mask topology-aware attention bias== that alternates between layers with strict ==1-hop kinematic masking== and layers with full attention, balancing local and global information flow.
- Incorporated per-joint semantic attributes (e.g., joint type, axis, motion limits) using ==Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)== to enrich kinematic token embeddings with functional context.

## Results

- On single-embodiment tasks with DROID, the full morphology-embedding approach achieved an average success rate of **47.4%**, substantially surpassing the **19.7%** of the vanilla π0.5 baseline.
- For multi-embodiment learning across DROID and SO101, the proposed policy consistently demonstrated superior Macro Success Rates compared to the baseline, indicating improved generalization and robustness.
- Ablations showed that a single temporal chunk (G=1) for kinematic tokens was optimal, and Auxiliary Kinematic Tokens consistently improved performance across configurations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly factorizing actions into joint-wise kinematic tokens provides a substantial performance boost, suggesting the importance of a structured action representation for VLA models.
> - A "Mix-Mask" strategy, which alternates layers with strict local kinematic attention and layers with global attention, effectively balances the trade-off between local kinematic message passing and overall task coordination.
> - Incorporating rich per-joint semantic attributes via FiLM further enhances policy performance, especially when combined with topology-aware attention, by providing crucial functional context for action generation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in robot learning: developing policies that can perform effectively across multiple distinct robot embodiments, a concept termed cross-robot policy learning. Recent advancements in robot learning have been significantly driven by large transformer-based policies, particularly vision-language-action (VLA) models such as π0.5, RT-1, and PaLM-E. These models demonstrate increasingly generalist capabilities by scaling to extensive and diverse datasets. However, a common limitation is their embodiment-agnostic nature, requiring them to infer kinematic structure and cross-joint coordination implicitly from observations. This implicit learning can reduce robustness when faced with variations in hardware, design upgrades, or deployment on different robot platforms, often necessitating fine-tuning or adaptation modules for each new robot.

Prior research has attempted to incorporate robot morphology explicitly, typically by representing it as a kinematic graph where nodes are actuated joints and edges denote physical connectivity. Two main architectural approaches have emerged:
1. **Graph Neural Network (GNN) policies**: These encode morphology by using message passing on a kinematic graph, aiming for body-wide coordination (e.g., NerveNet, SMP). A design challenge in GNNs is balancing local interactions with global coordination.
2. **Transformer policies with topology-aware attention**: These leverage transformers' self-attention mechanism for both local and global information exchange, injecting kinematic priors by modulating attention based on the kinematic graph. This modulation can be achieved through Hard-Mask attention, which restricts attention to 1-hop kinematic neighbors, or Soft-Mask attention, which adds a learnable, topology-conditioned bias to attention logits without strictly blocking any pairs. While Soft-Mask offers flexibility, Hard-Mask designs have often been reported as more stable and performant in prior work.

Despite these advancements, existing morphology-embedding methods face three identified challenges:
1. **Lack of a kinematic token interface**: Current VLA models, such as π0.5, compress joint-space structure into a compact set of action tokens, making it difficult to directly apply existing joint-level kinematic biasing schemes.
2. **Local–global trade-off in topology-aware attention**: Strongly enforcing local interactions (e.g., via hard masking) can improve kinematic message passing but may limit long-range coordination necessary for complex tasks.
3. **Missing joint semantics**: Existing methods primarily focus on connectivity and do not capture per-joint semantic attributes (e.g., actuation type, axis, limits), which can be crucial for disambiguating functional roles and enabling more structured action generation.

This research aims to address these specific limitations within the context of transformer-based VLA policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to improve cross-robot policy learning by explicitly embedding robot morphology into transformer-based VLA policies, thereby enhancing robustness within and across diverse embodiments. The motivation stems from the identified limitations of existing embodiment-agnostic VLA models and prior morphology-embedding techniques.

Specifically, the authors aim to achieve the following:
- **Provide a joint-wise action representation**: Current VLA models typically use compact action tokens that bundle all joint actions, hindering the application of joint-level kinematic biases. The objective is to introduce a token interface that factorizes actions across individual joints while managing temporal information effectively.
- **Balance local and global information exchange in topology-aware attention**: Previous topology-aware attention methods struggle with the trade-off between promoting local kinematic message passing and maintaining global coordination. The goal is to develop an attention biasing mechanism that can balance these aspects.
- **Incorporate per-joint semantic information**: Beyond mere connectivity, individual joints possess unique functional attributes. The objective is to integrate these per-joint semantics into the policy architecture to provide richer context for action generation.

By addressing these points, the research seeks to move beyond implicit kinematic learning in VLA models towards a more structured and embodiment-aware approach, ultimately leading to policies that generalize more effectively across heterogeneous robotic platforms and exhibit improved performance, even within a single embodiment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces an embodiment-aware transformer policy that integrates robot morphology into a VLA action policy, specifically building upon the π0.5 backbone. This integration is achieved through three main mechanisms: kinematic tokens, topology-aware attention bias, and joint-attribute conditioning.

#### 3.1. Kinematic Tokens (KT)

To address the lack of a joint-wise action representation, kinematic tokens are introduced. These tokens factorize the action sequence across joints while compressing temporal information.
- **Factorization and Temporal Chunking**: For a robot with `J` joints and an action horizon `H`, the scalar action for joint `j` at time `t` is `a_{t,j}`. Instead of coupling all joints into a single embedding per horizon (as in the original π0.5), the horizon `H` is split into `G` non-overlapping temporal chunks of size `g = H/G`. For each joint `j` and chunk `k`, a kinematic token `b_{j,T_k}` is formed by concatenating the `g` actions for that joint within that chunk. These vectors are then projected into `d`-dimensional embeddings `z_{j,T_k}` using a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). These kinematic tokens are appended to the token sequence, serving as additional context for the VLA action expert. In experiments, a single chunk (`G=1`) was found to perform best.
- **Auxiliary Kinematic Tokens (AKT)**: To increase the token capacity per joint, `M` auxiliary embeddings `z^{(m)}_{j,T_k}` are generated for each kinematic token `b_{j,T_k}` using independent lightweight encoders. These auxiliary tokens are also appended to the kinematic token sequence, providing a richer set of per-joint representations for the transformer to attend to.

#### 3.2. Topology-Aware Attention

The method encodes the robot's kinematic topology, represented as a graph `G = (V, E)` where `V` are joints and `E` are physical connections, as an inductive bias in self-attention. This modulation is applied only within the joint-to-joint attention block of the VLA token sequence.
The attention logits `logits^{(ℓ)}_{i,j}` at layer `ℓ` between query joint `i` and key joint `j` are modulated by an additive topology-dependent term `B^{(ℓ)}_{i,j}`:
`α^{(ℓ)}_{i,j} = softmax_j (logits^{(ℓ)}_{i,j} + B^{(ℓ)}_{i,j})`
Three variants of `B^{(ℓ)}_{i,j}` are considered:
- **Hard-Mask Family**: These variants use an adjacency-based hard bias that prevents attention to non-neighbor joints.
 - **Full-Mask**: Applies hard masking at every layer, setting `B^{(ℓ)}_{i,j} = -∞` if joints `i` and `j` are not 1-hop neighbors (or `i ≠ j`), and `0` otherwise. This strictly enforces local 1-hop interactions.
 - **Mix-Mask**: Alternates masked and unmasked layers. Even-numbered layers apply hard masking, while odd-numbered layers use full attention (`B^{(ℓ)}_{i,j} = 0`). This aims to balance local message passing with periodic global coordination.
- **Soft-Mask Family**: This variant uses a shortest-path distance (SPD)-based soft bias.
 - **SPD-based bias**: The bias term `B^{(ℓ)}_{i,j}` is parameterized by a learnable bias table indexed by the shortest-path distance `d(i, j)` between joints `i` and `j`: `B^{(ℓ)}_{i,j} = θ^{(ℓ)}_{d(i,j)}`. This biases attention towards kinematically closer joints without strictly blocking any connections. Various initialization strategies for `θ^{(ℓ)}_d` (Zero, Hard, Mix, Linear) were explored to improve stability.

#### 3.3. Joint-Attribute Conditioning

To incorporate semantics beyond connectivity, kinematic-token embeddings are conditioned on per-joint descriptors.
- **Per-joint Descriptors**: For each joint `j`, a descriptor `s_j` is defined, including features like joint type (prismatic/revolute), axis direction, hard motion limits, damping, friction properties, and stiffness.
- **Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)**: FiLM is used to map the descriptor `s_j` to feature-wise scale (`γ_j`) and shift (`β_j`) parameters. These parameters then modulate the kinematic-token embedding `z_j` through an affine transformation: `˜z_j = (1 + γ_j) ⊙ z_j + β_j`, where `⊙` denotes element-wise multiplication. This provides the topology-based message passing with joint-specific semantic context.

#### 3.4. Training Protocol

All model variants are initialized from a pre-trained π0.5 checkpoint (pi05-base). New morphology modules are zero-initialized to start near an identity mapping for stable optimization. Depending on the benchmark, either action policy fine-tuning (AP-FT) or full fine-tuning (Full-FT) is employed. AP-FT updates only the diffusion action expert, while Full-FT updates the entire model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation assesses the proposed morphology-aware transformer policies in both single-embodiment and multi-embodiment settings, using task success rate (SR) and its 95% confidence interval.

#### 4.1. Single-Embodiment Results on DROID (Franka Panda)

- **Kinematic Tokens (KT)**: Compared to the vanilla π0.5 baseline (Avg SR 19.7%), adding kinematic tokens (with a single chunk G=1) significantly improved the average success rate to 36.0%. This indicates that a joint-centric action representation is beneficial even without explicit topology or semantics.
- **Topology-aware Attention**: Starting from the kinematic token model (Avg SR 36.0%):
 - Mix-Mask increased Avg SR to 36.9%, suggesting that alternating local (masked) and global (unmasked) attention layers is effective.
 - Full-Mask, which enforces 1-hop locality at every layer, reduced Avg SR to 30.1%, indicating it can be overly restrictive.
 - Soft-Mask achieved an Avg SR of 26.1%, performing lower than both masked variants.
- **Joint-Attribute Conditioning (FiLM)**:
 - Adding FiLM without topology encoding improved Avg SR from 36.0% (KT alone) to 37.7%.
 - When combined with Mix-Mask, FiLM further improved Avg SR from 36.9% to 47.4%, achieving the highest overall performance.
 - The full morphology encoding (KT + Mix-Mask + FiLM) yielded substantial gains on specific tasks, improving Task 2 success rate by 5-fold (from 15.7% to 77.7%) and Task 3 by 2.3-fold (from 25.0% to 58.7%) compared to the baseline.

#### 4.2. Single-Embodiment Results on Unitree G1 Dex1

- The findings were consistent with DROID. The full morphology encoding (KT + Mix-Mask + FiLM) achieved the best performance (Avg SR 28.0%) compared to the baseline (Avg SR 24.7%).
- Mix-Mask improved Avg SR from 24.3% (KT alone) to 27.3%, and adding FiLM further increased it to 28.0%.

#### 4.3. Multi-Embodiment Results on DROID + SO101

- A single policy was jointly trained on a mixture of Panda and SO101 demonstrations, a setting involving different action space dimensionalities.
- The embodiment-aware policy (KT + Mix-Mask + FiLM) consistently outperformed the π0.5 baseline in Macro SR (average of Panda and SO101 SRs) throughout training. For instance, at 50k training steps, the proposed method achieved a Macro SR of 15.5% compared to π0.5's 5.0%. At 125k steps, it maintained a higher Macro SR of 20.7% versus 17.5% for π0.5.
- Per-embodiment analysis showed the method improved performance on DROID throughout training, while achieving comparable performance on SO101.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

- **Effect of temporal chunk size (G)**: Using a single chunk (G=1) for kinematic tokens yielded the best Avg SR (36.0%). Performance generally degraded as `G` increased (e.g., 33.3% at G=16), indicating that more aggressive temporal compression in kinematic tokens is beneficial.
- **Effect of Auxiliary Kinematic Tokens (AKT)**: AKT consistently improved Avg SR across configurations. Under Mix-Mask, AKT increased Avg SR from 37.0% to 47.3%, indicating that scaling kinematic token capacity can enhance performance.
- **Effect of bias initialization for Soft-Mask**: Various initialization strategies (Zero, Hard, Mix, Linear) for the Soft-Mask bias table were tested. Mix initialization performed best among Soft-Mask variants (Avg SR 28.1%). However, no Soft-Mask configuration, regardless of initialization, surpassed the Hard-Masked variants (e.g., Mix-Mask) in terms of overall performance, aligning with observations in related work about Soft-Mask's optimization sensitivity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a structured approach to integrate robot morphology into transformer-based VLA policies, addressing several limitations of previous methods. The consistent improvements in success rates observed across single- and multi-embodiment evaluations indicate that explicitly embedding morphology enhances policy robustness, both within a single robot's operational context and when generalizing across different robot platforms.

The introduction of kinematic tokens provides a joint-wise action representation that facilitates the application of morphology-aware attention mechanisms, overcoming a limitation of prior VLA token interfaces. The investigation into topology-aware attention bias, particularly the effectiveness of the Mix-Mask variant, offers insights into balancing local kinematic message passing with global coordination, which is crucial for complex manipulation tasks. Furthermore, the inclusion of joint-attribute conditioning demonstrates the value of incorporating semantic information beyond mere connectivity, providing a richer context for action generation.

The ability to train a single policy that performs better across diverse embodiments without extensive robot-specific adaptation or fine-tuning contributes to the development of more generalist robot controllers. This has implications for reducing the data and computational resources required for deploying robots in varied environments and with differing hardware. Future work could build upon these findings by exploring more advanced kinematic-token representations, developing more stable and performant Soft-Mask attention methods, and refining multi-embodiment training strategies, such as curriculum learning, to further improve generalization and maintain performance across a wider range of robots. This research contributes a step towards more adaptable and capable robotic systems.
