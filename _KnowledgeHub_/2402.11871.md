# From Real World to Logic and Back: Learning Generalizable Relational Concepts For Long Horizon Robot Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> The LAMP framework enables robots to autonomously invent symbolic, relational concepts and logic-based world models directly from raw, unannotated kinematic demonstrations. This allows for robust zero-shot generalization to tasks significantly more complex than observed during training, achieving up to 18x generalization factors in diverse real-world and simulated environments.

## Problem

- Robots lack the ability to autonomously acquire abstract, transferable concepts and world models from limited experience for long-horizon planning.
- Current methods rely heavily on hand-engineered symbolic models or extensive, annotated datasets, which restrict scalability and require significant human effort.
- Existing learning approaches often achieve limited generalization (e.g., 1x for imitation learning) or require prior expert knowledge of high-level actions or symbolic predicates.

## Method

- **LAMP** (Learning Abstract Models for Planning) autonomously learns ==PDDL-based world models== by directly integrating abstraction learning into the planning optimization process.
- It identifies '==Relational Critical Regions (RCRs)==' as salient interaction zones in relative object pose spaces using ==Gaussian Mixture Models== on clustered demonstration trajectories.
- **LAMP** invents discrete, ==symbolic relational concepts== from these RCRs and synthesizes generalizable high-level actions by observing transitions between abstract states.

## Results

- LAMP achieved zero-shot generalization factors up to **18x** in simulation (e.g., Cafe domain) and up to **6x** in real-world tasks (e.g., Jenga stacking) from limited training demonstrations.
- It solved **100%** of unseen test tasks, performing comparably to expert-engineered symbolic models (STAMP) and outperforming foundation model-based approaches (Code-as-Policies, **<35%** success).
- The framework demonstrated high sample efficiency, learning effective world models from as few as **40** successful demonstrations, significantly reducing data acquisition costs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-level robot actions can be derived from transitions into and out of salient regions defined by relative object poses, rather than absolute positions.
> - By converting continuous relative pose information into discrete symbolic relations, robots can bridge low-level kinematic experience with high-level logical reasoning.
> - Integrating the learning of abstractions directly with the planning problem leads to the discovery of effective, performance-improving abstract models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is actively seeking methods that enable robots to learn from limited experience and generalize that knowledge to new, more complex tasks in varied environments. A significant challenge in this area is the ability to acquire abstract, transferable concepts and world models autonomously. Current robot learning approaches typically address complementary problems, such as learning short-horizon tasks (e.g., closing a door) or imitating demonstrations in scenarios with minimal variations from training tasks (e.g., tying shoelaces). These methods often rely on extensive, annotated datasets or pre-defined high-level skills.

Specifically, the broader research landscape includes:
- **Hand-engineered symbolic models:** Traditionally, logic-based concepts and world models for robots are crafted by human experts. This approach is effective for known domains but necessitates considerable domain engineering effort, which restricts a robot's applicability to tasks foreseen prior to deployment. The lack of automation in creating these abstractions is a recognized bottleneck.
- **Learning state and action abstractions:** Some prior work has demonstrated the learning of unary predicates and actions, particularly for motion planning tasks. However, these methods have generally not extended to the more complex domain of mobile manipulation, which involves interacting with multiple objects in dynamic environments.
- **Foundation models (Large Language Models/Visual Language Models):** Recent advancements in foundation models have shown promise in composing high-level robot skills, translating natural language instructions into logical formulas, and even assisting in planning. However, these methods typically depend on expert-provided knowledge, such as pre-defined high-level robot actions and their execution procedures. Critically, research indicates that these models may struggle with planning soundness, exhibiting limited accuracy even in relatively simple block-stacking scenarios.
- **Learning logical concepts with prior knowledge:** Other approaches have demonstrated the learnability of logical concepts, but often under the assumption that high-level robot skills or actions are already known. Similarly, more complex concepts can be learned given an initial set of concepts. This suggests a dependency on a human-provided conceptual scaffolding.
- **Imitation learning and behavior cloning:** These methods learn control policies directly from expert demonstrations. While showing progress in dexterous manipulation, they are generally limited to tasks encountered during training, exhibiting a generalization factor of approximately 1x. They also often require a large number of demonstrations (thousands per task), which can be costly to obtain in real-world robotic settings.

This paper positions itself by addressing a fundamental gap: the autonomous invention of *both* logical concepts *and* high-level actions directly from raw, unannotated, unsegmented robot demonstrations, without any *a priori* expert knowledge of either. It aims to bridge the gap between low-level kinematic experience and high-level symbolic reasoning, thereby contributing towards the development of interpretable, scalable, and generalizable robot planning systems that can achieve zero-shot transfer to significantly more complex problems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable robots to autonomously invent symbolic, relational concepts and logic-based world models directly from a limited number of raw, unsegmented, and unannotated demonstrations. From these learned abstractions, the robot is intended to acquire knowledge that supports zero-shot generalization to tasks significantly more complex than those observed during training.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several critical limitations in current robot learning and planning paradigms:

- **Bridging the Human-Robot Generalization Gap:** Humans possess a remarkable ability to infer generalizable concepts from a few simple examples and apply this knowledge to solve problems of vastly greater complexity. For instance, observing a single "pick-and-place" action for a cup can provide sufficient information to conceptualize "clearing a cluttered table." Robots currently lag behind in this capability, primarily due to the absence of methods for autonomously inventing such abstract, generalizable concepts and world models.
- **Intractability of Low-Level Planning:** Planning at the level of primitive robot actions (e.g., joint movements, minor pose adjustments) is computationally intractable for long-horizon tasks, which may involve thousands of such actions. This necessitates the use of abstract representations.
- **Limitations of Hand-Crafted Abstractions:** The conventional approach of using hand-crafted abstract logical concepts and world models, while providing generalizability, suffers from requiring extensive domain engineering by human experts. This limits a robot's autonomy and scalability, as new tasks or environments often require significant re-engineering of the symbolic model. Furthermore, poorly designed human abstractions can lead to incorrect or infeasible solutions if they do not adequately satisfy refinability constraints.
- **Deficiencies of Existing Learning Methods:**
 - **Imitation Learning:** While capable of learning from demonstrations, imitation learning approaches typically perform well only on tasks very similar to those in the training data, resulting in a low generalization factor (often 1x). They also often require large quantities of annotated demonstrations, which are costly and time-consuming to acquire in robotics.
 - **Learning Symbolic Components Individually:** Many existing methods for learning symbolic representations or action models require *a priori* knowledge of either high-level actions or an initial set of symbolic predicates. The challenge of learning both simultaneously, without such expert scaffolding, has remained largely unaddressed.
 - **Foundation Model Reliance:** While powerful, foundation models often rely on expert-provided programming interfaces (APIs), pre-defined goal descriptions, and sample solution code for robot tasks. They also face inherent challenges in ensuring the logical soundness and correctness of generated plans.
- **Need for Interpretable and Scalable Systems:** The goal is to develop robot planning systems that are not only generalizable and scalable to diverse, complex scenarios but also interpretable. Interpretable models facilitate debugging, understanding of robot behavior, and ultimately, user trust and interaction.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to fundamentally advance robot autonomy, enabling them to move from raw sensory experience to sophisticated, generalizable, and interpretable symbolic reasoning for long-horizon planning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, called Learning Abstract Models for Planning (LAMP), aims to autonomously learn abstractions from kinematic demonstrations and represent them as PDDL-based world models. LAMP integrates the abstraction learning directly into the planning optimization problem, allowing the robot to learn abstractions that improve its performance. The methodology consists of three main stages: Learning Proto-Relations, Inventing Semantically Well-Founded Concepts for Logic, and Learning High-Level Actions and Logical World Models.

#### 3.1. Learning Proto-Relations (Relational Critical Regions - RCRs)

The foundational hypothesis is that high-level robot actions correspond to transitions into and out of salient regions within the environment. LAMP formalizes these salient regions as "Relational Critical Regions" (RCRs).
- **Generalization of Critical Regions:** Building upon the concept of critical regions (CRs) in the robot's configuration space, LAMP generalizes this notion to RCRs, which exist in the *relative pose spaces* among object pairs. This addresses the challenge of long-horizon planning involving multiple objects, where salient interactions are often relative rather than absolute.
- **Definition:** An RCR for object $x$ with respect to object $y$ is defined as a region in the set of poses of $x$ relative to $y$ that exhibits a high density of solutions for a given distribution of tasks. Formally, a region $\rho$ is an RCR if its criticality measure, $\mu(\rho)$, exceeds a predefined threshold $\theta$. $\mu(\rho)$ is calculated as the limit of the fraction of observed solution trajectories passing through $\rho$ divided by the measure of $\rho$ under a reference density.
- **Process:**
 1. **Data Conversion:** Kinematic-state trajectories from simple demonstrations are converted into trajectories of relative poses between all relevant object pairs.
 2. **Occupancy Matrix Creation:** These relative pose trajectories are clustered into pairwise occupancy matrices for each object-type pair ($\tau_i, \tau_j$).
 3. **Connected Component Extraction:** The `label` function from OpenCV is applied to extract connected components from these occupancy matrices, identifying regions of high activity.
 4. **Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) Fitting:** For each connected component, LAMP uniformly samples poses and fits a GMM (using `GaussianMixture` from scikit-learn). The parameters ($\mu_\gamma, \Sigma_\gamma$) of the GMM define a relational critical region predictor $\psi_\gamma$. A relative pose $p_{o_j o_i}$ is classified into $\psi_\gamma$ if its likelihood under the GMM exceeds a threshold $\epsilon$.
- **Functionality:** These trained GMMs act as generative predictors, capable of inferring RCRs in novel environments given object configurations, enabling zero-shot inference.

#### 3.2. Inventing Semantically Well-Founded Concepts for Logic (Relation Inventor)

RCRs define salient physical regions but are not sufficient for high-level logical reasoning. The Relation Inventor component transforms these continuous RCRs into a discrete, symbolic relational vocabulary.
- **Binary Relation Creation:** For each RCR predictor $\psi_k \in \Psi_{ij}$ (between object types $\tau_i$ and $\tau_j$), a unique binary relation $R^k_{ij} : O_{\tau_i} \times O_{\tau_j} \rightarrow \{\top, \perp\}$ is created. This relation is true iff the relative pose $p_{o_j o_i}$ falls within the region defined by $\psi_k$.
- **Additional Relations:**
 - **Negation/Absence:** An additional binary relation $R'_{ij} : O_{\tau_i} \times O_{\tau_j} \rightarrow \{\top, \perp\}$ is defined, which is true if none of the RCRs $R^k_{ij}$ are true for the object pair. This represents the "absence" of specific relations.
 - **Free Volume (Clearance):** For each RCR predictor $\psi_k$, a Boolean relation $R^{free}_{k_{ij}} : O_{\tau_i} \rightarrow \{\top, \perp\}$ is defined. This relation is true if the "free volume" within the predicted RCR $\psi_k$ is greater than the volume of a specific object type, intuitively modeling whether the region is clear for placement or manipulation.
- **Vocabulary Generation:** Given a new task, LAMP uses these learned RCR predictors to generate the specific relational vocabulary $V_T$ for the objects in that task.

#### 3.3. Learning High-Level Actions and Logical World Models (Action Inventor)

The final stage focuses on synthesizing generalizable, transferable high-level actions and their corresponding logical world models.
- **Hypothesis:** High-level actions are directly derived from observed transitions between RCRs in the training demonstrations.
- **Process:**
 1. **Abstract State Conversion:** Each raw kinematic state trajectory $\langle x_0, \dots, x_n \rangle$ from demonstrations is converted into a sequence of abstract states $\langle s'_0, \dots, s'_n \rangle$ using the invented relational vocabulary $V$.
 2. **Lifted State Representation:** These abstract states are then "lifted" to $\langle s_0, \dots, s_n \rangle$ by replacing concrete object identities with placeholder variables corresponding to their object types.
 3. **Transition Identification and Clustering:** For each lifted transition $C_{ij} = s_i \rightarrow s_j$, the sets of added relations ($C^+_{ij} = s_j \setminus s_i$) and deleted relations ($C^-_{ij} = s_i \setminus s_j$) are computed. Transitions that induce the same $\langle C^+, C^- \rangle$ sets are clustered together.
 4. **Action Induction:** Each cluster corresponds to a unique high-level action $\bar{a}_{ij}$.
 5. **Effect Learning:** The effects of an action $\bar{a}_{ij}$ are directly defined by the $C^+_{ij}$ (additions) and $C^-_{ij}$ (deletions) sets of its corresponding cluster.
 6. **Precondition Learning:** The precondition for an action is learned by taking the intersection of all lifted states $s_i$ where the action (from its cluster) was applicable. This generates a maximal, conservative precondition.
 7. **Precondition Pruning:** Spurious preconditions are removed. A relation is considered spurious if it is not parameterized by any objects changed by the action and does not change across any demonstrations where the action is executed.
 8. **Parameter Learning:** Unique parameters from the predicates in the learned precondition and effect are extracted and ordered to form the action's parameter list.
- **Action Interpreters:** LAMP autonomously constructs action interpreters by leveraging the generative properties of the learned GMMs for RCRs. For a grounded symbolic relation $R'$, its interpretation region $\Gamma_{R'}$ is the set of all low-level states where $R'$ is true. The interpreter for an abstract action $\bar{a}'$ with effects $R'_1 \land \dots \land R'_k$ is then defined as the intersection of the interpretation regions of its effects: $\Gamma_{\bar{a}'} = \Gamma_{R'_1} \cap \dots \cap \Gamma_{R'_k}$. This allows sampling of feasible low-level configurations for abstract actions.
- **Task and Motion Planning (TAMP):** The learned symbolic world model (vocabulary $V$ and actions $\bar{A}$) is integrated with a deterministic version of STAMP for TAMP. To mitigate potential inaccuracies in learned models, predicate abstraction is used to create a relaxed high-level planning problem, and a top-k planner generates multiple plans, which are then iteratively refined until a valid low-level execution is found.

This comprehensive approach allows LAMP to move from raw kinematic data to a fully functional, abstract, symbolic world model capable of supporting long-horizon planning and zero-shot generalization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of LAMP was conducted across five diverse and realistic domains, encompassing both simulated and real-world settings: Box Packing, Keva structure building, Jenga stacking, autonomous food delivery in a cafe, and setting a dinner table. Across all domains, LAMP was trained using a maximum of 200 demonstrations of simpler tasks, with only half of these demonstrations resulting in successful goal achievement.

#### 4.1. Generalization Factors

A key metric for evaluating generalization was the "generalization factor," defined as the ratio of the maximum number of objects in test tasks to the maximum number of objects in training tasks. This metric serves as a proxy for task complexity and planning horizon.
- **Performance:** LAMP achieved significant generalization factors, ranging up to 18x, in stark contrast to contemporary imitation learning approaches which typically exhibit a generalization factor of 1x.
 - **Cafe Domain:** LAMP, trained on demonstrations with 1 (simulated) or 2 (real-world) objects, successfully solved problems involving 18 objects in simulation and 8 objects in the real world, yielding generalization factors of 18x and 4x, respectively.
 - **Keva and Jenga Domains:** From demonstrations with three planks, LAMP built structures with 30 Keva planks in simulation (10x generalization) and 18 Jenga planks in real-world settings (6x generalization).
 - **Box Packing:** Achieved the maximum possible generalization factor of 4x, as the box could accommodate only four cans.
- **Implication:** These results indicate that LAMP enables robots to acquire knowledge that transfers to tasks of substantially greater scale and complexity than their training experience.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Transfer: Comparison with Baselines

LAMP's transferability was assessed on novel tasks, using only knowledge acquired from simpler demonstrations and without any prior exposure to the test scenarios. Its performance was compared against two baseline approaches:
- **STAMP (Stochastic Task and Motion Planning):** Representing approaches that rely on expert-provided, hand-crafted symbolic models.
- **Code-as-Policies (CoP):** Representing approaches that leverage foundation models for reasoning and skill composition.
- **Results:**
 - **LAMP:** Solved 100% of the unseen test tasks in the evaluation.
 - **STAMP:** Achieved similar performance (100% success), demonstrating that LAMP's autonomously learned models performed on par with human-engineered symbolic models.
 - **Code-as-Policies:** Solved less than 35% of the test tasks. The paper notes that CoP typically requires manually crafted inputs such as Python APIs for actions, detailed goal descriptions with hand-crafted predicates, and sample solution code, which are tasks LAMP automates. Additionally, CoP was allowed 25 attempts per problem, while LAMP did not require such multiple attempts for a problem to be considered solved.
- **Implication:** LAMP demonstrates that fully autonomous learning of symbolic world models can yield performance comparable to expert-engineered systems, surpassing current foundation model-based methods in generalization and autonomy for these types of tasks.

#### 4.3. Sample Efficiency and Robustness

The research also investigated the sample efficiency of LAMP, a critical factor given the high cost of robot demonstrations.
- **Results:** LAMP was observed to learn effective world models using as few as 40 goal-achieving demonstrations.
- **Implication:** This significantly reduces the data acquisition burden compared to modern behavior cloning or imitation learning approaches, which often require tens of thousands of demonstrations. The ability to generalize from fewer examples makes this approach more practical for real-world robotic deployment.

#### 4.4. Interpretability of Learned Concepts

Examples of invented relations and high-level actions (e.g., "Holding(Gripper, Can)", "Place-Cup") were provided, illustrating that the learned abstractions are interpretable. This characteristic is valuable for understanding and debugging robot behavior.

In summary, the empirical results demonstrate that LAMP successfully acquires transferable symbolic abstractions from raw robot experience, enabling robust zero-shot generalization to tasks with significantly increased complexity and object count, while maintaining high success rates and exhibiting strong sample efficiency compared to existing methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in robot autonomy and generalization, addressing several long-standing challenges in the field. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Autonomous Invention of Symbolic Knowledge:** The primary significance lies in being the first reported method that enables robots to autonomously invent both symbolic, relational concepts and complete logic-based world models directly from raw, unsegmented, and unannotated kinematic demonstration trajectories. This capability reduces the dependence on extensive human engineering or *a priori* expert knowledge, which has historically been a major bottleneck for scaling robotic applications.

- **Zero-Shot Generalization to Complex, Unseen Tasks:** The work demonstrates a substantial improvement in generalization capabilities, achieving zero-shot transfer to tasks that are significantly more complex (e.g., 18x more objects, novel goal configurations, much longer planning horizons) than those used for training. This addresses a core limitation of many current robot learning paradigms, particularly imitation learning, which often struggle to generalize beyond the immediate training distribution.

- **Reduced Data Requirements:** LAMP exhibits high sample efficiency, successfully learning effective world models from as few as 40 goal-achieving demonstrations. This is a critical advantage in robotics, where collecting large datasets is costly and time-consuming, making the approach more practical for real-world deployment.

- **Interpretability and Transparency:** By learning logic-based symbolic models, the approach yields interpretable abstractions. This inherent interpretability offers benefits for understanding robot reasoning, diagnosing failures, and building trust in autonomous systems, which is often lacking in end-to-end deep learning models.

- **Scalability for Long-Horizon Planning:** The learned abstract world models support effective planning over long horizons by converting complex continuous problems into more manageable discrete symbolic planning tasks. The integration with Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) frameworks, along with techniques to mitigate learning inaccuracies, enables robust execution in varied environments.

- **Foundation for Future Robot Capabilities:** This work lays a foundation for future advancements in robot intelligence.
 - **Easier Robot Retasking:** By autonomously learning transferable abstractions, the framework has the potential to make robot re-tasking more accessible to non-experts, as they would not need to manually define every new concept or action.
 - **Handling Stochastic and Partially Observable Environments:** The paper identifies extending the approach to handle stochastic environments with stronger theoretical guarantees as a promising future direction, building upon the deterministic foundation established here.
 - **Generalization to Novel Object Categories:** Future work aims to generalize learned concepts to entirely new object categories and novel geometries, further expanding robot adaptability.
 - **Complex Dynamics:** While currently limited to quasi-static assumptions, the approach could potentially be extended to tasks requiring force-controlled contact or closed-loop reactive policies, pushing the boundaries of what can be autonomously learned for complex manipulation.

In conclusion, this research marks a step toward developing robots that can autonomously acquire, represent, and utilize abstract knowledge to operate intelligently and flexibly in the real world, moving closer to human-like generalization capabilities.
