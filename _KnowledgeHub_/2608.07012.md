# Scenix: Sparse-View 3D Scene Reconstruction via Executable Scene Programs

> [!summary] Summary
> Scenix reconstructs editable 3D indoor scenes from a few uncalibrated RGB views by predicting an executable scene program. A LayoutVLM emits room blueprint plus per-object category, description, position, extent and orientation; an Asset Grounder reconciles entities with visual evidence and generates assets; a Critic-Editor-Verify loop refines placement. It beats single-view baselines, lifting F1@5% to 0.355 versus 0.192-0.207.

## Problem

- Text-conditioned scene generators produce plausible rooms but cannot faithfully reconstruct a specific observed environment from its actual images.
- Neural reconstruction needs dense calibrated views, depth, masks or supplied 3D layouts, and yields monolithic geometry that resists object-level editing.
- Existing indoor datasets lack paired multiview imagery with blueprints, long-form object descriptions, metric placement and observation-consistent targets needed for such supervision.

## Method

- **Scenix**'s ==LayoutVLM== autoregressively emits a serialized scene program from a variable number of uncalibrated views, jointly predicting blueprint, object identities, descriptions, centers, extents, orientations, supports.
- ==Asset Grounder== builds a multiview evidence inventory, reconciles it bijectively with predicted seeds via retain/replace/suppress/clone, then conditions image-guided open-vocabulary 3D asset generation.
- Residual ==Critic-Editor-Verify loop== renders a metric bird's-eye proxy, turns discrepancies into testable clauses, applies minimal move/rotate/resize edits, and verifies against blueprint constraints.

## Results

- On single-view SpatialGen, the **9B** model reaches F1@5% **0.355** and 3D IoU **0.179** versus **0.192-0.207** F1 and **0.046-0.134** IoU for SAM3D, Gen3DSR and 3D-Fixer.
- Asset Grounder lifts out-of-domain F1@5% from **0.244** to **0.311** (9B) and **0.234** to **0.308** (4B), cutting predicted object counts from **12.2** and **14.1** toward the **6.3** ground-truth mean.
- The agentic loop adds a further **+0.016** (9B) and **+0.039** (4B) F1@5%; the full pipeline improves raw F1@5% by **34.0%** and **48.3%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating reconstruction as program induction decouples scene structure from asset realization, so each stage improves independently while keeping objects individually addressable and editable.
> - Supervising only entities visible in the sampled views prevents conflating reconstruction with unconstrained hallucination, a transferable recipe for sparse-observation training targets.
> - Under distribution shift, most error is inventory over-generation rather than placement; cheap evidence-based pruning beats added model capacity for scene-level accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the problem of reconstructing structured and editable 3D indoor scenes from sparse, uncalibrated RGB images. This task presents challenges distinct from existing 3D generation and reconstruction paradigms.

Current generative models are capable of creating high-quality individual 3D assets (e.g., Hu et al. 2026), but they typically do not address the broader problem of composing these assets into a coherent, structured, and editable scene. Text-conditioned methods (e.g., Chen et al. 2024; Wan et al. 2026) can generate plausible indoor scenes based on textual prompts, but they lack the capacity to faithfully reconstruct a specific, observed environment. This limitation means they are not suitable for tasks requiring an accurate digital twin of a real space.

Neural reconstruction methods, such as those relying on radiance fields (e.g., Niemeyer et al. 2022), typically focus on recovering scene geometry. These methods often necessitate dense, calibrated visual inputs or auxiliary geometric priors like depth maps or accurate 3D layouts (e.g., Gen3DSR, 3DFixer, SAM3D). The outputs are frequently monolithic geometric representations (e.g., meshes, point clouds, radiance fields) that are difficult to edit at the object level, which limits their utility for applications requiring interactive manipulation of scene components. Furthermore, they often struggle with sparse and uncalibrated views, which are common in practical scenarios. Object-centric systems (e.g., Nie et al. 2020) attempt to estimate object cuboids or align CAD assets but are often constrained by the completeness of asset libraries and the challenges of occlusion.

Scenix differentiates itself by proposing a paradigm shift from geometric reconstruction to the direct prediction of an "executable scene program." This structured representation explicitly encodes semantic and spatial information, including room layout, object identities, rich descriptions, and precise metric transformations. This approach bypasses the limitations of geometry-only outputs by creating a representation that is inherently editable at the object level and can guide the instantiation of open-vocabulary 3D assets. Unlike methods requiring extensive priors such as camera calibration, depth information, floor plans, or manually annotated object masks, Scenix operates solely from sparse, uncalibrated RGB views. This makes it suitable for a wider range of real-world applications where such auxiliary data is unavailable. The introduction of the XScene dataset further contributes to the landscape by providing the necessary supervision for training models capable of this novel task, addressing a gap in existing indoor scene datasets which often lack complete and observation-consistent annotations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to synthesize a structured and editable 3D indoor scene from a limited number of uncalibrated RGB observations. This goal necessitates addressing several tightly coupled challenges inherent in sparse-view 3D scene reconstruction.

The motivation for Scenix arises from the limitations of existing approaches and the practical demands of generating usable 3D scene representations:

1. **Inference of Room Structure and Global Consistency**: Beyond generating individual high-quality assets, a robust system must infer the overarching room structure, establish correspondences between objects observed across sparse and potentially incomplete views, and recover a globally consistent spatial configuration. Traditional pipelines often separate these concerns, leading to compounded errors or a lack of holistic understanding.
2. **Bridging Perception and Realization**: While recent advances in generative models allow for the creation of diverse 3D assets, the challenge remains in interpreting sparse visual inputs to determine *which* assets to instantiate and *how* to compose them within a specific environment. The system needs to move beyond asset creation to scene understanding and structured realization.
3. **Editable 3D Representations**: Many 3D reconstruction methods produce monolithic geometric outputs (e.g., meshes or point clouds) that are difficult to modify at an object level. For applications like interior design, virtual reality content creation, or robotics, an editable representation where individual objects can be manipulated, replaced, or adjusted is crucial.
4. **Minimizing External Priors**: Existing methods frequently demand extensive auxiliary information, such as dense calibrated camera views, depth maps, predefined floor plans, or labor-intensive human-annotated object masks. This requirement severely restricts their applicability in general real-world scenarios where such rich data is often unavailable or expensive to acquire. The motivation is to achieve reconstruction from minimal, readily available inputs (a few RGB photos).
5. **Addressing Observation Inconsistency**: When reconstructing from sparse views, not all objects in a scene will be visible. Supervising hidden objects during training could conflate the task of reconstruction with unconstrained scene completion. Therefore, a method is needed that aligns its reconstruction target with the available visual evidence.

To address these motivations, Scenix formulates sparse-view 3D scene reconstruction as the direct prediction of an "executable scene program." This program, represented in a structured JSON format, explicitly encodes the room envelope (blueprint), object identities, detailed semantic descriptions, precise 3D positions, extents, orientations, and support relations. This structured representation serves as a common interface that can be learned from images, instantiated with generated assets, verified for consistency, and readily edited at the object level, thereby tackling the aforementioned challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Scenix operates as a multi-stage framework designed to predict a structured and editable 3D scene program from sparse, uncalibrated RGB observations. The overall process involves direct program induction, perception-grounded asset instantiation, and a closed-loop spatial refinement.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

A 3D scene is represented as S = (B, O, R), where B is the blueprint, O is a set of individually addressable objects, and R denotes object-object and object-room relations. Each object o_i is defined by its category (c_i), appearance description (d_i), 3D center (t_i), extent (s_i), orientation (r_i), and support relation (u_i). This executable program can be rendered, edited, and populated with generated assets.

#### 3.2. Pipeline Overview (Figure 2)

1. **Direct 3D Scene Program Induction (LayoutVLM)**:
 - **Vision-to-structure generation**: The LayoutVLM component is responsible for predicting an initial scene program, S_0 = (B, O_0), directly from a variable number of input RGB images I = {I_v}. This component avoids staged geometric estimations by autoregressively emitting a serialized scene program. It jointly infers room structure, object identities, semantic descriptions, and metric transformations. The model, based on a multimodal Vision-Language Model (VLM) architecture (specifically, fine-tuned Qwen3.5-4B or Qwen3.5-9B), learns to map raw image inputs to the structured JSON output.
 - **Observation-consistent learning**: To address the challenge of objects being hidden in sparse views, a specific annotation protocol is used during training. An object is included in the ground truth target (O_obs) only if a predefined number of its oriented-box corners (κ) are visible across the selected input views. Visibility is determined by camera frustum intersection and lack of occlusion by blueprint walls. This ensures that the model is not penalized for failing to reconstruct objects that are inherently unobservable from the given inputs. Camera parameters are utilized solely for offline target computation, not during inference. The model minimizes a cross-entropy loss over the serialized program tokens.

2. **Asset Grounder**:
 - This stage refines LayoutVLM's initial program and integrates visual evidence for asset generation.
 - An evidence-backed multiview inventory E = {e_j} is constructed from the input images, resolving cross-view identity and managing repeated instances. Each inventory item e_j encapsulates category, appearance, support type, confidence, and associated views.
 - An MLLM (Multimodal Large Language Model) reconciles the initial object set O_0 from LayoutVLM with the inventory E. This reconciliation process involves decisions to retain, replace, or suppress objects from O_0, and to clone inventory items not matched to any initial prediction. The goal is to produce a grounded object set O_g whose count matches the inventory (M) and where each inventory item is bijectively mapped to an active entity, preserving semantic family. This stage corrects omissions, duplicates, and category errors from the initial prediction. Crucially, it does not predict new poses but uses an image-guided planner for localizing any cloned items.
 - For each grounded object, a promptable segmenter proposes relevant image regions, and a VLM selects the most appropriate soft mask. This mask, along with the object's description, conditions a single-object reference image generator. If no reliable mask is available, text-only generation is employed. Finally, an image-conditioned 3D generator converts this reference into a mesh, with the scene program's extent guiding the fitting process during assembly.

3. **Residual Critic–Editor–Verify Loop**:
 - This closed-loop refinement mechanism addresses residual errors in position, scale, and orientation that may arise from initial predictions, grounding, or asset insertion.
 - Starting with the grounded scene program S_g, a metric bird's-eye proxy P(S_k) is rendered, showing footprints and facing directions. An auxiliary top-down hypothesis H(I) is generated from the input images, providing coarse position and wall cues, while the metric program and high-confidence relations remain authoritative.
 - **Critic**: In each iteration k, the Critic identifies discrepancies between P(S_k) and H(I) and converts them into object-specific clauses Q_k. Blueprint violations (e.g., objects extending beyond walls) are also introduced as mandatory constraints.
 - **Editor**: Based on Q_k, the Editor proposes minimal changes to S_k, such as translations, rotations, or footprint adjustments. Each proposed edit is validated against the scene schema before application.
 - **Verifier**: A fresh rendering P(S_k+1) is used to judge every clause in Q_k, producing pass/fail verdicts (a_k+1). Failed clauses and verifier feedback guide subsequent edits.
 - The loop terminates when all clauses pass, all footprints are within the blueprint, no valid edit can be applied, or a fixed iteration budget is exhausted.

#### 3.3. The XScene Dataset

A critical component of this methodology is the XScene dataset, developed because existing datasets lack the comprehensive supervision required for learning executable scene programs.
- **Data Construction**: XScene combines procedurally generated synthetic data with real-world imagery.
 - **Procedural Multiview Data**: Approximately 100,000 synthetic indoor scenes are generated using InfiniGen (Raistrick et al. 2023). Each scene includes room envelopes and object attributes (category, position, extent, orientation). Blueprint-aware camera trajectories render ten RGB images per scene, from which 1 to 10 views are randomly selected. An observation-consistent filtering protocol retains only objects whose visible corners meet a specified threshold across the chosen views, preventing supervision of occluded or out-of-view entities. InfiniGen's procedural object generators allow for parameter-to-text conversion using category-specific templates, yielding rich appearance descriptions.
 - **Real-image Data**: Approximately 10,000 images from the SUN RGB-D dataset (Song et al. 2015) are integrated to enhance real-world diversity. 2D labels from SUN RGB-D are used to prompt Hunyuan-turbo for object descriptions, which are then attached to the original 3D annotations. Manual 3D box annotations are geometrically corrected and transformed into a unified world coordinate system.
- **Dataset Statistics**: XScene comprises 110,179 cases and 560,184 images. Cases are split at the room level. InfiniGen provides scale and dense multiview supervision, while SUN RGB-D contributes real-world imagery and human-verified 3D annotations, spanning single to dense observation views.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Scenix involved assessing structured scene prediction, object grounding, and spatial refinement across in-domain, real-image, and out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Metrics and Setup

Predictions and ground truth were aligned by removing global translation and rotation. Performance was measured using Localization F1 (greedy object matching by normalized center distance), Class-only Metrics (location-agnostic class-count overlap), 3D IoU (oriented-box IoU), and Orientation Error (symmetry-aware yaw error). Experiments used fine-tuned Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B LayoutVLMs. The XScene test split included InfiniGen and SUN RGB-D cases, and OOD evaluation was performed on a subsampled SpatialGen dataset. Comparisons were made against single-view baselines (SAM3D, Gen3DSR, 3D-Fixer) using a shared 2D front end for fairness.

#### 4.2. Structured Scene Prediction (Table 3)

- **In-domain Performance**: On the XScene test set, SUN RGB-D scenes yielded higher Localization F1 and matched 3D IoU than InfiniGen scenes. This is attributed to SUN RGB-D's smaller and less cluttered scenes, which simplify localization under normalized distance thresholds. InfiniGen, representing the dominant portion of the synthetic training data, showed stronger class-level coverage. The 9B model consistently lowered yaw error on InfiniGen, indicating improved orientation reasoning, though matched 3D IoU remained similar to the 4B model.
- **Model Scale**: Increasing model capacity from 4B to 9B generally improved F1@5% and F1@10% across all datasets, with notable gains on InfiniGen and SpatialGen. This improvement primarily enhanced spatial localization, while other aspects like class-level performance or matched 3D IoU did not always scale proportionally.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Transfer**: On the SpatialGen OOD dataset, both Scenix variants experienced a clear drop in localization performance compared to in-domain results. Class-level recall, however, remained substantially higher than localization F1. The models also tended to over-generate objects (e.g., 9B predicting 12.2 objects vs. 6.3 ground truth mean), indicating challenges in inventory calibration and precise metric placement under distribution shifts. This observation motivated the subsequent refinement stages.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (Table 4, on SpatialGen OOD)

Ablations demonstrated the contributions of the Asset Grounder and the Critic–Editor–Verify loop.
- **Stage 1 (Asset Grounder)**: This stage, which includes reconciliation, blueprint containment, and axis snapping, provided the most significant performance boost for both models. F1@5% increased from 0.244 to 0.311 for 9B (+27.5%) and from 0.234 to 0.308 for 4B (+31.6%). The average predicted object count decreased considerably (e.g., 9B from 12.2 to 6.5), bringing it closer to the ground truth mean of 6.3. This indicated that precision gains largely stemmed from the pruning of unsupported, duplicated, or out-of-room entities.
- **Stage 2 (Critic–Editor–Verify Loop)**: The agentic refinement loop further improved performance. F1@5% increased by an additional 0.016 for 9B (from 0.311 to 0.327) and 0.039 for 4B (from 0.308 to 0.347). This stage primarily refines spatial parameters rather than the object inventory, so class precision remained largely unchanged. The full pipeline achieved F1@5% improvements over raw predictions of 34.0% for 9B and 48.3% for 4B, with Stage 1 contributing the majority of the gain and Stage 2 consistently adding value.

#### 4.4. Single View Analysis (Table 5)

When compared with single-view reconstruction baselines (SAM3D, Gen3DSR, 3D-Fixer) on a SpatialGen subset, Scenix demonstrated superior performance:
- Scenix (9B/4B) achieved F1@5% of 0.355/0.287, significantly outperforming baselines which ranged from 0.192 to 0.207.
- Localization recall was 0.463/0.564 for Scenix versus 0.150–0.171 for baselines.
- 3D IoU was 0.179–0.181 for Scenix versus 0.046–0.134 for baselines.
The paper attributes the baseline failures to a compounding error chain originating from 2D detection, where issues like fragmented masks, inconsistent labels, and errors in camera/monocular depth estimations propagate. These methods struggle to recover hidden structures or infer scene-level relations from partial observations. Scenix, by contrast, predicts identities and spatial relations *jointly* as a room-level program, decoupling structure from asset realization and leading to higher recall and geometric agreement.

#### 4.5. Visualizations (Figure 4)

Qualitative results illustrated Scenix's ability to generate coherent scene layouts that generally match input observations. The system demonstrated scene-level reasoning by recovering objects not visible in single-view baselines (e.g., a nightstand). However, some instances of rotational or diagonal-symmetry ambiguity and category errors (e.g., confusing a bookcase with a large shelf) were observed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Scenix framework represents a notable contribution to the field of 3D scene reconstruction, particularly for indoor environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Novel Scene Representation**: The introduction of "executable scene programs" as a structured and editable representation for 3D indoor scenes is a significant methodological advance. This explicit, semantic-rich format (JSON) goes beyond monolithic geometric reconstructions, making the output intrinsically interpretable, modifiable, and usable for downstream applications that require object-level manipulation.
2. **Reduced Input Requirements**: Scenix demonstrates the feasibility of reconstructing detailed 3D scenes from sparse and uncalibrated RGB views, without relying on extensive auxiliary priors such as camera parameters, depth maps, predefined floor plans, or manually annotated object masks. This significantly broadens the applicability of 3D scene reconstruction to more general and less controlled real-world scenarios.
3. **Comprehensive Scene Understanding**: By integrating LayoutVLM for structural induction, an Asset Grounder for perception-grounded asset generation, and a Critic–Editor–Verify loop for spatial refinement, Scenix addresses the complex challenges of inferring room structure, associating objects across incomplete observations, and ensuring globally consistent spatial configurations in a unified framework.
4. **Dataset Contribution**: The creation of the XScene dataset, comprising over 110,000 scenes with comprehensive, observation-consistent supervision, fills a critical gap in resources for training and evaluating models performing this type of structured 3D scene prediction. This dataset will likely support further research and development in the field.
5. **Performance Advancement**: The experimental results indicate that Scenix significantly outperforms existing single-view object-centric reconstruction baselines in structured scene prediction and object grounding. This performance gain, especially in recall and geometric agreement, suggests a more robust and holistic understanding of scenes. The ablation studies also highlight the importance and effectiveness of its multi-stage refinement process.

The potential impact of Scenix spans several domains:
- **Interior Design and Home Renovation**: Users could rapidly create editable 3D models of their homes from a few photos, facilitating design planning, furniture placement, and virtual walkthroughs.
- **Augmented Reality (AR) and Virtual Reality (VR)**: The framework could enable faster and more accessible creation of realistic, editable indoor environments for AR/VR experiences, lowering the barrier for content generation.
- **Robotics and Embodied AI**: Providing structured, semantic representations of environments could enhance robot navigation, object interaction, and general scene understanding for intelligent agents.
- **Digital Twin Creation**: Generating structured digital representations of real-world spaces could be valuable for facilities management, architectural visualization, and urban planning.
- **Content Creation and Gaming**: Expedited generation of detailed and editable indoor scenes could streamline workflows in game development and other forms of digital content creation.

In summary, Scenix offers a promising new direction for 3D scene reconstruction by focusing on structured, editable programs from minimal visual input, addressing key limitations of prior work and opening new avenues for practical applications.
