# OopsieVerse: A Safety Benchmark with Damage-Aware Simulation for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A simulation framework called OOPSIEVERSE allows robots to train and be evaluated with explicit awareness of physical damage, addressing a gap in current robotic safety benchmarks. This system improved safe task completion in both imitation and reinforcement learning, and achieved a 60% reduction in unsafe behaviors during sim-to-real transfer to a physical robot.

## Problem

- Existing robot simulation environments do not model or quantify physical damage, leading to policies that appear successful but are unsafe in real-world deployment.
- Current safety benchmarks typically use abstract costs or collision models, lacking explicit, physically-grounded damage signals for complex manipulation tasks.
- Developing generalizable safe robot manipulation policies is challenging due to the high cost and danger of real-world training and the limitations of brittle, task-specific safety methods.

## Method

- Introduces **DAMAGESIM**, a ==simulator-agnostic== framework that detects and quantifies mechanical, thermal, and fluid damage through explicit object ==health states==.
- Provides **OOPSIEBENCH**, a benchmark suite of 32 household tasks crafted to expose common damaging failure modes and contrast safe versus unsafe strategies.
- Enables integration with ==safe reinforcement learning== by framing damage accumulation as a constraint within a ==Constrained Markov Decision Process==.

## Results

- Imitation Learning policies trained with OOPSIEVERSE's damage feedback or curated data significantly improved Safe Task Completion Rates, while RL policies with damage penalties achieved up to **100%** safe success on specific tasks.
- Benchmarking of a state-of-the-art VLA model (GR00T) revealed a substantial gap between task completion and safe execution, with examples like **92%** task completion but only **4%** safe completion.
- Damage-aware policies trained in simulation demonstrated a **60% reduction** in unsafe behaviors during sim-to-real transfer to a Franka Panda robot compared to damage-unaware counterparts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating explicit, physically-grounded damage signals into simulations significantly improves the safety awareness of robotic policies.
> - Standard task completion metrics are insufficient for evaluating robotic safety, as many policies achieve goals through potentially destructive means.
> - Damage-aware training in simulation can yield policies that demonstrate observably safer behaviors when transferred directly to physical robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of capable robotic systems for complex manipulation tasks in real-world environments is a central goal in robotics. While advancements in robotic manipulation have been substantial, a critical challenge for deploying household robots remains physical safety. Current simulation environments, widely used for training and evaluating robotic systems, often lack the capability to detect, quantify, or represent physical damage that would occur from unsafe interactions in the real world. Consequently, existing manipulation benchmarks typically define success solely by task completion, overlooking the distinction between benign behaviors and those that achieve goals through destructive means. This abstraction can lead to policies that appear successful in simulation but are unsafe or destructive upon real-world deployment.

Previous efforts in ensuring robot safety have utilized control theory (e.g., control barrier functions, reachability analysis) for strong guarantees, but these methods are challenging to scale to complex, contact-rich manipulation due to requirements for explicit state definitions and accurate models. Learning-based approaches incorporate safety through learned functions or constraints, yet often rely on hand-designed unsafe scenarios or task-specific cost functions, limiting their generality.

Existing safety benchmarks, such as Safety Gym, GUARD, AI Safety Gridworlds, DSRL, and ReDMan, typically model safety through abstract costs, collisions, or proximity constraints, focusing primarily on navigation or low-dimensional control. These benchmarks do not inherently provide explicit models of physical damage relevant to manipulation. Large-scale manipulation benchmarks like BEHAVIOR-1K and RoboCasa offer diverse, realistic tasks but, similar to other simulators, define success purely by task completion, requiring manual encoding of safety considerations.

OOPSIEVERSE addresses this specific gap by providing a unified simulation framework and benchmark that explicitly models physically grounded damage. It complements existing safe learning methods and simulation benchmarks by introducing a reusable, physics-grounded notion of damage, thereby distinguishing safe from unsafe task execution independently of task success. This approach enables a more consistent and scalable way to train and evaluate safety-aware manipulation policies, which is crucial for the safe deployment of robots in human environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce OOPSIEVERSE, a novel simulation framework and benchmark designed to enable damage-aware household robot manipulation. This framework aims to facilitate the training and systematic evaluation of robotic policies by explicitly modeling the physical damage incurred during task execution.

The motivation behind OOPSIEVERSE stems from several critical limitations in current robotics research and deployment:
1. **Safety as an Overlooked Dimension:** Despite rapid advancements in robotic manipulation, the physical safety of robots and their surroundings remains a significant barrier to widespread household deployment. Traditional metrics for task success in simulation do not account for potential damage, creating a disconnect between simulated performance and real-world viability.
2. **Artificial Safety in Simulation:** Existing robotic simulators generally provide an artificial sense of safety. They do not model or measure the damage that would result from unsafe interactions, leading to policies that might "succeed" in simulation by employing behaviors that would be destructive or harmful in a physical environment (e.g., applying excessive force, mismanaging thermal interactions, or causing liquid spills).
3. **Limitations of Current Safety Approaches:** Existing methods for integrating safety often involve task-specific reward shaping or hand-designed constraints. These approaches are typically brittle, difficult to scale across diverse environments, and limited in their ability to address multiple sources of damage systematically.
4. **Cost and Danger of Real-World Training:** Training and evaluating complex robotic systems directly in the physical world is inherently difficult, expensive, and potentially dangerous, especially when interactions involve fragile objects or the robot itself. Simulation offers a harm-free alternative, but its utility for safety-critical applications is diminished without accurate damage modeling.
5. **Need for Generalizable Damage Signals:** There is a need for a general, physically-grounded, and task-agnostic signal that can detect, quantify, and represent damage across various interaction types. Such a signal would enable unified safety evaluation and learning, moving beyond abstract safety costs or task-specific constraints.

To address these motivations, OOPSIEVERSE aims to provide:
- A mechanism to augment existing Markov Decision Problems (MDPs) with explicit damage-related observations, rewards, and/or termination conditions.
- A method to convert common physical interactions—such as contact forces, temperature changes, and liquid interactions—into quantifiable mechanical, thermal, or fluid damage.
- A benchmark suite of household tasks specifically designed to highlight and measure the distinction between task completion and safe execution, allowing for systematic evaluation of safety-performance trade-offs.

By achieving these objectives, OOPSIEVERSE seeks to lay an open-source foundation for systematic and scalable research into safe robot manipulation, ultimately fostering the development of robots that are not only capable but also reliably safe.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OOPSIEVERSE is structured around two core components: DAMAGESIM, a simulator-agnostic damage detection framework, and OOPSIEBENCH, a suite of household tasks for safety evaluation.

#### 3.1. DAMAGESIM: Damage Detection and Quantification Framework

DAMAGESIM functions as a plugin for object-centric detection, tracking, and quantification of physical damage during robot navigation and manipulation. It conceptually extends a standard Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) into a "damage-aware POMDP" ($M_{DA}$). This extension augments the original POMDP state space ($S$) with a health state ($h \in S_h$), forming a health-augmented state space $S_{DA} = S \times S_h$. This augmented state provides a basis for new damage-aware observations, termination conditions, or reward functions.

The framework models damage through an explicit, unifying "health" state for each object and robot in the simulated environment. Health is tracked per link of an object, ranging from a maximum value ($h_{MAX}$, completely undamaged, set to 100) to 0 (completely damaged). An object's overall health is defined as the minimum health of its constituent links, providing a semantically meaningful aggregate. The environment's health state $h_t$ at any time $t$ is the collection of all object health values.

Link health values are continuously updated by a set of Damage Evaluation Models (DEMs), each responsible for a specific source of physical damage. The aggregated damage value across all DEMs at time $t$ reduces the object's health: $h_{e_i}(t) = h_{e_i}(t-1) - \sum_k d_{DEM_k}(t)$.

DAMAGESIM includes three primary DEMs:

1. **Mechanical Damage Evaluation Model:** This model captures harm from contact-induced forces that cause cracking, deformation, or breakage. It addresses both impulsive events (e.g., collisions, drops) and sustained/constrained contacts (e.g., compression, tension). Instead of complex physics, it uses an approximation derived from contact forces and link acceleration, which are commonly available in robotics simulators. For each link, contact forces are decomposed into components parallel ($F_\parallel(t)$, aligned with acceleration, representing impulsive effects) and orthogonal ($F_\perp(t)$, representing sustained/constrained loading) to the direction of acceleration. The effective mechanical load is calculated as $\epsilon_{mech}(t) = \alpha F_\parallel(t) + \beta F_\perp(t)$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are user-defined coefficients controlling sensitivity to impulsive versus sustained loading. Incremental mechanical damage is then computed as $d_{mech}(t) = \Lambda_{mech} \max(\epsilon_{mech}(t) - E_{mech}, 0)$, where $E_{mech}$ is a minimal load threshold (yield/fracture limit) and $\Lambda_{mech}$ scales the damage accumulation rate. These parameters are customizable per link to reflect material properties.

2. **Thermal Damage Evaluation Model:** This model assesses harm due to exposure to extreme temperatures (e.g., melting, burning, freezing). Its implementation is currently limited to simulators that provide temperature tracking, such as OmniGibson. It calculates damage based on an object's temperature $T(t)$ relative to predefined hot ($T_{hot}$) and cold ($T_{cold}$) thresholds. The damage is quantified as $d_{therm}(t) = \Lambda_{hot} \max(T(t) - T_{hot}, 0) + \Lambda_{cold} \max(T_{cold} - T(t), 0)$, where $\Lambda_{hot}$ and $\Lambda_{cold}$ are material-dependent accumulation rates.

3. **Fluid Damage Evaluation Model:** This model accounts for harm caused by liquid exposure (e.g., swelling, contamination, short-circuiting), also limited to simulators with fluid dynamics, like OmniGibson. Damage is determined by the amount of liquid particles in contact with a link, $c_\ell(t)$, relative to an exposure threshold $C_{fluid}$. Incremental fluid damage is $d_{fluid}(t) = \Lambda_{fluid} \max(c_\ell(t) - C_{fluid}, 0)$, with $\Lambda_{fluid}$ controlling the accumulation rate based on material resistance to liquids.

DAMAGESIM is designed to be simulator-agnostic in principle and has been instantiated in two distinct physics backends: OmniGibson (based on Nvidia Omniverse) and RoboCasa (based on DeepMind MuJoCo), demonstrating its portability.

#### 3.2. OOPSIEBENCH: Damage-Aware Benchmark Suite

OOPSIEBENCH comprises a suite of 32 task instances, covering 21 unique task designs, across both OmniGibson (17 tasks) and RoboCasa (15 tasks). These tasks are specifically crafted to expose policies to physically damaging failure modes prevalent in household manipulation. A key design principle is that each task typically allows for an "easy, tempting" unsafe strategy that achieves the goal but risks damage, alongside a safer strategy requiring more careful interaction.

The tasks encompass a range of common household interactions, including opening doors/drawers, operating appliances, pick-and-place of fragile objects, wiping surfaces, and navigation. Simulator-specific tasks are included to leverage the unique capabilities of each backend; for instance, OmniGibson tasks emphasize fluid and thermal risks, while RoboCasa tasks include longer-horizon composite activities. Examples of damaging shortcuts include slamming articulated objects (mechanical damage), operating near liquids (fluid damage), and handling objects near heat sources (thermal damage).

As part of OOPSIEBENCH, a dataset of 90 human-collected demonstrations across five tasks is provided, with an even split between demonstrations collected with and without real-time damage feedback from DAMAGESIM.

#### 3.3. Constrained MDP Formulation

For compatibility with existing safe reinforcement learning methods, OOPSIEVERSE can be mapped to a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP). In this formulation, the objective is to maximize task-specific reward while ensuring the cumulative physical damage, defined by $C(s_t, a_t) = \sum_k d_{DEM_k}(t)$, remains below a predefined safety budget $\hat{c}$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents a comprehensive evaluation of OOPSIEVERSE across multiple use cases, demonstrating its utility in enhancing safety in robotic manipulation.

#### 4.1. OOPSIEVERSE for Imitation Learning (IL)

The study investigated whether DAMAGESIM's real-time damage feedback could improve the safety of human-collected demonstrations and the resulting imitation learning policies. Human operators collected demonstrations for five tasks (Shelve Cereal Box, Lift Egg, Add Firewood, Pour Water, Wipe Countertop) under two conditions: with and without live health feedback. Flow-based IL policies were then trained using data from different regimes: all data, only data with health feedback, only data without health feedback, and health-filtered data (episodes or individual datapoints where health loss exceeded a threshold).

Results, averaged over 30 rollouts per task, indicated a consistent pattern: policies trained on unfiltered data or data collected without health feedback achieved high Task Completion Rates (TCRs) but exhibited a substantial gap to Safe Task Completion Rates (STCRs). For instance, on the "Wipe Countertop" task, the unfiltered policy achieved 100% TCR but only 35% STCR, signifying that task completion often involved unsafe behaviors. In contrast, policies trained on data influenced by OOPSIEVERSE's damage signal (either through real-time feedback during collection or post-hoc filtering) significantly improved STCRs with minimal reductions in TCRs. Visualizations for the "Shelve Cereal Box" task showed that policies trained on health-filtered data produced trajectories that avoided fragile objects, unlike the unfiltered policies. This suggests that OOPSIEVERSE provides sufficiently accurate feedback to guide human operators and enable automated curation for safer imitation learning.

#### 4.2. OOPSIEVERSE for Reinforcement Learning (RL)

The research explored DAMAGESIM's damage signal as a reward component for training safe policies via reinforcement learning. The task reward was augmented with a damage penalty: $r(s_t) = R_{task}(s_t) - w (\sum_k d_{DEM_k}(t))$. Three RL settings were evaluated: Diffusion Steering (DSRL) on a pre-trained flow-matching policy for "Shelve Cereal Box," fine-tuning a pre-trained Gaussian policy for "Move Cup of Water," and training an RL policy from scratch for "Place Plate."

Results demonstrated that DSRL improved safe task completion on "Shelve Cereal Box" from 13% to 33%. Fine-tuning a Gaussian policy for "Move Cup of Water" led to a larger gain, increasing safe success from 20% to 100%. For "Place Plate," training from scratch with DAMAGESIM's damage penalty achieved over 80% safe task completion, compared to nearly 0% when using only the task reward. These findings indicate that OOPSIEVERSE provides a practical reward signal for developing safe RL policies, both by refining existing policies and training new ones. Training curves visually confirmed that policies trained with the damage penalty maintained stable task completion while progressively reducing incurred damage.

#### 4.3. Benchmarking Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Policies on OOPSIEVERSE

The framework's capability to evaluate the safety of modern manipulation policies was demonstrated by benchmarking NVIDIA GR00T, a state-of-the-art VLA model, without task-specific fine-tuning. Evaluations were conducted on various tasks from both OmniGibson and RoboCasa, measuring Task Completion (C), Safe Completion (SC), and Average Environment Health.

The results consistently revealed a gap between task performance and safety for GR00T. Even when achieving high task success, the model frequently incurred damage, leading to substantially lower safe success rates and degraded environment health. For example, in "open microwave door," GR00T achieved 92% task completion but only 4% safe completion. Similarly, "Turn On Stove" had 88% task completion but 8% safe completion, with average environment health near 0. An additional evaluation of the pi0 VLA model on RoboCasa tasks showed 17.5% completion and 0% safe completion. These findings highlight that standard task-completion metrics alone are insufficient for assessing VLA policy performance, and OOPSIEVERSE effectively exposes their safety deficiencies.

#### 4.4. Sim-to-Real Transfer from OOPSIEVERSE

The transferability of safety improvements from simulation to real-world robots was tested. Policies trained in OmniGibson for "Shelve Cereal Box" and "Pour Water" using "without_live_feedback" (damage-unaware) and "filtered_episodes" (damage-aware) methods were directly transferred to a Franka Panda robot. Qualitative safety outcomes (observable object damage like breakage, spills, or unsafe contact) were reported.

Aggregated across both tasks (10 trials per method per task), the "filtered_episodes" policy achieved a 75% task completion rate, 65% safe completion rate, and an unsafe behavior rate of 15%. In contrast, the "without_live_feedback" policy achieved 70% task completion, 5% safe completion, and a 75% unsafe behavior rate. This represented a 60% reduction in unsafe behaviors for the damage-aware policies while maintaining comparable task performance. Examples included the damage-unaware policy spilling water on a laptop or toppling fragile objects, whereas the damage-aware policy exhibited safer behaviors. This indicates that the explicit damage signals provided by OOPSIEVERSE can lead to demonstrably safer policies on physical hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

OOPSIEVERSE presents a significant contribution to the field of robotics by addressing the long-standing challenge of integrating physical safety into robot manipulation training and evaluation in simulation. Its core significance lies in providing a unified, physically-grounded, and task-agnostic framework for damage awareness, which was previously lacking in general-purpose robotic simulators and benchmarks.

The potential impacts are substantial:

1. **Enabling Safer Robot Deployment:** By explicitly modeling and quantifying damage, OOPSIEVERSE allows researchers to develop and validate robot policies that are intrinsically safer. This is crucial for the deployment of robots in human-centric environments, such as homes and workplaces, where damage to objects or the robot itself is unacceptable.
2. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Safety:** The demonstrated sim-to-real transfer capabilities show that safety-aware policies trained within OOPSIEVERSE translate into genuinely safer behaviors on physical robots. This directly addresses a critical challenge in robotics, moving beyond task performance alone to ensure real-world reliability and trustworthiness.
3. **Advancing Research in Safe Reinforcement Learning and Imitation Learning:** OOPSIEVERSE provides concrete, measurable damage signals that can be directly used as rewards or constraints in reinforcement learning and for data curation in imitation learning. This opens new avenues for developing algorithms that optimize for both task success and safety, fostering a new generation of more responsible AI for robotics.
4. **Robust Benchmarking for Advanced Policies:** The framework serves as a valuable benchmark for evaluating state-of-the-art robot policies, including Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, for their safety characteristics. It reveals critical safety gaps that purely task-completion-based evaluations might miss, pushing the development of more robust and reliable generalist robot systems.
5. **Standardization and Accessibility:** By providing an open-source framework and benchmark, OOPSIEVERSE offers a standardized platform for systematic research on safe robot manipulation. Its implementation in widely used simulators (OmniGibson and RoboCasa) broadens accessibility, allowing a wider community of researchers to integrate damage awareness into their work.
6. **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** The real-time damage feedback mechanism in DAMAGESIM can also guide human teleoperators in collecting inherently safer demonstrations, which is a key step for human-in-the-loop robot learning and interaction.

Overall, OOPSIEVERSE moves beyond the artificial safety of conventional simulations by providing tangible, quantifiable measures of physical harm. This shift is expected to accelerate the development of highly capable and reliably safe robots, fostering greater trust and enabling their broader integration into society.

#### 5.1. Limitations

The authors acknowledge several limitations inherent in the current iteration of OOPSIEVERSE:

1. **Approximation of Physical Processes:** The damage evaluation models within DAMAGESIM are approximations of complex real-world physical processes (e.g., mechanical fracture mechanics, detailed burning effects, precise liquid dynamics). While these approximations are demonstrated to be sufficient for distinguishing safe from unsafe behaviors in robotic tasks, they may not perfectly reflect the exact magnitude or nature of damage that would occur in the real world.
2. **Manual Parameter Annotation:** The current computation of damage requires manual annotation of per-link or per-object parameters (e.g., material yield limits for mechanical damage, temperature thresholds for thermal damage, fluid resistance levels). This manual specification process can be labor-intensive, especially when scaling to a vast number of diverse objects. The authors suggest that this process could potentially be automated in the future by leveraging common sense knowledge derived from large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs).
