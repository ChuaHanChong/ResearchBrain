# SAFE: Multitask Failure Detection for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Gu et al. developed SAFE, an efficient multitask failure detection system for generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which leverages internal VLA features and functional Conformal Prediction. The system demonstrated superior failure detection performance and real-time computational efficiency on unseen robotic manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world experiments.

## Problem

- Generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models struggle with limited out-of-the-box success rates (30-60%) on novel, unseen tasks, hindering their safe deployment.
- Existing failure detection methods are either task-specific, inefficient for real-time robot control, or too restrictive for truly generalist policies.
- A critical gap exists for an efficient, multitask failure detection mechanism that can generalize zero-shot to unseen tasks and environments.

## Method

- **SAFE** extracts ==internal hidden state features== from Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which analysis shows contain task-generic success/failure information.
- A lightweight neural network (==MLP== or ==LSTM==) is trained to map these features to a scalar failure score, designed for efficiency and zero-shot generalization.
- ==Functional Conformal Prediction== establishes a principled, time-varying threshold to confidently flag failures in real-time, controlling the false positive rate.

## Results

- **SAFE** consistently outperformed or matched state-of-the-art baselines in ROC-AUC across various VLA models and benchmarks, demonstrating superior failure discrimination on unseen tasks in both simulation and real-world setups.
- The method introduced minimal computational overhead, adding less than **1ms** to VLA inference time, ensuring practical real-time robotic deployment.
- Utilizing functional Conformal Prediction, SAFE achieved proactive failure detection, often identifying impending failures earlier than average human observation time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models' internal latent feature spaces exhibit a distinct, task-generic 'failure zone' where failed rollouts tend to cluster.
> - This inherent separability within VLA features allows a simple, learned model to effectively predict task failure, even generalizing to unseen tasks.
> - Functional Conformal Prediction provides a principled way to establish dynamic failure thresholds, enabling real-time detection with quantifiable confidence guarantees.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in large-scale machine learning, particularly with Vision-Language Models (VLMs), have led to the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These generalist robotic policies, such as OpenVLA, π0, π0-FAST, RT-1, and RT-2, are capable of understanding diverse semantic concepts and executing a wide range of manipulation tasks based on natural language instructions. By leveraging large-scale pre-training on vast robotic datasets, VLAs aim to achieve unprecedented generalization capabilities in robotics.

However, a significant challenge remains: while VLAs perform admirably (80-90% success rates) on tasks they have seen during training, their performance drops substantially (30-60% success rates) when confronted with *unseen* tasks or novel environments out-of-the-box. This inherent limitation in generalization poses a critical barrier to their safe and reliable deployment in real-world scenarios. The core problem this paper addresses is the lack of effective and efficient failure detection mechanisms for these generalist VLAs.

The existing landscape of failure detection in robotics broadly falls into a few categories:
- **Task-Specific Failure Detectors:** Most traditional methods are trained and evaluated for specific tasks. This approach is impractical for generalist VLAs that are designed to handle a multitude of tasks, many of which may be novel during deployment. Re-training or calibrating a new detector for every new task is infeasible.
- **Task-Generic but Inefficient Methods:** Some recent works have explored task-generic failure detection. Methods like STAC measure action consistency by sampling multiple actions, while others query large VLMs. While generic, these often incur significant inference overhead, making them unsuitable for real-time robot control where timely intervention is crucial.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection:** Approaches based on OOD detection treat deviations from "normal" (successful) executions as failures. However, for generalist VLAs, encountering a novel task that is simply "different" from training data should not automatically be classified as a failure if the robot successfully completes it. This makes OOD detection overly restrictive for truly generalist policies.
- **Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) from LLMs/VLMs:** Inspired by hallucination detection in large language models (LLMs), some approaches leverage token-level uncertainty or semantic similarity in generated actions as proxies for potential failure. These are often training-free but might lack the direct predictive power needed for robust failure detection in robotics.

This paper directly addresses the critical gap of *efficient multitask failure detection for generalist VLAs* that can generalize *zero-shot* to unseen tasks. It distinguishes itself by proposing a method, SAFE, that is both effective in detecting failures across diverse tasks and computationally light enough for real-time robotic operation, leveraging internal VLA features rather than computationally intensive sampling or external model queries. This positions SAFE as a significant step towards bridging the gap between highly capable VLA policies and their reliable, safe deployment in complex, dynamic real-world environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enhance the safety and reliability of generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models when deployed in real-world settings. Given that VLAs, despite their impressive capabilities, still exhibit limited success rates (30-60%) on novel, unseen tasks, the authors identify a critical need for a robust mechanism to detect impending failures. The core problem statement is framed as the "multitask failure detection problem," where a failure detector must be trained on a diverse set of tasks and subsequently generalize to detect failures in completely unseen tasks and novel environments without any additional fine-tuning or data collection.

The key motivations driving this research are:

1. **Ensuring Robot Safety and Reliability:** In real-world robot deployment, unforeseen failures can lead to undesirable outcomes, including damage to the robot, its environment, or even injuries to nearby humans. A timely alert from a failure detector can enable the robot to stop, backtrack, or request human intervention, thereby mitigating risks and improving overall operational safety.
2. **Addressing VLA Generalization Gaps:** While VLAs are "generalist" in principle, their out-of-the-box performance on truly novel tasks is often insufficient for autonomous deployment. A failure detector acts as a crucial safeguard, allowing these powerful but imperfect policies to interact with their environments more safely.
3. **Inefficiency of Existing Methods for Generalist Policies:** Current failure detection methods are largely designed for specialist policies or require significant computational overhead (e.g., sampling multiple actions, querying large VLMs). Such approaches are impractical for real-time control of generalist VLAs that constantly encounter novel situations and demand immediate responses. The need is for an *efficient* and *scalable* solution that can keep pace with VLA inference speeds.
4. **Leveraging Internal VLA Knowledge:** The authors hypothesize and empirically demonstrate through t-SNE visualizations (Fig. 1) that VLA models, in their internal feature spaces, already possess high-level, task-generic knowledge about task success and failure. Failed rollouts, regardless of the specific task, tend to occupy a distinct "failure zone" in the latent space. This insight motivates the design of a detector that directly leverages these informative internal features.

By proposing SAFE, the authors aim to build an efficient, multitask failure detector that can accurately predict task failures in a timely manner, zero-shot, on unseen tasks, thereby making the deployment of generalist VLA models more practical and secure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, SAFE (Scalable Failure Estimation), is built upon a crucial empirical insight derived from analyzing the internal feature space of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

#### 3.1. Visual Analysis on VLA Latent Space

The authors first investigate the latent features extracted from various VLA models (e.g., π0-FAST, OpenVLA) using t-SNE dimensionality reduction. They observe that:
- Features corresponding to failed rollouts tend to cluster together in a distinct "failure zone" within the VLA's internal feature space (Fig. 1a).
- Crucially, this "failure zone" remains consistent *across different tasks*, even with varied instructions, objects, and environments (Fig. 1b). This suggests that VLAs learn a task-generic representation of success and failure.
- During a task execution, features from a successful rollout consistently stay outside this "failure zone," while features from a failing rollout evolve over time, eventually stepping into this zone when the robot encounters an error (Fig. 1c).
This observation forms the foundation for SAFE: if the VLA's internal features contain such separable information, a simple, learned model operating on these features can be effective for failure detection.

#### 3.2. Failure Detection by Feature Probing (SAFE Design)

SAFE is designed to learn from and predict failure based on the VLA's internal hidden state vectors (embeddings) from its final layer, before action decoding. The input to SAFE at time *t* is the sequence of VLA features up to that point, $e_{0:t} = \{e_1, \ldots, e_t\}$. The output is a scalar score $s_t$ indicating the likelihood of failure.
The authors explore two backbone designs for SAFE:
- **SAFE-MLP:** A simple multi-layer perceptron (MLP) takes each feature $e_t$ independently, projects it to a scalar using a function $g(\cdot)$, applies a sigmoid $\sigma(\cdot)$, and then accumulates these scores over time: $f_{MLP}(e_{0:t}) = \sum_{\tau=1}^t \sigma(g(e_{\tau}))$. This results in a score that increases with time and severity of failure. The MLP is trained using an L1 loss, which encourages higher scores for failed rollouts and lower scores for successful ones across all timesteps: $L_{MLP} = \sum_i [y_i \sum_t (t - s_t) + (1 - y_i) \sum_t s_t]$.
- **SAFE-LSTM:** An LSTM model sequentially processes the input feature stream $e_{0:t}$ and then projects its final hidden state to a scalar score using a sigmoid function: $f_{LSTM}(e_{0:t}) = \sigma(LSTM(e_{0:t}))$. This allows for temporal dependencies to be modeled. It is trained with a binary cross-entropy loss: $L_{LSTM} = \sum_i \sum_t [y_i \log(s_t) + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - s_t)]$.
Both models are intentionally kept simple (1-2 layers) to prevent overfitting and promote generalization to unseen tasks. Training uses trajectory-level annotations ($y_i=1$ for failure, $y_i=0$ for success) without requiring precise failure timesteps. Inverse class frequency weighting and L2 regularization are used to handle class imbalance and prevent overfitting.

#### 3.3. Threshold Selection by Conformal Prediction

To determine when a predicted score $s_t$ constitutes a failure (i.e., when $s_t > \delta_t$), SAFE employs the functional Conformal Prediction (CP) framework. CP provides a principled, distribution-free method to set a time-varying threshold $\delta_t$.
- A one-sided time-varying prediction band $C_\alpha = \{[-\infty, \text{upper}_t]\}$ is constructed, where $\text{upper}_t = \mu_t + h_t$ ($\mu_t$ is a time-varying mean, $h_t$ is a bandwidth).
- This band is calibrated on *successful rollouts* from a hold-out calibration set ($D_{eval-seen}$).
- For a user-specified significance level $\alpha \in (0,1)$, CP guarantees that for any new *successful* rollout, its $s_t$ will remain below $\text{upper}_t$ for all times $t$ with probability $1-\alpha$.
- Conversely, if $s_t$ of a test rollout exceeds $\text{upper}_t$, a failure flag is confidently raised with a confidence of $1-\alpha$. This allows for a theoretically grounded control over the false positive rate.

**Approach Summary:** SAFE extracts VLA internal features, learns a simple neural network (MLP or LSTM) to map these features to a failure score based on successful and failed rollouts, and then uses functional Conformal Prediction to set a robust, time-varying threshold for real-time failure detection. The method is designed for efficiency and zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by Gu et al. are extensive, spanning multiple state-of-the-art VLA models (OpenVLA, π0, π0-FAST, π*0), diverse simulation benchmarks (LIBERO-10, SimplerEnv), and real-world robotic setups (Franka Emika Panda, WidowX). SAFE is compared against a comprehensive suite of baselines, including uncertainty quantification methods from LLM literature (Token Uncertainty, Sample Consistency), embedding distance-based methods, learned OOD detectors (LogpZO, RND), and action consistency methods (STAC).

The main findings and results are:

1. **Superior Failure Discrimination (ROC-AUC):**
 - **Consistent SOTA Performance:** SAFE (both SAFE-MLP and SAFE-LSTM) consistently outperforms or performs on par with all baselines across all simulation and real-world benchmarks, especially on *unseen tasks*.
 - **Simulation Benchmarks:** On average across simulation benchmarks, SAFE-MLP and SAFE-LSTM outperform the best baselines by 4-5% ROC-AUC on unseen tasks. For instance, on OpenVLA+LIBERO, SAFE-MLP achieves 73.47% ROC-AUC on unseen tasks, significantly higher than the next best, Cosine dist. k-NN (69.45%). Similarly, on π0-FAST+LIBERO, SAFE-LSTM achieves 84.48%, comparable to the best baseline, STAC (85.31%), but with vastly superior efficiency.
 - **Real-World Experiments:** In real-world Franka and WidowX experiments, SAFE-MLP demonstrates the best performance (88.42% ROC-AUC on unseen tasks for OpenVLA+WidowX, and 64.16% for π0-FAST+Franka).
 - **Baseline Performance Insights:** Token uncertainty methods (Max prob., Avg prob., Max entropy, Avg entropy) show poor performance, aligning with findings in LLM UQ literature. Sample consistency methods (e.g., Action total var., Cluster entropy) and STAC perform well, but their requirement for multiple action samples makes them computationally expensive and impractical for real-time control. Embedding distance methods perform reasonably well, validating the authors' insight about VLA features containing task-relevant information, but SAFE further improves upon this by learning more abstract representations.

2. **Favorable Trade-off between Accuracy and Timeliness:**
 - Using functional Conformal Prediction (CP) to set a time-varying threshold, SAFE achieves a superior balance between balanced accuracy (Bal-acc) and average detection time (T-det). As shown in Fig. 4, SAFE's curves are positioned towards the top-left corner, indicating high accuracy and proactive detection.
 - SAFE can detect failures significantly earlier than when a human might typically identify them (before the ground truth average failure time). This "forecasting" ability is crucial for enabling early intervention and preventing the robot from getting stuck or causing harm.

3. **Alignment with Human Judgment:**
 - Qualitative evaluations (Fig. 5, Fig. 6b-d) demonstrate that SAFE's detected failure events align well with human intuition. Examples include imprecise insertions, oscillatory movements, missed grasps, policy freezing, and object slips. For successful rollouts, failure scores plateau after task completion.

4. **Computational Efficiency:**
 - SAFE's design, using only 1-2 layer MLPs or LSTMs, results in minimal computational overhead. It adds less than 1ms (or <1%) to the VLA's inference time, making it highly practical for real-time robotic deployment. This contrasts sharply with sample-based baselines that can increase inference time by 150-220%.

5. **Feature Space Analysis Validation:**
 - The detailed analysis of VLA latent spaces (Fig. 1, Fig. 8) reinforces the central hypothesis that VLA internal features do capture high-level, task-generic information about task success and failure. While the exact structure of the "failure zone" might vary across different VLAs and tasks, the separability generally holds, allowing SAFE to learn effective detectors.

In summary, the results firmly establish SAFE as a highly effective, efficient, and generalizable solution for multitask failure detection in Vision-Language-Action models, addressing a critical safety and reliability concern for their real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of SAFE represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning and the practical deployment of generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Enhanced Safety and Reliability for Generalist Robots:** This work directly tackles one of the most pressing challenges for real-world robotic systems: ensuring safe operation, especially when policies might fail on novel tasks. By providing a timely and accurate failure detection mechanism that generalizes zero-shot to unseen tasks, SAFE enables proactive intervention. This means robots can stop, backtrack, or request human help *before* catastrophic errors occur, minimizing potential damage, hazards, and downtime. This is crucial for applications in manufacturing, logistics, service robotics, and household automation.

2. **Practical Deployment of VLAs:** Generalist VLAs hold immense promise for revolutionizing robotics, but their limited out-of-the-box success rates on unseen tasks have been a bottleneck. SAFE significantly lowers this barrier by providing a crucial safety net. It makes the deployment of VLAs more practical and trustworthy in dynamic, unstructured environments where encountering novel situations is the norm. The ability to deploy a single failure detector that works across many tasks, rather than training one per task, is a paradigm shift for scaling robot capabilities.

3. **Computational Efficiency for Real-time Control:** Unlike many existing task-generic failure detection methods that incur high computational overhead (e.g., requiring multiple action samples or large VLM queries), SAFE is exceptionally efficient. Its minimal inference time addition (<1% of VLA inference) ensures that real-time robot control is not compromised. This efficiency is paramount for responsive and fluid robotic operation in real-world scenarios.

4. **Leveraging Internal Model Knowledge:** The core insight that VLAs' internal latent features contain generic, high-level information about task success and failure is highly impactful. It suggests that advanced generalist models are not just "black boxes" but encapsulate valuable, interpretable signals regarding their performance and potential pitfalls. This understanding can inspire further research into model interpretability and self-monitoring capabilities in complex AI systems.

5. **Foundation for Future Robot Autonomy:** SAFE serves as a critical stepping stone for more advanced robot autonomy. With reliable failure detection, future work can focus on:
 - **Automated Recovery Policies:** Triggering pre-trained fallback policies or learned recovery behaviors when a failure is detected, enabling robots to self-correct.
 - **Interactive Learning and Human-in-the-Loop Systems:** Facilitating more intelligent human-robot collaboration, where the robot can proactively ask for help or learn from human interventions specifically when it's most needed.
 - **Policy Improvement:** Using failure signals to guide adaptive learning processes or fine-tuning of VLA policies, potentially through methods like activation steering, to mitigate recurring failure modes.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as noted by authors):**
While impactful, the authors acknowledge limitations, primarily focusing on manipulation tasks. Future work could explore generalization across different embodiments, modalities beyond vision-language (e.g., haptics, force), sim2real transfer robustness, or applications in action-less video scenarios. Additionally, leveraging information from multiple internal layers of VLAs, rather than just the last layer, presents a promising avenue for extracting richer failure signals, though this might increase complexity and reduce transferability across diverse VLA architectures. The potential for extending to online or adaptive conformal prediction is also noted as a future direction.

In conclusion, SAFE significantly contributes to making generalist VLA models safer and more deployable, shifting the focus from simply achieving tasks to ensuring their robust and reliable execution in dynamic and unpredictable environments.
