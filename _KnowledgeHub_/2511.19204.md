# Reference-Free Sampling-Based Model Predictive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework that enables robots to autonomously discover diverse and complex locomotion patterns on standard CPU hardware without pre-defined gaits or extensive training. The framework successfully generated agile behaviors such as velocity-adaptive trotting, galloping, jumping, and handstands for quadrupedal robots, achieving real-time performance with a reduced number of samples.

## Problem

- Many advanced robotic control methods rely on handcrafted priors like gait patterns, predefined contact sequences, or intricate reward functions, limiting autonomy and adaptability.
- State-of-the-art sampling-based MPC methods often require significant computational resources, such as GPU acceleration and thousands of parallel rollouts, for real-time operation.
- Generalization challenges exist, as current approaches often struggle to adapt to novel environments, tasks, or robot morphologies without extensive re-engineering or retraining.

## Method

- A ==Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)== control framework was developed to operate in a receding-horizon loop, focusing on reference-free motion discovery.
- A cubic Hermite spline parameterization was introduced to jointly sample joint position and velocity control points, enhancing exploration efficiency and dynamic consistency for CPU-based simulation.
- A diffusion-inspired noise annealing scheme was implemented, adapting exploration variance across internal planning iterations and along the prediction horizon, alongside a 'best trajectory tracking' mechanism for execution stability.

## Results

- The Go2 quadruped robot demonstrated robust, velocity-adaptive gaits (trotting, galloping) and performed vertical jumps on real hardware, achieving a peak height of **0.7** m.
- Complex behaviors like backflips for the Go2 and walking for the G1 humanoid were successfully discovered in simulation, showing generalization across different robot morphologies.
- The framework achieved real-time performance at 50 Hz on a Mac Studio M3 CPU, using 30 sampled trajectories for the Go2 quadruped and 70 for the G1 humanoid in simulation, showcasing high computational efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex, diverse locomotion patterns, including velocity-adaptive gaits, jumping, and dynamic handstands, can emerge autonomously from high-level cost objectives without explicit gait or contact references.
> - Real-time MPC for complex robotic systems is achievable on standard CPU hardware using significantly fewer samples (e.g., 30 trajectories for a quadruped) compared to typical state-of-the-art methods.
> - The joint optimization of position and velocity control points via cubic Hermite splines improves dynamic consistency and leads to higher success rates and lower costs compared to position-only splines.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot control presents a persistent challenge in balancing solution quality with computational efficiency. The current landscape of advanced robotic control broadly encompasses three main paradigms: learning-based methods, trajectory optimization (TO), and sampling-based approaches.

**Learning-based methods**, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), have demonstrated the capacity to generate complex and agile movements for robots. However, their practical application is often constrained by requirements for extensive offline training data, which can limit their adaptability and generalization to novel environments or tasks not encountered during training. While efforts are ongoing to enhance sample efficiency, RL typically remains too computationally intensive for online adaptation. Furthermore, the reliance on simplified models to accelerate training can impede the transfer of learned policies from simulation to physical robots (sim-to-real gap). RL methods also frequently necessitate the engineering of intricate reward functions, incorporating elements such as phase clocks or air-time penalties, to induce desired contact behaviors.

**Trajectory optimization (TO)**, in contrast, leverages derivative information to compute high-quality control solutions. This approach requires accurate and reliable gradients, which can be challenging to obtain or maintain in contact-rich robotic scenarios. Consequently, many TO methods often rely on predefined contact sequences, limiting their flexibility. While contact-implicit TO methods have emerged to address this limitation by removing the need for predefined contact schedules, enabling demonstrations of complex whole-body behaviors, they often employ simplified contact models or approximations to maintain computational tractability. These simplifications can introduce discrepancies with real-world dynamics, leading to approximate solutions. Additionally, contact-implicit TO still typically requires carefully designed cost functions to guide the system towards effective contact sequences.

**Sampling-based methods** offer an alternative by performing derivative-free optimization, which is inherently suitable for parallel computation and well-suited for problems involving non-smooth dynamics, such such as those with contact. Early naive sampling approaches, like random search, exhibited limitations in sample efficiency and convergence speed. However, advancements, particularly Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, have improved performance through sophisticated sampling strategies and trajectory averaging. Despite these advancements, many existing sampling-based MPC methods for legged robots still rely on gait references derived from heuristics, predefined foot swing trajectories, or explicit contact sequences. Furthermore, state-of-the-art MPPI implementations often necessitate high-performance computing resources, such as GPU acceleration, to achieve real-time control through thousands of parallel trajectory rollouts.

This paper contributes to the sampling-based MPC landscape by proposing a framework that specifically aims to address the aforementioned limitations of existing approaches. It seeks to eliminate the reliance on handcrafted gait patterns and high computational resources, positioning itself as a method for emergent locomotion discovery that operates efficiently on standard CPU hardware with a reduced number of samples, thereby challenging the established paradigm of explicit motion referencing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework that facilitates the discovery of complex robotic locomotion patterns without relying on predefined gait patterns, contact sequences, or extensive offline pre-training. This framework is designed to operate in real-time on standard CPU hardware, minimizing the computational resources typically required by other advanced control methods.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current robotic control strategies:

1. **Dependence on Handcrafted Priors:** Many existing methods, including both reinforcement learning (RL) and trajectory optimization (TO), frequently require substantial human engineering in the form of reference gait patterns, predefined contact schedules, or finely tuned reward functions. This reliance restricts the autonomy of robot control systems, limits the diversity of discoverable behaviors, and reduces their adaptability to unforeseen environmental conditions or task variations. The authors aim to overcome this by enabling emergent behaviors purely from high-level objectives, allowing the robot to autonomously discover optimal contact-making and contact-breaking strategies.

2. **Computational Intensity:** While sampling-based MPC methods, such as MPPI, have shown promise, many state-of-the-art implementations demand significant computational power, often requiring GPU acceleration and thousands of parallel trajectory rollouts to achieve real-time performance. This hardware requirement can limit accessibility and deployability on robots with constrained onboard computing resources. A key motivation is to achieve real-time control on standard CPU hardware with a reduced number of sampled trajectories, making the approach more efficient and broadly applicable.

3. **Generalization and Adaptability Challenges:** Learning-based methods can struggle with generalization to tasks or environments not explicitly included in their training data. Traditional trajectory optimization, while precise, can be rigid due to its reliance on derivative information in non-smooth contact scenarios and its common use of predefined contact sequences. The authors are motivated to develop a flexible framework that can generalize across different robot morphologies and tasks without significant re-engineering or retraining, demonstrated by testing on both quadrupedal and humanoid robots.

4. **Dynamic Consistency and Exploration:** In sampling-based control, the choice of search space parameterization significantly impacts exploration efficiency and the dynamic consistency of generated trajectories. Prior approaches often sample only position references, potentially leading to inconsistencies. The motivation includes developing a parameterization that jointly samples position and velocity control points, enhancing both exploration capabilities and the dynamic realism of generated reference trajectories, while also preventing joint limit overshoots.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Enable autonomous discovery of diverse, dynamically consistent locomotion behaviors (e.g., trotting, galloping, jumping, handstands) purely through high-level cost function objectives.
- Achieve real-time control efficiency on standard CPU hardware with a low number of sampled trajectories.
- Develop a cubic Hermite spline parameterization that jointly optimizes position and velocity control points for improved exploration and dynamic consistency.
- Demonstrate the framework's versatility and adaptability across different robot platforms (quadruped, humanoid) and complex tasks without requiring reference tracking or offline pre-training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed control framework is built upon the **Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)** control scheme, operating within a receding-horizon loop. At each control step, MPPI refines a nominal control sequence, generates perturbed trajectories, evaluates them against a cost objective, and updates the nominal sequence through importance-weighted averaging. The framework introduces several enhancements to the standard MPPI methodology to achieve reference-free, efficient, and robust control.

#### 3.1. A. Search-Space Parameterization

The control strategy distinguishes between high-level trajectory exploration and low-level torque regulation. Instead of sampling directly in torque space, which often requires massive parallel GPU simulation, the method samples reference trajectories in joint space. These joint-space references are then tracked by a low-level Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller that generates torque commands. This decoupling allows for efficient CPU-based simulation.

A central component is the **cubic Hermite spline parameterization**. For each actuated degree of freedom, the control sequence is parameterized by $K$ nodes. Each node $k$ has two optimization variables: joint position $\theta^q_k = q(t_k)$ and joint velocity $\theta^v_k = \dot{q}(t_k)$. The joint trajectory between nodes is reconstructed using standard cubic Hermite interpolation functions. This joint position and velocity parameterization provides local control over the shape of the generated reference trajectories, leading to dynamically more consistent PD targets compared to position-only splines. To prevent joint limit overshoots, a derivative clamping mechanism is applied, bounding per-node derivatives based on the distance to the nearest joint limit. The floating-base motion of the robot is not directly parameterized but emerges from the forward dynamics rollout and contact interactions.

#### 3.2. B. Noise Annealing

The framework incorporates a **diffusion-inspired annealing scheme** for structuring the Gaussian noise used to perturb the nominal control sequence. This scheme adapts the variance of exploration both across internal planning iterations and along the prediction horizon.

1. **Trajectory-level annealing**: The variance of exploration is reduced over successive internal iterations ($i = I, \dots, 1$) at each control step. This transitions the optimization from broad exploration in early iterations to finer exploitation as the nominal trajectory is refined.
2. **Action-level annealing**: Exploration is increased for control points further in the horizon ($k = 0, \dots, K-1$). Control decisions closer to execution receive smaller perturbations to maintain stability, while those further out are explored more aggressively as they have more opportunities for refinement in future planning steps.

These two effects are combined isotropically into a per-node covariance matrix $\Sigma^i_{\theta_k}$, which is precomputed to ensure larger variance for later nodes and earlier iterations, and smaller variance for near-term nodes and later iterations.

#### 3.3. C. Algorithm Description

The overall MPPI algorithm proceeds as follows:
1. **Initialization**: A nominal sequence of joint position ($\theta^q$) and velocity ($\theta^v$) spline control points is initialized (e.g., from a stable standing configuration for positions and zeros for velocities).
2. **Sampling and Rollout**: At each control step, the algorithm generates $N$ perturbed control point sequences by sampling around the nominal points using the annealed noise schedule. These perturbed sequences are interpolated into candidate trajectories. Each candidate trajectory is then rolled out in a physics simulator (Simple), yielding simulated robot states over the prediction horizon.
3. **Cost Evaluation**: A modular cost function evaluates each trajectory. This cost function comprises running costs (penalizing deviations in base position/orientation, joint angles, contact velocities, and contact forces) and a terminal cost (encouraging velocity tracking via base displacement). Task-specific behaviors are achieved by adjusting the weights of these cost components without altering the algorithmic framework.
4. **Weighting and Update**: Importance weights ($\omega_n$) are assigned to each trajectory based on its cost ($S_n$) using an exponential weighting scheme (softmax smoothing with min-max normalization), where a temperature parameter $\lambda$ controls selectiveness. The nominal control sequence is then updated as a weighted average of all candidate trajectories. New nominal control points are extracted by resampling the updated trajectory at the spline node times.
5. **Execution**: The executed torque command is computed by a low-level PD controller, mapping the best computed joint position and velocity targets from the nominal trajectory to actuator torques, which are then clipped to actuator limits.

#### 3.4. D. Best Trajectory Tracking

A distinction is made between the nominal trajectory ($\tau_0$), which evolves through importance-weighted averaging, and the *best* trajectory ($\tau_{\text{best}}$), from which actions are ultimately executed. This ensures that the robot always executes actions from a verified, fully simulated trajectory rather than an untested weighted mixture. This strategy aims to provide safety guarantees and maintain monotonic performance improvement during iterative refinement.

#### 3.5. E. Real-time State Prediction and Warm-Starting

To mitigate the effects of computation delays in real-time MPC, the method predicts the robot's state at the moment the optimization is completed. This predicted state serves as the starting point for the subsequent optimization instance. The previously best trajectory ($\tau_{\text{best}}$) is then shifted forward by the number of actions already executed during the delay, and its tail is padded. This warm-starting strategy maintains solution continuity across receding-horizon steps and avoids re-optimizing from scratch. Unlike approaches that constrain initial actions across all rollouts, this method allows all samples to explore freely from the start of their planning horizons, maximizing the effective use of the limited sample budget.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed framework through hardware experiments on the Go2 quadruped and simulation studies, including validation on the G1 humanoid. The findings demonstrate the framework's ability to discover diverse locomotion strategies without reference tracking, achieve real-time performance on CPU, and adapt across various platforms and tasks.

#### 4.1. A. Motion Discovery

The framework consistently enables the discovery of complex motion strategies from high-level goals without explicit gait references.

1. **Velocity-Adaptive Gaits (Go2 Quadruped)**:
 - **Standing Still (0 m/s)**: The robot adopted a stable four-feet-in-contact posture, maintaining balance through leg adjustments.
 - **Trotting (0.5 m/s)**: A trotting gait emerged, characterized by alternating pairs of diagonal legs in contact.
 - **Galloping (2.0 m/s)**: As the desired velocity increased, the robot transitioned to a more dynamic gait with extended flight phases, resembling a galloping or bounding pattern.
 - **Adaptation**: The system demonstrated smooth transitions between these gaits purely by varying the desired forward velocity in the cost function, without requiring specific gait commands or contact sequences. An extended planning horizon of 0.9 seconds was found beneficial for locomotion discovery.

2. **Robustness (Go2 Quadruped)**:
 - The Go2 quadruped exhibited robustness to physical disturbances during 10 minutes of continuous operation.
 - It resisted direct physical pushes from various directions and manual pulls on individual legs, generating corrective stepping motions to maintain balance.
 - When the supporting mattress was rotated or translated, the robot performed turning or walking motions to restore its original target yaw angle or position.
 - In an extreme test, the robot, when lifted, rotated by over 90 degrees, and placed back down, walked to recover its target pose.

3. **Jumping (Go2 Quadruped)**:
 - **Vertical Jump**: By specifying a terminal cost to achieve a target base height increase (from 0.325 m to 0.7 m), the robot autonomously discovered a complete jumping strategy. This involved a multi-phase maneuver: crouching, rapid leg extension for launch, body orientation control during flight, and controlled landing absorption. This behavior was successfully validated on the real Go2 robot, achieving a peak height of 0.7 m.
 - **Backflip (Simulation)**: In simulation, by targeting a 180-degree pitch orientation at the highest point, the robot discovered a backflip maneuver. This involved a coordinated crouch, explosive takeoff, mid-air rotation, and precise landing absorption to complete a full 360-degree rotation from a 0.5 m elevated platform.

4. **Dynamic Handstand Balancing (Simulation)**:
 - When commanded to achieve an inverted pose (45-degree pitch angle) from a standard quadrupedal stance, the robot executed a dynamic swing maneuver to transition into a handstand. The controller then identified stabilizing strategies, continuously repositioning legs and adjusting contact patterns to maintain balance for approximately 1 second with low orientation error.

5. **Humanoid Locomotion (G1 Humanoid Simulation)**:
 - The framework demonstrated generalization by successfully discovering a walking gait on the G1 humanoid robot in simulation, despite its different morphology and increased degrees of freedom (37 DoFs compared to Go2's 12 DoFs).
 - The same high-level cost function and algorithmic framework were applied without modification.
 - While effective, this humanoid experiment required an increase from 30 to 70 parallel sample trajectories, which prevented real-time execution in the current setup but served as a proof-of-concept for cross-platform motion discovery.

#### 4.2. B. Real-time Performance and Sample Efficiency

The method achieved real-time performance on standard CPU hardware (Mac Studio M3) at 50 Hz for the Go2 quadruped using as few as 30 sampled trajectories. For the G1 humanoid in simulation, 70 samples were required, demonstrating computational efficiency compared to other state-of-the-art MPPI methods often requiring GPU acceleration and thousands of rollouts.

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contributions of the cubic Hermite splines and best trajectory tracking.

1. **Cubic Hermite Splines**:
 - Compared to cubic and quadratic splines (without velocity targets), the cubic Hermite spline parameterization consistently yielded lower mean trajectory costs and lower variance across walking, handstand, and jumping tasks.
 - For walking, Hermite splines achieved a 10/10 success rate with an average cost of 913.9, compared to 6/10 success and 1124.1 cost for cubic splines, and 0/10 success for quadratic.
 - In jumping, Hermite splines achieved the highest mean height (68.4 cm), surpassing cubic (63.8 cm) and quadratic (47.7 cm) splines.
 - For handstands, Hermite splines showed superior stability with lower variance in pitch trajectories and an average cost of 811.7 compared to cubic (1540.2) and quadratic (1882.2) splines, which also exhibited occasional failures.

2. **Best Trajectory Tracking**:
 - Executing actions from the `τ_best` (best sampled trajectory) rather than `τ_0` (nominal trajectory) resulted in marginally lower average trajectory costs (e.g., 913.9 vs. 924.9 for walking) and comparable success rates. This suggests that explicit safeguarding of execution against untested weighted mixtures contributes to more reliable performance and prevents degradation during iterative refinement. The optimizer failed to improve upon the shifted previous solution for a significant portion of control steps (27.9% for walking, 29.7% for jumping, 27.1% for handstand), highlighting the value of consistently executing the best known solution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a sampling-based Model Predictive Control framework that contributes to the field of robotic locomotion control through several advancements and implications.

Firstly, the development of a **reference-free motion discovery** mechanism challenges the conventional need for handcrafted gait patterns, predefined contact sequences, or extensive offline pre-training. By enabling robots to autonomously discover diverse and dynamic locomotion behaviors—ranging from adaptive trotting and galloping to jumping, backflips, and handstand balancing—purely from high-level cost objectives, the framework enhances robotic autonomy and adaptability. This capability could reduce development time and engineering effort for new robotic applications, especially in unstructured or dynamic environments where prior motion knowledge is unavailable or rapidly becomes obsolete.

Secondly, the achievement of **real-time control on standard CPU hardware with a limited number of sampled trajectories** (as few as 30 for quadrupedal tasks) represents a notable advancement in computational efficiency. This contrasts with many state-of-the-art MPPI methods that typically necessitate GPU acceleration and thousands of parallel rollouts. The reduction in hardware requirements and computational burden makes advanced robot control more accessible and deployable on platforms with constrained onboard processing capabilities, potentially broadening the adoption of sophisticated MPC techniques in various robotic systems.

Thirdly, the demonstration of the framework's **generality and adaptability across different robot morphologies and complex tasks** is significant. The same algorithmic structure and cost function formulation successfully enabled locomotion on both a 12-DOF Go2 quadruped and a 37-DOF G1 humanoid robot in simulation. This cross-platform applicability suggests that the approach offers a flexible and robust foundation for controlling a wide range of robotic systems without requiring substantial re-engineering for each new platform or task. This adaptability is particularly valuable for accelerating the deployment of new robotic hardware.

The cubic Hermite spline parameterization, which jointly samples position and velocity control points, contributes to both **improved exploration and dynamic consistency** of generated trajectories. By providing local control over trajectory shape and implementing derivative clamping to respect joint limits, the method generates more feasible and dynamically sound reference motions for the low-level PD controller, thus enhancing the overall stability and performance of the system.

While the current formulation acknowledges limitations regarding the optimal smoothness or efficiency of the emergent motions compared to direct torque optimization, its role as a **flexible mechanism for motion discovery** is impactful. The framework can generate diverse candidate behaviors, contact sequences, and strategies that could serve as robust warm starts for more advanced, higher-level solvers or be integrated into larger, hybrid control pipelines. This positions the research as a foundational step for future work, which could involve combining this exploratory capability with trajectory optimization or learning-based refinement techniques to further polish and optimize the discovered motions for specific task requirements.
