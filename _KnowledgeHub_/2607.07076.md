# PriGo: Test-Time Primitive Guidance to Diffusion and Flow Policies for Adaptive Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> PriGo introduces a plug-and-play test-time primitive guidance framework to enhance the robustness and generalization of pretrained diffusion and flow policies for robotic manipulation. It integrates a primitive classifier with a differentiable guidance mechanism, leading to improved task success rates by up to 26 absolute points in real-world scenarios.

## Problem

- Imitation learning policies, especially diffusion and flow models, exhibit brittleness and poor generalization to distribution shifts (e.g., object variations, unseen tasks) in robotic manipulation.
- These policies often learn superficial correlations between observations and low-level actions, failing to grasp the underlying structural intent of manipulation behaviors.
- Existing primitive-based control methods often require policy retraining, use limited primitive sets, or treat primitive guidance as a weak constraint, not explicitly enforcing structured actions.

## Method

- A ==plug-and-play test-time framework==, **PriGo**, is introduced to guide pretrained diffusion and flow policies without requiring retraining.
- A lightweight primitive classifier (**PANet**) predicts ==probabilistic manipulation primitive distributions== from visual observations and language instructions.
- A ==differentiable primitive guidance mechanism== is applied during inference to steer generated actions towards consistency with the predicted primitive distributions, enhancing structured behavior.

## Results

- The Primitive Action Network (PANet) achieved **94.0%** accuracy on the LIBERO benchmark for classifying manipulation primitives, demonstrating its capability to infer structural intent.
- PriGo consistently improved success rates for both diffusion and flow policies across various simulation benchmarks (LIBERO, SIMPLER, CALVIN), yielding **3-7 percentage point** gains in robustness and generalization.
- In real-world robotic tasks, PriGo-DP enhanced performance by an average of **26 absolute percentage points** over the vanilla CogACT for long-sequence manipulation, with a marginal **15%** inference latency increase.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Injecting a compact set of manipulation primitives, inspired by human-like compositional task structure, can enhance robustness and generalization in robotic policies.
> - Automatically generating ground-truth primitive labels from robot-centric action frames provides a stable and generalizable method for training primitive classifiers.
> - Applying differentiable primitive guidance directly during the test-time inference of diffusion and flow policies effectively enforces structured manipulation behaviors without modifying or retraining the original policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements through imitation learning (IL), a paradigm where policies learn complex visuomotor behaviors from expert demonstrations without explicit reward functions. Recent developments, particularly in diffusion and flow-based policies, have further improved the quality of action generation by modeling multi-modal action distributions and integrating large-scale vision-language models (VLMs). These approaches have demonstrated strong performance in generating complex behaviors.

Despite these successes, a critical limitation in the current research landscape is the brittleness of existing policies when faced with distribution shifts, such as variations in objects, lighting, distractors, or unseen tasks. This vulnerability is often attributed to policies learning superficial correlations between observations and low-level actions, rather than grasping the underlying structural intent of manipulation behaviors. Consequently, while generated actions might locally resemble demonstrations, they may fail to maintain the global motion structure necessary for successful task completion, leading to issues like unstable grasping or incorrect action sequencing in long-horizon tasks.

To address this, researchers have explored incorporating structured action priors. Human manipulation inherently possesses a compositional structure, where complex tasks are broken down into reusable motion primitives (e.g., grasping, pushing, pulling, rotation). This perspective treats actions not merely as continuous trajectories but as sequences guided by these compact structural priors, aiming for semantically consistent behaviors.

Prior efforts in primitive-based control include compositional networks that combine primitives, and the use of large language models (LLMs) to decompose task instructions into primitive prompts. More recent works have focused on disentangling primitives into discrete types with associated parameters. However, these methods often limit the primitive set to basic actions, thereby restricting their applicability to complex tasks, or treat primitives as additional inputs rather than mandatory constraints, which can result in generated actions not adhering to the required primitives. Other approaches attempt to improve generalization through guided sampling or test-time adaptation, leveraging pretrained models, LLMs, or task-aware initialization, but these primarily focus on task-level objectives or reward guidance, not explicitly enforcing structured manipulation behaviors at the action generation level.

PriGo distinguishes itself within this landscape by focusing on *test-time primitive guidance*. Unlike prior primitive-conditioned approaches that often require retraining policies or treating primitives as soft inputs, PriGo introduces a plug-and-play framework that refines actions during inference without modifying the pretrained backbone policy. It introduces an explicit differentiable primitive guidance mechanism that steers action generation toward structured manipulation behaviors, offering a novel way to enhance robustness, long-horizon execution, and generalization under distribution shifts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a recognized limitation in state-of-the-art imitation learning policies, particularly those based on diffusion and flow models, when applied to robotic manipulation. Despite their ability to generate complex visuomotor behaviors from demonstrations, these policies often exhibit brittleness and poor generalization capabilities under distribution shifts. Such shifts include variations in objects, lighting, distractors, or entirely novel task scenarios.

The underlying cause of this limitation is identified as the tendency of current policies to learn superficial correlations between observations and low-level actions. Instead of understanding the semantic or structural intent behind manipulation behaviors, policies often mimic surface-level action patterns. This can lead to locally plausible but globally inconsistent or ineffective actions. For instance, in a task like opening a door, a policy might pull the handle before the key is rotated, or generate unstable grasping motions if environmental conditions change slightly. These errors are compounded in long-horizon manipulation tasks, where small local inaccuracies can accumulate and lead to overall task failure.

Inspired by the compositional nature of human manipulation, where complex tasks are naturally broken down into a sequence of simpler, reusable motion primitives (e.g., grasp, push, pull, rotate), the key objective of this work is to inject this structured understanding into robotic policies. Rather than treating actions as continuous, unconstrained trajectories, the authors propose that primitives can provide a compact structural prior to guide policies towards semantically consistent behaviors.

Specifically, the core objectives are:
1. **Develop a test-time adaptive framework:** Create a system that can improve the robustness and generalization of existing, pretrained diffusion and flow policies without requiring them to be retrained. This "plug-and-play" capability is a central design goal, aiming for flexible integration.
2. **Introduce a primitive prediction mechanism:** Design a lightweight module, PANet, capable of inferring the appropriate manipulation primitive distributions directly from visual observations and language instructions. This module is intended to provide the necessary high-level guidance.
3. **Propose a differentiable primitive guidance mechanism:** Implement a method to refine the actions generated by diffusion and flow policies during inference. This mechanism should use the primitive distributions predicted by PANet to steer the generated trajectories toward structured and semantically consistent behaviors, effectively acting as a constraint.
4. **Demonstrate improved robustness and generalization:** Empirically validate that the proposed framework consistently enhances policy robustness, improves performance in long-horizon task execution, and strengthens generalization capabilities across various simulation benchmarks (LIBERO, CALVIN, SIMPLER) and real-world robotic tasks, for both diffusion and flow-based policies.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between low-level action generation and high-level structural intent by explicitly guiding policies with manipulation primitives, thereby addressing the brittleness and generalization challenges prevalent in current imitation learning approaches for robotic manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PriGo is presented as a primitive-guided test-time adaptation framework for robotic manipulation policies. It is designed to be plug-and-play, integrating with pretrained diffusion and flow policies without requiring retraining. The framework comprises two main components: a primitive classifier (PANet) and a differentiable primitive guidance mechanism.

#### 3.1. Primitive Representation and Classification (PANet)

To introduce structured action priors, PriGo defines a compact set of eight manipulation primitives: {"idle", "grasp", "release", "push", "pull", "rotation", "push+rotation", "pull+rotation"}. This taxonomy is chosen for its balance between structural expressiveness and cross-task generalization, aiming to avoid issues like long-tail imbalances or semantic ambiguity that overly fine-grained decompositions might introduce.

**Primitive Action Hard Classification:** An automatic labeling method is developed to generate ground-truth primitive labels from expert demonstration actions. This method, detailed in Definition 3.1, classifies an action $a^k := (x, r, w)$ (translation, rotation, gripper width) at frame $k$ into one of the eight primitives. The classification relies on thresholds ($\tau_{trans}$, $\tau_{rot}$, $\tau_w$) for translational magnitude (∥$x^k$∥), rotational magnitude (∥$r^k$∥), and gripper width change (|$w^k - w^{k-1}$|), along with the direction of translation for push/pull. This robot-centric action frame formulation ensures simplicity and stable cross-task consistency, enabling automatic generation of primitive labels for training a classifier without manual annotation.

**PANet Architecture and Training:** PANet is designed to predict primitive distributions directly from observations. It utilizes DINOv2 as the visual encoder and T5 as the text encoder. Features from these two modalities are fused using a multimodal causal Q-Former, which then feeds into a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) classification head. The Q-Former has 3 layers, an embedding dimension of 1280, a cross-attention dimension of 768, 8 attention heads, and 32 queries. The MLP classification head reduces the fused feature dimension (1280) to 8, corresponding to the number of primitive types. PANet is trained using cross-entropy loss, with ground-truth labels derived from the automatic hard classification. It predicts probabilistic primitive distributions, which enhances robustness during ambiguous transitions and supports multi-primitive guidance.

#### 3.2. Primitive Guidance Mechanism

This mechanism is responsible for refining generated actions during inference by aligning them with the predicted primitive distributions.

**Soft Primitive Distribution:** To enable differentiable guidance, a soft primitive distribution, $p_k$, is induced from the generated actions $a^k$. This is achieved using differentiable score functions $f(a^k)$ that capture elements like translational, rotational, and gripper movements, followed by a temperature-scaled softmax operation (Definition 3.2). This yields a probability distribution over primitive categories based on the continuous action.

**Primitive Guidance Loss:** The primitive guidance is formulated as a cross-entropy loss, $L_{PG}(a^k, O^k) = - \frac{1}{B} \sum_{k=1}^B H(\hat{y}^k, p^k)$, where $H(\cdot, \cdot)$ is the cross-entropy between the primitive distribution predicted by PANet ($\hat{y}^k = \text{PANet}(O^k)$) and the soft primitive distribution ($p^k$) derived from the generated action $a^k$. This probabilistic formulation encourages smooth transitions between primitives and handles situations where multiple primitives might be active during transitional stages.

#### 3.3. Integration with Diffusion and Flow Policies

PriGo integrates this differentiable primitive guidance into the inference phase of pretrained diffusion and flow policies.

**Diffusion Policy with Primitives Guidance (PriGo-DP):** For diffusion policies, which iteratively denoise a random action to generate a final action, the primitive guidance mechanism modifies the denoising step. After an initial estimate of the noise-free action $\hat{a}^k_0$ is obtained, a gradient term derived from the primitive guidance loss, $- \eta \nabla_a L_{PG}(\hat{a}^k_0, O^k)$, is subtracted from the estimated action. This steers the denoising updates toward primitive-consistent trajectories, as described in Equation (3).

**Flow Policy with Primitives Guidance (PriGo-FP):** For flow policies, which learn a continuous transformation from noisy to expert actions via a velocity field, the guidance is applied during the integration step. After an initial action estimate $\hat{a}^k_1$ is obtained from the velocity field, a gradient term $- \eta \nabla_a L_{PG}(\hat{a}^k_1, O^k)$ is subtracted to refine the action, as shown in Equation (4). This is equivalent to defining a guided velocity field that enforces conformity to desired structured primitives.

**Theoretical Error Bound:** The supplementary material presents a proposition (Proposition A.1) demonstrating that the primitive-guided updates lead to a strictly tighter error bound compared to unguided policies. This suggests accelerated convergence toward the target action $a^*$ due to the introduced guidance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate PANet's robustness, PriGo's ability to improve pretrained policies, its generalization to real-world tasks, and the contribution of its individual components.

#### 4.1. Analysis of PANet Robustness

- **Primitive Classification Accuracy:** PANet achieved a 94.0% accuracy on the LIBERO benchmark. Analysis of the confusion matrix indicated that most misclassifications occurred between semantically adjacent primitives (e.g., "push+rotation" and "push" or "rotation"), suggesting that errors largely remained within similar local action manifolds.
- **Sensitivity to Thresholds:** PriGo's performance was found to be stable across a broad range of values for the auto-labeling thresholds ($\tau_{trans}$, $\tau_{rot}$, $\tau_w$), indicating that the framework is not overly sensitive to these hyperparameter choices.

#### 4.2. Simulated Evaluation

- **LIBERO Benchmark:** PriGo consistently improved manipulation performance across diverse tasks and generalization settings (spatial, object, goal, long-horizon).
 - For flow-based policies (π0 and SmolVLA), PriGo (PriGo-FP) yielded improvements of 3-5 percentage points in success rate.
 - For diffusion-based policies (DP and CogACT), PriGo (PriGo-DP) also showed improvements of 3-5 percentage points.
 - This consistent enhancement was observed regardless of whether the backbone policy was a large-scale VLM (π0, CogACT) or a lightweight one (DP), attributed to PriGo's ability to enforce more structured actions.
- **SIMPLER Benchmark:** PriGo-DP (CogACT with PriGo) outperformed existing VLA models.
 - Under the Visual Matching setting, PriGo-DP delivered an average improvement of 5 percentage points compared to the vanilla CogACT.
 - Under the Variant Aggregation setting, which introduces higher distribution shifts, PriGo-DP showed an average improvement of 7 points, demonstrating enhanced test-time robustness without retraining.
- **CALVIN Benchmark:** PriGo-DP (3DDA with PriGo) outperformed other diffusion policies for long-horizon tasks.
 - It increased the average length of correctly predicted trajectories by 0.8 compared to the vanilla 3DDA, improving zero-shot long-horizon generalization even for non-VLA models.

#### 4.3. Real-Robot Evaluation

Experiments were conducted on a Franka Emika Panda robot for three sequential table-service tasks in restaurant-style scenarios.
- With limited demonstration samples (12-20), PriGo-DP achieved an average success rate of 81%.
- Compared to the vanilla CogACT, PriGo-DP demonstrated an average performance improvement of 26 absolute points across the three tasks, indicating significant benefits for long-sequence tasks in real-world scenarios.
- Qualitative results (Figure 4 and Figure 6 in supplementary) illustrated that PriGo-DP produced action sequences that aligned well with required primitive types, such as correct rotation for placing a plate, push and release for a bowl, and grasping/release for a cup. In contrast, the baseline often failed to generate structured actions, leading to errors like inverted plates, unnecessary rotations, or incorrect grasping.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Contribution of Primitive Types:** An ablation study on the composition of the primitive set demonstrated that using the full set of eight primitive actions yielded superior performance.
 - Omitting the distinction between "push" and "pull" primitives resulted in the most significant performance degradation, as policies deviated in direction, leading to task failure.
 - Excluding hybrid primitives ("push+rotation", "pull+rotation") also led to a performance drop, though less severe, with observations indicating that hybrid primitives contributed to smoother action outputs.
- **Visualization of Primitive Actions:** UMAP and t-SNE visualizations of primitive action instances showed clear clustering for the same primitive types, confirming the distinctness and utility of the defined primitive representation.
- **Max-Probability vs. Sampling:** An evaluation (Table 6 in supplementary) showed that selecting the maximum-probability index for discrete primitive output resulted in negligible performance difference (0.2% lower average success rate) compared to probability-based sampling, leading to the adoption of the simpler maximum-probability approach due to its lower computational overhead.
- **Inference Overhead:** On a real robot, PriGo introduced a 15% latency increase (from 181 ms/step for CogACT to 208 ms/step). However, with effective control frequency of 77 Hz (for k=16 actions), it still exceeded real-time requirements. Crucially, PriGo reduced the average task completion time by 10% (from 2.85 minutes to 2.56 minutes) due to enhanced structural coherence and smoother subtask execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PriGo addresses a fundamental limitation in current imitation learning policies for robotic manipulation: their brittleness and poor generalization under distribution shifts, largely due to learning superficial action correlations rather than underlying structural intent. By introducing a primitive-guided test-time adaptation framework, this work offers a novel and flexible approach to imbue policies with a higher-level understanding of task structure.

The significance of PriGo lies in several key aspects:

1. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The consistent performance improvements observed across diverse simulated benchmarks (LIBERO, SIMPLER, CALVIN) and challenging real-world tasks demonstrate that PriGo effectively mitigates the issues of brittleness and improves generalization. This is particularly crucial for long-horizon manipulation, where the accumulation of minor errors in unstructured policies often leads to task failure.
2. **Plug-and-Play Adaptability:** A major impact of PriGo is its "plug-and-play" design. It can be seamlessly integrated into *pretrained* diffusion and flow policies, whether lightweight or large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, without requiring extensive retraining of the backbone policy. This drastically lowers the barrier to adoption, allowing researchers and practitioners to enhance existing policies with structured guidance efficiently.
3. **Structured Action Guidance:** The differentiable primitive guidance mechanism forces policies to generate actions that are semantically consistent with predicted primitives. This moves beyond simple reward-based or task-level guidance by imposing constraints directly on the action generation process, leading to more coherent and interpretable robotic behaviors.
4. **Novel Primitive Taxonomy and Automatic Labeling:** The introduction of a compact yet expressive set of eight manipulation primitives, coupled with an automatic primitive labeling method, simplifies the process of integrating primitive-level understanding. This approach balances expressiveness with stability across tasks, avoiding the complexities of overly fine-grained or ambiguous primitive definitions.
5. **Practical Real-World Applicability:** The demonstrated success in real-robot experiments, particularly the significant performance gains in complex table-service tasks with limited demonstrations, highlights PriGo's practical utility. The marginal increase in inference latency is offset by a notable reduction in overall task completion time, making it suitable for reactive manipulation scenarios.

The potential impact of PriGo is substantial. It provides a robust and accessible method for improving the reliability and adaptability of robotic manipulation systems. By enabling policies to reason about and execute actions with a structural understanding akin to human intuition, PriGo could accelerate the deployment of intelligent robots in diverse, less-structured environments. Future research could explore tighter integration of primitive guidance within end-to-end training pipelines and investigate how incorporating historical robot states or larger-scale training data could further enhance the guidance mechanism's effectiveness and PANet's generalization capabilities.
