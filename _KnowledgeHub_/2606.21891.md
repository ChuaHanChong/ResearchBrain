# Learning the ARTS of Search for Automated Discovery

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, Agentic Reasoning for Tree Search (ARTS), enhances automated scientific discovery by decoupling hypothesis quality from experimental execution and managing long-term search context. This approach achieved a 15.3% relative improvement in mean normalized scores across 22 benchmark tasks, with its test-time training variant enabling smaller models to match larger ones at 5x lower inference cost.

## Problem

- Existing score-based heuristic search algorithms conflate a hypothesis's inherent quality with its experimental execution quality, leading to premature abandonment of promising ideas.
- The accumulation of search history exceeds the context windows of large reasoning models, causing information loss through summarization or degraded reasoning over long contexts.
- Reasoning models guiding automated searches can suffer from diversity collapse and sensitivity to the exploration-exploitation tradeoff, often committing to suboptimal directions.

## Method

- **ARTS** introduces a modular agent design with a 'scientist' (LRM) for high-level reasoning and an 'executor' (coding agent) for code implementation, allowing the scientist to diagnose outcomes beyond just scores.
- The scientist leverages an `INSPECT` tool for detailed node analysis, baseline auditing, and uses verbalized sampling to promote diverse hypothesis exploration.
- ARTS* incorporates test-time training (TTT) via LoRA adapters, fine-tuning a smaller scientist model with the search tree history to encode experience into its weights and overcome context length limitations.

## Results

- ARTS achieved state-of-the-art performance, securing the best score on 16 out of 22 tasks and demonstrating a **15.3%** relative improvement in mean normalized score over leading prior methods.
- ARTS showed higher robustness with the best inter-quartile mean (**0.93**) and lower optimality gap, particularly on harder ML tasks where it sometimes surpassed human-best scores.
- Test-time training in ARTS* boosted a smaller Qwen3-4B scientist's mean normalized score by **40%** (from **0.72** to **1.01**), enabling it to match or exceed the performance of larger, more expensive models at approximately **5x** lower inference cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly distinguishing between hypothesis quality and execution quality enables the search agent to prevent premature abandonment of promising ideas, even after preliminary implementation failures.
> - Diverse hypothesis exploration, guided by verbalized sampling and deep reasoning, effectively covers a wider range of strategies and prevents diversity collapse.
> - Test-time training can distill extensive search experience into model weights, allowing smaller, more cost-effective models to achieve high performance on long-term search tasks by overcoming context window constraints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges in automated scientific discovery, framing it as an iterative search process across hypotheses and experiments. The advent of large reasoning models (LRMs) has facilitated the development of AI scientists capable of conducting such searches, leading to advancements in areas like combinatorics, algorithm improvement, chemical synthesis, and hypothesis generation for antimicrobial resistance. However, these systems often rely on extensive sampling budgets and repeated execution of expensive experiments, particularly in machine learning (ML) research where each hypothesis necessitates coding, training, debugging, and evaluation. Scaling automated research therefore requires more efficient search algorithms.

Existing search algorithms generally fall into three categories: linear, tree-based, and evolutionary. Linear search methods, such as ReAct and AutoResearch, follow a single trajectory, making it difficult to revisit and refine initially unpromising hypotheses. Tree-based methods (e.g., AIDE, AIRA) and evolutionary algorithms (e.g., FunSearch, AlphaEvolve) allow for broader exploration but rely on score-based heuristics. This approach can conflate the inherent quality of a hypothesis with the quality of its experimental execution, potentially leading to the premature abandonment of promising ideas that had flawed initial implementations. Furthermore, reasoning models guiding these searches can suffer from diversity collapse and sensitivity to the exploration-exploitation tradeoff, often committing to suboptimal directions. A significant practical challenge is the accumulation of extensive search history, which can exceed the context window of LRMs, leading to information loss through summarization or pruning, or degraded reasoning over long contexts. This research introduces an approach designed to mitigate these limitations by integrating agentic reasoning and test-time training into the search process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient search algorithm for automated scientific discovery that addresses the limitations of existing methods, particularly in the context of expensive ML research tasks. The motivation stems from several identified shortcomings:

1. **Conflation of Hypothesis Quality and Execution Quality**: Current score-based heuristic search algorithms evaluate the merit of a hypothesis solely on its experimental outcome. A promising hypothesis with a preliminary or flawed execution might yield a low score, causing it to be ranked below a less innovative hypothesis that was well-implemented. This can lead to the search agent prematurely abandoning potentially valuable research directions. For instance, an untuned Transformer model might score lower than a tuned LSTM on a sequence modeling task, leading heuristic methods to favor further exploration of LSTMs even if the Transformer has greater potential. The authors aim to enable the search agent to distinguish between a fundamentally weak hypothesis and an implementation error (e.g., a bug, suboptimal hyperparameters, or insufficient training).

2. **Context Length Limitations and Information Loss**: As the iterative search progresses, the accumulated history of experiments, including hypotheses, code, logs, and scores, can quickly outgrow the context window of large reasoning models. Prior methods typically address this by summarizing or pruning the search history, which can result in the loss of critical information necessary for informed decision-making. Retaining the full history, conversely, can degrade the reasoning reliability of models over extended contexts. The researchers are motivated to develop a mechanism that allows the search agent to effectively leverage the entire search history without being constrained by context length, thereby preserving valuable learned experience.

3. **Diversity Collapse and Suboptimal Trajectories**: Reasoning models used in automated discovery can exhibit diversity collapse, where they tend to propose similar or incrementally modified hypotheses, limiting the exploration of fundamentally different solutions. Additionally, search procedures can be sensitive to the exploration-exploitation tradeoff, often committing to locally optimal but globally suboptimal directions. The objective is to design a system that encourages the exploration of diverse hypotheses and prevents the search from getting stuck in narrow, less impactful research avenues.

In summary, the key motivation is to enable a more intelligent, robust, and efficient automated discovery process by allowing the AI agent to reason deeply about experimental outcomes, learn from cumulative experience, and explore a broader, higher-quality space of hypotheses.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, Agentic Reasoning for Tree Search (ARTS), leverages a Large Reasoning Model (LRM) to navigate the search space by explicitly distinguishing between hypothesis quality and execution quality. It introduces a modular design with a "scientist" agent for high-level reasoning and an "executor" agent for code implementation. A variant, ARTS$^*$, incorporates test-time training to address context length limitations.

#### 3.1. Agentic Reasoning for Tree Search (ARTS)

ARTS operates by allowing an LRM, termed the "scientist," to inspect prior experimental data and reason about outcomes to guide the search.

- **Modular Agent Design**: ARTS separates the roles of the scientist and the executor.
 - **Scientist**: An LRM (OpenAI o3) responsible for selecting which node (experiment) in the search tree to expand and generating the next hypothesis. This involves reasoning over past experiments, their hypotheses, execution errors, and performance.
 - **Executor**: A coding agent (Gemini 3 Flash) responsible for implementing the scientist's hypothesis by modifying code based on a selected parent node's validated code state. It has tools like `READ_FILE`, `WRITE_FILE`, and `VALIDATE` and iteratively refines code until a valid score is achieved or an action budget is exhausted.

- **Scientist's Tools and Mechanisms**:
 - **Node Inspection (`INSPECT` tool)**: The scientist can access the full details of any prior node, including its code, training curves, and program output. This allows the scientist to diagnose *why* an experiment succeeded or failed, distinguishing between faulty implementations, bad hyperparameters, insufficient training, or a fundamentally weak hypothesis. For example, it can identify a sparse-reward run that never received positive reward, leading to a proposal for reward shaping instead of a larger model.
 - **Baseline Audit**: The initial `R` calls to the scientist are reserved for "audits," where it can only read task instructions, data, baseline code, and evaluation scripts without expanding nodes or changing code. This helps the scientist understand structural biases, metric properties, or information-loss steps in the baseline, informing subsequent hypothesis generation.
 - **Hypothesis Sampling (Verbalized Sampling)**: To promote diverse exploration and mitigate diversity collapse, ARTS uses an adapted version of Verbalized Sampling. The scientist enumerates `K` candidate hypotheses, each with an associated probability. An external sampler then draws one candidate, often from the tail of the distribution at deeper nodes, to encourage broader exploration beyond the most probable incremental tweaks.
 - **Memory Module**: To manage the growing search tree, the scientist maintains an editable memory module. After each experiment, it writes a concise insight (e.g., score, failure mode) to this memory, appending only new insights. This memory is accessible at all times, providing a summarized context that is less prone to context window overflow.

#### 3.2. ARTS$^*$ : Test-Time Training the Scientist

To further address context length limitations and instill search experience into the model weights, ARTS$^*$ employs test-time training (TTT).

- **Test-Time Training (TTT)**: When the search tree exceeds context limits, the scientist (specifically, a Qwen3-4B model) is fine-tuned using the current search tree via LoRA adapters. This process aims to encode the knowledge from prior experiments directly into the model's weights, effectively serving as a memory mechanism.
- **Rollout and Reward**: TTT involves a Group-Reinforce Policy Optimization (GRPO) approach. Given the current search tree, the scientist proposes `N` actions (pairs of parent node `p_t` and hypothesis `h_t`). Each action is executed by the executor, yielding a score `s_t`. A percentile-based reward function is used:
 - `r_t = -0.5` if `s_t` is null (execution failed).
 - `r_t = -0.2` if `s_t < s_base`.
 - `r_t = 0` if `s_base <= s_t < s_70(G_t)` (score is above baseline but below 70th percentile of all scores).
 - `r_t = 1` if `s_t >= s_70(G_t)` (score is at or above the 70th percentile).
 This dense, adaptive reward signal encourages improvements while preventing saturation. The `N` rewards form a GRPO group, used to update the LoRA adapter weights. These new nodes are added to the tree before the next batch of rollouts, teaching the model both parent selection and hypothesis exploration for validation score gains.
- **Diversity Preservation**: Despite training on score-based rewards, the verbalized sampling mechanism ensures that the sampled hypotheses within a group remain diverse, preventing the policy from collapsing to a narrow set of solutions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ARTS and ARTS$^*$ was conducted across 22 tasks from MLGym and MLEBench benchmarks, under an 8-hour wall-clock budget per run, with results averaged over three independent runs.

#### 4.1. Performance Against Prior Methods (ARTS)

- **Overall Improvement**: ARTS achieved the best score on 16 out of 22 tasks, demonstrating a 15.3% relative improvement in the mean normalized score compared to leading algorithms (Linear Search, AIRA, MLEvolve).
- **Robustness Metrics**: ARTS attained the highest inter-quartile mean (IQM) among all methods (0.93 vs. 0.87 for MLEvolve), indicating superior typical performance not driven by outliers. It also achieved a lower optimality gap, meaning it closed more of the distance to the human-best score on tasks.
- **Performance Progression**: ARTS typically showed a slower start in the first one to two hours but caught up mid-run and finished above the strongest contemporary methods. This pattern suggests that ARTS effectively uses its early budget for broader exploration rather than prematurely committing to locally good hypotheses, leading to better utilization of the inference budget.
- **Hard Tasks**: ARTS demonstrated largest gains on harder tasks from MLEBench and MLGym (e.g., HMS Brain Activity, RSNA Brain Tumor), where reasoning about what to try next is crucial. On some tasks, ARTS even surpassed human-best scores (e.g., HMS Brain Activity, RSNA Brain Tumor).

#### 4.2. Reasons for ARTS's Outperformance

The improvements of ARTS were attributed to three factors:

- **Finding 1: Failure Attribution Prevents Premature Abandonment**: ARTS's scientist inspects node code and reasons whether a poor score resulted from a weak hypothesis or an implementation fault. This allows promising hypotheses to be retained and refined even after preliminary execution failures.
 - *Examples*: On HMS Brain Activity, ARTS identified that regressions were due to undertraining with heavy augmentation rather than a flawed spectrogram-CNN family, leading to further refinements and the best task score. Similarly, on APTOS, a 0.000 QWK score was correctly attributed to an aggressive learning rate rather than a ConvNeXt backbone failure, allowing subsequent recovery to QWK 0.92. On Vesuvius, a low F0.5 was traced to a broken validation split rather than the wide-input idea itself, enabling repair and eventual achievement of the best task score. This contrasts with score-only methods that often abandon such families.

- **Finding 2: Diverse Hypothesis Exploration**: ARTS distributes its expansions across diverse hypothesis families more effectively than score-based methods, which tend to concentrate around a small set of hypotheses.
 - *Quantitative Metrics*: ARTS showed wider coverage (4.43 axes per run vs. 4.05 for MLEvolve) and higher entropy (1.73 vs. 1.35 for MLEvolve, 1.48 for AIRA) in its hypothesis distribution. Human validation revealed 45% of ARTS's expansions introduced completely new hypotheses, compared to 3% for AIRA and 0% for MLEvolve. TF-IDF distance between hypotheses also indicated greater lexical diversity for ARTS.
 - *Qualitative Examples*: On Vesuvius ink-detection, ARTS explored five distinct axes as siblings at the root (architecture, slice representation, loss, training data, augmentation), while AIRA focused primarily on tweaking architectures down a single deep chain.

- **Finding 3: Higher Quality Hypothesis Generation**: ARTS allocates its exploration budget to higher-quality hypotheses. The scientist's ability to inspect logs and diagnose specific failures leads to well-reasoned proposals that address identified bottlenecks, rather than random mutations or extensions of high-scoring branches.
 - *Qualitative Examples*: On MetaMaze, ARTS correctly diagnosed that the baseline's flattening of input destroyed maze geometry, first fixing this with a CNN encoder before combining it with an LSTM recurrent policy to surpass human-best. In contrast, AIRA proposed an LSTM early but then abandoned it after combining it with other unstable techniques.

#### 4.3. Impact of ARTS$^*$ (Test-Time Training)

- **Improved Performance for Smaller Models**: Test-time training significantly improved the performance of a smaller Qwen3-4B scientist. It raised its mean normalized score by 40% (from 0.72 to 1.01) on MLGym tasks.
- **Matching Frontier Models at Lower Cost**: The TTT-updated Qwen3-4B scientist matched or surpassed the performance of the o3 scientist on about half of the tasks at approximately 5x lower inference cost.
- **Effective Long-Term Search**: TTT proved beneficial when a promising direction was initially found but later discarded or became unstable due to context issues or implementation challenges. For instance, on the partially observable MetaMaze task, the TTT-trained Qwen3-4B scientist rediscovered and optimized an LSTM-based solution that heuristic methods had pruned and even the o3 scientist had abandoned after initial instability. This indicates that TTT instills useful search experience into the scientist's weights, reducing dependence on increasingly long context for extended search.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Executor and Scientist Models**: The choice of executor model significantly impacted performance, even with a fixed scientist. Similarly, with a fixed executor, o3 proved to be the most reliable scientist for harder tasks.
- **Component Contribution**: Removing the `audit` or `verbalized sampling` components led to the largest drops in normalized score, highlighting their critical role in ARTS's effectiveness.
- **TTT Specifics**: The percentile-based reward function for TTT outperformed alternatives. Single-step GRPO was more effective than tree-per-episode rollouts. Training with TTT was observed to preserve diversity, with strategy category coverage and entropy generally increasing over rollouts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Agentic Reasoning for Tree Search (ARTS) and its test-time training variant (ARTS$^*$) represents a notable advancement in automated scientific discovery. By explicitly decoupling hypothesis quality from execution quality, ARTS enables more sophisticated diagnosis of experimental failures. This allows the system to preserve promising research directions even when initial implementations are flawed, leading to a more robust and persistent exploration of the solution space. The qualitative analysis supports this, showing how ARTS avoids premature abandonment of ideas and consistently refines them to achieve better outcomes.

The emphasis on diverse hypothesis exploration, facilitated by verbalized sampling and reasoning-guided parent selection, allows ARTS to cover a wider range of strategies compared to score-only methods. This broad exploration, coupled with the generation of higher-quality, reasoned hypotheses, is crucial for open-ended research problems where multiple distinct approaches might lead to breakthroughs.

The introduction of test-time training in ARTS$^*$ addresses the fundamental challenge of context window limitations in large language models during extended searches. By distilling search experience into the model's weights, ARTS$^*$ enables smaller, more cost-effective models to achieve performance comparable to or exceeding frontier closed-source models. This has significant implications for making advanced automated discovery more accessible and scalable by reducing computational demands. The rediscovery and optimization of memory-based solutions on tasks like MetaMaze demonstrate the ability of ARTS$^*$ to overcome previous limitations and find human-best or even superior solutions.

The modular design of ARTS allows for independent upgrades of scientist and executor models, ensuring future adaptability. However, the system's reliance on the quality of these underlying models and the potential for misuse in sensitive domains necessitate continued ethical considerations, including strict task constraints, logging, and human oversight in deployment. The research contributes to the broader field of AI agents for research by offering a framework that integrates deep reasoning, effective memory management, and efficient learning from experimental feedback, potentially accelerating discovery in complex domains like machine learning and beyond.
