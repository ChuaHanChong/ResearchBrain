# Model-Based Reinforcement Learning for Control under Time-Varying Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zürich devised an optimistic Model-Based Reinforcement Learning framework capable of adapting to time-varying system dynamics by strategically managing stale data. This approach establishes sublinear dynamic regret guarantees and demonstrates superior performance over stationary MBRL baselines on continuous control tasks and a real-world RC car.

## Problem

- Current Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) typically assumes stationary system dynamics, which does not hold for real-world scenarios like hardware degradation or changing operating conditions.
- Applying standard MBRL in non-stationary environments leads to biased dynamics models, suboptimal control policies, and inaccurate uncertainty estimates due to the accumulation of outdated historical data.
- Existing non-stationary learning solutions often address simpler contexts like tabular RL or bandit problems, lacking frameworks for continuous state-action spaces and non-linear dynamics.

## Method

- An episodic ==Model-Based Reinforcement Learning== (MBRL) framework was introduced, specifically designed for time-varying dynamics using a ==frequentist variation-budget model== within ==Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces==.
- Two data management strategies, ==Full Resets== (**R-OMBRL**) and ==Sliding Window== (**SW-OMBRL**), were developed to restrict the dynamics model training to recent data, preventing bias from stale information.
- For practical implementation, ==Bayesian Neural Networks== (deep ensembles) were used to model dynamics and uncertainty, with policy optimization adapting an ==optimistic MBRL== approach and incorporating ==soft resets== for model parameters.

## Results

- Theoretical analysis established ==dynamic regret bounds== for the proposed algorithms (**R-OMBRL**, **SW-OMBRL**), demonstrating their ability to achieve sublinear dynamic regret in time-varying environments.
- On continuous control benchmarks (e.g., Pendulum, HalfCheetah, Hopper), **R-OMBRL** and **SW-OMBRL** consistently outperformed stationary MBRL baselines by effectively adapting to evolving dynamics and reducing regret accumulation.
- Real-world experiments using an RC car confirmed the algorithms' practical efficacy, with **R-OMBRL** successfully adapting to decaying throttle conditions during a parking maneuver where stationary methods failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Standard uncertainty estimates from models like Gaussian Processes are insufficient in non-stationary environments, as accumulating stale data introduces a temporal bias not captured by typical variance.
> - Explicitly restricting the influence of outdated data is crucial for maintaining calibrated uncertainty estimates and achieving sublinear dynamic regret in systems with evolving dynamics.
> - There is a bias-variance trade-off in determining the optimal data buffer size: smaller buffers enable rapid adaptation but offer less data for model training, while larger buffers provide more data but risk increased bias from stale dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) have demonstrated efficacy in managing complex dynamical systems, particularly through learning dynamics models for planning. Existing MBRL algorithms, however, commonly operate under the assumption of stationary system dynamics. This assumption implies that the system's behavior remains constant throughout the learning process.

In practical applications, this stationarity assumption is often violated. Real-world systems are subject to various forms of non-stationarity, such as hardware degradation (wear, drift), changes in operating conditions (e.g., varying loads on vehicles), or environmental shifts. When confronted with such time-varying dynamics, traditional MBRL methods, which typically reuse all historical data, can lead to biased models and suboptimal control performance. This issue arises because older data may no longer accurately represent the current system state, causing a mismatch between the learned model and the true dynamics.

Research into learning under non-stationary conditions has been explored in various machine learning contexts. In the bandit and Bayesian optimization fields, models like time-varying Gaussian Process (GP) bandits use concepts such as stochastic drift or variation budgets to offer dynamic regret guarantees. However, these methods typically focus on immediate reward maximization and do not address the more complex RL setting involving high-dimensional policies learned through trajectories. In RL, non-stationarity has been investigated primarily in tabular settings, often by controlling cumulative changes in rewards and transitions or without prior knowledge of model variation. Some model-based approaches for non-stationary environments, like those building on UCRL for optimistic exploration, exist but are generally confined to finite state-action spaces.

This work addresses the challenge of non-stationarity in MBRL by focusing on continuous state-action spaces and non-linear dynamics, which are prevalent in real-world systems. It aims to develop theoretical understanding and practical algorithms that can adapt to evolving system dynamics, moving beyond the stationary assumptions of much current MBRL theory and practice. The paper distinguishes itself by providing dynamic regret guarantees for optimistic MBRL in continuous settings and proposing mechanisms for managing stale data, an area less explored in this specific context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable sample-efficient Model-Based Reinforcement Learning for control in time-varying non-linear systems with continuous state-action spaces. The authors aim to develop algorithms that can adapt to evolving system dynamics while maintaining robust performance.

The motivation for this work stems from a critical limitation in current learning-based control methods: the widespread assumption of stationary system dynamics. This assumption is frequently invalid in real-world scenarios due to factors such as:
- **System Drift and Wear:** Physical components of robotic systems or industrial machinery can degrade over time, altering their dynamic characteristics.
- **Changing Operating Conditions:** Vehicles might operate under varying loads, or systems might be deployed across different environmental regimes, leading to shifts in dynamics.
- **Environmental Variation:** External factors can influence system behavior, causing dynamics to evolve unpredictably.

When these non-stationary conditions are present, applying standard MBRL techniques leads to suboptimal outcomes. Specifically, data collected in the past may become "stale" or non-representative of the current system dynamics. If a learning algorithm continues to use all historical data without discrimination, it can result in:
- **Biased Dynamics Models:** The learned model will be an aggregate of past and present dynamics, potentially misrepresenting the current system state.
- **Degraded Control Performance:** Policies derived from biased models will be ineffective or even unsafe for the actual, evolving system.
- **Uncalibrated Uncertainty:** Uncertainty estimates, crucial for effective exploration in MBRL, may not accurately reflect the model's true error if they do not account for temporal drift.

Therefore, a fundamental question arises: how should RL algorithms adapt to time-varying dynamics to maintain performance and reliability?

The authors pursue this question through several key contributions:
- **Formalization:** They introduce an episodic MBRL framework designed for time-varying dynamics, incorporating a variation-budget model to quantify temporal drift.
- **Theoretical Analysis:** They derive dynamic regret bounds for optimistic MBRL algorithms that employ restricted data buffers. This analysis aims to show how the algorithm's performance is influenced by the length of the retained data horizon and the total variation in the dynamics.
- **Key Insight:** Their theoretical analysis indicates that explicitly limiting the influence of outdated data is necessary. This limitation is required to maintain calibrated uncertainty estimates and achieve sublinear dynamic regret in non-stationary environments.
- **Algorithmic Development:** Based on these theoretical insights, the authors propose practical modifications to existing optimistic MBRL algorithms, enabling their application to high-dimensional systems by using neural networks and adaptive data management.
- **Empirical Validation:** They demonstrate the improved performance of their proposed methods against stationary MBRL baselines on continuous control benchmarks featuring non-stationary dynamics.

These objectives collectively aim to bridge the gap between theoretical MBRL guarantees (often under stationarity) and the practical challenges of deploying learning-based control in dynamic, real-world environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research develops an optimistic Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) framework for control under time-varying dynamics. The methodology encompasses problem formulation, theoretical analysis using Gaussian Processes (GPs), and practical algorithms implemented with deep learning.

**Problem Formulation**
- **Control Objective:** The goal is finite-horizon control, maximizing the cumulative reward $J(\pi, f)$ over a horizon $T$, where $f$ represents the system dynamics and $\pi$ is the control policy. The system state $x_t$ evolves according to $x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t) + w_t$, with Gaussian noise $w_t$.
- **Time-Varying Dynamics:** The core challenge is addressed by considering dynamics $f^*_n$ that are unknown and vary across episodes ($n$). Within each episode, the dynamics are assumed to be fixed, but they can change from one episode to the next.
- **Performance Metric:** Dynamic regret ($R_N = \sum_{n=1}^N (J(\pi^*_n, f^*_n) - J(\pi_n, f^*_n))$) is used to evaluate performance. This metric compares the agent's performance against the sequence of episode-wise optimal policies ($\pi^*_n$), which is appropriate for non-stationary environments.
- **Non-Stationarity Model:** Temporal variation is quantified using a frequentist model based on Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS). Each component of the dynamics $f^*_n$ is assumed to lie within a common RKHS with a bounded norm. Non-stationarity is captured by a "variation budget" $P_N$, which bounds the cumulative change in dynamics across episodes: $\sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \|f^*_{n+1} - f^*_n\|_{H_{d_x k}} \leq P_N$.

**Gaussian Process Dynamics Models (Theoretical Analysis)**
For theoretical analysis, the unknown dynamics are modeled using Gaussian Processes (GPs). GPs are chosen for their expressiveness, Bayesian nature, and ability to quantify uncertainty, which facilitates theoretical guarantees.
- **GP Model:** Each component of the $d_x$-dimensional dynamics $f^*_{n,j}$ is modeled independently with a GP, using a kernel $k$. The GP provides posterior mean ($\mu_{m:l,j}$) and variance ($\sigma^2_{m:l,j}$) functions, which are critical for capturing uncertainty. These are derived from data collected over a range of episodes, $D_{m:l}$.
- **Maximum Information Gain ($\gamma_N(k)$):** This metric quantifies the complexity of learning the true function $f^*$ from data and is used in the theoretical bounds.
- **Stale Data Problem:** The paper highlights that in non-stationary settings, fitting a GP with all historical data (as is common in stationary contexts) biases the posterior mean towards outdated dynamics. Critically, the GP variance, which typically quantifies statistical uncertainty, does not inherently capture this temporal mismatch, leading to uncalibrated uncertainty.
- **Forgetting Mechanisms:** To address stale data, two explicit mechanisms are proposed for controlling the data used to train the GP:
 - **Full Resets (R-OMBRL):** The data buffer is periodically emptied every $H$ episodes, retaining only data from the most recent $H$ episodes.
 - **Sliding Window (SW-OMBRL):** The data buffer maintains only the most recent $w$ episodes, continuously discarding older samples.
- **Calibration Under Drift (Lemma 1):** This lemma extends GP confidence bounds to the non-stationary case. It shows that the true prediction error $|f^*_{n,j}(z) - \mu_{m:n-1,j}(z)|$ is bounded by two terms: a standard epistemic uncertainty term ($\beta_n(\delta, n-m)\sigma_{m:n-1,j}(z)$) and an additive temporal bias term ($\xi_{n-m} \sum_{s=m}^{n-1} \|f^*_{s+1,j} - f^*_{s,j}\|_{H_k}$). This bias term directly quantifies the accumulated drift from the retained data horizon, confirming that temporal drift introduces a systematic error not captured by standard GP variance.

**Optimistic Model-Based RL Framework**
The analysis of calibrated uncertainty under drift motivates the adaptation of an optimistic MBRL framework.
- **Performance Difference Bound (Lemma 2):** This lemma quantifies how model error propagates to control performance. It decomposes the difference between returns under true and learned dynamics into an uncertainty term (scaled by $\lambda_{n,m}$, relating to accumulated posterior standard deviation) and a temporal bias term ($B_{n,m}$, related to accumulated drift in the retained dataset). Importantly, $B_{n,m}$ is independent of the policy.
- **Optimistic Planning:** At each episode $n$, the policy $\pi_n$ is optimized by solving an optimistic planning problem: $\pi_n = \arg \max_\pi (J(\pi, \mu_{m:n-1}) + \lambda_{n,m} \Sigma_{m:n-1}(\pi, \mu_{m:n-1}))$. Here, $\Sigma_{m:n-1}$ is the trajectory-wise accumulated epistemic uncertainty, acting as an intrinsic reward to guide exploration toward less-modeled regions. The coefficient $\lambda_{n,m}$ balances reward maximization and exploration.
- **Algorithmic Modification:** The core modification is to restrict the data buffer used for both model learning and policy optimization to recent data, as per the resetting or sliding window mechanisms.

**Practical Modifications and Algorithms**
While GPs are useful for theory, they scale poorly to high-dimensional systems. Therefore, for practical implementation, the authors propose using Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs), specifically deep ensembles, to represent the dynamics model $M_\phi = (\mu_\phi, \sigma_\phi)$ for estimating both the mean dynamics and epistemic uncertainty.
- **Policy Optimization:** A parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained using a variant of the MBPO algorithm on the optimistic objective.
- **Exploration Weight:** In practice, the theoretically derived exploration weight $\lambda_{n,m}$ can be treated as a tunable parameter or adaptively tuned online.
- **Soft Resets:** Beyond data buffer management, the practical algorithms also incorporate soft resets of the model and policy parameters (Eq. 15), where parameters are periodically perturbed towards their initial values. This helps balance retaining learned structure with adapting to new dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical findings regarding dynamic regret bounds and empirical results demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed algorithms in simulated and real-world non-stationary environments.

**Theoretical Findings**
- **Dynamic Regret Bound (Theorem 1):** The paper establishes a dynamic regret bound for optimistic MBRL algorithms employing either the full reset (R-OMBRL) or sliding window (SW-OMBRL) mechanisms. The regret $R_N$ is bounded as $\tilde{O}\left( N \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_p^3}{p}} + \gamma_p p^{3/2} P_N \right)$, where $N$ is the total number of episodes, $p$ is the buffer size (reset period $H$ or window size $w$), $\gamma_p$ is the maximum information gain over $p$ episodes, and $P_N$ is the total variation budget. The $\tilde{O}$ notation hides logarithmic factors.
- **Interpretation of Regret Bound:** The bound comprises two main terms:
 - **Learning Term:** $N \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_p^3}{p}}$ reflects the benefit of reusing data. A larger $p$ (more data) can reduce this term.
 - **Drift Term:** $\gamma_p p^{3/2} P_N$ represents the cost of accumulating stale data. A larger $p$ increases the influence of past dynamics, amplifying this term.
- **Bias-Variance Trade-off:** This decomposition highlights a bias-variance-type trade-off in selecting the buffer size $p$. A small $p$ enables strong adaptivity but provides less data for model training, potentially increasing the learning term. A large $p$ provides more data but incurs a larger bias from stale dynamics, increasing the drift term.
- **Sublinear Regret:** The analysis shows that by carefully selecting $p$, the proposed methods can achieve sublinear dynamic regret even when $P_N > 0$ (i.e., non-stationary dynamics). This contrasts with standard stationary MBRL (where $p=N$), which would yield sublinear regret only if $P_N=0$. For specific choices of $p$ (e.g., $p \propto \gamma_N^{1/4} N^{1/2}$), the regret can be bounded as $O(\gamma_N^{11/8} (1+P_N) N^{3/4})$, which is sublinear for common kernels.

**Empirical Findings**

The empirical evaluation uses deep ensembles (BNNs) for dynamics modeling and compares R-OMBRL and SW-OMBRL against SOMBRL, a state-of-the-art stationary MBRL method that trains on all collected data. Experiments are conducted on continuous control benchmarks with artificially induced non-stationarity (exponential decay of actuator strength).

- **GP Dynamics (Pendulum):** Initial experiments on the Pendulum environment with GP dynamics (Figure 1) demonstrate that both R-OMBRL and SW-OMBRL successfully adapt to a sudden change in dynamics (halving max action), recovering performance and reducing cumulative regret. The SOMBRL baseline, relying on all past data, remains biased and exhibits linear regret accumulation.
- **High-Dimensional Continuous Control (BNN Dynamics):**
 - **Diverse Environments and Decay Rates (Figure 2):** On Pendulum, HalfCheetah, and Hopper environments, with varying rates of actuator strength decay, R-OMBRL and SW-OMBRL consistently reduce regret accumulation compared to the stationary SOMBRL. The stationary baseline fails to track the evolving dynamics, leading to rapidly increasing regret.
 - **Onset of Non-Stationarity (Figure 3):** Across a broader range of MuJoCo environments (InvertedDoublePendulum, Ant, Walker2d, HumanoidStandup, Humanoid), the methods are evaluated under initially stationary dynamics followed by the onset of non-stationarity. All methods perform similarly during the stationary phase. However, after non-stationarity begins, R-OMBRL and SW-OMBRL adapt effectively by restricting their data buffers, resulting in significantly lower regret compared to SOMBRL. These results suggest scalability to higher-dimensional tasks.
- **Hardware Experiments (RC Car, Figure 4):** Real-world experiments on an RC car platform performing a dynamic parking maneuver under decaying throttle confirm simulation findings. As the task transitions from a drift-based maneuver to a standard parking problem, R-OMBRL successfully adapts and completes the task. SOMBRL, due to accumulating outdated transitions from high-throttle regimes, fails to adapt and shows degraded performance. R-OMBRL achieves higher and more stable returns.
- **Effect of Soft Resets (Figure 5, Appendix XI-C):** Ablation studies on the soft reset parameters ($\alpha$) for R-OMBRL indicate that moderate perturbation rates (e.g., $\alpha=0.2$) offer the best trade-off between stability and plasticity, preserving learned structure while enabling adaptation. Aggressive perturbations (e.g., $\alpha=1.0$) can significantly degrade performance, particularly in transient phases.

In summary, the main findings confirm that in time-varying environments, explicitly limiting the influence of stale data through mechanisms like data buffer resets or sliding windows is critical for maintaining calibrated uncertainty and achieving robust control performance. The proposed R-OMBRL and SW-OMBRL algorithms demonstrate improved adaptation and reduced dynamic regret across various continuous control tasks in both simulated and real-world settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a fundamental limitation in Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL): the assumption of stationary system dynamics. By developing and validating methods for control under time-varying dynamics, the work contributes to the advancement of robust and adaptable learning-based control systems.

The significance of this work can be highlighted in several areas:
- **Theoretical Foundation for Non-Stationary MBRL:** The paper provides a formal theoretical framework for optimistic MBRL in time-varying environments. By introducing a variation-budget model for non-stationarity and deriving dynamic regret bounds for continuous state-action spaces, it extends existing theoretical guarantees which were predominantly limited to stationary or tabular settings. This theoretical underpinning clarifies the necessity of managing stale data for achieving meaningful performance in dynamic environments.
- **Key Insight on Stale Data:** The analysis rigorously demonstrates that in non-stationary settings, standard GP uncertainty estimates are insufficient due to an uncaptured temporal bias. The explicit identification that limiting the influence of outdated data is crucial for maintaining calibrated uncertainty and achieving sublinear dynamic regret is a significant insight. This insight directly informs the design of more robust MBRL algorithms.
- **Practical Algorithms for Real-World Challenges:** The proposed R-OMBRL and SW-OMBRL algorithms offer practical solutions for incorporating these theoretical insights into scalable MBRL systems. By adapting to deep ensembles for dynamics modeling and integrating data buffer management strategies, these algorithms are suitable for high-dimensional and complex control tasks.
- **Empirical Validation across Diverse Settings:** The comprehensive empirical evaluation, spanning various continuous control benchmarks with different non-stationarity patterns and culminating in real-world hardware experiments, substantiates the practical efficacy of the proposed methods. The consistent outperformance of R-OMBRL and SW-OMBRL over stationary baselines demonstrates their adaptability and robustness. The hardware experiment on an RC car is particularly impactful, showcasing the algorithms' ability to translate simulation findings to physical systems.

The potential impact of this research is substantial, particularly for applications where system dynamics are inherently non-stationary:
- **Robotics:** Robots deployed in long-term tasks will inevitably experience wear, component degradation, or changing payloads. This work can enable robots to continually adapt their control policies, improving longevity, safety, and performance in varying conditions.
- **Autonomous Systems:** Autonomous vehicles, drones, and other mobile robots operate in diverse and changing environments (e.g., weather, road conditions, cargo changes). Adaptive MBRL can lead to more reliable and safer autonomous operations.
- **Industrial Control:** Manufacturing processes, machinery, and smart infrastructure can exhibit drift over time due to operational changes or aging equipment. This research offers methods to maintain optimal control and performance in such dynamic industrial settings.
- **Enhanced Reliability and Trust:** By providing methods that account for evolving dynamics and offer theoretical guarantees, this work can contribute to building more reliable and trustworthy AI systems in critical applications.

Future directions, such as developing adaptive mechanisms for selecting the reset period or window size, exploring alternative non-stationarity patterns (e.g., abrupt shifts, cyclic variations), and extending the framework to non-episodic settings, indicate avenues for further strengthening the impact of this work.
