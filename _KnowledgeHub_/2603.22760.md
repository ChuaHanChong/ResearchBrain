# SG-VLA: Learning Spatially-Grounded Vision-Language-Action Models for Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The SG-VLA framework facilitates Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for mobile manipulation in complex household environments by integrating multi-modal sensory inputs and a progressive multi-task learning scheme with auxiliary decoders. This approach achieved an average success rate of 0.73 on the SetTable subset of the ManiSkill-HAB benchmark, representing a 22% improvement over VLA models without auxiliary tasks.

## Problem

- VLA models typically exhibit strong performance in constrained tabletop environments but struggle to adapt to the complexities of mobile manipulation in real-world domestic settings.
- Mobile manipulation tasks require navigating high-dimensional continuous action spaces and demand robust spatial and scene-level reasoning, which is not adequately addressed by standard VLA configurations.
- Direct imitation learning, often used for VLA models, provides an insufficient supervisory signal for acquiring the rich, multi-faceted understanding necessary for complex mobile manipulation.

## Method

- SG-VLA enhances a pre-trained VLM backbone by incorporating multi-modal inputs, including multi-view RGB observations, explicit depth cues, and short temporal history for comprehensive spatial understanding.
- The framework employs five auxiliary decoders to provide dense supervisory signals by reconstructing interpretable intermediate states such as global robot position, joint configurations, grasp affordances, target-object relative pose, and segmentation masks.
- A progressive multi-stage training scheme is utilized to effectively integrate auxiliary decoders and an optional flow matching action head, adapting decoders first, then jointly refining representations, and finally training the action head in isolation.

## Results

- SG-VLA achieved an average success rate of 0.73 on the SetTable subset of ManiSkill-HAB, which is a 22% improvement over the best input modality configuration without auxiliary tasks.
- Individual auxiliary tasks, particularly joint position prediction, provided substantial improvements, yielding an average success rate of 0.71 and excelling in place tasks (0.67) and drawer operations (0.70 Open, 0.90 Close).
- The flow matching action head improved pick tasks (from 0.13 to 0.27) and place tasks (0.70 to 0.80), demonstrating its value for precise manipulation, though it showed decreased performance on some mobility-oriented tasks like opening fridges or drawers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Enriching VLA models with multi-view RGB and explicit depth information significantly improves performance in mobile manipulation, leading to a substantial increase in success rates from 0.04 to 0.60.
> - Auxiliary tasks provide crucial dense supervision, enabling the VLM backbone to learn more spatially grounded and manipulation-aware latent representations that are critical for complex tasks.
> - A progressive multi-stage training approach is essential for effectively integrating diverse auxiliary objectives without degrading the VLM's foundational capabilities, outperforming naive co-training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a significant paradigm for robotic control, leveraging large-scale pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to translate natural language commands into robotic actions. This field has demonstrated capabilities in learning a range of skills and improving generalization and semantic understanding in robotic control.

Much of the observed success in VLA models, however, has been primarily in constrained, tabletop environments involving single or dual-arm manipulators. These settings typically feature static objects and less complex action spaces. The broader research landscape is increasingly focused on extending these capabilities to more complex, real-world domestic scenarios, which necessitate mobile manipulation. Mobile manipulation tasks introduce substantial challenges not present in tabletop settings, including partial observability, high-dimensional continuous action spaces that integrate navigation and manipulation, and a heightened requirement for robust spatial and scene-level reasoning.

Current approaches to household robotics often fall into two categories: modular systems that decompose tasks into separate navigation and manipulation components, or end-to-end learning methods that frequently struggle with the integrated complexity of unified control. Modular systems, while effective, can limit the direct influence of a foundation model's rich pre-trained knowledge on fine motor control. End-to-end VLA models, when directly applied to mobile manipulation, have shown performance limitations, suggesting insufficient leverage of their semantic reasoning for these complex tasks.

This work addresses the gap between VLA models designed for constrained environments and the demands of mobile manipulation in household settings. It investigates methods to enhance VLA models to better handle complex spatial understanding and high-dimensional control for tasks like picking, placing, opening, and closing in dynamic environments, positioning itself as an advancement towards more general-purpose domestic robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework for learning spatially-grounded Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that can effectively control mobile manipulators in complex household environments. The authors hypothesize that existing VLA models, when trained solely through direct imitation learning, provide an insufficient supervisory signal to acquire the rich, multi-faceted understanding required for these tasks.

The key motivations for this work stem from several challenges inherent in mobile manipulation within domestic settings:

- **Complex Action Space:** Mobile manipulation necessitates controlling a high-dimensional continuous action space (13-dimensions in this work), which involves coordinated base motion (3D pose), arm articulation (7-dimensional joint angles), torso height (1D scalar), and gripper actuation (2D for fingers). Standard imitation learning methods often struggle to effectively learn robust control policies for such complex, integrated action spaces.
- **Lack of Spatially Grounded Perception:** Robots operating in dynamic household environments must simultaneously reason about global scene layout for navigation, fine-grained object geometry for interaction, and their own state relative to the environment. Conventional VLA models often lack the mechanisms to robustly integrate these complementary spatial perspectives.
- **Insufficient Supervisory Signal:** Direct imitation learning, which typically maps observations and language instructions directly to actions, provides sparse feedback, particularly for nuanced spatial relationships and intermediate states critical for complex manipulation. This limited supervision can hinder the model's ability to develop interpretable and robust latent representations.
- **Bridging the Tabletop-to-Mobile Gap:** There is a recognized performance gap when transferring VLA models, which have shown success in constrained tabletop scenarios, to the complexities of mobile manipulation in unstructured domestic environments. The goal is to address this gap by enhancing both the model's sensory inputs and its learning objectives.

To address these motivations, the research introduces the SG-VLA framework, which aims to strengthen both perception and representation learning through two main strategies:
1. **Enriching Sensory Input:** Incorporating multi-modal inputs, including multi-view RGB observations, depth cues, and short temporal history, to provide a more comprehensive spatial understanding of both global scene structure and local manipulation context.
2. **Denser Learning Signals via Auxiliary Co-training:** Co-training a suite of auxiliary decoders to reconstruct interpretable intermediate signals (such as global robot position, joint configurations, grasp affordances, target-object relative pose, and segmentation masks) from shared visual-language features. These auxiliary objectives are intended to provide dense supervision, encouraging the backbone to develop spatially grounded and manipulation-aware latent representations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SG-VLA is designed as a lightweight 1.3B parameter model leveraging a pre-trained VLM backbone and an optional action expert for mobile manipulation.

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

The core of SG-VLA consists of a pre-trained Prismatic VLM backbone, which itself comprises:
- **Visual Encoder:** Fuses features from DINOv2 (for spatial understanding) and SigLIP (for semantic representations) using a dual-encoder approach, processing both RGB and depth inputs from multiple camera viewpoints.
- **Large Language Model (LLM) Backbone:** Qwen2.5-0.5B, serving as the central reasoning component, processing textual instructions and integrating multi-modal information.
- **Trainable Projector:** Maps high-dimensional vision features into the language embedding space for seamless fusion.

Additionally, an optional 100M parameter Flow Matching action expert, based on $\pi_0$, can generate continuous, high-precision actions conditioned on the latent representations from the VLM backbone. This dual-prediction architecture allows flexibility: the model can predict discrete action tokens directly from the LLM or leverage the action expert for continuous actions, depending on task requirements. The output is a 13-dimensional action vector for base pose, torso height, arm joint angles, and gripper state.

#### 3.2. Input Modalities

To enhance spatial and situational awareness, SG-VLA systematically integrates several input modalities:
- **Multi-view RGB Inputs:** Combines observations from both a head-mounted camera (global perspective) and a hand-mounted camera (close-up manipulation context).
- **Depth Information:** Incorporates normalized depth maps from both head and hand cameras to provide explicit geometric understanding of object distances and spatial relationships, normalized via the formula $p_{obs} = 1 - \tanh(\frac{\text{depth value}}{1000})$.
- **Temporal Context:** Includes historical observations from the past 4 timesteps, aiming to leverage sequential information for action planning.

The combination of multi-view RGB and depth inputs is explored for its contribution to complex spatial relationship understanding.

#### 3.3. Auxiliary Decoders

Five auxiliary decoders operate on the VLM backbone’s latent representations, providing complementary supervision signals for various aspects of robotic manipulation. These decoders encourage the backbone to learn more interpretable and spatially aware representations.

- **MLP-based Decoders:**
 - **Global Position Decoder:** Predicts the robot's 2D (x, y) coordinates using an MLP Projector and a 3-layer MLP. The loss is Mean Squared Error (MSE) (Eq. 2).
 - **Grasp Success Decoder:** Performs binary classification to predict the grasping state (true/false) using an MLP Projector and a 3-layer MLP. The loss is Cross-Entropy (Eq. 2).
 - **Object Pose Decoder:** Predicts 7-dimensional object poses (3D position + quaternion orientation) using an MLP Projector, a 3-layer MLP, and quaternion normalization. The loss combines MSE for position and a term for quaternion similarity (Eq. 3).
 These decoders use lightweight MLPs as their targets are relatively low-dimensional vectors.

- **Transformer-based Joint Pose Decoder:**
 - Predicts the 12-dimensional joint configuration of the robot's arm using a Transformer architecture with learnable mask tokens that attend to VLM features. This design addresses the interdependencies across joint angles, leveraging self-attention to capture kinematic structure. Fixed sine-cosine positional encodings and MSE loss are used (Eq. 4).

- **CNN-based Mask Decoder:**
 - Generates 128x128 binary segmentation masks for target objects using a 4-stage Transpose Convolution network with Batch Normalization and GELU activation. CNNs are chosen for their effectiveness and efficiency in dense image reconstruction. Binary Cross-Entropy loss is used (Eq. 4).

The overall training utilizes a multi-task loss function (Eq. 5), which is a weighted sum of the main action prediction loss and each auxiliary loss, allowing independent operation of decoders on shared VLM representations.

#### 3.4. Training Scheme

A progressive multi-stage training approach is adopted to effectively integrate auxiliary decoders and the flow-matching action head with the pre-trained VLM backbone, while preserving its foundational capabilities. This addresses preliminary findings that direct co-training with randomly initialized decoders can degrade performance due to noisy gradients.

- **Stage 1: Decoder Adaptation:** The gradient flow from auxiliary decoders to the VLM backbone is frozen. Only the discrete action token prediction path from the VLM updates the backbone. Decoders learn to interpret fixed VLM representations without disrupting the backbone, while the VLM continues learning discrete action token prediction.
- **Stage 2: Joint Refinement:** Full gradient flow is enabled, allowing auxiliary task losses to backpropagate through the entire network. This stage refines VLM representations to better capture manipulation-relevant features under combined supervision, benefiting the primary action prediction.
- **Stage 3: Isolated Action Head Training:** All parameters of the pre-trained VLM backbone are completely frozen. Only the Flow Matching action head is trained. This isolation allows the action head's denoising objective to converge without interference, developing robust continuous action generation capabilities from the frozen latent representations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SG-VLA was conducted on the ManiSkill-HAB benchmark, using simulation-based trajectories for home rearrangement tasks like TidyHouse, PrepareGroceries, and SetTable, which decompose into Pick, Place, Open, and Close subtasks.

#### 4.1. Input Modalities

Table 2 presents a comparison of models utilizing different input modalities, trained on the SetTable dataset.
- **Baseline Performance:** The original OpenVLA model (∼7B parameters) with single-view RGB achieved an average success rate of 0.04, indicating limitations in complex mobile manipulation with standard VLA configurations.
- **Multi-view and Depth Impact:** Enhancing OpenVLA with multi-view and depth inputs improved the average success rate to 0.32 (an 8-fold increase), with notable gains in drawer manipulation tasks.
- **Qwen-based Model Performance:** A smaller Qwen2.5-0.5B based VLM (Base VLM) with multi-view and depth inputs achieved an average success rate of 0.60, nearly doubling the performance of the enhanced OpenVLA variant.
- **Modality Ablation:**
 - Multi-view inputs alone (Base VLM) resulted in an average success rate of 0.52.
 - Adding depth information further increased performance to 0.60, a 15% relative improvement, underscoring the value of explicit geometric data.
 - Incorporating temporal history (past 4 timesteps) led to a performance degradation to 0.49, suggesting the model struggled to effectively utilize or integrate this information for the evaluated tasks.

#### 4.2. Auxiliary Tasks and Progressive Training

Table 3 evaluates the impact of auxiliary tasks and the progressive training scheme, with models trained on the SetTable subset.
- **Naive Co-training Failure:** Directly co-training all auxiliary decoders simultaneously with the VLM backbone ("+ all" without progressive training) resulted in a degraded average success rate of 0.51, compared to the 0.60 of the best input modality configuration without auxiliary tasks. This supports the hypothesis that noisy gradients from untrained decoders interfere with VLM representations.
- **Progressive Training Effectiveness:** The progressive training approach, when applied to all auxiliary tasks ("+ all" with progressive training), achieved an average success rate of 0.73, representing a 22% improvement over the best input modality configuration alone (0.60). This validates the two-stage training strategy.
- **Individual Auxiliary Task Contributions:**
 - **Joint Position (qpos):** Provided the most substantial and consistent improvements, yielding an average success rate of 0.71. It particularly excelled in place tasks (0.67) and drawer operations (0.70 Open, 0.90 Close), suggesting enhanced understanding of manipulation dynamics.
 - **Grasp Label (is grasped):** Offered moderate but reliable improvements (0.66 average), with notable gains in pick tasks (0.30), indicating better manipulation state awareness.
 - **Global Position (global pos):** Showed task-specific benefits, significantly improving fridge opening (0.90) and drawer opening (1.00) tasks, suggesting value for navigation-heavy scenarios, but struggled with pick-and-place.
- **Segmentation and Object Position (seg+obj pos):** Table 4 demonstrates that adding segmentation masks and object position prediction consistently benefited all pick and place tasks across SetTable, PrepareGroceries, and TidyHouse, increasing the average success rate from 0.27 to 0.47. Place tasks showed substantial gains (55-81% improvements), while pick tasks showed consistent, albeit more modest, improvements.
- **Synergistic Effects:** The combined auxiliary training ("+ all" with progressive training) generally matched or exceeded the best individual auxiliary task performance, indicating complementary rather than redundant information.

#### 4.3. Action Head

Table 5 compares SG-VLA with and without the flow matching action head.
- **Mixed Effects:** The flow matching action head resulted in an overall average performance decrease from 0.73 to 0.69, suggesting selective benefits.
- **Task-Specific Performance:**
 - **Pick Tasks:** Benefited substantially, more than doubling success rates from 0.13 to 0.27, indicating value for precise grasping.
 - **Place Tasks:** Showed moderate improvements (0.70 to 0.80), suggesting help with precise positioning.
 - **Open Tasks (Fridge and Drawer):** Experienced notable performance drops (Fridge: 0.87 to 0.76; Drawer: 0.77 to 0.60). This suggests the continuous action generation struggled with mobility-oriented control, where discrete action tokens might be more suitable.
These results support a task-adaptive action generation approach, leveraging continuous control for manipulation-heavy tasks and discrete actions for mechanism operations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work demonstrates that Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models can be effectively adapted for complex mobile manipulation tasks in household environments through specific architectural and training enhancements. The SG-VLA framework introduces a methodology that leverages both enriched multi-modal sensory inputs and dense supervisory signals from auxiliary tasks, applied through a progressive training scheme.

The findings indicate that the combination of multi-view depth inputs and structured auxiliary supervision contributes to the development of more spatially grounded and interpretable latent representations within the VLA backbone. This enhanced understanding of the scene, robot state, and object properties leads to improved control precision and higher success rates in tasks involving picking, placing, and operating articulated objects like fridges and drawers.

The progressive multi-stage training strategy is shown to be crucial for integrating diverse auxiliary objectives without degrading the VLM's foundational capabilities, offering a blueprint for effective multi-task learning in similar contexts. Furthermore, the analysis of the flow matching action head highlights the potential benefits of continuous action generation for fine-grained manipulation, while also pointing to the need for task-adaptive control strategies that might selectively utilize discrete actions for broader mobility or mechanism operations.

The significance of this research lies in its contribution to scaling VLA models beyond confined tabletop settings to the complexities of real-world domestic robotics. The improvements demonstrated suggest a viable direction for developing more capable and general-purpose robots that can interact with and navigate unstructured household environments. Potential impact includes accelerating the development of domestic assistance robots, improving automation in home environments, and providing a foundation for future research exploring additional modalities, auxiliary objectives, and real-world deployment.
