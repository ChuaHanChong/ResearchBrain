# No Free Checker: A Survey of Verifiers for Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of roughly 150 verifiers for robot policies: mechanisms mapping context and candidate behavior to a score, used to evaluate and to train vision-language-action policies. It groups them by judge source into human, rule-based/formal, learned/pretrained, and model-intrinsic families, and compares them on availability versus credibility. The central finding is that credibility falls as availability rises: no free checker.

## Problem

- Verifier claims rest on a single agreement rate that omits the false-positive/false-negative split, the confidence interval, and what kind of rollout was scored.
- Robotics has not measured how far a policy can be optimized against a verifier before its score stops tracking actual task performance; reward hacking stays undeveloped here.
- Conformal margins shipped with verifiers assume exchangeability, but training a policy against a verifier shifts the deployment distribution and voids the stated coverage.

## Method

- Two comparison axes: ==availability== (verdict cost, earliness, density) and ==credibility== (how much a high score asserts about the task), applied across a ==judge-source taxonomy== of four verifier families.
- Four uses ordered by whether the score is searched against: curation and ranking tolerate average error, while training reward and runtime gating require no exploitable wrong-scoring region.
- A ==nine-metric reporting checklist== making verifier claims comparable, including ==proxy gain== that transfers, the ratio of panel-confirmed gain to training-verifier gain across checkpoints.

## Results

- Auditing **thirteen** verifier and policy-evaluation resources, two world-model evaluators never measured verifier error on the generated rollouts they actually score, the deployment distribution.
- GE-Sim 2.0 scores one verifier twice and finds an **eight-point** accuracy gap between generated and recorded rollouts, attributed to world-model artifacts rather than task difficulty.
- Every agreement rate and downstream gain collected was measured on non-adversarial candidates; benchmarks splitting safety from success agree that policies completing more tasks are not safer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The closer the judge sits to the model being scored, the cheaper and denser the verdict, and the harder it becomes to show the score reflects the task.
> - A score consumed as a training reward or runtime gate needs exploitability evidence, not an agreement rate measured on candidates nobody searched against.
> - Model-intrinsic scores describe the model, not the task, so a failure that looks familiar to the policy still receives a high score.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the rapidly evolving fields of robot learning, particularly concerning vision-language-action (VLA) policies and robot world models. As these models scale in capability and complexity, the challenge of verifying their behavior and the quality of their training data becomes increasingly critical. The authors draw a parallel to the recent progress in large language models (LLMs), where verification signals are essential for robust development.

Historically, verification in robotics has been challenging due to the inherent complexities of the physical world, such as costly and imperfect sensory observations, expensive real-robot rollouts, the need for real-time verification to prevent failure, and the continuous nature of task performance. Existing verification mechanisms ("verifiers") are diverse, ranging from simple success detectors to complex temporal-logic specifications. This survey addresses the need for a systematic understanding of these diverse approaches, their underlying trade-offs, and how they are currently validated. It provides a structured overview of the current state of the art, synthesizing approximately 150 distinct methods to clarify their characteristics, strengths, and limitations within the context of the physical world's constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this survey are:

1. **Systematically Characterize Existing Verifiers:** To provide a comprehensive overview of approximately 150 verifiers currently employed in robot learning, grouping them into distinct families based on the source of judgment.
2. **Analyze Trade-offs between Availability and Credibility:** To characterize verifiers along two key properties: "availability" (cost, speed, and frequency of verdicts) and "credibility" (how accurately a high score reflects actual task performance, and resistance to gaming). The survey aims to identify inherent trade-offs between these two properties.
3. **Examine Verifier Validation Methods:** To investigate the methods currently used to validate verifiers themselves, identifying their strengths and weaknesses, particularly in the context of optimizing against a verifier.
4. **Propose Standardized Metrics:** To put forth a set of concrete, checkable metrics for evaluating verifiers, thereby promoting more rigorous and comparable reporting in future research.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in robot verification:

- **Costly and Imperfect Task Success Inference:** Determining successful task completion in the physical world is expensive, often relying on imperfect sensory observations.
- **Expensive Policy Evaluation:** Testing robot policies frequently requires real-robot rollouts, which consume significant physical time and limit large-scale sampling.
- **Need for Early Verification:** Verification often needs to occur before a task is fully completed to prevent failures or hardware damage.
- **Continuous Performance Evaluation:** Robot behaviors often exhibit continuous variations in progress, safety, and execution quality, requiring more nuanced feedback than simple success/failure labels.
- **Lack of Systematic Understanding:** The proliferation of diverse verifier approaches necessitates a structured analysis to understand their comparative strengths and limitations in robot learning applications, especially in the context of rapidly scaling models. The authors specifically highlight the observation that "there is no free checker," meaning that obtaining both high availability and high credibility simultaneously is inherently costly in the physical world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors conducted a survey of approximately 150 works related to verification in robot learning. Their methodological approach involved:

1. **Definition of a Verifier:** A verifier is formally defined as a mapping `verifier : (context, candidate) -> score`, where the context includes observations and goals, and the candidate can range from a single action to an entire policy. The score can be boolean, scalar, vector-valued, or a distribution.
2. **Characterization Properties:** All surveyed verifiers were characterized along two core properties:
 - **Availability:** Quantified by the cost of a verdict, how early in a rollout the verdict arrives, and how often a verdict can be requested. Higher availability implies cheaper, earlier, and denser verdicts.
 - **Credibility:** Quantified by how much a high score indicates actual task performance. Credibility decreases if the judgment is easily gameable or self-serving.
3. **Categorization by Judge Source:** The verifiers were grouped into four families based on who or what supplies the judgment:
 - **Human Verifiers (Section 2):** Judgment provided directly by a person (e.g., comparing trajectories, scoring trajectories, intervening during execution, validating generated rollouts).
 - **Rule-Based and Formal Verifiers (Section 3):** Judgment determined by predefined criteria (e.g., task predicates, temporal-logic specifications, formal safety conditions). This family is further divided by what the criterion reads: full trajectory, terminal state, or physical constraint.
 - **Learned and Pretrained Verifiers (Section 4):** Judgment produced by a neural model, either trained specifically for the task or directly from pretraining. These are analyzed based on their application roles: evaluating behavior/policies, inference-time verification, and providing feedback for policy learning.
 - **Model-Intrinsic Verifiers (Section 5):** Judgment derived from signals already computed by the policy or predictive model (e.g., action uncertainty, model uncertainty, learned reachability). These are classified into policy-based self-verification, prediction-based outcome verification, and task selection.
4. **Analysis of Verifier Validation (Meta-verification):** The authors dedicate a section (Section 6) to how verifiers themselves are validated. They identify three existing measures in the literature:
 - Agreement with human labels on a fixed set of rollouts.
 - The performance of the policy trained using the verifier.
 - The verifier's behavior under reward hacking (optimization attempts to satisfy the verifier without achieving the true task).
5. **Proposal of New Metrics:** Based on their analysis of validation shortcomings, the authors propose nine specific metrics designed to make verifier claims more checkable and comparable across different studies. These metrics address aspects like rollout count, error rates by type, rollout type, human agreement, calibration, cross-embodiment transfer, proxy gain transfer, verifier error over training, and false positives under search.

The survey structure systematically presents each verifier family, its typical applications, and its relative position on the availability-credibility spectrum. This comparative framework allows the authors to articulate their central finding, the "no free checker" principle.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core finding of this survey is the **"no free checker"** principle: there is a fundamental trade-off between the availability and credibility of verifiers for robot policies. As the source of judgment moves closer to the model being evaluated (i.e., becoming more autonomous), a verification signal becomes cheaper, faster, and denser (higher availability), but the evidence that the signal genuinely reflects actual task performance becomes more challenging to establish (lower credibility).

**Findings Across Verifier Families:**

- **Human Verifiers (High Credibility, Low Availability):** Human judgments offer the most direct reference to task intent, ensuring high credibility. However, they are costly, subjective, inconsistent, and sparse due to the human effort required. They primarily serve to validate or train other, cheaper verifiers. Methods include comparing trajectories (e.g., RoboArena), scoring trajectories (e.g., Reward Sketching), intervening during execution (e.g., HG-DAgger, Sirius), and validating generated rollouts (e.g., GigaWorld-1).
- **Rule-Based and Formal Verifiers (High Credibility, Moderate Availability):** These verifiers are inexpensive and repeatable once the necessary state information is available, offering strong, sometimes formal, guarantees. Their credibility is high, provided that predefined criteria, state estimates, and dynamics assumptions accurately represent the task. This family includes temporal-logic scoring (e.g., ConformalSTL, TGPO), geometry-based scoring (e.g., RINSE), model-written rules (e.g., Eureka, Code-as-Monitor), terminal-state goal predicates (e.g., LIBERO, CALVIN), invariance certificates (e.g., PNCBF), and symbolic feasibility checks (e.g., Text2Motion). A key limitation is their reliance on accurate state information and assumptions, which may not hold in real-world perception-based systems.
- **Learned and Pretrained Verifiers (Moderate Credibility, High Availability):** Neural models provide judgments that are inexpensive to query and can offer dense feedback across many tasks and trajectories. Their credibility, however, depends on model accuracy, calibration, and generalization beyond the training/validation data. These verifiers are used for evaluating behavior and policies (e.g., VIP, Robometer, TOPReward, WorldEval), inference-time verification (e.g., RoboMonkey, KnowNo, Foresight), and feeding feedback into policy learning (e.g., RECAP, RoboCat, DataMIL). This is the largest group in the survey, indicating a trend towards leveraging large models for verification.
- **Model-Intrinsic Verifiers (Lowest Credibility, Highest Availability):** These verifiers use signals already computed by the policy or predictive model (e.g., action uncertainty, latent discrepancy, reachability value), making them the cheapest to obtain as they require minimal additional computation or external supervision. However, these signals describe the model itself rather than direct task performance, resulting in indirect and often lower credibility. Examples include policy-based self-verification (e.g., SAFE, Sentinel, MG-Select), prediction-based outcome verification (e.g., VIPER, MOPO, Nakamura et al.'s latent-space reachability), and task selection based on model-derived values (e.g., ACCEL). A key limitation is the risk of familiar-looking failures receiving high scores.

**Findings on Validating the Verifier:**

- **Existing Validation Metrics are Insufficient:** The survey identified three common validation metrics: agreement with a fixed reference (often human labels), the performance of the policy trained by the verifier, and behavior under optimization (reward hacking).
- **Limitations of Agreement Rates:** Agreement rates with human labels (e.g., ManiRewardBench, RoboReward) often lack crucial details such as the split between false positives/negatives, confidence intervals, and the specific type of rollout (e.g., generated vs. real, simulation vs. hardware). They also fail to account for errors in the human reference itself. The reported rates are specific to the rollouts they were computed on and may not generalize.
- **Divergence of Agreement and Downstream Gain:** There is a noted divergence between a verifier's agreement rate and the actual performance gain of a policy trained with it. Training intrinsically drives policies toward inputs where the verifier's score might be wrong but appear high.
- **Reward Hacking is Underexplored in Robotics:** The critical issue of reward hacking (policies satisfying the verifier without achieving the true task) is well-studied in LLMs but largely undeveloped in robotics. Existing measurements of verifier quality are often taken on non-adversarial candidates, thereby overstating the verifier's value when used in a training loop or as a runtime gate. Optimization against a verifier can invalidate its initial calibration and increase its error.

**Proposed Metrics for Validating a Verifier:**

The authors propose nine specific metrics for evaluating verifiers, designed to address current shortcomings and enhance comparability:

1. **Independent rollout count:** Number of trials per condition for success rates.
2. **Error rate by type:** False positives and false negatives counted separately.
3. **Rollout type:** Whether rollouts are teleoperated, scripted, or policy-generated.
4. **Agreement with human labels:** Agreement rate, label count, and inter-annotator agreement.
5. **Calibration:** Score minus observed success rate within each score bin.
6. **Cross-embodiment transfer:** Error on held-out vs. training embodiments.
7. **Proxy gain that transfers:** Ratio of true gain (from a panel of unseen verifiers) to proxy gain (from the training verifier), reporting both absolute differences.
8. **Verifier error over training:** Error on initial vs. final checkpoint rollouts.
9. **False positives under search:** Candidates found by actively searching for inputs the verifier wrongly accepts.

These metrics aim to provide a more comprehensive picture of a verifier's reliability, especially when it is used in closed-loop optimization or safety-critical applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey makes several significant contributions and has the potential for considerable impact on the field of robot learning:

- **Comprehensive Systematization:** By organizing a large body of work (approximately 150 papers) into a coherent taxonomy, the survey provides a much-needed structured understanding of robot policy verifiers. This systematization clarifies the landscape, making it easier for researchers and practitioners to navigate the diverse approaches.
- **Identification of Fundamental Trade-offs:** The articulation of the "no free checker" principle—the inverse relationship between a verifier's availability and its credibility—is a critical insight. This principle highlights an inherent challenge in developing robot policies and serves as a guiding principle for future research and design decisions. It forces a conscious choice between ease of use and trustworthiness.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The survey identifies significant gaps in current research, particularly regarding the robust validation of verifiers when subjected to optimization. It pinpoints the need for methods that achieve both high availability and high credibility simultaneously. This points towards future directions such as hybrid verifier systems, more robust learned models, and advanced meta-verification techniques that are resistant to reward hacking.
- **Call for Standardized Evaluation:** The proposal of nine concrete metrics for evaluating verifiers directly addresses a lack of standardized and comprehensive reporting in the field. Adopting these metrics could lead to more rigorous empirical evaluations, making research findings more comparable, reproducible, and trustworthy. This could significantly accelerate progress by enabling clearer benchmarks for verifier performance.
- **Informing Practical Applications:** The detailed analysis of each verifier family, including their strengths, weaknesses, and typical applications, provides practical guidance for selecting appropriate verification mechanisms at various stages of robot learning. This includes deciding on suitable verifiers for filtering training data, generating reward signals, ranking candidate actions, or evaluating final policy performance. For instance, it highlights that learned and model-intrinsic verifiers, while highly available, necessitate careful validation of their actual task performance.
- **Addressing Safety and Reliability Concerns:** By emphasizing the limitations of verifiers, especially under optimization, the work underscores critical concerns for robot safety and reliability. Understanding how verifiers can be gamed is paramount for deploying robots in real-world scenarios, particularly in safety-critical applications. The call for metrics on "false positives under search" directly addresses this.

In summary, "No Free Checker" acts as a foundational reference, offering both a panoramic view of the current state of robot policy verification and a critical analysis of its challenges, while providing a roadmap for more rigorous and impactful future developments.
