# Can Vision-Language Models See Squares? Text-Recognition Mediates Spatial Reasoning Across Three Model Families

> [!summary] Summary
> This research reveals that Vision-Language Models (VLMs) exhibit a substantial and consistent degradation in spatial localization when processing non-textual visual information compared to text symbols, suggesting a reliance on an internal text-recognition pathway for high-fidelity spatial reasoning. Across Claude Opus, ChatGPT 5.2, and Gemini 3 Thinking, F1 scores for black-cell detection dropped by 34.1 to 53.8 points when grids were presented as abstract squares instead of text.

## Problem

- A gap exists in understanding the underlying mechanisms of spatial reasoning in VLMs, particularly whether their capabilities are uniform across diverse visual inputs.
- The prevailing assumption that VLMs possess robust and generalizable spatial reasoning abilities for arbitrary visual content needed to be critically examined.
- There was a need to isolate spatial localization from semantic understanding to probe fundamental limitations in VLM visual processing without confounding factors.

## Method

- Fifteen 15x15 binary grids were programmatically generated with varying densities.
- Each grid was rendered under two ==visual encoding conditions==: as text symbols (periods and hash marks) and as unsegmented abstract squares.
- Three frontier VLMs (Claude Opus, ChatGPT 5.2, Gemini 3 Thinking) were prompted to transcribe these grids, and performance was measured using ==cell accuracy and black-cell F1 score==.

## Results

- All three frontier VLMs showed a substantial performance drop in F1 score for black-cell detection when processing unsegmented squares compared to text symbols, with gaps ranging from **34.1** to **53.8** percentage points.
- Claude Opus and ChatGPT 5.2 maintained stable F1 scores across varying densities in the text condition, while Gemini 3 Thinking experienced a catastrophic performance collapse at higher text densities.
- In the unsegmented squares condition, Gemini 3 Thinking achieved the highest F1 scores on sparse grids (averaging **68.0%** for ≤**20%** density) but rapidly collapsed at higher densities, frequently generating hallucinated geometric patterns.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLMs appear to have two distinct implicit processing pathways for spatial information: a robust text-recognition pathway and a significantly weaker visual-feature pathway for non-textual content.
> - The perceived strong spatial reasoning in VLMs might be largely an artifact of their implicit OCR capabilities, leading to an overestimation of their general visual spatial acuity.
> - Different VLMs exhibit distinct qualitative failure modes in the non-textual condition, ranging from under-counting and displacement (Claude) to massive over-counting (ChatGPT) and hallucinating stereotyped patterns (Gemini).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The study addresses a gap in the understanding of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) capabilities in spatial reasoning. Prior research has demonstrated VLMs' proficiency in various visual understanding tasks, leading to an implicit assumption of robust spatial reasoning over diverse visual inputs. However, a growing body of work has begun to evaluate and identify limitations in VLM spatial reasoning, particularly concerning fine-grained configurations, 3D spatial understanding, and counting tasks.

Existing benchmarks like Visual Spatial Reasoning (VSR), SpatialBench, SpatialVLM, SpatialRGPT, and comprehensive surveys have revealed persistent weaknesses in VLMs' ability to perform detailed spatial tasks, including metric depth estimation, proximity, and compositional transformations. Research has also consistently noted VLMs' struggles with counting objects and fine-grained localization, with efforts made to improve these specific aspects in models like CLIP. Furthermore, limitations in visual encoders, such as CLIP, which are trained for global semantic alignment rather than fine-grained spatial features, have been identified. Recent proposals aim to enhance fine-grained visual understanding through region-text contrastive objectives. The role of OCR and document understanding, where VLMs demonstrate implicit text recognition, is also an active area of study, with models like DocVLM explicitly integrating OCR capabilities.

This work contributes to this landscape by isolating spatial localization from semantic understanding, using a highly simplified visual stimulus—binary grids. Unlike other benchmarks that focus on complex natural or 3D scenes, this paper employs a minimal setup to probe a fundamental aspect of VLM visual processing. By comparing performance on text-encoded grids versus non-textual square-encoded grids, the study aims to uncover whether VLMs' spatial reasoning relies predominantly on an internal text-recognition pathway, even when processing image data, rather than a general, high-fidelity visual pathway for arbitrary objects. This approach differentiates it from studies that aim to enhance 3D perception or compositional counting in complex environments, instead focusing on the most basic form of spatial discrimination.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the underlying mechanisms of spatial reasoning in frontier Vision-Language Models (VLMs), specifically examining whether their apparent spatial capabilities are mediated by text recognition. The authors aim to challenge the prevailing assumption that VLMs possess robust spatial reasoning abilities that apply uniformly across arbitrary visual inputs.

The motivation stems from the observation that while VLMs demonstrate proficiency in tasks such as image description, chart interpretation, and diagrammatic reasoning, the precise nature of their visual processing for spatial information remains ambiguous. The hypothesis is that VLMs may implicitly rely on an internal Optical Character Recognition (OCR)-like pathway to achieve high-fidelity spatial reasoning when visual elements can be interpreted as text. Conversely, their "native" visual pathway, responsible for processing non-textual elements, might exhibit significantly weaker spatial localization capabilities.

To test this hypothesis, the study's specific objectives include:
1. **Exposing Fundamental Limitations:** To design a simple experiment capable of revealing potential fundamental limitations in VLM spatial localization when visual elements lack a textual identity.
2. **Cross-Model Validation:** To determine if any observed limitations are specific to a single VLM architecture or represent a more general property across independent model families. This involves evaluating models from different organizations with distinct architectures and training data.
3. **Quantifying Performance Discrepancy:** To quantify the performance gap between spatial reasoning on visually identical information presented as text symbols versus abstract filled squares.
4. **Characterizing Failure Modes:** To qualitatively analyze the distinct ways different VLMs fail in the non-textual condition, providing insight into the nature of their limitations.

By using binary grids, the researchers sought to create a controlled environment where spatial localization is the primary challenge, minimizing confounding factors related to semantic understanding or complex scene analysis. This deliberate simplification was intended to isolate the core mechanism of how VLMs process positional information from visual stimuli.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a controlled experimental design to assess the spatial reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) under different visual encoding conditions.

#### 3.1. Grid Generation

Fifteen binary grids, each 15x15 cells in size (225 cells total), were programmatically generated. The grids varied in density, with the percentage of filled cells ranging from 10.7% (24 filled cells) to 41.8% (94 filled cells). The distribution of filled cells was designed to include both isolated cells and contiguous clusters, introducing variability in localization difficulty.

#### 3.2. Visual Encoding Conditions

Each of the 15 grids was rendered as a PNG image under two primary visual encoding conditions:
- **Text symbols:** Empty cells were represented by a period ('.') and filled cells by a hash symbol ('#'), both rendered using a monospace font (DejaVu Sans Mono, 28pt). These images were processed by the VLM's visual encoder as photographs of text.
- **Unsegmented squares:** Filled cells were rendered as black squares and empty cells as white squares, with no visible gridlines separating them. This meant that adjacent filled cells merged into larger, contiguous black regions.

A third condition, "segmented squares" (with gridlines), was initially tested on Claude Opus. However, its performance was observed to be nearly identical to the unsegmented squares condition, leading to its exclusion from the cross-model evaluation for parsimony.

#### 3.3. Models and Prompting

Three frontier Vision-Language Models from different organizations were evaluated:
- **Claude Opus:** Accessed via the claude.ai web interface (version claude-opus-4-6).
- **ChatGPT 5.2:** Accessed via the chatgpt.com Plus Auto mode.
- **Gemini 3 Thinking:** Accessed via gemini.google.com with "extended thinking" enabled.

Each model received a standardized instruction prompt that explicitly specified the desired output format (a 15x15 grid of '.' and '#' characters). The prompt also prohibited the use of external code or image-processing tools, requiring the models to transcribe the grids solely based on visual inspection. Each visual encoding condition (text symbols and unsegmented squares) was run in a separate, clean session for each model. Grids were presented in batches of five.

#### 3.4. Metrics

Model performance was quantified using two primary metrics, computed over all 3,375 cells (15 grids × 225 cells):
- **Cell Accuracy:** The fraction of all 225 cells (filled or empty) that were correctly classified by the model.
- **Black-cell F1 Score:** The harmonic mean of precision and recall, specifically for the detection of filled cells. The F1 score was considered the more informative metric because overall cell accuracy can be inflated by the high number of correctly classified empty cells, which are generally easier to identify.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments revealed a consistent and substantial performance disparity across all evaluated Vision-Language Models (VLMs) when comparing their ability to localize spatial information based on text symbols versus abstract filled squares.

#### 4.1. Single-Model Results (Claude Opus)

Initial evaluation of Claude Opus demonstrated a significant performance difference between the text-symbol and square conditions.
- **Text symbols:** Claude Opus achieved a cell accuracy of 90.6% and an F1 score of 83.4%.
- **Segmented squares:** This condition resulted in 64.9% cell accuracy and 32.6% F1.
- **Unsegmented squares:** Performance was similar to segmented squares, with 66.9% cell accuracy and 29.6% F1.
The minimal difference between segmented and unsegmented squares indicated that gridlines provided negligible benefit for spatial localization.

#### 4.2. Cross-Model Replication

The performance gap between text and squares conditions was replicated across all three evaluated models:
- **Claude Opus:** The F1 score dropped from 83.4% (text) to 29.6% (unsegmented squares), representing a gap of 53.8 points.
- **ChatGPT 5.2:** The F1 score decreased from 84.0% (text) to 39.2% (unsegmented squares), a gap of 44.8 points.
- **Gemini 3 Thinking:** This model exhibited an F1 score drop from 62.8% (text) to 28.7% (unsegmented squares), a gap of 34.1 points.

While all models showed a substantial text-vs-squares F1 gap, Gemini's smaller gap was attributed to its comparatively lower performance in the text condition rather than superior performance in the squares condition.

#### 4.3. Density Effects Across Models

Analysis of performance across varying grid densities revealed distinct patterns:
- **Text Condition:**
 - Claude Opus and ChatGPT 5.2 maintained stable F1 scores across the entire density range (10.7% to 41.8% filled cells), indicating robust text recognition.
 - Gemini 3 Thinking performed comparably at low densities (up to ~32% filled cells, 64-100% F1) but experienced a catastrophic collapse at higher densities (≥35% filled cells, 11-46% F1). At high densities, Gemini often generated hallucinated geometric patterns rather than transcribing the input text.
- **Unsegmented Squares Condition:**
 - All three models exhibited generally poor F1 scores across densities.
 - Gemini 3 Thinking achieved the highest F1 scores on sparse grids (grids 1-5, ≤20% density), averaging 68.0% F1, compared to 44.3% for ChatGPT and 30.2% for Claude. This suggested Gemini's visual pathway might be more precise for discrete, isolated objects.
 - However, Gemini's advantage reversed sharply above 20% density, collapsing to 5-20% F1, and frequently generating identical L-shaped tetromino patterns regardless of input.
 - Claude Opus and ChatGPT 5.2 maintained more stable, albeit low, performance across all densities in this condition.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Failure Mode Analysis

Each model exhibited distinct qualitative failure modes in the unsegmented squares condition:
- **Claude Opus:** Consistently under-counted filled cells, often predicting fewer than existed (e.g., 45 predicted vs. 60 actual). While detecting approximate regions, it failed to precisely localize individual cells, frequently displacing them.
- **ChatGPT 5.2:** Demonstrated massive over-counting, especially at high densities, predicting significantly more filled cells than present (e.g., 112-133 predicted for 78-94 true cells). It also occasionally lost grid dimensions, producing rows with incorrect character counts. This suggested hallucination of filled cells in the vicinity of true clusters.
- **Gemini 3 Thinking:** At moderate to high densities, Gemini frequently abandoned the input and generated stereotyped geometric patterns (e.g., L-shapes, crosses) in the squares condition. In the text condition, at high densities, it generated simple ASCII art (e.g., letters E, H, S) instead of the grid. This indicated a fallback to learned visual prototypes when its processing capacity for detailed input was exceeded.

The diversity of these failure modes, despite a shared fundamental limitation, suggests that the deficit is not an artifact of a specific architecture or training, but a systemic property of current VLM designs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research indicate a fundamental limitation in the spatial reasoning capabilities of current frontier Vision-Language Models (VLMs) when dealing with non-textual visual elements. The consistent and substantial performance gap observed across three independent model families suggests that VLMs may predominantly rely on a text-recognition pathway for high-fidelity spatial localization.

#### 5.1. The Text-Recognition Pathway Hypothesis

The study proposes that VLMs function as if they possess two distinct implicit processing pathways for spatial information:
- **Text-recognition pathway:** This pathway identifies characters in images, maps them to discrete tokens, and maintains their sequential (and thus spatial) positions with high fidelity. This internal OCR-like process effectively converts visual information into a linguistic domain where models typically exhibit strong positional reasoning. The robustness of this pathway varies, with Claude and ChatGPT demonstrating greater stability across densities than Gemini, which collapses at moderate text density.
- **Visual-feature pathway:** This pathway encodes non-textual visual content into latent representations primarily optimized for semantic understanding. It appears to capture approximate spatial relationships (e.g., object clusters) but loses precise, coordinate-level localization information. While generally weaker, Gemini exhibited a stronger visual-feature pathway for discrete, isolated objects at low densities, though this advantage rapidly diminished with increasing visual complexity.

This hypothesis suggests that the perceived spatial competence of VLMs in many tasks might be largely attributed to their ability to perform implicit OCR, leading to an overestimation of their general visual spatial acuity.

#### 5.2. Alternative Explanations

The research considers and rebuts alternative explanations for the observed performance gap. Arguments against **image downsampling** as the primary cause include: text images having identical pixel dimensions regardless of density (yet Claude/ChatGPT maintain high accuracy); downsampling applying equally to both conditions (thus not explaining the gap); Gemini's bimodal collapse being inconsistent with smooth degradation from resolution loss; and hallucinated outputs being structured rather than ambiguous. Similarly, **sequence length or decoding constraints** are argued against because both text and squares conditions require identical output lengths, yet only the squares condition triggers severe failure, indicating the critical variable is the visual input's textual recognizability, not output complexity.

#### 5.3. Implications

These findings have significant practical implications for applications that require precise spatial reasoning over non-textual visual elements. Fields such as medical imaging, autonomous navigation, advanced document analysis (beyond simple text), and scientific visualization often depend on the accurate interpretation of non-linguistic visual patterns. The observed degradation in the visual-feature pathway suggests that current VLM performance on text-heavy benchmarks may not accurately reflect their capability for tasks involving arbitrary visual localization. Therefore, deploying VLMs in such critical domains without addressing this limitation could lead to reduced reliability or erroneous outputs.

#### 5.4. Toward Bridging the Gap

The paper outlines several potential strategies for improving VLM spatial reasoning:
- **Textual scaffolding:** Augmenting visual inputs with coordinate labels, row/column numbers, or cell annotations could reroute spatial reasoning through the more robust text-recognition pathway.
- **Hybrid encoding:** Incorporating text labels (e.g., "B"/"W" or "0"/"1") directly into visual cells could create a bridge between the two processing pathways.
- **Output format variation:** Requiring coordinate-list outputs instead of grid transcriptions could help differentiate between limitations in visual perception and those in generating structured output sequences.
- **Architectural interventions:** Training visual encoders with explicit spatial-coordinate prediction objectives, rather than solely relying on image-caption matching, could enhance native visual spatial resolution.

#### 5.5. Limitations and Future Work

The study acknowledges limitations, including evaluating three models in a single trial per condition (though across 15 grids), potential hidden preprocessing by web interfaces, and a lack of prompt sensitivity analysis. Future work could involve multi-trial repetitions, API-based evaluations for stricter control over image parameters, prompt variations, and testing open-source VLMs with known architectures (e.g., LLaVA, Qwen-VL) to correlate behavioral findings with specific encoder properties. Varying grid sizes could also explore the capacity limits of the text-pathway advantage.

In conclusion, the research identifies a fundamental and generalizable limitation in contemporary VLMs: their reliance on text recognition to achieve high-fidelity spatial localization. When visual elements lack a textual analog, spatial reasoning capabilities degrade significantly, highlighting a critical area for future research and development in multimodal AI.
