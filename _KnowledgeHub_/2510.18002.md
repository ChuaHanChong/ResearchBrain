# Humanoid Goalkeeper: Learning from Position Conditioned Task-Motion Constraints

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Hong Kong and Shanghai AI Laboratory developed an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework for humanoid goalkeeping, integrating position-conditioned human motion priors via an adversarial scheme. The system achieved an 80.92% success rate in simulation and 70% on hardware using MoCap, demonstrating agile, human-like whole-body ball interceptions across a wide range.

## Problem

- Existing robotic goalkeeping systems are often confined to smaller platforms (e.g., quadrupeds), rely on fixed motion primitives, and have narrow interception ranges, lacking the dynamic whole-body agility required for humanoids.
- Humanoid robots face unique challenges in generating natural, human-like whole-body motions and covering a wide guarding range with equivalent response times due to their many degrees of freedom and complex balance requirements.
- Current control approaches for dynamic humanoid interactions often use separate perception, planning, and control modules, or rely on fixed motion tracking, lacking an integrated, end-to-end learning policy for real-time dynamic interactions.

## Method

- An end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) framework using PPO in IsaacGym was developed, optimizing for both task success (intercepting the ball) and motion resemblance to human demonstrations.
- Position-conditioned adversarial motion priors (AMP) were incorporated, with region-specific discriminators trained to differentiate between expert human motions and robot-generated motions, encouraging region-appropriate movement styles.
- The system integrates an internal ball estimator trained with noise (e.g., position perturbation, random dropouts) and a post-task stability reward to enhance sim-to-real transfer, robustness to perception uncertainties, and continuous task execution.

## Results

- The proposed Humanoid Goalkeeper achieved an **80.92%** simulation success rate and **67.84%** motion alignment with expert demonstrations, outperforming baselines that lacked explicit task or motion conditioning.
- In hardware experiments, the system achieved a **70%** success rate using a motion capture system and a **46.7%** success rate with an onboard camera, demonstrating functional real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 robot.
- The framework generalized to other dynamic interaction tasks like ball escaping (e.g., 5/5 success for squat escape) and demonstrated the ability to perform continuous goalkeeping without requiring manual resets to default postures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Jointly optimizing task success and motion resemblance via region-conditioned adversarial priors significantly improves both performance metrics compared to optimizing for either alone, highlighting the benefits of integrated task-motion constraints.
> - Explicit motion and task segmentation (dividing AMP and task rewards by goal region) is crucial for achieving high motion fidelity and task-aligned behaviors, preventing policies from overfitting to specific goal areas.
> - Training with an integrated ball estimator and perception noise is vital for successful sim-to-real transfer, allowing the humanoid to perform robustly with real-world sensors like depth cameras despite field-of-view limitations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Developing robotic soccer capabilities, particularly goalkeeping, serves as a benchmark for evaluating motion intelligence, integrating perception, decision-making, and agile motor control. Previous research has explored dynamic object interactions with mobile robots and quadrupeds, demonstrating capabilities in tasks such as badminton, table tennis, mobile-manipulator-based catching, throwing, and soccer. These systems have achieved millisecond-level interactions and shown proficiency in specific domains like consecutive actions or multi-agent coordination.

However, existing robotic goalkeeping systems, especially those using quadrupedal platforms, exhibit limitations. These include being confined to smaller platforms, relying on fixed motion primitives, and operating within narrow interception ranges. While advancements in reinforcement learning (RL), imitation-driven motion priors, and human motion recovery pipelines have opened new possibilities, their application to real-world humanoid tasks, particularly those requiring dynamic whole-body agility and wide operational coverage, remains an area with underexplored challenges. Previous approaches to whole-body control often involve strict mimicry of reference trajectories with fixed time phases, limiting them to non-interactive tasks or predefined interaction moments. Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) offer a less temporally constrained integration of expert data but have primarily been explored in simpler locomotion tasks for real-world humanoids.

This work addresses these gaps by focusing on humanoid goalkeeping, which necessitates generating natural, human-like whole-body motions and covering a wider guarding range with an equivalent response time, a more complex challenge than tasks involving primarily lower limbs or slower objects. It seeks to develop an end-to-end RL policy that enables fully autonomous, highly dynamic, and human-like robot-object interactions, moving beyond methods that rely on separate teleoperation or fixed motion tracking for whole-body control. By integrating human motion priors conditioned on perceptual inputs via an adversarial scheme, the research aims to produce a practical and scalable solution for dynamic interactions between robots and moving objects, advancing adaptive and lifelike robotic behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a reinforcement learning framework for autonomous goalkeeping with humanoid robots in real-world environments. This framework aims to enable the robot to perform agile, autonomous, and naturalistic interceptions of fast-moving balls, encompassing a wide operational range and initiating from arbitrary postures.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical challenges and limitations observed in the existing field of robotic goalkeeping:

1. **Humanoid-Specific Challenges:** Prior work on dynamic robotic interactions, particularly goalkeeping, has predominantly focused on quadrupedal platforms or kid-sized robots. Humanoid robots introduce distinct challenges:
 - **Generating Natural, Human-like Whole-Body Motions:** Achieving motions that resemble human actions requires intricate coordination of numerous degrees of freedom across the entire body, which is significantly more complex than controlling quadrupedal locomotion. Existing methods often rely on predefined motion primitives or teleoperation, limiting the adaptability and naturalism of movements.
 - **Covering a Wider Guarding Range with Equivalent Response Time:** Humanoid goalkeeping demands rapid, full-body responses to dynamic stimuli across a broad area. This necessitates policies that can execute highly dynamic actions, such as lateral shifts, jumps, and squats, within extreme time constraints, to effectively cover diverse interception regions.

2. **Limitations of Existing Control Approaches:**
 - **Reliance on Separate Control Stages:** Many current robot control systems for dynamic interactions utilize separate modules for perception, planning, and low-level control, or depend on fixed motion tracking. This can lead to less integrated and less adaptable behaviors.
 - **Lack of End-to-End Learning for Dynamic Interactions:** The development of a single, end-to-end reinforcement learning policy that directly maps perceptual inputs to whole-body control outputs for dynamic, human-robot interactions in real-time has been an underexplored area for humanoids.

3. **Advancing Robotic Physical Intelligence:** Robotic goalkeeping represents a demanding subtask within robotic soccer, requiring high levels of physical intelligence. Successfully addressing its challenges is considered a crucial step towards fully autonomous, real-time physical intelligence in robots, paving the way for more adaptive and lifelike humanoid behaviors beyond specific pre-programmed tasks.

To overcome these challenges, the research proposes a framework that learns a single end-to-end RL policy. This policy integrates multiple human motion priors, conditioned on perceptual inputs, into the RL training process through an adversarial scheme. This approach is intended to optimize for both task performance and motion realism simultaneously, without the need for strict temporal alignment to reference trajectories. The objectives include demonstrating the method's effectiveness in real-world scenarios and its generalization capability to related dynamic interaction tasks, such as ball escaping and grabbing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Humanoid Goalkeeper system employs an end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) framework designed to autonomously perform goalkeeping skills while adaptively conforming to human motion priors. The method integrates position-conditioned task and motion constraints within an adversarial training scheme.

#### 3.1. A. Goalkeeper Training through RL

The learning system is optimized for two objectives: task success and motion resemblance to human demonstrations.

1. **Training Environment:**
 - **Algorithm:** Policies are trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.
 - **Simulator:** Training is conducted in the IsaacGym simulator.
 - **Task:** The robot is trained to intercept a flying ball before it crosses the goal line.
 - **Observation Space:** The actor network receives observed ball position ($O_{ball}$), proprioceptive observations ($O_p$ including base angular velocity, projected gravity vector, joint positions, joint velocities, and previous action), and a history of these inputs (length $T$) for temporal information. The critic network includes additional inputs such as base linear velocity, the end target region, end-effector target, ball velocity, and hand positions, along with reach distance. All ball-related observations are in the robot's local frame.
 - **Episode Setup:** Each episode starts with a ball launched toward the robot. The goal line is divided into $k$ regions ($R = 0, 1, 2, \dots, k$). A random landing position ($p_{land}$) within a chosen region is sampled, and the ball's velocity is set accordingly.

2. **Position-Conditioned Task Rewards:**
 - A position-conditioned task reward ($r_t$) guides the robot to intercept the ball.
 - **Dynamic Target ($p_{target}$):** When the ball is far from the robot ($d_x > d_{th}$), the target is the predicted landing point ($p_{land}$). When the ball is close ($d_x \leq d_{th}$), the target switches to the actual ball position ($p_{ball}$).
 - **Reward Formulation:** A smooth, distance-based reward uses a sigmoid function to encourage the end-effector (hand) to reach $p_{target}$. The reward is conditioned on the landing region $R$, directing the robot to use the appropriate hand (e.g., left hand for left region).
 - **Dynamics Modulation:** A term $\nu_{(R)}$ is introduced to encourage whole-body motions (e.g., lateral shifts, jumps) specific to the region $R$, promoting coordinated end-effector movements. Additional reward terms enforce general motion constraints and hardware feasibility.

3. **Post-Task Stability:**
 - Episodes are set to 3 seconds, longer than typical ball flight time, to allow for post-task stabilization.
 - A reward term ($r_{post}$) encourages the robot to maintain a normalized posture after ball interception or passage, promoting balance and recovery. This reward is active only after the ball has stopped or passed behind the robot.
 - **Continuous Training:** To facilitate learning continuous keeping behaviors, a subset of environments is reset to joint configurations sampled from other ongoing environments, rather than a default pose. This encourages the robot to initiate new trials from diverse, active postures.

#### 3.2. B. Optimization with Motion Constraints

A position-conditioned adversarial motion prior (AMP) reward is incorporated to encourage region-appropriate movement styles.

1. **Reference Motion Curation:**
 - **Human Motion Extraction:** Human motions are extracted from self-recorded RGB videos using existing pipelines (e.g., GVHMR).
 - **Retargeting:** These extracted motions are retargeted to the Unitree G1 robot.
 - **Motion Library:** The resulting joint position sequences ($q_t$) are stored in a region-specific motion reference buffer.

2. **Optimize with Motion Constraints:**
 - **Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP):** Motion constraints are implemented using position-conditioned AMP rewards. Each region $R$ is associated with a dedicated reference motion slot and a corresponding discriminator $D^{(R)}$.
 - **Discriminator Training:** Each $D^{(R)}$ is trained to distinguish between joint position transitions ($q_t, q_{t+1}$) from expert reference motions ($d^{(R)}_M$) and those generated by the policy ($d^{(R)}_\pi$), conditioned on region $R$. The objective minimizes squared differences between discriminator outputs and target labels (1 for expert, -1 for policy).
 - **Soft AMP Reward:** Unlike direct AMP, the reward is applied "softly." Gaussian samples are generated around each executed motion, and the policy is rewarded based on the highest discriminator score among these samples. This promotes smooth alignment with the reference motion distribution while allowing task objectives to refine precise execution.

#### 3.3. C. Aligning Sim2Real Perception

Addressing perception challenges and the sim-to-real gap is critical for real-world deployment. The robot observes the ball's position relative to its torso frame ($O_{ball} \in \mathbb{R}^3$).

1. **Ball Estimator:**
 - An internal ball estimator is integrated into the training loop.
 - **Function:** This estimator identifies the landing region (for task and motion conditioning) and predicts ball positions and trajectories by utilizing historical observations.
 - **Training:** The position estimator is trained with Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, and the region estimator uses Cross-Entropy Loss.

2. **Training Noises:**
 - To simulate real-world perception uncertainties, noise is introduced into $O_{ball}$ during training.
 - **Position Noise:** Random perturbations of up to 5cm (half the ball's radius) are applied to mimic sensing errors.
 - **Random Dropouts:** $O_{ball}$ is occasionally set to zero after 0.4s of flight, simulating occlusions or out-of-field-of-view (FoV) situations.
 - **Post-Interception:** Once the ball stops, $O_{ball}$ is set to zero, encouraging the robot to hold its final pose.
 - **Hardware Perception Systems:** The system supports two modalities: a motion capture (MoCap) system using markers, and an onboard depth camera (Intel RealSense D435i) with an IR filter for ball detection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation assesses the method's effectiveness in goalkeeping, motion resemblance, and smoothness through simulation and hardware experiments. The goal area is partitioned into six regions, each with corresponding expert motions.

#### 4.1. A. Evaluation Metrics

- **$E_{succ}$ ($\uparrow$):** Success rate of blocking the ball.
- **$E_{ee task}$ ($\downarrow$):** Closest distance between the end-effector (hand) and the ball during a trial.
- **$E_{pos(R)}$ ($\downarrow$):** Pose error to expert motion from ball target region R, processed via Dynamic Time Warping (DTW).
- **$E_{pos(min)}$ ($\downarrow$):** Lowest pose error between executed motion and expert motion among all regions.
- **$E_{match(R)}$ ($\uparrow$):** Indicator of whether the closest-matching motion aligns with the ball target region R.
- **$E_{dof acc}$ ($\downarrow$):** Joint acceleration.
- **$E_{smth}$ ($\downarrow$):** Action smoothness.

#### 4.2. B. Simulation Results

The proposed Humanoid Goalkeeper achieved the highest task success rate and motion resemblance compared to baseline methods.

- **Overall Performance:** The Humanoid Goalkeeper achieved an $E_{succ}$ of 80.92% ($\pm$1.72) and an $E_{match(R)}$ of 67.84% ($\pm$1.49). This supports the hypothesis that aligning task constraints with motion constraints enhances overall performance.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Without Task Constraints:** Resulted in a low $E_{succ}$ (9.44%) despite good motion resemblance ($E_{match(R)}$ 64.33%), indicating that motion priors alone do not guarantee task success.
 - **Without Motion Constraints:** Exhibited a moderate $E_{succ}$ (31.69%) but poor motion resemblance ($E_{match(R)}$ 8.83%), suggesting that task rewards alone lead to less human-like motions.
 - **Without AMP Division:** Achieved a competitive $E_{succ}$ (76.11%) but significantly lower $E_{match(R)}$ (25.73%) than the full method, indicating that explicit motion segmentation improves motion fidelity.
 - **Without Task Division:** Lower $E_{succ}$ (62.08%) and $E_{match(R)}$ (39.18%) compared to the full method.
 - **Without Division (both AMP and Task):** The lowest performance among division ablations with $E_{succ}$ (64.83%) and $E_{match(R)}$ (19.09%).
- **Robustness to Difficulty:** Under "Speed-Hard" (0.4s – 0.7s flight) and "Range-Hard" ($\pm$2.0m width), success rates decreased (64.25% and 63.91% respectively), demonstrating the method's scalability while acknowledging limitations under extreme conditions.
- **Motion Smoothness:** The proposed method achieved an $E_{smth}$ of 0.042 ($\pm$0.001), indicating smooth actions. The "w.o. Task Constraints" baseline, despite low success, achieved the best smoothness (0.030 $\pm$0.001), suggesting a trade-off where generic motion regularity might be prioritized over task effectiveness without specific task goals.
- **Region-Conditioned Behavior (Fig. 5):** The visualization demonstrated clear and consistent clusters of robot-performed motions aligning with their respective ball target regions for the proposed method. Baseline methods, especially those without division, showed mixed motions across regions, confirming the effectiveness of region-conditioned policy learning in preserving task-aligned motion structure.

#### 4.3. C. Region-Specific Hardware Evaluation

Hardware experiments were conducted for 30 trials (5 trials per region across 6 regions).

- **Overall Hardware Success (MoCap):** The Humanoid Goalkeeper (using MoCap) achieved a total success rate of 21/30 (70%). Failures were predominantly in the upper regions (e.g., Right-Up: 1/5, Left-Up: 3/5), which demand precise end-effector alignment and wide-range whole-body motions.
- **Comparison to Baselines (MoCap):** Non-division baselines (w.o. AMP Division, w.o. Division) tended to overfit to one side of the goal. For instance, "w.o. AMP Division" achieved 5/5 in Left-Low but 0/5 in Right-Low and Right-Up, indicating a lack of generalization across the full goal area. The proposed method demonstrated a smaller sim-to-real performance drop.
- **Motion Visualization (Fig. 6):** Visualizations of ball and hand trajectories showed that the proposed method guided the end-effector accurately to the interception region from the outset. Non-division baselines often failed to initiate early lateral steps for balls targeting side regions, leading to missed saves.

#### 4.4. D. Performance with Camera Perception

- **Success Rate (Camera):** The success rate dropped to 14/30 (46.7%) when using the onboard camera.
- **Limitations:** This decline was attributed primarily to the narrow camera field of view (FoV), which limited the observable time window of the ball, particularly for lower regions where the ball quickly exited the view.
- **Robustness:** Despite FoV constraints, the camera-based system achieved high success rates in upper regions where the ball remained visible longer (e.g., 3/5 in Right-Up and Left-Up), demonstrating robustness of the method to onboard perception.
- **Smoothness:** The camera-based system exhibited improved $E_{dof acc}$ scores, suggesting that perceptual constraints naturally suppressed large, abrupt motions, leading to smoother behaviors.

#### 4.5. E. Continuous Keeping

The proposed pipeline demonstrated the ability to perform continuous goalkeeping skills without manual resets to default states. Snapshots (Fig. 9) showed the robot transitioning directly from the terminal posture of one successful interception to the initiation of a subsequent one, highlighting its potential for uninterrupted, match-like behavior.

#### 4.6. F. Generalization Tasks

The framework was extended to two additional dynamic interaction tasks: ball escaping and ball grabbing.

- **Escape Task:**
 - **Jump Escape:** 3/5 success rate, $E_{pos(min)}$ 1.49, $E_{dof acc}$ 46.79.
 - **Squat Escape:** 5/5 success rate, $E_{pos(min)}$ 1.12, $E_{dof acc}$ 16.19.
 - The robot successfully reacted to incoming balls within 0.5 seconds, with jump motions involving higher joint accelerations.
- **Grabbing Task:**
 - **Grab Tennis:** 1/5 success rate, $E_{pos(min)}$ 0.99, $E_{dof acc}$ 18.25.
 - The low success rate was attributed to the absence of a soft bag model during training and the uncontrollable deformability and orientation of the real-world soft bag (Fig. 10). Despite this, the robot reliably moved towards the predicted ball landing location.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a novel reinforcement learning framework for enabling humanoid robots to perform autonomous, agile, and human-like goalkeeping tasks in real-world scenarios. The work demonstrates advancements in humanoid control for dynamic object interaction, addressing specific challenges that differentiate humanoids from other robotic platforms.

**Key Contributions and Advancements:**

1. **Humanoid-Level Autonomous Goalkeeping:** This work represents a significant step towards developing the first humanoid-level autonomous goalkeeper capable of executing complex, full-body movements to intercept fast-moving objects across a wide operational range.
2. **End-to-End Policy Learning with Integrated Constraints:** The method proposes a unified reinforcement learning framework that jointly optimizes for task success and motion realism. By integrating position-conditioned human motion priors via an adversarial scheme, it overcomes limitations of previous approaches that relied on separate optimization stages or fixed motion tracking.
3. **Enhanced Adaptability and Naturalism:** The learned end-to-end policy enables the robot to generate highly dynamic, human-like whole-body motions from arbitrary postures, which contributes to more natural and adaptable robot behaviors in interactive tasks.
4. **Robustness to Perception Noise and Generalization:** The incorporation of a ball estimator and training-time noise (position noise, random dropouts) improves the sim-to-real transferability and robustness of the policy across different perception modalities (MoCap and onboard camera). Furthermore, the framework's ability to generalize to related tasks like ball escaping and grabbing highlights its potential for broader application in dynamic humanoid-object interactions.
5. **Continuous Task Execution:** The demonstration of continuous goalkeeping without requiring resets to a default posture indicates a step towards more practical and match-like robotic capabilities, reducing operational overhead and increasing autonomy.

**Potential Impact:**

The advancements presented in this paper have implications beyond robotic soccer. The methodology for integrating observation-conditioned task-motion constraints could be extended to other complex, dynamic interaction tasks for humanoid robots, such as human-robot collaboration, industrial manipulation, or assistance in unstructured environments. By fostering more natural and agile robotic movements, this research contributes to the development of robots that can operate more effectively and intuitively alongside humans. The focus on real-world feasibility through sim-to-real alignment techniques, including perception noise modeling, is crucial for deploying advanced robotic skills in practical applications.

**Current Limitations and Future Directions:**

Despite its contributions, the paper identifies areas for further development:

1. **Global Observations and High-Level Planning:** The current framework lacks global observations and a high-level planning module, limiting the robot's ability to perform consecutive goalkeeping actions in a match-level context, such as strategic repositioning after each interception. Future work could integrate such a planner to enable more sophisticated, long-term behaviors.
2. **Refinement of the Perception Module:** The perception system requires further refinement to handle complex real-world scenarios. Currently, the robot reacts to any detected ball within its field of view, regardless of whether it targets the goal. Future improvements could involve integrating active sensing capabilities to overcome camera FoV limitations and enhance intelligent decision-making regarding relevant stimuli.
3. **Expanded Interaction Skills:** To further advance humanoid-object interaction skills, future research could explore training the robot to actively grab the ball with its hand rather than simply punching it away, posing an appealing and valuable challenge for dexterous manipulation.

Overall, this work presents a foundational step towards enabling highly dynamic and naturalistic interactions for humanoid robots, with a clear path for future research to enhance autonomy, perception, and skill versatility.
