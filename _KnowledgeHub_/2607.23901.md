# SHARE: Towards Head-Mounted AR with User-Centric SLAM in Shared Human-Robot Workspaces

> [!summary] Summary
> SHARE is a user-centric multi-agent SLAM system for human-robot collaboration that prioritizes augmented reality (AR) user experience. It achieves an average motion-to-photon latency of 13.22 ms for AR users, a 43.3% reduction over baselines, while maintaining robot tracking accuracy below 2 cm ATE across diverse environments.

## Problem

- Existing multi-agent SLAM systems in AR-powered human-robot collaboration (HRC) incur high motion-to-photon latency (100-500 ms), exceeding the critical 20 ms threshold for comfortable AR experiences.
- Traditional multi-agent SLAM frameworks treat all agents uniformly in resource allocation, failing to account for the heterogeneous and stringent latency requirements of AR users versus the more tolerant demands of robot control.
- Frequent visual overlap among multiple agents in small indoor HRC environments leads to redundant map merging computations, reducing efficiency and increasing latency in multi-agent SLAM.

## Method

- **SHARE** implements an agent-specific ==Quality-of-Experience (QoE) model== that quantifies tracking performance by integrating real-time accuracy estimations with latency, prioritizing AR user experience based on psychophysical principles.
- A ==PID scheduler== dynamically adjusts agent frame transmission rates based on a continuous monitoring of each agent's QoE, optimizing the balance between latency and accuracy in response to changing visual environments.
- A ==visual overlap analysis== methodology reduces redundant computation by probabilistically skipping map merging operations when agents have highly overlapping fields of view, assessed using 6-DOF camera poses.

## Results

- SHARE reduced AR user motion-to-photon latency to an average of **13.22 ms**, representing a **43.3%** improvement over the COVINS-G baseline and consistently remaining below the **20 ms** AR comfort threshold.
- The system maintained an Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) below **2 cm** for both AR users and robots across all evaluated environments, outperforming COVINS-G in **13 out of 15** trajectory-agent combinations.
- The visual overlap analysis successfully removed an average of **43.72%** of map merge operations; a user study showed **65%** of participants preferred SHARE, with a **24.6%** improvement in robot task completion rate over COVINS-G.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prioritizing the stringent latency demands of human AR users while leveraging the higher latency tolerance of robots in HRC scenarios significantly enhances overall system performance and user experience.
> - Adaptive resource allocation, driven by real-time accuracy estimation and a continuous feedback loop via a PID controller, is essential for maintaining optimal QoE for heterogeneous agents in dynamic multi-agent SLAM.
> - Computational efficiency in multi-agent SLAM within small, shared workspaces can be effectively improved by strategically identifying and reducing redundant map merging through lightweight visual overlap assessment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses challenges at the intersection of Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC), Augmented Reality (AR) interfaces, and Multi-agent Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM). These fields are converging to enable seamless interaction between humans and robots in shared physical spaces, with applications ranging from collaborative assembly to domestic assistance.

Existing multi-agent SLAM systems, particularly those utilizing edge computing, primarily focus on homogeneous agent configurations (e.g., multiple robots or multiple user devices) and scalability for large environments. While these systems offload computationally intensive tasks to edge servers to overcome the resource limitations of mobile agents, they typically treat all agents uniformly in terms of resource allocation and latency requirements. This uniform approach often results in performance bottlenecks, particularly for latency-sensitive AR applications. Prior research in edge-assisted SLAM (e.g., Edge-SLAM, C2TAM, CVI-SLAM, CCM-SLAM, COVINS, COVINS-G, SwarmMap, CloudSLAM, Map++) has focused on improving scalability and addressing domain-specific latency concerns for robotic teams, but often overlooks the unique, stringent latency demands of AR-specific adaptations in real-world settings.

Similarly, SLAM solutions developed for head-mounted AR devices have primarily concentrated on optimizing on-device computation, sensor fusion, or edge-assisted offloading for multiple human users. Works like RoVAR and SLAM-Share have explored robust multi-agent tracking or real-time multi-user AR, respectively, but their focus remains on human-centric scenarios or specific hardware augmentations. These systems have not extensively considered the heterogeneous coexistence of human AR users and robots, nor have they leveraged the differing latency tolerances between these agent types.

This research distinguishes itself by focusing on the heterogeneous nature of HRC environments. It introduces a user-centric approach that explicitly differentiates and prioritizes the experience of AR users while maintaining tracking accuracy for robots. The paper claims to be the first to address the stringent latency requirements of head-mounted AR by dynamically balancing the differing latency tolerances of heterogeneous human and robot agents and exploiting redundancy opportunities specific to small indoor HRC environments. This approach of applying Quality-of-Experience (QoE) modeling and adaptive resource scheduling to multi-agent SLAM in mixed human-robot teams represents an advancement in the field, aiming to bridge the gap between robot-centric SLAM optimizations and human-centric AR experience requirements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to reduce the latency in current AR-powered Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) multi-agent SLAM systems while concurrently maintaining robust tracking accuracy for all agents. This objective is driven by several detailed motivations and aims to address specific challenges identified in the current research landscape.

The primary motivation stems from the critical bottleneck of latency in AR-HRC applications. Augmented Reality (AR) interfaces impose stringent motion-to-photon latency requirements, ideally below 20 milliseconds (ms), to prevent user discomfort and maintain visual coherence between virtual and real-world environments. Delays exceeding this threshold can lead to perceptual instability, spatial misalignment, and a phenomenon known as "judder" or "swim," severely degrading user experience. Existing multi-agent SLAM frameworks, which often offload intensive computations to edge servers, typically report latencies ranging from 100 ms to 500 ms, significantly exceeding AR's requirements. This forms the central research question: "Can we reduce the latency in current AR-powered HRC multi-agent SLAM systems while maintaining good tracking accuracy?"

To tackle this, the authors identify three specific challenges:

1. **Heterogeneous Latency Requirements:** Conventional multi-agent SLAM systems treat all agents uniformly in resource allocation. However, AR headsets demand extremely low latency due to real-time rendering and user interaction, whereas users exhibit a significantly higher tolerance for robot control latency (up to 750 ms to 1 second). The first challenge is: "How can we realize a user-centric strategy to effectively prioritize the latency of AR applications for human users, while exploiting the looser latency requirement on the robot?" The motivation here is to design differentiated Quality-of-Experience (QoE) policies that model these varying latency tolerances. This involves developing an agent-specific QoE function that captures the distinct priorities, such as latency sensitivity for AR and accuracy criticality for robots, and the diminishing marginal sensitivity to performance improvements observed in user experience research. Real-time accuracy estimation, crucial for QoE modeling, is also a motivation given the absence of ground truth in real-world deployments.

2. **Dynamic Tracking Conditions Among Homogeneous Agents:** SLAM tracking performance is highly dependent on the visual environment. Even agents of the same type (e.g., two AR users) can experience different QoE due to variations in visual features (e.g., feature-rich vs. featureless environments). Inadequate visual features can lead to increased processing needs and reduced accuracy, while redundant feature processing can unnecessarily increase latency. The second challenge is: "How can we reasonably balance latency and accuracy to optimize the QoE of multi-agent SLAM systems in response to dynamics in visual environments?" The motivation is to move beyond fixed thresholds or rule-based prioritization, which are insufficient for rapid, complex environmental changes. Instead, an adaptive mechanism is needed to continuously monitor conditions and adjust resource allocation (e.g., transmission priorities, frame rates) to maintain stable QoE across all agents, regardless of their immediate visual context.

3. **Inefficient Computation from Map Redundancy:** In small-scale indoor HRC environments, multiple agents often have overlapping fields of view. This frequent visual overlap can lead to a large amount of redundant computation in map merging operations, a core component of multi-agent SLAM. These repeated merging processes for incrementally similar geometric information contribute to computational latency (`τ_comp`) with only marginal accuracy improvements. The third challenge is: "How to address inefficiency from map redundancy in AR-powered HRC SLAM scenarios?" The motivation is to develop strategies that selectively reduce these redundant map merging operations by identifying periods of high visual overlap, thereby minimizing computational overhead while preserving tracking accuracy.

By addressing these specific motivations and challenges, the paper aims to develop a system, SHARE, that provides a robust and perceptually acceptable AR experience for human users in collaborative robotics scenarios, without compromising the operational accuracy of robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SHARE addresses the identified challenges through a multi-faceted system design comprising three primary components: QoE Modeling, a PID Scheduler, and Visual Overlap Analysis. These components are integrated into an edge-assisted multi-agent SLAM architecture built upon the COVINS-G framework.

#### 3.1. System Architecture Overview

SHARE operates within an edge-assisted SLAM architecture where mobile agents (AR users, robots) perform local sensing and initial pose estimation (frontend), while an edge server coordinates global optimization and map management (backend). The core components of SHARE are distributed: a DNN evaluation module operates within each agent's frontend, while QoE modeling and the PID scheduler are housed in a "Monitor Module" on the edge server, and visual overlap analysis is integrated into a "Merge Decider" that interacts with the global map backend.

#### 3.2. Agent-specific QoE Modeling

To address the heterogeneous latency requirements (Challenge 1), SHARE introduces an agent-specific Quality of Experience (QoE) model. This model quantifies tracking performance by integrating real-time accuracy estimations with latency measurements, drawing inspiration from psychophysical and multi-attribute utility theories (e.g., Weber-Fechner law, MAUT) which suggest diminishing marginal sensitivity to performance improvements.

The QoE for each agent *i* is formulated as: `QoEi = e^(-βi * Pi) * (1 - e^(-γi * Qi))`
- **Agent-centric factor penalty (`Pi`):** This term captures the dominant QoE bottleneck for each agent type, enforcing steep degradation when violated.
 - **For AR services:** `Pi = d - d_0`, where `d` is motion-to-photon latency (ms) and `d_0` is the 20 ms AR threshold. `β_i` controls the severity of latency-induced QoE degradation.
 - **For Robot services:** `Pi = a_0 - a`, where `a` is tracking accuracy (cm) and `a_0` is the 1 cm minimum acceptable accuracy. `β_i` controls accuracy-induced QoE degradation.
- **Marginal performance contribution (`Qi`):** This term models auxiliary benefits with diminishing returns.
 - **For AR services:** `Qi = a`, where `a` is accuracy. `γ_i` determines the rate at which accuracy improvements contribute to QoE.
 - **For Robot services:** `Qi = (d + θ_i)^-1`, where `d` is latency and `θ_i` is a normalization constant. `γ_i` determines the rate at which latency improvements contribute to QoE.

##### 3.2.1. Deep Neural Network-based SLAM Accuracy Estimation

As ground-truth accuracy is unavailable in real-world scenarios, a lightweight deep neural network (DNN) based on MobileNetV2 is deployed on each mobile agent for real-time SLAM error estimation.
- **Training Data Generation:** Offline, a custom monocular SLAM pipeline (pyslam) processes visual frames to produce estimated poses, which are compared against ground-truth poses from a dataset (SenseTime SLAM dataset) to generate per-frame localization error labels. Simultaneously, internal SLAM states, such as Point Spatial Distribution (PSD) matrices and eight scalar features (e.g., brightness, contrast, keypoints, tracking quality), are extracted.
- **Network Architecture:** The DNN processes three complementary input modalities through dedicated branches:
 1. **Visual Branch:** Processes keyframes via a pre-trained MobileNetV2 backbone.
 2. **PSD Branch:** Encodes the spatial arrangement of inlier feature points (response strength, inlier indicator, estimated depth) through convolutional layers.
 3. **Scalar Branch:** Processes an 8-element vector of image-level statistics and tracking-quality indicators through a fully connected layer.
 The outputs from these branches are concatenated and passed through a two-layer fully connected network to produce a scalar predicted tracking error.

#### 3.3. PID Scheduling Pipeline

To address dynamic tracking conditions among homogeneous agents (Challenge 2) and optimize QoE, SHARE employs a Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) scheduling framework. The PID scheduler continuously monitors the QoE of each agent and adjusts its frame transmission rate (`R(t)`) as the primary control mechanism.
- **Error Signal:** The deviation of an agent's `QoE_current(t)` from a `QoE_target` serves as the error signal: `err(t) = QoE_target - QoE_current(t)`.
- **PID Controller:** The scheduler calculates a control output `u(t)` using the standard PID formulation: `u(t) = K_p * err(t) + K_i * ∫err(τ)dτ + K_d * derr(t)/dt`. This allows for immediate response (proportional), compensation for persistent deficits (integral), and anticipation of future trends (derivative).
- **Rate Adjustment:** The control output `u(t)` is used to adjust the frame transmission rate: `R(t) = R_prev + u(t)`.
- **Parameter Tuning:** `K_p`, `K_i`, `K_d` parameters are determined via a grid search on a separate dataset to balance latency and accuracy.

#### 3.4. Visual Overlap Calculation Methodology

To address inefficiency from map redundancy in shared workspaces (Challenge 3), SHARE implements a selective map merging framework based on visual overlap analysis.
- **Pose-based Overlap Volume Calculation:** Instead of computationally intensive keypoint matching, overlap is assessed using only the 6-DOF poses (position and orientation) of two cameras.
 - **Camera Frustum Model:** Each agent's visible volume is represented as a truncated pyramid defined by fields of view and clipping distances.
 - **Point-in-Frustum Testing:** Given agent poses, a 3D grid sampling technique approximates the continuous overlap volume by testing if discrete points lie within the frustums of both cameras.
 - **Intersection-over-Union (IoU):** The ratio `R_IoU = V_overlap / V_union` quantifies the relative overlap.
- **Spatial Redundancy Reduction:** Map merges are probabilistically skipped to reduce computational load without significant accuracy loss.
 - When a new merge request is generated with overlap volume `V_new`, a skip probability `P_skip` is calculated as `P_skip = max(0, 1 - α_m * (V_new / V_bar))`, where `V_bar` is the historical mean overlap volume from past merges and `α_m` is a scaling parameter. This prioritizes merges with higher visual overlap (i.e., less redundant information).

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

SHARE is implemented on the COVINS-G multi-agent SLAM framework.
- **Hardware:**
 - **AR Users:** Meta Quest 3 headsets augmented with RealSense D435i cameras connected to BOSGAME Mini PCs (Intel N100) to allow direct camera access and processing.
 - **Robot:** TurtleBot 4, running ROS 2 navigation components and a custom SLAM functionality within an LXC subsystem.
 - **Edge Server:** Lenovo ThinkPad T14 (Intel i7, 4 cores) running Ubuntu 20.04.
- **Networking and Synchronization:** All devices are connected via a 5.0 GHz Wi-Fi network, with temporal synchronization achieved using Network Time Protocol (NTP) referenced to a motion capture workstation, ensuring millisecond-level timing accuracy.
- **SLAM Frontend:** The pose estimation module combines ORB-SLAM3 Visual-Inertial Odometry, IMU-PARSAC, and an Extended Kalman Filter for enhanced robustness.

This comprehensive methodology allows SHARE to adapt resource allocation based on agent-specific needs and environmental dynamics, while optimizing computational efficiency, thereby aiming to deliver a low-latency and accurate SLAM experience for heterogeneous HRC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SHARE involved controlled real-world field studies and synthesized datasets, comparing its performance against Streamed ORB-SLAM3 and COVINS-G, and also included an ablation study and a user study.

#### 4.1. Latency Analysis

- **AR Users:** SHARE consistently maintained motion-to-photon latency below the critical 20 ms AR threshold. In the field study, it achieved an average latency of 13.22 ms, representing a 43.3% reduction compared to COVINS-G. COVINS-G frequently exceeded this 20 ms threshold. In synthesized environments (Warehouse and Firestation), SHARE similarly reduced latency by 43.1% and 39.4%, respectively, while COVINS-G often failed to meet the AR requirement. This demonstrated SHARE's ability to prioritize AR user experience effectively across diverse conditions.
- **Robot:** For the robot agent, SHARE exhibited marginally higher latency (typically below a 10% increase) compared to its AR user performance, averaging around 25 ms. This is within the acceptable tolerance for most robot tasks, confirming the system's strategic resource allocation that prioritizes AR latency.
- **Comparison with Baselines:** Both SHARE and COVINS-G significantly outperformed Streamed ORB-SLAM3, highlighting the efficiency benefits of edge-assisted multi-agent SLAM over raw data streaming. SHARE achieved these low latencies even with a modestly provisioned edge server (4 cores).

#### 4.2. Accuracy Analysis (Absolute Trajectory Error - ATE)

- SHARE consistently achieved ATE errors below 2 cm for both AR users and the robot across all field study and synthesized datasets.
- In the field study, SHARE outperformed COVINS-G in 13 out of 15 trajectory-agent combinations. Instances where COVINS-G showed slightly lower ATE were limited to short trajectories with restricted viewpoint diversity, suggesting a minor trade-off in early-stage keyframe diversity for SHARE's adaptive frame dropping. This effect diminished with longer trajectories.
- In the feature-sparse Firestation emulation, SHARE demonstrated stronger relative accuracy gains compared to COVINS-G, attributed to its PID scheduler adapting frame transmission rates in challenging visual conditions.
- Similar to latency, both SHARE and COVINS-G significantly outperformed Streamed ORB-SLAM3 in terms of accuracy, confirming the benefits of collaborative map construction.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study (on Field Study data)

- **Impact of QoE Modeling:** Comparing SHARE to an "Equal Baseline" (identical QoE allocation for all agents) showed that differentiated QoE was beneficial. The Equal Baseline resulted in higher AR user latency on longer trajectories and increased localization error for AR users. It also led to substantially higher robot ATE, demonstrating that a lack of agent-specific prioritization degrades overall system performance.
- **Impact of PID Scheduler:** When comparing SHARE to a "Linear Baseline" (linear frame dropping without agent or environment awareness), the Linear Baseline showed comparable AR latency on some trajectories but incurred significantly higher ATE for both AR users and the robot. This indicated that adaptive, context-aware scheduling (PID) is critical for maintaining accuracy. A "Random Baseline" (non-tuned PID parameters) performed worse than SHARE and Linear Baseline in latency, confirming the importance of informed PID tuning.
- **Impact of Map Merging Redundancy Reduction:** SHARE's visual overlap calculation methodology successfully removed an average of 43.72% of map merge operations compared to COVINS-G across the 5 trajectories. This reduction had limited impact on overall accuracy, validating the efficiency and accuracy of the redundancy removal strategy.
- **Impact of DNN Frame Evaluator:** A comparative analysis of the DNN-based error estimator against other methods (Linear Regression, Random Forest, Multi-Layer Perceptron, Contrastive-style CNN) showed that SHARE's DNN achieved the lowest Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of 0.43 cm (35% better than the next best) and was the second fastest (8.2 ms inference time), demonstrating an effective balance of accuracy and efficiency.

#### 4.4. User Study

A user study with 20 participants evaluated SHARE against COVINS-G, Linear Baseline, and Equal Baseline in a robot control task via AR.
- **Reachability (Objective):** SHARE achieved the highest robot task completion rate, with relative improvements of 24.6% over COVINS-G, 29.1% over Linear Baseline, and 65.1% over Equal Baseline.
- **Predictability (Subjective):** Users rated robot movement predictability significantly higher with SHARE. COVINS-G, Linear Baseline, and Equal Baseline scored 79.79%, 52.13%, and 58.51%, respectively, compared to SHARE's score.
- **Responsiveness (Subjective):** SHARE consistently received the highest responsiveness ratings, improving by 12.1% (COVINS-G), 15.4% (Linear Baseline), and 21.7% (Equal Baseline) over the baselines.
- **Preference:** 65% (13 out of 20) participants preferred SHARE most, and no participant preferred it least, resulting in a net preference score of +13. Other baselines received negative net preference scores.
- **Statistical Analysis:** Friedman tests and repeated-measures ANOVA confirmed statistically significant omnibus effects for Predictability (`χ²=19.62, p<.005`), Responsiveness (`χ²=13.24, p<.05`), and Reachability (`χ²=24.44, p<.005`), supporting the conclusion that SHARE yielded the best performance across all metrics.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that SHARE successfully addresses the core problem of high AR latency in HRC while maintaining high tracking accuracy for both human and robot agents. Its user-centric design, adaptive scheduling, and computational optimizations collectively contribute to superior objective performance and significantly enhanced user perception and preference in real-world collaborative tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development and evaluation of SHARE represent a notable advancement in the field of Human-Robot Collaboration (HRC) and Augmented Reality (AR), with several areas of significance and potential impact:

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Addressing Critical AR Latency:** SHARE is presented as the first system to specifically address the stringent latency requirements of head-mounted AR within a multi-agent SLAM framework for HRC. By consistently achieving latency well below the 20 ms threshold, it overcomes a major perceptual barrier that limits the usability and comfort of AR in collaborative tasks.
- **Pioneering User-Centric SLAM:** The introduction of a first-of-its-kind Quality-of-Experience (QoE) model for heterogeneous HRC agents is a significant conceptual contribution. This model quantifies the differential impact of latency and accuracy on human AR users versus robots, enabling a principled approach to resource allocation that prioritizes the most sensitive agents.
- **Adaptive Resource Management:** The use of a PID scheduler to dynamically adjust transmission priorities based on real-time QoE and environmental visual dynamics demonstrates an effective adaptive resource management strategy. This allows the system to respond to varying tracking conditions and agent needs, optimizing performance across diverse scenarios.
- **Computational Efficiency in Confined Spaces:** The methodology for visual overlap calculation and probabilistic map merge reduction specifically targets the redundancy inherent in multi-agent SLAM within small-scale indoor HRC environments. This optimization reduces computational overhead without sacrificing accuracy, making edge-based SLAM more efficient for common HRC settings.
- **Real-world Applicability:** The implementation and evaluation using commercial AR headsets (Meta Quest 3) and a widely used ground robot (TurtleBot 4), combined with validation through real-world field studies and user studies, underscore the practical applicability and robustness of SHARE. The system being open-sourced further facilitates its adoption and extension by the research community.
- **DNN-based Accuracy Estimation:** The integration of a lightweight deep neural network for real-time SLAM error estimation without ground-truth data is a significant practical enhancement for adaptive systems, as it enables continuous self-assessment of tracking quality in live deployments.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Enhanced HRC Applications:** SHARE has the potential to enable more seamless, intuitive, and comfortable AR-powered HRC applications. This includes, but is not limited to, AR-guided collaborative assembly in manufacturing, more effective domestic cleaning assistance robots, and improved coordination in first-response operations where humans and robots work closely in shared environments. The statistically significant improvements in user perception (predictability and responsiveness) can lead to increased user trust and efficiency in human-robot interactions.
- **Broader Impact on Edge Computing:** The insights and methodologies developed for SHARE, particularly QoE differentiation and context-aware data fusion, are not exclusive to SLAM. They could be generalized and applied to other edge-based multi-agent systems serving heterogeneous clients with diverse Quality-of-Service (QoS) requirements, extending its impact beyond the robotics and AR domains.
- **Future AR/XR Device Compatibility:** By demonstrating its core mechanisms are decoupled from specific sensor configurations and that compute requirements are met by modestly provisioned hardware, SHARE is positioned to leverage future advancements in AR/XR hardware, such as the direct sensor access becoming available on newer head-mounted displays (e.g., Magic Leap 2, Samsung Galaxy XR). This suggests long-term relevance and ease of migration to evolving device ecosystems.
- **Catalyst for Research:** The open-sourcing of SHARE is expected to serve as a catalyst for further research into user-centric HRC, adaptive SLAM systems, and resource management in heterogeneous edge environments. It provides a foundation upon which future optimizations for areas like high-speed industrial robots, adaptive PID gain scheduling, and large-scale outdoor environments can be built.

In conclusion, SHARE provides a scientifically grounded and practically validated solution to a critical challenge in AR-enabled HRC. Its contributions to user-centric design, adaptive resource management, and computational efficiency are significant, with the potential to fundamentally improve how humans and robots collaborate in shared physical spaces and to influence the design of future multi-agent, edge-assisted systems.
