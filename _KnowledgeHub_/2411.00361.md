# Direct Preference Optimization for Primitive-Enabled Hierarchical RL: A Bilevel Approach

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed DIPPER, a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework that formulates HRL as a bi-level optimization problem and uses primitive-regularized Direct Preference Optimization to stabilize higher-level policy learning and ensure feasible subgoal generation. This approach achieved up to a 40% improvement over state-of-the-art baselines in complex, sparse-reward robotic tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents often struggle with complex, long-horizon tasks, especially in environments with sparse rewards.
- Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) methods face non-stationarity issues, where the higher-level policy's learning becomes unstable due to the continuous evolution of the lower-level policy.
- The higher-level policy in HRL can generate infeasible subgoals that are beyond the current capabilities of the lower-level policy, leading to inefficient learning and suboptimal overall performance.

## Method

- The work formulates Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning as a ==bi-level optimization== problem to rigorously model the interdependencies between higher-level and lower-level policies.
- **DIPPER** utilizes Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for the higher-level policy, decoupling its learning from the non-stationary reward signals influenced by the evolving lower-level policy.
- A primitive regularization mechanism is integrated, derived from the bi-level formulation, which grounds subgoal proposals in the lower-level policy's value function to ensure the generation of feasible and achievable subgoals.

## Results

- DIPPER achieved up to a **40%** improvement in success rates compared to state-of-the-art hierarchical and non-hierarchical baselines across challenging robotic tasks with sparse rewards.
- The framework demonstrated effective mitigation of infeasible subgoal generation, evidenced by consistently low values for the 'Subgoal Distance Metric,' indicating that higher-level policies predicted feasible subgoals.
- DIPPER showed robust mitigation of non-stationarity, leading to high values for the 'Lower Q-Function Metric' for predicted subgoals, confirming the generation of high-reward-inducing and achievable subgoals.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the higher-level policy's objective from the volatile, non-stationary reward signals generated by an evolving lower-level policy can significantly improve training stability in HRL.
> - Regularizing subgoal generation with respect to the lower-level policy's value function provides a principled way to ensure that proposed subgoals are feasible and achievable, enhancing overall learning efficiency.
> - A rigorous bi-level optimization framework can effectively model and jointly optimize hierarchical policies, enabling a more stable and effective approach to complex, long-horizon tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents often face challenges in solving complex, long-horizon tasks, particularly when environmental rewards are sparse. Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) is a paradigm that addresses this by decomposing such tasks into a hierarchy of sub-tasks. In goal-conditioned HRL, a higher-level policy generates subgoals for a lower-level policy (primitive policy) to achieve, thereby enabling temporal abstraction and improving exploration efficiency.

Despite these advantages, HRL methods contend with two primary issues:
1. **Non-stationarity:** The learning process of the higher-level policy can become unstable because the lower-level policy's behavior continuously evolves during training. This shift in the lower-level policy alters the effective reward function and transition dynamics experienced by the higher-level, creating a non-stationary environment.
2. **Infeasible subgoal generation:** The higher-level policy may propose subgoals that are beyond the current capabilities of the lower-level policy, leading to inefficient learning and suboptimal overall performance.

Previous research efforts to mitigate non-stationarity have included simulating optimal lower-level behavior, relabeling transitions in replay buffers (e.g., Hindsight Experience Replay), or leveraging privileged information such as expert demonstrations or preferences. However, existing methods have been noted for lacking a unified, mathematically rigorous framework that explicitly models the bidirectional dependencies between hierarchical policies.

Concurrently, Preference-based Learning (PbL) has emerged as an approach to guide policy learning using human feedback when explicit reward signals are unavailable. Initial PbL methods, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), typically involve first learning a reward model from preferences and then optimizing a policy based on this model. More recent advancements, specifically Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), bypass the explicit reward model learning and subsequent RL training step by directly optimizing the policy using a KL-regularized maximum likelihood objective.

This work positions itself at the intersection of HRL and DPO. It aims to develop a principled mathematical formulation for HRL, modeling it as a bi-level optimization problem, and then to leverage DPO to address the non-stationarity and infeasible subgoal generation challenges that have persisted in HRL. By integrating DPO with a primitive regularization mechanism, the proposed framework seeks to stabilize higher-level policy training and ensure the generation of achievable subgoals, thereby improving performance in complex robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel HRL framework, named DIPPER (DIrect Preference Optimization for Primitive-Enabled Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning), capable of effectively solving complex, long-horizon tasks, particularly in sparse reward environments. This overarching goal is motivated by two fundamental and persistent challenges in hierarchical reinforcement learning:

1. **Mitigating Non-stationarity (C1):** In off-policy HRL, the higher-level policy's learning is destabilized by the continuous evolution of the lower-level policy. As the lower-level policy improves or changes its behavior, the effective reward function and transition dynamics perceived by the higher-level policy become non-stationary. This makes it difficult for the higher-level policy to converge to an optimal strategy, as its environment is constantly shifting. The authors argue that this instability is partly due to the higher-level policy's direct reliance on a non-stationary lower-level reward signal.
2. **Preventing Infeasible Subgoal Generation (C2):** The higher-level policy may propose subgoals that the current, possibly suboptimal, lower-level policy cannot achieve within its given horizon. When the lower-level policy fails to reach assigned subgoals, it provides noisy or misleading feedback to the higher-level, hindering credit assignment and preventing effective learning. This can lead to degenerate solutions where the higher-level policy repeatedly generates unachievable targets.

The authors motivate their approach by positing that these persistent challenges stem from the lack of a mathematically rigorous formulation that fully captures the intricate inter-dependencies between hierarchical policies. They propose that a bi-level optimization framework could provide a more principled way to model and jointly optimize these policies while explicitly addressing their interactions.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives and contributions of the work are:

- **To establish a rigorous mathematical formulation of HRL as a bi-level optimization problem.** This aims to explicitly capture the interdependencies between the higher and lower-level policies, laying a groundwork for principled solution development.
- **To mitigate non-stationarity by decoupling higher-level learning from the non-stationary lower-level reward signal.** This is achieved by leveraging Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to train the higher-level policy using preference feedback, rather than the raw environment reward that depends on the evolving lower-level policy.
- **To address infeasible subgoal generation by incorporating a primitive regularization mechanism.** Derived from the bi-level formulation, this mechanism grounds subgoal proposals in the lower-level policy's value function, ensuring that the higher-level policy generates subgoals that are feasible and achievable for the primitive policy.
- **To demonstrate improved performance in complex robotics tasks.** Through extensive experimentation on challenging navigation and manipulation benchmarks with sparse rewards, the objective is to show that the proposed DIPPER framework can achieve substantial performance gains over existing state-of-the-art HRL and non-hierarchical baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, DIPPER (DIrect Preference Optimization for Primitive-Enabled Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning), addresses the identified challenges by formulating HRL as a bi-level optimization problem and integrating a primitive-regularized Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective for the higher-level policy.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Setup and Problem Formulation

The framework employs a standard hierarchical setup with a higher-level policy and a lower-level (primitive) policy. The overall task horizon $L$ is decomposed into $T$ higher-level steps, each comprising $K$ lower-level primitive steps ($L = T \times K$).
- **Higher-Level MDP:** Defined by $(S, G, A_H, p_H, r_H)$, where $S$ is the state space, $G$ is the goal space (with $A_H = G$), $p_H$ is the higher-level transition model, and $r_H$ is the environment reward. The higher-level policy $\pi_H$ selects subgoals $g_t$ for $K$ primitive steps. The higher-level objective is to maximize expected cumulative reward and entropy: $J_H(\pi_H, \pi_L^*(\pi_H)) := E[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} r(s_t, g_t, g^*) + \lambda H(\pi_H)]$. Critically, the transition model $p_H$ and hence the higher-level reward depend on the optimal lower-level policy $\pi_L^*$.
- **Lower-Level MDP:** Defined by $(S, A_L, p_L, r_L)$. The lower-level policy $\pi_L$ is goal-conditioned, generating primitive actions $a_k$ to achieve the subgoal $g_t$ within $K$ timesteps. The lower-level reward $r_L$ is sparse, indicating success if the agent reaches within a certain distance of $g_t$. The lower-level objective is to maximize its expected cumulative reward and entropy: $V_L(\pi_H) = E[\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} r_L(s_{t+k}, a_k, g_t) + \lambda H(\pi_L)]$.

The paper identifies the challenges of non-stationarity (C1) and infeasible subgoal generation (C2) as stemming from the higher-level's dependence on the evolving and potentially suboptimal lower-level policy, which makes $r_H$ and $p_H$ non-stationary.

#### 3.2. Bi-Level Optimization Formulation

To provide a principled framework, HRL is formulated as a bi-level optimization problem. The higher-level policy optimization is the upper-level problem, constrained by the lower-level policy optimization.
The higher-level objective, which assumes an optimal lower-level policy, is recast as a constrained optimization problem:
$\max_{\pi_H} J(\pi_H, \pi_L^*(\pi_H))$ subject to $\pi_L^*(\pi_H) = \arg\max_{\pi_L} V_L(\pi_H)$.
This is then transformed into an equivalent constrained optimization:
$\max_{\pi_H, \pi_L} J(\pi_H, \pi_L)$ subject to $V_L(\pi_H) - V_L^*(\pi_H) \geq 0$.
By relaxing this constraint and forming an approximate Lagrangian, the objective becomes:
$\max_{\pi_H, \pi_L} E[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (r(s_t, g_t, g^*) + \lambda H(\pi_H) + \lambda(V_L(s_t, g_t) - V_L^*(s_t, g_t)))]$.
This Lagrangian objective aims for the higher-level policy to achieve the final goal while generating feasible subgoals for the lower-level, thus mitigating C1 and C2. However, directly optimizing this requires knowing $r_H$ as a function of $\pi_L$ and is prone to instability.

#### 3.3. DIPPER's DPO-based Solution

DIPPER addresses the challenges in optimizing the Lagrangian by:
1. **Decoupling higher-level learning from non-stationary rewards:** Instead of directly using the environment reward $r(s_t, g_t, g^*)$, DIPPER leverages Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The total reward $\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} R_t$ (where $R_t = r(s_t, g_t, g^*) + \lambda(V_L(s_t, g_t) - V_L^*(s_t, g_t))$) is related to the optimal higher-level Q-function and value function via Bellman equations. For maximum entropy RL, the advantage function $A_H^*(s_t, g_t, g^*)$ can be expressed as $\beta \log(\pi_H^*(g_t|s_t, g^*))$.
2. **Deriving the Primitive-Regularized DPO Objective:** By substituting this relationship and rearranging, the environment reward term $\sum r(s_t, g_t, g^*)$ is expressed in terms of $\log \pi_H^*(g_t|s_t, g^*)$ and the primitive regularization term $\lambda(V_L(s_t, g_t) - V_L^*(s_t, g_t))$. This entire expression is then used within the Bradley-Terry model for preference learning, leading to the core DIPPER objective (Equation 13). This objective optimizes the higher-level policy using preference feedback, implicitly incorporating the primitive regularization.
3. **Practical Algorithm and Approximations:**
 - To make the objective tractable, the optimal lower-level value function $V_L^*(s_t, g_t)$ is approximated by $V_L^m(s_t, g_t)$, obtained from $m$ iterations of lower-level value function updates.
 - The term $V_L$ in the regularization is ignored, leading to a practical maximum likelihood DIPPER objective (Equation 15). This simplified objective still captures the essence of mitigating non-stationarity and promoting feasible subgoal generation.
 - The gradient analysis of this objective shows that it increases the likelihood of preferred trajectories and decreases that of dis-preferred ones, with weighting based on the inaccuracy of the implicit reward model.

#### 3.4. Training Procedure

The practical algorithm (Algorithm 1 in Appendix A.1) outlines an iterative process:
- Collect higher-level trajectories using $\pi_H$ and lower-level trajectories using $\pi_L$.
- Generate preference feedback from these trajectories to form a dataset $D$.
- Update the lower-level value function $V_L$ to get $V_L^m$.
- Optimize the higher-level policy $\pi_H$ using the derived DPO objective (Equation 15) with sampled preference data.
- Optimize the lower-level policy $\pi_L$ using a standard RL algorithm (SAC).

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

DIPPER is evaluated on four challenging robotic tasks with sparse rewards: maze navigation, pick and place, push, and a Franka kitchen environment. The maze task features randomly generated mazes for generalization testing, and the kitchen task requires completing a sequence of subtasks for a final sparse reward. For harder tasks, one human demonstration is provided and an imitation learning objective is used at the lower level, applied consistently across all baselines for fair comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that DIPPER effectively addresses the limitations of HRL and achieves improved performance on complex robotic tasks, particularly in sparse reward settings.

#### 4.1. Performance Against Baselines

DIPPER achieved up to a 40% improvement over state-of-the-art baselines in sparse reward scenarios.

- **Comparison with DPO Baselines:**
 - **DIPPER-No-V (DPO without primitive regularization):** DIPPER consistently outperformed DIPPER-No-V. This indicates the importance of the primitive regularization mechanism in promoting the generation of feasible subgoals, which contributes to superior performance.
 - **DPO-FLAT:** DIPPER significantly outperformed the single-level DPO-FLAT baseline. This highlights the advantages of the hierarchical structure, including temporal abstraction and improved exploration, over a non-hierarchical DPO approach.

- **Comparison with Hierarchical Baselines:**
 - **SAGA:** While SAGA performed well in the maze navigation task, DIPPER significantly outperformed it in harder manipulation tasks. This suggests that DIPPER's approach provides better mitigation of non-stationarity in more complex, long-horizon settings.
 - **PIPER (RLHF-based):** DIPPER outperformed a variant of PIPER (without HER) across all tasks. This implies that the DPO-based higher-level policy optimization in DIPPER offers greater training stability and efficacy in mitigating non-stationarity compared to methods relying on learning a separate reward function via RLHF and then applying RL.
 - **RAPS:** RAPS performed exceptionally well in the maze navigation task but struggled in other sparse, complex manipulation tasks. DIPPER demonstrated stronger performance in these more challenging environments, indicating its robustness without relying on extensive pre-defined behavior priors.
 - **HAC:** HAC, which attempts to mitigate non-stationarity by simulating optimal lower-level primitive behavior and relabeling, performed well in the maze task but struggled in harder environments. DIPPER outperformed HAC in three out of four tasks.
 - **HIER (Vanilla HRL):** As expected, DIPPER consistently outperformed the vanilla HRL baseline (HIER), reinforcing the necessity of explicitly addressing non-stationarity and infeasible subgoal generation in HRL.

- **Comparison with Non-Hierarchical Baselines:**
 - **DAC (Discriminator Actor Critic) and FLAT (single-level SAC):** Both non-hierarchical baselines, even with access to a demonstration (DAC), largely failed to make progress in these complex tasks. This underscores that a hierarchical structure is critical for effective performance in the evaluated environments.

#### 4.2. Mitigation of HRL Limitations (C1: Non-stationarity, C2: Infeasible subgoal generation)

The paper introduced two metrics to quantify the mitigation of HRL limitations:

- **Subgoal Distance Metric (Figure 3):** This metric measures the average distance between subgoals predicted by the higher-level policy and the subgoals actually achieved by the lower-level primitive. DIPPER consistently generated low average distance values compared to baselines (DIPPER-No-V, HAC, RAPS, HIER). Low distances indicate that the higher-level policy is predicting feasible subgoals (addressing C2) and that the lower-level policy is behaving optimally to achieve them. This optimal lower-level behavior, being more stable, inherently mitigates non-stationarity (C1).
- **Lower Q-Function Metric (Figure 4):** This metric evaluates the average Q-function values for the subgoals predicted by the higher-level policy. DIPPER consistently led to large Q-function values, implying that the lower-level policy anticipates high returns for the predicted subgoals. High Q-values confirm that DIPPER generates high-reward-inducing and feasible subgoals, directly addressing both the non-stationarity (C1) and infeasible subgoal generation (C2) problems.

#### 4.3. Impact of Design Choices (Ablation Studies)

Ablation studies analyzed the effect of key hyperparameters:

- **Regularization Weight $\lambda$ (Appendix A.3, Figure 5):** The results showed that an appropriate value for $\lambda$ is essential. If $\lambda$ is too small, the benefits of primitive regularization are diminished, leading to poor performance. Conversely, if $\lambda$ is too large, the higher-level policy might be overly constrained to predict trivial subgoals, failing to achieve the overall task.
- **Max-Entropy Parameter $\beta$ (Appendix A.3, Figure 6):** The parameter $\beta$, which controls exploration in the maximum-entropy formulation, also requires careful tuning. Excessive $\beta$ values can lead to over-exploration, potentially hindering optimal subgoal prediction, while insufficient $\beta$ may limit exploration, resulting in suboptimal subgoal predictions.

In summary, the empirical results demonstrate DIPPER's ability to achieve robust performance in challenging HRL tasks by effectively leveraging a principled bi-level formulation and DPO to overcome the issues of non-stationarity and infeasible subgoal generation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces DIPPER, a novel framework that significantly contributes to the field of Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning by providing a principled approach to address its long-standing limitations. The work's significance lies in its multi-faceted approach, combining a formal mathematical framework with a robust learning algorithm.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**

- **Principled Bi-Level Optimization Formulation:** A notable contribution is the rigorous mathematical formulation of HRL as a bi-level optimization problem. This formalization provides a structured way to understand and model the inherent interdependencies between higher and lower-level policies, which the authors argue was previously lacking. This theoretical foundation can guide future research in designing more coherent and stable HRL algorithms.
- **Effective Mitigation of HRL Challenges:** DIPPER directly tackles the two fundamental challenges in HRL: non-stationarity and infeasible subgoal generation. By using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for the higher-level policy, DIPPER effectively decouples its learning from the volatile, non-stationary reward signals influenced by the evolving lower-level policy. Furthermore, the incorporation of a primitive regularization mechanism, derived from the bi-level formulation, ensures that the higher-level policy generates subgoals that are achievable within the current capabilities of the lower-level primitive. The empirical results using the proposed "Subgoal Distance Metric" and "Lower Q-Function Metric" provide evidence for this effective mitigation.
- **Improved Performance in Complex Robotics Tasks:** The experimental results demonstrate that DIPPER achieves substantial performance improvements (up to 40% higher success rates) over several state-of-the-art hierarchical and non-hierarchical baselines in challenging, sparse-reward robotic navigation and manipulation tasks. This suggests that DIPPER offers a more stable and effective method for learning control policies in complex, real-world scenarios where other methods often struggle.
- **Advancement of Preference-Based Learning in HRL:** The research showcases the applicability and benefits of DPO, a relatively newer preference-based learning technique, within a hierarchical context. By adapting DPO to a "token-level MDP setting" and integrating it with primitive regularization, the work extends the utility of DPO to complex, multi-step robotic tasks, bypassing the instability often associated with RLHF's two-step reward model learning and policy optimization.

**Potential Impact:**

The conceptual advancements and empirical performance of DIPPER could stimulate further research in several directions:

- **More Stable and Generalizable HRL:** The framework's ability to stabilize higher-level learning and ensure feasible subgoal generation can pave the way for more robust HRL systems that are less susceptible to training instabilities and can generalize better across varying task complexities.
- **Efficient Learning in Sparse Reward Environments:** The strong performance in sparse reward settings is particularly impactful for robotics, where designing dense reward functions is often impractical. DIPPER's approach could accelerate the development of autonomous agents for tasks requiring long-horizon planning and precise manipulation.
- **Integration with Other Techniques:** The preference-based nature of DIPPER makes it compatible with human-in-the-loop training, potentially allowing for more intuitive and efficient guidance of complex agent behaviors. Future work might explore its combination with expert demonstrations or other forms of human feedback.
- **Theoretical Foundations for HRL:** The bi-level optimization formulation provides a theoretical lens through which to analyze and design hierarchical systems, potentially leading to new theoretical guarantees or insights into the optimal interplay between hierarchical levels.

While the paper acknowledges limitations, such as the need for further analysis on generalization to out-of-distribution states/actions and applicability to high-dimensional subgoal spaces, DIPPER represents a significant step towards developing effective and principled hierarchical control policies for intricate robotic applications.
