# Constraint-Aware Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Static Shape Estimation of Co-Manipulative Continuum Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> A constraint-aware physics-informed neural network estimates static shape of tendon-driven co-manipulative continuum robots, whose two arms and shared flexible object form a closed chain. Built on a geometric variable-strain model, it adds a null-space-projected equilibrium residual plus a configuration-level loop-closure residual to the training loss, cutting errors under noisy labels and predicting equilibrium about 100,000 times faster than an iterative nonlinear solver.

## Problem

- Solving the constrained nonlinear equilibrium of coupled continuum arms for each new tendon input is too slow for real-time prediction, optimization, or control.
- Purely data-driven surrogates ignore governing mechanics, so predictions can violate static equilibrium or loop closure, especially with unseen inputs or noisy labels.
- Prior physics-informed continuum-robot work targets single open-chain arms, never the simultaneous equilibrium and geometric constraints of multi-arm closed-chain coupling.

## Method

- ==Projected static equilibrium residual==: the equilibrium equation is mapped onto the ==null space of the constraint Jacobian==, eliminating unknown closed-chain reaction forces from the loss, at the core of the **Constraint-Aware PINN**.
- ==Configuration-level geometric closure residual== built from the logarithm of the relative transform between connection frames, enforcing loop closure alongside the differentiable ==geometric variable-strain== kinematics.
- ==Gradient-magnitude-balanced multi-term loss== plus simulation pretraining and experimental fine-tuning with a dual-branch network predicting marker displacements and auxiliary generalized coordinates.

## Results

- With **140** samples and **50%** label noise, the network cuts relative configuration error, equilibrium residual, and closed-chain residual by **67.88%**, **67.35%**, and **88.06%** versus a matched data-driven network.
- Trained on the full dataset it reaches **0.1597%** relative configuration error at **0.1773 ms** inference, about **100,000x** faster than the **17.97 s** nonlinear solver.
- Experimental fine-tuning drops marker RMSE from **2.657 mm** to **0.497 mm**, raises R-squared from **-0.788** to **0.937**, and lifts sub-millimeter predictions from **37.30%** to **94.96%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Physics residuals act mainly as regularizers: their benefit is negligible on clean data but grows sharply as label noise rises, so scale physics weight with data quality.
> - Projecting equilibrium onto the constraint null space removes unknown reaction forces, making closed-chain systems trainable without ever estimating Lagrange multipliers.
> - Learned surrogates trade accuracy for speed; the solver stays five orders more accurate, so use surrogates where millisecond latency matters more than micrometer precision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Continuum robots (CRs) are recognized for their ability to navigate complex and constrained environments due to their compliant, continuously deforming backbones. However, this inherent compliance can lead to limitations in stiffness, payload capacity, and positioning accuracy during manipulation tasks. To overcome these limitations, collaborative configurations, such as co-manipulative continuum robots (CCRs), have been developed. CCRs involve multiple CRs mechanically coupled through a shared manipulated object, forming a closed-chain mechanism. This closed-chain configuration introduces significant challenges in static modeling, as the system must simultaneously satisfy both static equilibrium and geometric loop-closure constraints.

Traditional model-based approaches, such as Cosserat rod models and the Geometric Variable Strain (GVS) formulation, can accurately capture the distributed deformation and coupled behavior of CCRs. The GVS formulation, in particular, offers a finite-dimensional representation of Cosserat rod mechanics, allowing for a unified description of continuum arms and flexible objects. However, determining the static configuration for a given actuation input using these model-based methods requires solving a constrained nonlinear equilibrium problem, which is computationally intensive. This computational burden limits their applicability in real-time tasks such as prediction, optimization, and control.

To address the computational limitations, data-driven surrogate models, specifically Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), have been explored. ANNs can learn the mapping from actuation variables to robot configurations rapidly after training. Nevertheless, purely data-driven models do not inherently enforce physical principles. This can lead to predictions that violate static equilibrium or closed-chain constraints, particularly when faced with limited or noisy training data or unseen inputs. Furthermore, collecting extensive, representative experimental datasets for complex CCRs is often resource-intensive.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) represent an advancement that combines data-driven learning with the enforcement of governing physical laws. PINNs integrate physical equations and constraints directly into the learning objective, allowing data to guide the input-output mapping while physics-based residuals penalize mechanically inconsistent predictions. This approach aims to reduce data dependency and improve physical consistency. While PINNs have been applied to various CR problems, including static model approximation, inverse kinematics, shape reconstruction, and model predictive control, existing studies have primarily focused on individual, open-chain CRs. The simultaneous satisfaction of complex equilibrium and geometric constraints arising from closed-chain CCRs, with their multiple continuum arms and a shared flexible object, had not been comprehensively addressed by prior PINN research. This paper directly contributes to filling this gap by developing a constraint-aware PINN specifically designed for static shape estimation of closed-chain CCRs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a constraint-aware Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) for the static shape estimation of co-manipulative continuum robots (CCRs) operating as closed-chain systems. This involves enabling the network to predict physically consistent equilibrium configurations from tendon actuation inputs by incorporating both static equilibrium and geometric loop-closure constraints into the learning process.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and limitations observed in the field of continuum robotics:

- **Complexity of Co-Manipulative Continuum Robots (CCRs):** CCRs, formed by multiple continuum arms coupled through a flexible object, create a closed-chain mechanism. This configuration necessitates that the system satisfies both static equilibrium and geometric loop-closure constraints simultaneously. The inherent mechanical coupling and the generation of internal reaction forces make static modeling of CCRs significantly more complex than modeling individual, open-chain continuum robots.
- **Computational Cost of Model-Based Approaches:** Existing model-based formulations, such as the Geometric Variable Strain (GVS) formulation, can accurately describe CCR mechanics. However, solving the resulting constrained nonlinear equilibrium problem for each new actuation input is computationally expensive. This high computational cost precludes their use in applications requiring real-time performance, such as predictive control, online optimization, or rapid configuration updates.
- **Physical Inconsistency and Data Dependency of Purely Data-Driven Methods:** While Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) offer computational efficiency for input-output mappings, purely data-driven models do not inherently incorporate physical laws. Consequently, they may generate predictions that violate fundamental physical principles (e.g., static equilibrium, geometric closure), especially when trained with limited, noisy, or unrepresentative data. The prohibitive cost and time required to collect large, comprehensive experimental datasets for CCRs further highlight the limitations of purely data-driven approaches.
- **Gap in Physics-Informed Neural Network Applications for Closed-Chain Systems:** Although PINNs have shown promise in integrating physics into machine learning for continuum robots, previous studies have predominantly focused on individual continuum or soft robots. There was a lack of a comprehensive PINN framework capable of simultaneously enforcing the dual requirements of static equilibrium and geometric loop-closure constraints, which are critical for accurate and reliable shape estimation of closed-chain CCRs.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to provide a solution that offers:
- **Accuracy and Physical Consistency:** Ensuring that the predicted robot configurations are not only close to the actual state but also adhere to the fundamental physical laws governing the CCR.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Reducing the inference time significantly compared to iterative nonlinear solvers, thereby enabling real-time applications.
- **Robustness to Data Limitations:** Improving performance under conditions of limited and noisy training data, mitigating the need for extensive data collection.
- **Simulation-to-Experiment Transferability:** Developing a framework that allows pretraining in simulation and subsequent fine-tuning with experimental data, bridging the simulation-to-real gap.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves developing a constraint-aware PINN for static shape estimation of closed-chain CCRs, leveraging the Geometric Variable Strain (GVS) formulation.

#### 3.1. Static Geometric Variable Strain (GVS) Modeling

The foundation of the physics-informed approach is a detailed GVS model of the CCR system. The system comprises two continuum robots mechanically coupled via a flexible object, forming a closed kinematic chain.

- **GVS Kinematics:** A deformable rod is parameterized by a material coordinate $X$. The pose $g(X)$ of a local frame along the rod is defined by its position $p(X)$ and orientation $R(X)$. The spatial evolution of the pose is governed by the strain twist $\xi(X)$, which accounts for bending, torsion, shear, and axial deformation. The continuous strain field is approximated using a finite set of spatial basis functions and generalized GVS coordinates $q$. The nonlinear pose field is reconstructed through spatial integration using the exponential map and a fourth-order Zanna collocation scheme. The geometric Jacobian $J(q, X)$ is derived for evaluating generalized loads and constraints.
- **Static Equilibrium:** The internal elastic wrench $F_e(X)$ is related to strain deviation via a linear elastic constitutive law involving the cross-sectional stiffness matrix $K(X)$. This is used to derive the generalized stiffness matrix $K$ and generalized elastic force $Q_e = Kq$. The generalized tendon actuation force $\tau_a = B_q(q)u$ incorporates tendon routing geometry. Gravitational loading $F_g(q)$ is computed by mapping the gravity twist to the generalized coordinates using the geometric Jacobian. The unconstrained static equilibrium equation is then expressed as $Kq = B_q u + F_g$.
- **Closed-Chain Geometric Constraints and Jacobian:** The mechanical coupling forms closed-loop connections, imposing geometric constraints. For each connection, a relative transformation $g_{A_i B_i}$ between connection frames is defined. The configuration-level closure error $e_i(q)$ is derived by mapping the relative transformation to its six-dimensional twist representation and projecting it onto constrained wrench directions. All individual closure errors are combined into a global geometric constraint vector $e(q)$. The constraint Jacobian $A(q)$ describes the differential relationship imposed by the closed-chain connections.
- **Constraint-Consistent Static Equilibrium:** Internal reaction forces maintaining geometric constraints are represented by Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$. The constrained static equilibrium equation is $B_q(q)u + F_g(q) - Kq + A^T(q)\lambda = 0$. To eliminate the unknown $\lambda$, the equilibrium equation is projected onto the null space of the constraint Jacobian using the orthogonal projector $P(q) = I - A^T(q)(A(q)A^T(q))^{-1}A(q)$. This yields the physics residual $E_{phys}(q, u)$. Since this residual enforces static equilibrium only in constraint-consistent directions and does not independently enforce geometric loop closure, it is combined with the configuration constraint vector $e(q)$ to form the complete static residual $E(q, u)$. The static configuration $q^*$ satisfies $E(q^*, u) = 0$.

#### 3.2. Physics-Informed Static Shape Estimation (PINN)

The PINN is designed to learn the mapping from tendon tension inputs $u$ to GVS coordinates $q$, bypassing the iterative solution of $E(q^*, u) = 0$.

- **Network Architecture:** A fully connected feedforward neural network is employed, consisting of four hidden layers, each with 128 neurons and hyperbolic tangent activation functions. A linear output layer maps normalized tendon inputs to 16 standardized GVS coordinates.
- **Loss Function:** The training objective function combines three types of loss terms:
 - **Supervised Data Loss ($L_{data}$):** Measures the squared difference between the network's predicted standardized GVS coordinates ($\hat{e}q$) and the reference standardized GVS coordinates ($e_q$) from training data.
 - **Static Equilibrium Loss ($L_{phys}$):** Quantifies the consistency with static equilibrium by evaluating the squared $L_2$ norm of the projected static equilibrium residual $E_{phys}(\hat{q}, u)$, where $\hat{q}$ are the physical GVS coordinates predicted by the network.
 - **Geometric Constraint Loss ($L_{con}$):** Enforces geometric consistency of the closed chain by evaluating the squared $L_2$ norm of the configuration-level constraint residual $e(\hat{q})$.
 - **Total Loss:** $L = \lambda_{data}L_{data} + \lambda_{phys}L_{phys} + \lambda_{con}L_{con}$. The loss weights ($\lambda$) are dynamically selected based on the relative gradient magnitudes of individual loss terms during initialization to balance their contributions.
- **Training:** The gradients for network parameter updates are computed using automatic differentiation, backpropagating through both the neural network and the differentiable GVS model operations (forward kinematics, tendon actuation, constraint Jacobian, residuals). The Adam optimizer is used for training.

#### 3.3. Simulation-to-Experiment Transfer Framework

To apply the PINN to a physical CCR system, a two-stage approach is used:

- **Simulation Pretraining:** A PINN is initially trained using simulation-generated data. For experimental transfer, the network's input is tendon displacements ($\Delta\ell$), and it has two output branches: one for predicted marker displacements ($\Delta\hat{p}$) and another for GVS generalized coordinates ($\hat{q}$). The $\hat{q}$ output serves as an auxiliary physical state to enforce GVS forward kinematics, tendon length relations, and the projected static equilibrium and closed-chain geometric constraints.
- **Experimental Fine-Tuning:** The pretrained PINN is fine-tuned using experimental data, specifically measured tendon displacements and Vicon marker positions. Since GVS coordinates cannot be directly measured, a frozen copy of the simulation-pretrained PINN provides auxiliary generalized coordinate predictions for a consistency loss term. The contribution of the physics-based terms to the total loss is reduced during fine-tuning to allow adaptation to experimental measurements while retaining physical consistency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented two main sets of results: a simulation study comparing the PINN with an ANN and an iterative nonlinear solver, and an experimental study evaluating the PINN on a physical CCR platform.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Dataset Generation and Training Setup:** A dataset of 14,641 configurations was generated using a MATLAB-implemented GVS model for a CCR with 16 generalized coordinates. Training sets of 28, 70, and 140 samples were used, with Gaussian noise levels of 0%, 25%, and 50% applied to GVS coordinate training labels. Both ANN and PINN used identical network architectures and optimization settings (four hidden layers of 128 neurons, hyperbolic tangent activation, 52,240 trainable parameters). PINN loss weights were calibrated based on initial gradient magnitudes.
- **PINN and ANN Comparison:**
 - Under noise-free training conditions, the PINN and ANN exhibited similar prediction accuracy, with the PINN showing only minor improvements in relative error.
 - However, as training label noise increased, the PINN demonstrated substantial performance gains over the ANN. With 140 training samples and 50% noise, the PINN achieved a 67.88% reduction in relative configuration error, a 67.35% reduction in the projected equilibrium residual, and an 88.06% reduction in the closed-chain residual compared to the ANN.
 - The PINN consistently produced lower projected equilibrium and closed-chain residuals, particularly under noisy conditions, indicating that the physics-based loss terms acted as effective regularizers, enhancing robustness and promoting mechanically consistent predictions. For example, at 50% noise, the PINN's relative error decreased from 2.38% (28 samples) to 1.04% (140 samples), while the ANN's errors remained substantially higher.
- **PINN and Nonlinear Static Solver Comparison:**
 - The iterative nonlinear static solver provided a more accurate equilibrium solution, yielding a relative $\ell_2$ error of 0.0053%, significantly lower than the PINN's 0.1597%. The solver also produced substantially smaller physical residuals.
 - In terms of computational efficiency, the PINN demonstrated a significant advantage, requiring only 0.1773 ms per prediction. In contrast, the nonlinear solver took 17.97 seconds per solution. This represents approximately a 1.01 × 10^5 times lower median computation time for the PINN, while maintaining a relative configuration error below 0.16%.

#### 4.2. Experimental Results

- **Experimental Setup:** A physical two-arm tendon-driven CCR platform was used, featuring 0.4 m spring steel backbones and a 0.22 m Nitinol flexible object. Tendon actuation was controlled by Dynamixel servo motors. A Vicon motion-capture system recorded the 3D positions of eight reflective markers distributed along the CCR for configuration measurement. The dataset comprised 817 static configurations (tendon displacements and marker positions).
- **Simulation Pretraining for Experimental Transfer:** The PINN was pretrained using 1331 simulated configurations. This pretrained network achieved a marker RMSE of 0.164 mm and an R^2 value of 0.9987 on the simulation test set. The network was configured to take tendon displacements as input and output both marker displacements and GVS generalized coordinates, the latter serving as an auxiliary physical state for enforcing GVS model constraints.
- **Experimental Fine-Tuning:** The simulation-pretrained PINN was fine-tuned using the experimental dataset (571 training, 122 validation, 124 test samples). During fine-tuning, the physics loss terms' contribution was reduced to allow adaptation to the experimental measurements while preserving physical consistency.
- **Experimental Evaluation and Results:**
 - Fine-tuning significantly improved the model's performance on the held-out experimental test set. The overall marker RMSE decreased from 2.657 mm (before fine-tuning) to 0.497 mm (after fine-tuning), representing an 81.3% reduction.
 - The mean marker error decreased from 1.981 mm to 0.382 mm, and the maximum marker error was reduced from 9.244 mm to 2.282 mm.
 - The coefficient of determination (R^2) increased from -0.788 to 0.937, indicating a substantial improvement in the model's ability to explain the variance in marker displacements.
 - The percentage of marker predictions within 1 mm of the Vicon measurements increased from 37.30% to 94.96%.
 - The improvements were distributed across all eight measured marker locations, with the largest marker RMSE after fine-tuning being 0.827 mm at CR1 M4. This indicates that the fine-tuned model's predictions align closely with the physical CCR's behavior across its configuration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of continuum robotics by introducing a robust and computationally efficient method for static shape estimation of co-manipulative continuum robots (CCRs). The development of a constraint-aware Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) specifically for closed-chain CCRs addresses several critical challenges that have limited the practical application of these complex robotic systems.

One significant impact is the enhanced **physical consistency and accuracy** of shape estimation. By embedding both static equilibrium and geometric loop-closure constraints directly into the PINN's learning objective, the model generates predictions that adhere to the fundamental physics of the CCR. This contrasts with purely data-driven methods, which can produce physically unrealistic or inconsistent configurations, particularly in scenarios involving limited or noisy training data. The simulation results demonstrate a substantial reduction in prediction errors and residual violations compared to a data-driven ANN under challenging data conditions, highlighting the PINN's improved robustness.

Another key contribution is the **computational efficiency** achieved. The PINN offers a median inference time approximately 10^5 times faster than an iterative nonlinear solver, while maintaining a high level of prediction accuracy (e.g., 0.1597% relative configuration error). This acceleration is critical for enabling real-time applications of CCRs, such as real-time shape prediction, closed-loop control, online optimization, and human-robot interaction where rapid updates of the robot's state are necessary.

The research also provides a valuable **simulation-to-experiment transfer framework**. The ability to pretrain the PINN using simulation data and then fine-tune it with limited experimental measurements effectively bridges the reality gap between models and physical systems. The experimental validation showed a substantial reduction in marker RMSE and a significant increase in R^2 after fine-tuning, confirming the method's practicality and adaptability to real-world CCR platforms. This reduces the dependency on extensive and costly experimental data collection, making the development and deployment of CCRs more feasible.

The methodology is grounded in the Geometric Variable Strain (GVS) formulation, which provides a comprehensive and differentiable model for continuum robots. This choice of physical model, combined with automatic differentiation, allows for an effective integration of complex continuum mechanics into the neural network architecture.

The potential impact extends to various applications where CCRs are employed, including minimally invasive surgery, industrial manipulation in constrained environments, and advanced manufacturing. By providing accurate, physically consistent, and computationally efficient shape estimation, this work lays a foundation for more reliable control strategies, improved task execution, and safer interaction with the environment for these compliant robots. Future extensions to dynamic modeling, online adaptation to uncertainties, and integration into full control loops could further broaden its utility and establish PINNs as a standard tool for complex continuum robotic systems.
