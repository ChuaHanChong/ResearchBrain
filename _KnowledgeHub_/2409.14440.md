# Admittance Visuomotor Policy Learning for General-Purpose Contact-Rich Manipulations

> [!summary] Summary
> AdmitDiff Policy is a diffusion-based visuomotor framework for contact-rich manipulation with a multi-fingered hand. It fuses vision, proprioception and wrist force/torque readings, uses multiple prediction heads to jointly plan end-effector trajectories, finger joints and desired contact forces, and executes them through an admittance controller. Consistency distillation enables single-step denoising. Across five tasks it cut contact force 48.8% and raised success 15.3%.

## Problem

- Vision-based teleoperation gives no interactive contact feedback and estimates wrist pose and finger articulation imprecisely, so collected demonstrations lack reliable force-position accuracy.
- Existing diffusion visuomotor policies plan trajectories from images alone, neglecting force control accuracy and smooth transitions between contact stages in multi-stage tasks.
- Prior compliance-learning work predicts controller stiffness against a manually tuned constant goal wrench, which cannot track the varying forces demanded by frequent contact-state switching.

## Method

- A sub-$400 teleoperation rig: T265 stereo-IMU wrist tracking, Leap Motion gesture retargeting via a keypoint-vector optimization, and ERM vibration motors mapping 0-20 N contact force to haptic feedback.
- The **AdmitDiff Policy** network conditions a ==diffusion noise predictor== on force, dual-camera images and proprioception through ==FiLM==, fusing multi-level upsampled features and using separate ==Multi-Head Conv-Blocks== per action modality.
- ==Consistency distillation== from an ==EDM==-trained teacher into a student model gives single-step denoising; predicted force-position waypoints are Interp1d/Slerp interpolated and tracked by a mass-spring-damper ==admittance controller==.

## Results

- Average success across insertion, door opening, dragging, wiping and drawing reached **83%** versus **60%** for Diffusion Policy, **70%** for Consistency Policy and **73%** for the ablated no-force-control variant.
- Mean contact force dropped about **48.8%** and its standard deviation **52.0%** against baselines; on door opening, force fell **53.92%** and force variation **76.51%** versus Diffusion Policy.
- Distillation cut inference from roughly **100 ms** to **7 ms**; dragging-task force tracking held about **-4.5 N** with **0.8 N** RMSE, and teleoperation beat AnyTeleop by **11%** success and **4.82 s**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting a dynamic desired force trajectory, rather than compliance parameters against a fixed target wrench, is what lets one policy span heterogeneous contact primitives.
> - Force feedback helps at two independent points: it improves demonstration quality during collection and stabilizes execution; the ablation shows each contributes separately.
> - Faster single-step inference alone can add action jitter and worsen force fluctuation; the gain only materializes when paired with a high-frequency compliance controller.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses challenges in enabling robots to perform complex, general-purpose manipulation tasks in contact-rich environments. Such tasks, common in daily human activities, often involve multiple stages (e.g., reaching, pulling, detaching for opening a drawer) and necessitate precise force adjustments. Current robotic systems face difficulties with these tasks due to high-dimensional observation spaces, multimodal action distributions, and the need for seamless transitions between various contact states.

Traditionally, model-based methods for robot control are often insufficient in complex manipulations because they rely on precise dynamics models and state estimation, which can be difficult to obtain or maintain in uncertain environments. Data-driven methods, particularly those leveraging learning from demonstrations (LfD), offer an alternative but are contingent upon the availability of extensive, high-quality demonstration data.

Within the realm of data collection for LfD, human teleoperation is an intuitive and efficient approach. Vision-based teleoperation systems are appealing due to their lower cost and ease of deployment compared to expensive tracker-based virtual reality devices or wearable gloves. However, existing vision-based systems often struggle with providing interactive feedback to the operator, precise detection of human hand movements, and ensuring the quality of collected demonstrations, especially concerning compliant contact. Current solutions for haptic feedback, such as artificial skin or pneumatic gloves, are often costly and prone to damage.

Regarding robot policy learning, existing data-driven methods for contact-rich manipulations primarily focus on robot trajectory planning, with less emphasis on continuous force control that causally influences task completion. While compliant control strategies like impedance and admittance control are integrated into learning-based manipulation, they often rely on predefined or predicted stiffness parameters, or assume constant goal wrenches, which are inadequate for tasks involving numerous contact states and frequent transitions.

Recent advancements in diffusion-based visuomotor policies have shown promise in generating high-dimensional data that aligns with desired action distributions, offering advantages for planning and control in multi-fingered systems. However, these policies typically focus on perception and planning, often lacking sufficient attention to the integration of continuous force control, smooth stage transitions, and overall control accuracy. This paper positions itself by addressing these specific limitations, integrating a novel teleoperation system for improved data collection with a diffusion-based visuomotor policy that explicitly incorporates dynamic contact force prediction and admittance control for enhanced performance in general-purpose contact-rich manipulations, particularly for multi-fingered robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observation that robots performing general-purpose manipulation tasks in daily environments must be capable of precise and adaptive execution of actions involving object contact. These actions are often multi-stage and require distinct force control strategies, with smooth transitions between stages being particularly challenging. The authors identify a critical gap: while visual and proprioceptive data are common modalities, contact force is an essential, often underutilized, modality that can compensate for their deficiencies in collision perception, high-precision grasping, and efficient manipulation.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

1. **To develop a low-cost, user-friendly teleoperation system for efficient data collection:** The aim is to overcome the limitations of existing teleoperation methods, specifically by providing intuitive contact force feedback to the operator through vibration motors, integrating multimodal perception (including vision and proprioception), and enabling precise pose tracking using dual stereo cameras. This system is designed to accelerate data collection while ensuring the acquisition of high-quality, compliant robot demonstrations at a total cost below $400.
2. **To propose a novel admittance visuomotor policy framework (AdmitDiff Policy) for continuous, general-purpose, contact-rich manipulations:** This objective involves designing a diffusion-based model that can plan not only action trajectories but also desired contact forces from multimodal observations (contact force, vision, and proprioception). The framework seeks to explicitly address the need for continuous force control and smooth stage transitions, particularly for multi-fingered hand robots, where existing diffusion policies have demonstrated insufficient attention to these aspects.
3. **To integrate an admittance controller for smooth and compliant action execution:** The policy's predicted dynamic contact forces and action trajectories are intended to be executed smoothly and compliantly through an admittance controller. This integration aims to refine robot actions by interpolating force-position trajectories, thereby enhancing the robot's ability to handle complex contact operations and reduce action jitters.
4. **To evaluate the framework's generalization capabilities across challenging tasks:** The research seeks to demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed AdmitDiff Policy by conducting a comparative evaluation against state-of-the-art methods on five distinct, challenging contact-rich multi-stage manipulation tasks, each focusing on different action primitives. The evaluation intends to quantify improvements in success rates and the quality of contact force control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed AdmitDiff Policy framework integrates a specialized teleoperation system for data collection with a diffusion-based visuomotor policy and an admittance controller for action execution.

**A. Force-Interactive Hand-Arm Teleoperation System**
A low-cost, user-friendly teleoperation system was developed for collecting multimodal demonstration data, including compliant robot movements and contact force information. The system comprises three main components:
- **Wrist Spatial Pose Control Module:** An Intel RealSense Tracking Camera T265, mounted on the operator's palm using a custom glove, estimates the 6-DoF position and orientation of the wearer's wrist. The T265 utilizes stereo cameras and an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) for Visual-Inertial Odometry, providing accurate spatial tracking.
- **Gesture Detection and Remapping Module:** An Ultraleap Leap Motion Controller estimates the keypoint coordinates of human hand gestures. These are then kinematically retargeted to generate joint angles for a multi-fingered robot hand, enabling precise gesture-based control. A cost function minimizes the keypoint vector error and the change in robot joint angles to optimize the mapping.
- **Wrist-Mounted Force Feedback Module:** Eccentric Rotating Mass (ERM) vibration motors are attached to the operator's palm. An STM32 microcontroller generates Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) signals to control the vibration intensity, which is linearly mapped to the contact force (0-20 N) sensed by a wrist Force/Torque sensor on the robot. This provides real-time haptic feedback to the operator.

The slave-side system consists of a 6-DoF UR5-CB3 robotic arm, a LEAP Hand as the end-effector, an OptoForce 6-Axis Force/Torque Sensor for wrist force feedback, and RealSense D435i and UVC cameras for visual feedback. The entire teleoperation system aims to balance cost and effectiveness, providing stable wrist pose estimation and force feedback.

**B. AdmitDiff Policy Design and Training**
The core of the framework is a diffusion-based imitation learning policy designed to predict future desired end-effector positions, orientations, and contact forces.
- **Policy Design:** The policy takes global and local camera images, proprioceptive data, and current contact force measurements as input features. These multimodal inputs are linearly projected into a unified feature space via a Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) module, which conditions the diffusion model for accurate noise prediction. The noise prediction network is structured similarly to existing diffusion policies, using ResNet-18 as a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) encoder and a 3-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) for low-dimensional data encoding. To enhance precision and mitigate information loss, features are extracted from multiple levels during upsampling, normalized, and fused. Multiple prediction heads, each a Multi-Head Conv-Block with four convolutional layers, are employed to predict distinct features for conditional denoising (end-effector pose, hand joint movements, and contact force), allowing for a more precise loss function formulation.
- **Policy Training and Consistency Distillation:** To address the computational intensity of iterative multi-step denoising in diffusion models, which can compromise real-time performance for admittance control, a consistency distillation approach is introduced. Inspired by the Consistency Model (CM), this method trains the model to produce consistent predictions across various points along a Probability Flow Ordinary Differential Equation (PFODE) trajectory, ultimately enabling single-step denoising. A teacher model (π_T_θ) is initially trained using the EDM framework with a Denoising Score Matching (DSM) loss. This teacher model's parameters are then frozen, and a student model (π_S_φ), initialized with the teacher's parameters, is trained using a consistency loss (L_CTM + L_DSM). The student model is optimized to predict actions (A_t') at any earlier time from an action (A_t) at time t. The policy generates trajectory points at 10 Hz.

**C. Admittance Force Control**
An admittance controller is utilized to refine robot actions, ensuring smooth and precise manipulation based on the policy's force-position trajectory predictions.
- **Trajectory Interpolation:** The AdmitDiff Policy predicts force-position trajectory points at 0.1-second intervals. Before these points are fed to the admittance controller, they are interpolated to generate a dense trajectory curve. One-dimensional linear interpolation (Interp1d) is used for target positions and contact force, while spherical linear interpolation (Slerp) is applied for target rotations, ensuring smooth and continuous reference trajectories.
- **Admittance Model:** The controller employs a standard admittance model (Equation 7), which models the robot arm as an adjustable mass-spring-damper system. This model regulates the interaction between the robot end-effector and the environment during contact tasks. The desired acceleration (¨p(t)) is calculated based on the inertia (M_d), damping (B_d), and stiffness (K_d) matrices, along with feedback (F(t), p(t), ˙p(t)) and desired (F_d(t), p_d(t)) force and position vectors. The M_d, B_d, and K_d parameters are manually designed for each specific task to optimize force tracking performance.
- **Action Execution:** The calculated accelerations are then integrated to update velocities and positions (Equation 9), which are subsequently used to control the robotic arm, enabling dynamic adaptation of motion based on real-time contact force variations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the AdmitDiff Policy framework was conducted through comprehensive experiments on both the teleoperation system and the policy's performance in contact-rich manipulation tasks.

**A. Performance of Robot Teleoperation**
The developed teleoperation system was benchmarked on the Telekinesis Benchmark, which includes five multi-finger robotic teleoperation tasks.
- The system demonstrated superior precision and speed compared to a SpaceMouse+Leap system and AnyTeleop, while also offering improved user experience.
- On average, the proposed system reduced task completion time by 8.94 seconds compared to SpaceMouse+Leap and 4.82 seconds compared to AnyTeleop.
- It achieved an average increase in success rate of 26% over SpaceMouse+Leap and 11% over AnyTeleop.
- The system's version without force feedback (w/o FF) also performed well but was outperformed by the full system, indicating the benefit of haptic feedback. For instance, in "Pickup Box Object," the full system achieved a 20/20 success rate with an average time of 11.1s, compared to 19/20 and 14.5s for the w/o FF version.
- The teleoperation system components collectively cost below $400.

**B. Experiment Setups**
Five challenging contact-rich multi-stage manipulation tasks were designed to evaluate the AdmitDiff Policy:
1. **Insertion:** Grasping and twisting a charger plug into a hole.
2. **Door Opening:** Applying force to rotate a handle and push open a door.
3. **Dragging:** Pulling a drawer by inserting fingers into the handle.
4. **Wiping:** Grasping an eraser and applying force to clean a whiteboard.
5. **Drawing:** Grasping a marker and maintaining consistent force while following a trajectory.
For each task, 50 demonstration trajectories were collected using the teleoperation system, followed by pre-processing (Rotation6D conversion, low-pass filtering). Each task was trained over 400 epochs (approximately 8 hours).

**C. AdmitDiff Policy Performance**
The AdmitDiff Policy was compared against state-of-the-art visuomotor policies: Diffusion Policy (DP), Consistency Policy (CP), and an ablation study version termed Admit Policy without Force Control (Admit w/o FC).
- **Inference Time:** Consistency distillation significantly reduced inference time. The Diffusion Policy exhibited an inference time of approximately 100 ms, while the Consistency Model (CM) achieved about 7 ms, establishing better conditions for responsive admittance control.
- **Success Rate on Manipulation Tasks:** Across twenty trials per method per task:
 - Admit Policy (Ours) achieved the highest average success rate of 83%.
 - This represented an average success rate increase of 15.3% compared to the other three methods.
 - In tasks requiring high precision in force control, such as Insertion, the method improved the success rate by up to 30% (90% for Admit Policy vs. 60% for Diffusion Policy).
 - Even in the Drawing task, which had low success rates across all methods due to high demands for spatial understanding, Admit Policy maintained a competitive advantage (45%).
- **Performance of Contact Force Tracking:** In the Dragging task, the contact force was maintained stably at approximately -4.5 N along the negative X-axis. The force tracking Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) was around 0.8 N, indicating stable and compliant task execution despite minor fluctuations.
- **Performance Comparison of Contact Force Control:**
 - AdmitDiff Policy consistently demonstrated the most stable contact force control across all five tasks, characterized by lower variance and more controlled median force, particularly evident in tasks like Insertion and Wiping.
 - Diffusion Policy and Consistency Policy showed higher variance, especially in force-intensive tasks like DoorOpening, indicating less stable control.
 - The AdmitDiff Policy achieved an average reduction of approximately 48.8% in mean contact force and 52.0% in standard deviation across the five tasks compared to other methods.
 - Specific analysis of the DoorOpening task revealed that Admit Policy with the contact force control module reduced mean contact force by up to 53.92% and force variation by 76.51% compared to Diffusion Policy. Even without the control module (Admit w/o FC), incorporating force feedback reduced force variation by up to 49.21%. The Consistency Policy exhibited slight action jitters, leading to larger force fluctuations than the Diffusion Policy, highlighting the value of the AdmitDiff Policy's force feedback and admittance control in minimizing jitters and optimizing force control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that advances the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving significant contact interaction. The significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Robotic Capabilities for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The AdmitDiff Policy framework significantly improves robots' ability to perform continuous, general-purpose, contact-rich manipulation tasks. By integrating dynamic contact force prediction with an admittance controller, the framework achieves higher success rates and smoother, more efficient contact compared to existing methods. This capability is critical for deploying robots in unstructured environments where precise force control and adaptability are necessary.
- **Accessible and Efficient Data Collection:** The development of a low-cost, force-interactive hand-arm teleoperation system provides an efficient and user-friendly means of gathering high-quality, compliant multimodal demonstration data. This addresses a major bottleneck in data-driven robot learning, making it more accessible for researchers and practitioners to collect the necessary datasets for complex manipulation skills without requiring expensive specialized equipment. The ability to collect data at a cost below $400 can democratize access to advanced robot learning paradigms.
- **Advancement in Learning-Based Control Architectures:** The integration of diffusion models with explicit dynamic contact force prediction and an admittance controller represents a notable architectural advancement. Previous diffusion policies primarily focused on visual and proprioceptive aspects. This work highlights the critical role of predicted force trajectories in robust manipulation, offering a new direction for designing visuomotor policies that are inherently more capable of handling physical interactions. The consistency distillation technique further makes these advanced policies practical for real-time robotic control by significantly reducing inference latency.
- **Improved Safety and Robustness:** The observed reduction in mean contact force (by 48.8% on average) and standard deviation (by 52.0% on average) across various tasks indicates a more controlled and compliant interaction with the environment. This not only suggests safer operation (reducing the risk of damage to objects or the robot itself) but also implies increased robustness to environmental uncertainties and variations.
- **Broad Generalization Potential:** The framework's demonstrated effectiveness across five distinct manipulation tasks, each representing a different action primitive (grasping, twisting, pushing, pulling, rubbing, scratching), indicates its strong generalization capabilities. This suggests that the AdmitDiff Policy can learn and adapt to a wide variety of tasks without extensive re-training or task-specific tuning, moving towards more general-purpose manipulation systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a foundation for future exploration into more complex manipulation scenarios and finer-grained control. The authors indicate future work will focus on extending the method to more complex tasks and exploring finger-level admittance control, which could lead to even more dexterous and precise robotic manipulation capabilities.
