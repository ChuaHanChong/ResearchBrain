# Visual Language Maps for Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper introduces VLMaps, a spatial map representation that fuses visual-language model features with 3D reconstructions of environments, enabling robots to interpret and execute complex, open-vocabulary natural language commands. The system allows for zero-shot spatial goal navigation and dynamic, embodiment-specific obstacle map generation, outperforming existing methods in simulation and demonstrating real-world applicability.

## Problem

- Traditional geometric maps provide spatial precision but lack semantic information, hindering interpretation of abstract commands like "go to the kitchen."
- Existing VLM-based navigation methods offer open-vocabulary object recognition but struggle with spatial precision, persistence, and complex spatial relationships (e.g., "in between the sofa and the TV").
- Current robot navigation systems cannot dynamically adapt obstacle maps to different robot types or interpret nuanced language descriptions of traversability.

## Method

- A Visual-Language Map (VLMap) is constructed by fusing pixel-level visual-language embeddings (from LSeg) into a dense, top-down 2D grid map derived from 3D ==point cloud== data, creating a spatially consistent and language-indexed representation.
- Open-vocabulary landmark localization is achieved by comparing CLIP text embeddings of natural language queries with averaged visual-language embeddings in the VLMap to generate semantic segmentation masks.
- An LLM (Codex) is used to interpret complex natural language instructions and generate executable Python code, calling parameterized navigation primitives that ground spatial goals using the VLMap and dynamically generated, embodiment-specific obstacle maps.

## Results

- VLMaps consistently outperformed baselines (CoW, LM-Nav, CLIP Map) in multi-object navigation, achieving **59%** success for 1 subgoal and **15%** for 4 subgoals in simulation.
- The framework demonstrated significant superiority in zero-shot spatial goal navigation, reaching **62%** success for 1 subgoal and **10%** for 4 subgoals in simulation, while baselines achieved near **0%** for complex spatial tasks.
- Real-world experiments with a HSR mobile robot successfully completed 10 out of 20 language-based spatial goals, including complex instructions like "move between the chair and the wooden box."

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fusing visual-language model (VLM) features directly into 3D geometric maps creates a novel map representation that grounds open-vocabulary language with spatial precision.
> - Large Language Models (LLMs) can generate executable robot code for complex, multi-step natural language instructions, enabling zero-shot spatial navigation.
> - A single underlying visual-language map can dynamically generate embodiment-specific obstacle maps, facilitating multi-robot coordination and adaptability across different robot types.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical challenge at the intersection of robotics, computer vision, and natural language processing: enabling robots to understand and execute complex, open-vocabulary navigation commands in physical environments. It positions itself as a significant advancement by bridging a long-standing gap between traditional geometric mapping and recent progress in visual-language grounding.

**Evolution of Robot Navigation and Semantic Understanding:**

Historically, robot navigation has relied on **geometric maps** (e.g., occupancy grids or point clouds) generated through SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) techniques. These maps are precise for path planning but lack semantic information, making it difficult for robots to interpret abstract commands like "go to the kitchen" or "find the book." Efforts to integrate **semantic mapping** have primarily focused on augmenting 3D maps with predefined object classes using convolutional neural networks (CNNs). While successful, these methods are limited to a closed set of categories, struggling with generalization to novel objects or environments.

More recently, the field of **Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN)** has emerged, focusing on training agents to follow natural language instructions. Early VLN approaches often involved learning end-to-end policies or navigating on topological graphs in simulated environments. While promising, these methods typically require vast amounts of task-specific data and often lack the low-level planning capabilities or continuous state space generalization needed for real-world deployment.

**The Rise of Zero-Shot Capabilities with Large Pre-trained Models:**

A paradigm shift has occurred with the advent of large-scale pre-trained **Visual-Language Models (VLMs)** like CLIP and LSeg, trained on Internet-scale image-text pairs. These models exhibit remarkable **zero-shot generalization** capabilities, allowing them to understand and ground language to novel visual concepts without explicit fine-tuning. This has led to new approaches in robot navigation, such as:

- **CLIP on Wheels (CoW):** Uses CLIP to generate saliency maps for target object categories, enabling zero-shot object navigation by guiding exploration towards high-saliency regions.
- **LM-Nav:** Combines GPT-3 and CLIP to parse language instructions into landmark sequences and navigate topological graphs.

**The Identified Gap: Disjoint Language Grounding and Spatial Mapping:**

The authors critically observe that while these VLM-based methods enable open-vocabulary object goal navigation, they predominantly use VLMs as "critics" to match observations to descriptions. Crucially, this language grounding remains **disjoint from the process of constructing and leveraging a persistent spatial map of the environment.** This disconnect leads to several limitations:

1. **Lack of Spatial Precision:** Current VLM-based methods struggle with spatial goals beyond simple object detection (e.g., "in between the sofa and the TV," "three meters to the right of the chair").
2. **Limited Persistence and Correspondence:** They may not recognize independent observations of the same object, nor build persistent representations that can be shared or updated over time.
3. **Inability to Adapt to Embodiment:** They cannot dynamically generate obstacle maps tailored to different robot types (e.g., a large ground robot vs. a flying drone) based on natural language descriptions of traversability.

**VLMaps' Contribution: Fusing Language into Spatial Maps:**

This paper directly addresses these limitations by proposing **VLMaps**, a novel spatial map representation that *fuses pretrained visual-language features directly with a 3D reconstruction of the physical world*. By spatially anchoring VLM features, VLMaps aim to combine the open-vocabulary power of VLMs with the spatial precision and persistence of classic geometric maps. This positions VLMaps as a crucial step towards more sophisticated, human-interpretable, and adaptable robotic navigation systems. The concurrent work NLMap is mentioned as also exploring queryable scene representations for LLM-based robot planning, highlighting the growing interest in this specific intersection.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robot navigation system that can interpret and execute complex, open-vocabulary natural language commands by grounding them precisely within a spatial map of the environment. The authors are motivated by the inherent limitations of existing approaches and aim to leverage the power of large pre-trained visual-language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs) to achieve this.

**Core Motivation:**

Humans effortlessly navigate their environments using "cognitive maps" that link spatial memory with semantic knowledge, allowing them to localize landmarks at various levels of abstraction (e.g., "a book," "on the shelf," "in the living room") and reason about traversability. Replicating this capability in robots is challenging. Traditional robotic mapping provides spatial precision but lacks semantic understanding, while recent VLM-based navigation struggles with spatial accuracy and persistence despite open-vocabulary object recognition. The central motivation is to **combine the best of both worlds**: the spatial precision of geometric maps with the open-vocabulary generalization and semantic richness offered by VLMs.

**Key Objectives of VLMaps:**

The authors articulate several specific objectives for their VLMaps framework:

1. **Spatial Visual-Language Map Creation:** To autonomously build a persistent spatial map representation (VLMap) from robot video feed (RGB-D data) by directly fusing pretrained visual-language features (from models like LSeg) with a 3D reconstruction of the physical world. This map should be intrinsically "language-indexed" without requiring additional labeled data.
2. **Open-Vocabulary Landmark Localization:** To enable the direct localization of landmarks and arbitrary spatial references within the VLMap using free-form natural language. This goes beyond mere object recognition to include concepts like "in between the sofa and the TV" or "three meters to the right of the chair."
3. **Language-Conditioned Obstacle Map Generation:** To allow for the dynamic, on-the-fly generation of embodiment-specific obstacle maps from a list of natural language-described obstacle categories. This objective aims to facilitate multi-robot coordination and adaptation across different robot types (e.g., a ground robot versus a drone navigating the same environment).
4. **Zero-Shot Spatial Goal Navigation:** To translate complex natural language commands (potentially multi-step and incorporating spatial relationships) into executable navigation goals directly localized in the map. This process should leverage the code-writing capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate parameterized API calls for navigation primitives, achieving zero-shot performance without additional data collection or model fine-tuning.
5. **Enhanced Navigation Capabilities:** To demonstrate that VLMaps enable navigation according to more complex language instructions and lead to more effective long-horizon multi-object goal navigation compared to existing methods, especially for spatial open-vocabulary tasks.

In essence, the motivation is to empower robots with a more human-like understanding of their surroundings, allowing them to interpret and act upon rich, nuanced natural language commands in a spatially aware manner.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The VLMaps approach is built upon the core idea of fusing semantic information from pre-trained visual-language models directly into a 3D geometric map. This enables an open-vocabulary, spatial map representation that facilitates various downstream navigation tasks. The methodology is broken down into four key components:

**A. Building a Visual-Language Map (VLMap):**
The foundational step involves creating a VLMap, which is a dense, top-down grid map where each cell contains a compact visual-language embedding.
1. **Sensor Input:** The robot continuously captures RGB-D (color and depth) images along with its estimated camera pose (odometry), typically obtained from a standard RGB-D SLAM system (like RTAB-Map in real-world experiments).
2. **Pixel-level Embeddings:** For each incoming RGB image, the authors use **LSeg**, a language-driven semantic segmentation model. LSeg's visual encoder is designed to map each pixel of an image into an embedding vector within the **CLIP feature space**. This provides dense, pixel-level visual-language features that are inherently aligned with textual descriptions.
3. **3D Back-projection:** Using the depth data and camera intrinsics, each pixel's 2D coordinates and its depth value are back-projected to obtain a 3D point in the camera's local frame. These 3D points are then transformed into the global world frame using the robot's estimated camera pose.
4. **Top-down Grid Projection and Fusion:** Each 3D point's global coordinates are then projected onto a 2D ground plane to determine its corresponding cell in a predefined top-down grid map. The key innovation here is the *fusion* of the visual-language features: if multiple 3D points (potentially from different camera views or frames) project to the same grid cell, their LSeg pixel embeddings are *averaged*. This averaging process effectively integrates multi-view semantic information, creating a robust and spatially consistent VLMap where each grid cell stores an averaged visual-language embedding.

**B. Localizing Open-Vocabulary Landmarks:**
Once the VLMap is built, it can be queried using natural language to localize objects or semantic regions.
1. **Text Encoding:** A user provides a list of desired categories or landmarks as natural language text (e.g., "chair," "sofa," "kitchen area"). The pre-trained **CLIP text encoder** (frozen during this process) converts these text descriptions into a list of corresponding text embedding vectors.
2. **Similarity Computation:** The flattened grid cell embeddings from the VLMap (Q) are then compared against the text embeddings (E) by computing a pixel-to-category similarity matrix (S = Q ⋅ E^T). This matrix quantifies how semantically similar each map location is to each queried language category.
3. **Segmentation Mask Generation:** By applying an `argmax` operation across the category dimension of the similarity matrix and reshaping the result, a semantic segmentation mask is generated for the top-down map. Each cell in this resulting mask is assigned the label of the language category it is most similar to, effectively providing open-vocabulary semantic segmentation directly on the spatial map.

**C. Generating Open-Vocabulary Obstacle Maps:**
A unique capability of VLMaps is the dynamic generation of obstacle maps tailored to different robot embodiments, all from the same underlying VLMap.
1. **Initial Occupancy Map:** A baseline occupancy map (O) is first created by projecting all 3D points from the depth cloud onto the top-down grid, filtering out points too high or too low (e.g., ceiling or floor).
2. **Embodiment-Specific Obstacle Definitions:** Users define a list of obstacle categories using natural language for a specific robot embodiment (e.g., for a ground robot, "table" might be an obstacle; for a drone, it might not).
3. **Obstacle Localization and Fusion:** The open-vocabulary landmark localization method (Sec. III-B) is applied to identify the regions corresponding to these defined obstacle categories. The union of the resulting segmentation masks for the chosen obstacle categories forms a preliminary obstacle mask.
4. **Refinement:** This mask is then intersected with the initial occupancy map (O) to remove false positives (e.g., an object incorrectly classified as an obstacle but appearing on the floor where it's traversable for a drone at a certain height). The final result is a binary obstacle map specifically configured for that robot embodiment.

**D. Zero-Shot Spatial Goal Navigation from Language:**
The VLMaps framework integrates with Large Language Models (LLMs) to enable complex, multi-step spatial navigation from natural language commands.
1. **LLM for High-Level Planning:** The system uses an LLM (e.g., Codex, fine-tuned with few-shot prompting) to interpret verbose natural language instructions and decompose them into a sequence of executable subgoals.
2. **Code Generation:** Crucially, the LLM is primed to generate executable Python robot code. This code calls a set of predefined, parameterized navigation primitive functions (e.g., `robot.move_to_left('counter')`, `robot.move_in_between('sink', 'oven')`, `robot.move_forward(3)`). The ability of LLMs to generate structured code for logic (loops, conditionals) allows for highly complex and flexible command execution.
3. **Grounding Navigation Primitives:** The navigation primitives leverage the VLMap:
 - For object-centric goals (e.g., `move_to_object('chair')`), the VLMap's landmark localization (Sec. III-B) is used to find the coordinates of the target object.
 - For spatial goals (e.g., "left of X," "in between X and Y," "N meters north of X"), the VLMap localizes the reference object(s), and predefined geometric offsets or calculations are applied to derive the precise target coordinates.
4. **Low-Level Execution:** An off-the-shelf navigation stack (e.g., ROS navigation stack) then takes these localized coordinates and the embodiment-specific obstacle map (from Sec. III-C) to plan and execute a collision-free path for the robot.

This modular yet integrated approach allows VLMaps to leverage the strengths of various advanced AI models while providing a robust, spatially aware foundation for robot autonomy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world environments to validate the effectiveness of VLMaps across various navigation tasks.

**A. Simulation Setup:**
- **Habitat Simulator (Matterport3D dataset):** Used for multi-object and spatial goal navigation tasks, providing realistic indoor scenes.
- **AI2THOR Simulator:** Utilized for evaluating open-vocabulary multi-embodiment obstacle map generation due to its support for multiple agent types (e.g., LoCoBot and drone).
- **Baselines:**
 - **LM-Nav:** Graph-based navigation using GPT-3 and CLIP to parse instructions and plan on topological graphs.
 - **CLIP on Wheels (CoW):** Language-based object navigation using CLIP and GradCAM for saliency maps and path planning.
 - **CLIP-features-based map (CLIP Map):** An ablative baseline similar to VLMaps but using raw CLIP visual features averaged onto the map, without the LSeg enhancement.
 - **Ground Truth (GT) Map:** Provides an upper bound on performance, having perfect semantic knowledge.

**B. Multi-Object Navigation:**
- **Task:** Navigate to a sequence of 4 randomly chosen object types (from 30 categories) in 91 different trajectories.
- **Metrics:** Success Rate (SR) for reaching 1 to 4 subgoals consecutively and independent subgoal SR.
- **Results (Table I):**
 - **VLMaps consistently outperform all baselines.** For 1 subgoal, VLMaps achieve 59% SR, significantly higher than CoW (42%), CLIP Map (33%), and LM-Nav (26%).
 - **Superior long-horizon performance:** The performance gap widens for longer sequences (4 subgoals), where VLMaps maintain 15% SR, while CoW drops to 3%, and others are near zero.
 - **Reason for Success:** Qualitative visualization (Fig. 4) and quantitative analysis (Table V, VI in Appendix) show that VLMaps generate much cleaner and more accurate object masks compared to CoW (which is noisy due to GradCAM) and CLIP Map (which suffers from simple CLIP feature averaging). This leads to more reliable path planning towards correct goals.

**C. Zero-Shot Spatial Goal Navigation from Language:**
- **Task:** Navigate to 4 different spatial locations per trajectory (21 trajectories in 7 scenes), specified by complex language instructions (e.g., "east of the table," "in between the chair and the sofa," "move forward 3 meters," "with the counter on your right").
- **Metric:** In-a-row success rate.
- **Results (Table II):**
 - **VLMaps demonstrate significant superiority.** For 1 subgoal, VLMaps achieve 62% SR, whereas CoW manages only 33%, CLIP Map 19%, and LM-Nav a mere 5%.
 - **Unrivaled for complex spatial tasks:** For 4 subgoals, VLMaps achieve 10% SR, while all other baselines drop to 0%.
 - **Key takeaway:** This highlights VLMaps' core strength: its ability to accurately localize and navigate to precise spatial references defined by natural language, a capability largely absent in prior VLM-based navigation systems.

**D. Cross-Embodiment Navigation:**
- **Task:** Evaluate navigation efficiency for different robot embodiments (LoCoBot and drone) using obstacle maps dynamically generated from VLMaps in the AI2THOR simulator.
- **Metrics:** Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL).
- **Setups:** LoCoBot with "ground map" (standard obstacles), Drone with "ground map," and Drone with "drone map" (customized obstacles, e.g., ignoring tables).
- **Results (Table III):**
 - **Similar SR:** All setups achieve comparable success rates (around 53-56% for 1 subgoal).
 - **Improved Efficiency (SPL) for Customized Maps:**
 - The Drone using its **"drone map" (customized)** achieves higher SPL values (e.g., 45.4% for 1 subgoal) compared to the Drone using the generic "ground map" (41.8%).
 - This demonstrates that VLMaps successfully generate obstacle maps that lead to **more efficient navigation paths** by allowing the drone to fly over obstacles traversable for its embodiment (Fig. 5).
 - **Generalization of Efficiency:** The SPL values for the Drone with the "drone map" are comparable to the LoCoBot with the "ground map," suggesting that VLMaps help generalize navigation efficiency across different embodiments.

**E. Real Robot Experiments (HSR mobile robot):**
- **Setup:** HSR mobile robot in a semantically rich indoor scene. VLMap created from 374 RGB-D frames.
- **Task:** Test 20 different language-based spatial goals.
- **Results:**
 - **Successful Real-World Deployment:** The robot successfully completed 10 out of 20 navigation goals.
 - **Demonstrated Spatial Capabilities:** Six successful trials involved complex spatial goals (e.g., "move between the chair and the wooden box," "move to the south of the table"). Other successes included relative positioning ("move 3 meters right") and repetitive instructions ("move between the keyboard and the laptop twice").
 - **Identified Failure Modes:** Failures were attributed to real-world challenges: inaccurate depth data introducing noise into map creation, odometry drift impacting landmark indexing accuracy, and action noise affecting navigation performance.
- **Conclusion:** The real-world experiments, despite some failures due to hardware/environmental noise, successfully demonstrate the practical applicability of VLMaps for zero-shot spatial goal navigation on physical robots.

In summary, the results consistently show that VLMaps significantly advance the state-of-the-art in language-based robot navigation, particularly for tasks involving complex spatial understanding and cross-embodiment adaptability, outperforming existing baselines in both simulation and real-world tests.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of VLMaps represents a significant step forward in embodied AI and robot autonomy, offering a novel paradigm for how robots can perceive, understand, and navigate their environments using natural language.

**Key Contributions and Advancement of State-of-the-Art:**

1. **Bridging the Semantic-Spatial Divide:** The most critical contribution is the **direct fusion of visual-language model features into 3D geometric maps**. This effectively bridges the long-standing gap between open-vocabulary semantic understanding and precise spatial mapping, a capability largely missing in prior work. This allows robots to combine "what" an object is with "where" it is and "how" it relates to other objects.
2. **Truly Spatial Language Grounding:** Unlike previous VLM-based navigation methods that primarily focus on object-centric goals, VLMaps enable grounding of complex spatial expressions (e.g., "in between X and Y," "N meters to the east of Z"). This significantly enhances the robot's ability to understand and execute human-like, nuanced instructions.
3. **Zero-Shot, Data-Efficient Operation:** By leveraging powerful pre-trained VLMs (LSeg, CLIP) and LLMs (Codex), VLMaps operate in a zero-shot manner, requiring no additional labeled data or task-specific fine-tuning. This drastically reduces the data collection and annotation burden, making the system highly adaptable to new environments and tasks.
4. **Flexible and Adaptable Navigation Policies:** The use of LLMs to generate executable Python code for navigation primitives offers unprecedented flexibility. It allows the robot to parse and act upon complex instructions involving logical structures (loops), quantitative measures (distances), and intricate spatial relationships, moving beyond rigid, predefined command sets.
5. **Multi-Embodiment and Shared Mapping Capabilities:** The ability to dynamically generate embodiment-specific obstacle maps from the same underlying VLMap is highly impactful. This facilitates seamless map sharing and collaboration among heterogeneous robot teams, where different robots (e.g., ground robots, drones) can interpret and navigate the same environment according to their unique physical constraints and affordances.

**Potential Impact:**

1. **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** VLMaps pave the way for more intuitive and natural human-robot communication. People can simply tell a robot where to go using everyday language, including spatial references, without needing to learn complex programming interfaces or precise coordinates. This makes robots more accessible and user-friendly for a wider range of applications.
2. **Increased Robot Autonomy and Versatility:** Robots equipped with VLMaps can operate more autonomously in unstructured and novel environments. They can adapt to new tasks, reconfigure their understanding of obstacles, and perform complex long-horizon missions without constant human intervention or extensive pre-mapping.
3. **Foundation for Cognitive Robotics:** By building a persistent, queryable, and semantically rich spatial memory, VLMaps lay crucial groundwork for future research in cognitive robotics. This could lead to robots capable of more sophisticated spatial reasoning, planning, and learning from experience in a manner closer to human cognition.
4. **Applications Across Domains:** The impact could span various fields:
 - **Household Robotics:** More intelligent robot vacuum cleaners or assistants that can understand commands like "clean around the sofa" or "go to the kitchen sink."
 - **Industrial Automation:** Autonomous inspection robots that can navigate complex layouts based on natural language instructions for specific areas or machinery.
 - **Search and Rescue/Exploration:** Robots that can be deployed into unknown environments and tasked with finding "survivors near the collapsed wall" or "exploring the area behind the debris."
 - **Multi-Robot Systems:** Improved coordination and task allocation in heterogeneous robot fleets where different agents perform specialized tasks based on a shared, dynamically adaptable environmental understanding.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge limitations such as sensitivity to 3D reconstruction noise (from depth sensors and odometry drift) and potential ambiguities in landmark indexing in cluttered scenes. These are common challenges in robotics. Future work aims to address these by integrating better visual-language models and extending the framework to handle dynamic objects and moving humans, further increasing the robustness and real-world applicability of VLMaps. Despite these challenges, VLMaps present a compelling and practical approach to equipping robots with advanced spatial and semantic understanding for navigation.
