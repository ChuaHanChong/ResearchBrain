# FIRMGrasp: A Friction-Informed Risk Margin for Robust Grasp Synthesis

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Maryland, College Park developed FIRMGrasp, a framework that incorporates friction uncertainty into robotic grasp quality assessment by introducing risk-sensitive metrics based on Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). This approach enabled the identification of friction-sensitive grasps and improved the predictive accuracy of grasp success under varying contact conditions in simulations.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic grasp quality metrics assume deterministic parameters, such as a fixed friction coefficient, leading to discrepancies between predicted and real-world grasp success.
- Grasps synthesized using classical metrics often fail during execution due to unmodeled variability in contact conditions, particularly friction.
- Existing methods for accounting for uncertainty are either computationally expensive (Monte Carlo) or do not integrate uncertainty during the synthesis phase for friction.

## Method

- The paper introduces **FIRMGrasp**, a framework that models the friction coefficient as a random variable and integrates ==Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)== into grasp quality assessment.
- Two novel risk-sensitive grasp quality metrics are defined: the ==CVaR margin (Q_β)== and its closed-form analytic counterpart (==ε^(β)==), which generalize the classical ==Ferrari-Canny margin==.
- A =="sample, certify, and score" pipeline== is proposed, allowing for efficient pre-calculation of risk-adjusted friction and computation of ε^(β) to rank and select robust grasps.

## Results

- Under adverse friction priors, the risk-adjusted metric ε^(β) diverged substantially from nominal metrics (e.g., Pearson correlation with ε_nom dropped from **0.95** to **0.32**), identifying distinct friction-sensitive grasps.
- In simulated dynamic perturbation trials, ε^(β) demonstrated superior predictive validity for object retention under adverse friction, achieving AUCs of **0.629** (six-axis shake) and **0.78** (lift with lateral pull), outperforming baseline metrics.
- Grasp synthesis using a differentiable risk-adjusted objective reduced the fraction of friction-sensitive grasps from **67.7%** to **57.6%** and increased the median adverse-tail margin.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The risk-adjusted metrics, Q_β and ε^(β), provide a probabilistic closure certificate, guaranteeing force closure with a probability of at least β, and exhibit monotonicity with respect to the confidence level β.
> - The analytic risk-adjusted margin (ε^(β)) avoids computationally expensive Monte Carlo sampling for evaluation, making it efficient for large datasets.
> - Optimizing for even mild risk conditions during grasp synthesis can significantly improve overall robustness against more adverse friction scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic grasping research frequently employs quality metrics to rank and select grasps for various manipulation tasks. A widely used metric is the Ferrari-Canny epsilon, which quantifies the stability of a grasp based on the largest ball inscribed within its grasp wrench space (GWS). However, a significant limitation of classical analytical grasp quality metrics is their assumption of deterministic parameters, such as a fixed friction coefficient, object pose, and contact configuration. This deterministic approach often leads to discrepancies between predicted and observed grasp success in real-world scenarios where environmental parameters exhibit variability.

This work addresses this limitation by integrating uncertainty, specifically in the friction coefficient, directly into grasp quality assessment and synthesis. Previous attempts to account for uncertainty have largely relied on empirical methods like Monte Carlo statistics over nominal metrics, which can be computationally expensive and still maintain fixed parameters during the sampling process. Other contemporary research focuses on different forms of uncertainty, such as uncertain contact normals, object surface models, or post-synthesis robustness against multimodal uncertainty. In contrast, this paper focuses on incorporating friction uncertainty *before and during* grasp synthesis.

The proposed FIRMGrasp framework builds upon and extends the analytical line of work that includes metrics like the min-weight metric used in FRoGGeR. While FRoGGeR offers a differentiable and computationally efficient metric for grasp synthesis, it, like the Ferrari-Canny epsilon, operates under a fixed Coulomb friction coefficient. FIRMGrasp explicitly generalizes these existing metrics by treating the friction coefficient as a variable rather than a constant, thus allowing the true friction cone at each contact to be unknown within a defined uncertainty set.

Furthermore, this research aligns with a growing interest in risk-sensitive formulations for decision-making under uncertainty in robotics. The use of Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) as a foundational risk measure in FIRMGrasp draws parallels with its successful application in areas such as motion planning, reinforcement learning, and belief-space control. By providing a metric that quantifies robustness to friction variability, FIRMGrasp complements existing grasp synthesis methods, including both analytical and learning-based approaches, by offering a mechanism to score and rank candidate grasps based on their friction sensitivity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed fragility of robotic grasps synthesized using traditional quality metrics when encountered with real-world variability in contact conditions. Specifically, classical metrics assume a single, deterministic friction coefficient. This assumption often leads to a discrepancy between theoretical grasp quality and actual performance, where grasps predicted as stable at synthesis time can fail during execution due to unmodeled variations in friction. Figure 1 in the paper illustrates this issue, showing how two grasps rated similarly by a nominal epsilon margin diverge in performance under adverse friction conditions. The authors also highlight that similar issues arise with pose errors, indicating a broader need for uncertainty-aware grasp assessment.

Given this context, the authors establish the need to treat grasp quality not as a deterministic property but as a probability that a grasp maintains force closure across the plausible range of friction coefficients that may be encountered. This probabilistic perspective motivates the adoption of risk-sensitive formulations.

The key objectives of this work are:
1. **To define novel risk-sensitive grasp quality metrics**: The authors aim to introduce two new metrics, the CVaR margin (Q_β) and its closed-form analytic counterpart (ε^(β)). These metrics are designed to evaluate a grasp's behavior under the adverse tail of the friction distribution, rather than at a single nominal friction coefficient. A specific objective is to ensure that these new metrics generalize the classical Ferrari-Canny margin, such that they reduce to it under a degenerate, point-mass friction prior.
2. **To establish theoretical properties of the new metrics**: This objective includes proving that the risk-sensitive margins exhibit monotonicity with respect to the confidence level (β), enabling users to tune the level of conservatism. Additionally, the authors aim to demonstrate the differentiability of the metrics with respect to grasp parameters, which is crucial for enabling gradient-based optimization in future synthesis pipelines. A third theoretical objective is to provide a formal probabilistic closure certificate, guaranteeing that a positive risk-sensitive margin implies force closure with a specified minimum probability (at least β).
3. **To demonstrate the practical utility in risk-aware grasp synthesis and evaluation**: The research aims to implement and experimentally validate that the proposed metrics can identify grasps that are friction-sensitive but might be misclassified as high-quality by nominal, friction-unaware metrics. Furthermore, a goal is to show that these risk-adjusted metrics predict actual grasp success and stability during simulated execution, particularly under adverse friction conditions, thus providing a valuable tool for selecting robust grasps from a candidate pool.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this paper integrates the concept of Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) into the established framework of grasp wrench space (GWS) analysis to account for friction uncertainty.

### 4. Theoretical Framework

1. **Grasp Parameterization and Quality Function**: A grasp is defined by a tuple `g = (C, q_h, X_o)`, representing the contact set, hand configuration, and object pose. The Ferrari-Canny epsilon metric `ε(g, μ)` is used as the underlying scalar grasp quality function, which is positive for force closure.
2. **Friction Uncertainty Model**: The friction coefficient `μ` is modeled as a scalar random variable following a probability distribution `p_μ`. The paper establishes that primitive wrenches, which define the GWS, are affine functions of `μ` (Lemma 1). Consequently, the GWS `W(μ)` scales linearly with `μ`.
3. **Risk-Adjusted Metrics based on CVaR**:
 - **Risk-Adjusted Friction (v_β)**: Defined as `CVaR_β(μ)`, representing the mean of the adverse friction tail for a given confidence level `β`.
 - **Risk-Adjusted Wrench Body (W^(β)(θ))**: This is the GWS constructed using the risk-adjusted friction `v_β`. This effectively represents the reduced wrench capability available to the grasp under adverse friction conditions.
 - **Risk-Adjusted Margin (ε^(β)(g))**: Defined as the inscribed-ball radius of the `W^(β)` body (Equation 11). This metric provides an analytic, closed-form assessment of grasp quality that accounts for friction uncertainty. It generalizes the classical Ferrari-Canny margin, as it reduces to it under a point-mass friction prior.
 - **CVaR Margin (Q_β(θ))**: Defined as `CVaR_β(ε(g, μ))` (Equation 12), which calculates the mean of the worst (1-β)-fraction of margin outcomes over the entire friction distribution. This directly quantifies the robustness by considering the tail behavior of the margin distribution itself.
4. **Theoretical Properties**:
 - **Probabilistic Closure Certificate (Theorem 1 and Corollary 2)**: A key theoretical result stating that if `Q_β(θ) > 0` or `ε^(β)(g) > 0`, the grasp is force-closed with a probability of at least `β`.
 - **Monotonicity (Theorem 2)**: `Q_β(θ)` is non-increasing in `β`, allowing users to adjust the conservatism of the metric.
 - **Differentiability (Theorem 3)**: `Q_β(θ)` is differentiable almost everywhere with respect to grasp parameters `θ`, opening possibilities for gradient-based optimization.

### 5. Grasp Synthesis and Evaluation Pipeline (Algorithm 1)

The paper proposes a "sample, certify, and score" strategy to approximate risk-sensitive robust grasp synthesis.
1. **Sampling**: Initial palm poses and finger preshapes are sampled around the object.
2. **Refinement and Initial Certification**: Each sample is refined using the FRoGGeR min-weight nonlinear program. Only grasps that achieve force closure (`ℓ* > 0`) are accepted and their contact sets and primitive wrenches are serialized.
3. **Risk-Aware Scoring**: For each accepted grasp, the risk-adjusted friction `v_β` is pre-calculated from the friction prior. The `W^(β)` body is then assembled, and its signed inscribed-ball radius, `ε^(β)`, is computed. This analytic calculation avoids Monte Carlo sampling per grasp.
4. **Selection**: Grasps are ranked by their `ε^(β)` values, and the highest-scoring grasp is selected. While Theorem 3 establishes differentiability, this initial pipeline uses it for scoring, not gradient-based optimization, though a synthesis study explores a differentiable risk-adjusted objective `ℓ*^(β)`.

### 6. Experimental Validation

Experiments were conducted using grasp datasets for multiple anthropomorphic hands (LEAP, Allegro, RealHand L6, Shadow Hand) generated by a Drake-backed FRoGGeR-derived pipeline.
1. **Object Set**: Includes geometric primitives (sphere, cube, box, cylinder) and YCB objects.
2. **Friction Priors**: Two priors were used: a "nominal" tight Gaussian `N(0.7, 0.1^2)` and an "adverse" bimodal mixture `0.5 U(0.7, 1.0) + 0.5 U(0.1, 0.3)` to stress friction sensitivity.
3. **Analytic Evaluation**: All grasps were evaluated analytically by computing `ε^(β)` at `β=0.9`, avoiding Monte Carlo sampling during evaluation.
4. **Baselines**: Comparisons were made against the nominal Ferrari-Canny epsilon (`ε_nom`) at `μ=0.7` and FRoGGeR's min-weight metric (`ℓ*`).
5. **Simulated Execution Studies**: Grasps were replayed in a Drake physics simulator and subjected to two types of dynamic perturbations at various friction coefficients (`μ ∈ {0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.7}`):
 - **Gravity-off six-axis shake**: To test wrench-space quality and object retention under dynamic forces.
 - **Gravity-on lift with lateral pull**: To probe weight-bearing retention against gravity and lateral disturbances.
 - Performance was assessed using AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) and Spearman correlation against realized robustness.

### 7. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the FIRMGrasp metrics and their performance in evaluating and synthesizing robust grasps:

- **Aggregate Metric Divergence**: Under a nominal Gaussian friction prior, the risk-adjusted metric ε^(β) demonstrated a high Pearson correlation (r = 0.95) with the nominal Ferrari-Canny margin ε_nom across 1,599 LEAP Hand and Allegro Hand grasps. This indicates that under stable friction conditions, FIRMGrasp aligns with conventional metrics. However, under an adverse bimodal friction prior, the correlation dropped significantly to 0.32 with ε_nom and 0.52 with FRoGGeR's min-weight ℓ*. This divergence suggests that ε^(β) identifies distinct grasp characteristics related to friction sensitivity that are not captured by friction-unaware baselines. A substantial proportion (53%) of grasps certified by the nominal ε_nom were found to lose force closure in the adverse friction tail, with this sensitivity varying by object shape (e.g., 5% for spheres vs. 86% for mustard bottles).

- **Synthesis Under the Risk Objective**: When FRoGGeR synthesis was conducted using a differentiable risk-adjusted min-weight objective (ℓ*^(β)) instead of the nominal ℓ*, the fraction of friction-sensitive grasps (those losing closure in the adverse tail) decreased from 67.7% to 57.6%. Concurrently, the median adverse-tail margin (ε^(β)) increased, indicating that optimizing for mild risk conditions can improve robustness against harsher adverse friction.

- **Per-Object Analysis**: A detailed analysis of representative RealHand L6 grasps on seven objects confirmed that ε^(β) under the nominal prior tracked ε_nom. However, under the adverse prior, ε^(β) differentiated grasps, showing positive margins for robust objects like the tennis ball and cylinder, but negative margins for more friction-sensitive objects such as the cube, box, and mustard bottle. This analysis also empirically confirmed the theoretical property of monotonicity of ε^(β) in the confidence level β. The probabilistic closure certificate β_c varied from 1.0 for robust objects to 0.46 for the mustard bottle, directly quantifying the confidence of closure under adverse conditions.

- **Cross-Dataset Validation**: The divergence pattern was reproduced across independent grasp datasets. On 800 LEAP and 799 Allegro FRoGGeR-synthesized grasps, and 400 Shadow Hand DexGraspNet grasps, the fraction of grasps certified robust (ε^(β) > 0) under a nominal prior was high (99.4% for LEAP, 100% for Allegro, 33.2% for Shadow Hand). Under the adverse prior, these fractions dropped significantly to 42.9%, 50.9%, and 3.8% respectively. This consistent reduction across different synthesizers and hands suggests that FIRMGrasp identifies a general characteristic of grasp robustness to friction uncertainty.

- **Predictive Validity Under Dynamic Perturbation**:
 - **Gravity-off Six-Axis Shake**: In simulated shake trials involving 302 established-contact grasps across seven objects, ε^(β) demonstrated superior predictive validity for object retention under adverse friction. It achieved an AUC of 0.629, outperforming ℓ* (0.578) and ε_nom (0.534). This indicates that ε^(β), which does not have access to the realized friction during its calculation, better orders realized robustness than the baselines. The Spearman correlation also followed this trend (0.25 for ε^(β) vs. 0.14 for ℓ* and 0.05 for ε_nom).
 - **Gravity-on Lift with Lateral Pull**: In gravity-on lift trials with lateral pull for 51 established-contact grasps, ε^(β) again exhibited higher predictive accuracy, with an AUC of 0.78, compared to ℓ* (0.75) and ε_nom (0.67). Among grasps that ε_nom certified, those also certified by ε^(β) achieved a 70% success rate under the most adverse friction (μ=0.2), significantly higher than the 25% for grasps certified only by ε_nom.

Overall, the results indicate that FIRMGrasp metrics effectively identify friction-sensitive grasps, improve synthesis outcomes for robustness, and provide more accurate predictions of dynamic grasp performance under uncertain friction compared to traditional metrics.

### 8. Significance and Potential Impact

The FIRMGrasp framework introduces a principled approach to integrating friction uncertainty into robotic grasp quality assessment and synthesis, addressing a long-standing limitation of classical deterministic metrics. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

- **Enhanced Grasp Robustness and Reliability**: By explicitly accounting for the distribution of friction coefficients, FIRMGrasp enables the synthesis and selection of grasps that are inherently more robust to real-world variations in contact friction. This can lead to a reduction in grasp failures during execution, particularly in unstructured or variable environments where friction properties are not precisely known or constant.
- **Improved Predictive Power**: The demonstrated ability of FIRMGrasp metrics to predict dynamic object retention under adverse friction conditions in simulations, surpassing conventional metrics, suggests practical utility. This predictive capability allows for more informed pre-execution decision-making, where robots can select grasps with a higher likelihood of success even when faced with uncertain contact properties.
- **A New Dimension for Grasp Assessment**: FIRMGrasp provides a unique "friction-volatility-aware" axis for evaluating grasp quality. It identifies friction-sensitive grasps that traditional metrics might incorrectly rate as high-quality, offering a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of grasp stability. This can guide the design of more effective grasp planners and controllers.
- **Computational Efficiency for Uncertainty-Aware Grasping**: The analytic evaluation protocol for ε^(β), which calculates the risk-adjusted margin through a single inscribed-ball computation without extensive Monte Carlo sampling per grasp, makes the approach computationally feasible for real-time applications or large-scale grasp datasets.
- **Strong Theoretical Foundation**: The establishment of properties such as monotonicity in confidence level and differentiability with respect to grasp parameters provides a solid mathematical basis. The differentiability, in particular, opens avenues for future work in gradient-based, end-to-end differentiable grasp optimization, potentially leading to highly efficient synthesis methods that directly optimize for robust grasps.
- **Generalizability Across Robotic Systems**: The validation across multiple anthropomorphic hands (LEAP, Allegro, Shadow Hand) and public datasets indicates that the FIRMGrasp methodology is broadly applicable and not limited to specific robotic platforms or synthesis pipelines. Its agnostic nature to the grasp generator means it can be integrated with a variety of existing and future grasp generation techniques.
- **Impact on Robotic Manipulation Research**: This work contributes to the broader field of robotic manipulation by offering a formal, risk-sensitive framework for handling uncertainty. It sets a precedent for how other forms of uncertainty (e.g., pose, contact normal) could be integrated using similar risk-sensitive approaches, potentially leading to a new generation of highly adaptive and reliable robotic manipulators.

While the work currently relies on simulated validation, and hardware validation remains a future step, the theoretical rigor and comprehensive simulation results suggest that FIRMGrasp represents a significant step towards enabling robots to perform complex manipulation tasks more reliably in dynamic and uncertain real-world settings.
