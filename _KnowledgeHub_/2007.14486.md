# A Lifelong Learning Approach to Mobile Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Lifelong Learning for Navigation (LLfN) is a self-supervised framework where a mobile robot improves on a classical DWA local planner by detecting its own suboptimal recovery behaviors, mining nearby successful state-action pairs from a streaming buffer as pseudo-labels, and training a complementary policy with Gradient Episodic Memory. Running fully onboard under a 600-datapoint budget, it cuts traversal time and eliminates recovery behaviors and collisions across three environments without forgetting earlier ones.

## Problem

- Classical planners run fixed hyper-parameters, need in-situ human tuning or demonstration, and repeat identical failures no matter how often they revisit an environment.
- Learning-based navigation generalizes poorly to unseen environments and suffers catastrophic forgetting when environments are encountered sequentially rather than jointly.
- Prior lifelong navigation work targets mapping or vision-language goals, deciding where to navigate, and assumes offboard compute plus pre-collected labels unavailable on resource-limited robots.

## Method

- Complementary-policy design: a learned network runs alongside the untouched ==DWA== planner, and a heuristic scoring function D (forward speed above 0.15 m/s) picks whichever action scores higher at execution.
- Self-supervised label mining via A_correct: for each low-scoring state, search the 300-step streaming buffer for the ==nearest-neighbor== LiDAR state with an acceptable action, and store that pair as a behavior-cloning target.
- ==Gradient Episodic Memory== as the continual learner: gradient updates are projected so their inner product with per-environment exemplar gradients stays non-negative, under a fixed shared memory of 300 exemplars.

## Results

- Across **198** simulated trials in three environments, traversal time drops monotonically with more experience, while sequential training's performance on earlier environments visibly diverges upward from LLfN's.
- In **60** physical Jackal trials, LLfN cut time versus DWA from **38.36** to **28.32** s, **28.05** to **19.98** s, and **39.80** to **21.80** s, with **zero** recoveries or collisions.
- LLfN beat per-environment Individual Models on the first two environments (**28.32** vs **30.17** s, **19.98** vs **23.41** s), indicating positive backward transfer; each model trains under **two minutes** on the onboard CPU.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Keeping the classical planner and learning only a complementary policy for rare failure cases shrinks the learning problem enough for onboard training under tight memory budgets.
> - A planner's own fallback signal, here recovery behaviors, is a free self-supervision label; nearest-neighbor retrieval of successful nearby actions substitutes for expert demonstration.
> - The approach inherits the base planner's competence: when the initial policy fails everywhere, no successful neighbors exist and no learning signal can be mined.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Mobile robot navigation systems have traditionally relied on static methods that require manual, environment-specific adjustments, often performed by human experts. These classical approaches, such as DWA (Dynamic Window Approach), while broadly applicable, do not intrinsically improve with experience and may repeatedly encounter the same suboptimal behaviors. The inability of these systems to adapt and learn online presents a limitation for robots operating in dynamic or novel environments without continuous human intervention.

Concurrently, machine learning-based navigation has demonstrated potential for performance improvement from past experience within a specific environment. However, these methods often face two significant challenges:
1. **Generalization:** Learned policies may not generalize effectively to new or unseen environments, necessitating re-learning from scratch.
2. **Catastrophic Forgetting:** When a learning system is trained on new tasks or environments sequentially, it frequently loses the ability to perform tasks learned previously. This is a prevalent issue in neural networks and limits their utility in real-world scenarios where robots encounter diverse environments over time.

The concept of lifelong or continual learning addresses the challenge of catastrophic forgetting in machine learning by enabling models to learn new tasks without significantly degrading performance on previously learned ones. While significant progress has been made in continual supervised learning, particularly in areas like image classification, its application to complex robotic tasks such as navigation, especially without expert labels and under real-time, resource-constrained conditions, remains a less explored area. Previous lifelong learning efforts in robotics often focused on mapping or vision-language navigation rather than on the direct learning of navigation behaviors across different environments.

This paper positions its Lifelong Learning for Navigation (LLfN) framework as a bridge between these areas. It aims to integrate the adaptive learning capabilities of machine learning with the stability of classical planners, specifically addressing the challenges of self-improvement with experience and mitigating catastrophic forgetting in resource-constrained mobile robot navigation across a sequence of distinct environments. The work distinguishes itself by focusing on *how* to navigate rather than *where* to navigate, and by implementing the learning process entirely onboard a physical robot.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a self-improving lifelong learning framework for mobile robot navigation. This framework, termed Lifelong Learning for Navigation (LLfN), is designed to enable a mobile robot to enhance its navigation capabilities solely based on its own experience, and to retain previously acquired navigation skills even after learning in new environments.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several limitations observed in current robot navigation paradigms:

- **Static Nature of Classical Methods:** Traditional navigation algorithms, such as those relying on fixed parameters, do not intrinsically improve from accumulated experience. Robots utilizing these methods may repeatedly encounter and fail to resolve the same navigation challenges, requiring expert intervention for parameter tuning or recalibration. This reliance on human experts for in-situ adjustment limits robot autonomy and efficiency. The proposed LLfN framework seeks to overcome this by allowing the robot to dynamically improve its performance in a self-supervised manner.
- **Challenges in Learning-Based Navigation:** While machine learning offers the potential for performance improvement, existing learning-based navigation methods typically struggle with generalization to new environments and are highly susceptible to catastrophic forgetting. A robot trained in one environment may perform poorly in another or forget how to navigate its original environment after training in a new one. This necessitates re-training or maintaining separate models for each environment, which is impractical for robots operating across diverse and sequential settings. LLfN aims to address this by enabling adaptation to new environments while preserving knowledge of previous ones.
- **Resource Constraints on Physical Robots:** Deploying advanced learning algorithms on physical mobile robots often faces practical limitations regarding memory and computational power. Many learning approaches require extensive offboard computation or large datasets. A key motivation for LLfN is to demonstrate its implementation and effectiveness entirely onboard a physical robot platform, operating within realistic memory and computation budgets.

Specifically, the paper identifies three main contributions that align with these objectives:
- Developing a self-improvement strategy that complements an initial static planner. This strategy dynamically increases navigation performance with experience by identifying and learning from suboptimal behaviors, minimizing learning overhead by deploying it in conjunction with the initial planner.
- Implementing a lifelong learning scheme that allows a robot to adapt to new environments without forgetting previously learned navigation capabilities.
- Demonstrating the entire LLfN framework on a physical robot platform, adhering to limited memory and computation constraints.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Lifelong Learning for Navigation (LLfN) framework is designed to enable a mobile robot to improve its navigation performance online and across different environments, under a limited memory budget. It operates by learning an auxiliary policy ($\pi_\theta$) that complements an initial static planner ($\pi_0$), particularly in situations where $\pi_0$ performs suboptimally.

**A. Framework Components:**

1. **Initial Sampling-Based Navigation Planner ($\pi_0$):** This is a classical, static planner (e.g., DWA in the experiments) that generates motion commands. It does not improve with experience on its own.
2. **Learnable Policy ($\pi_\theta$):** A neural network parameterized by $\theta$ that learns to map states ($s$) to actions ($a$), providing alternative motion commands. This policy is continually updated.
3. **Scoring Function ($D: S \times A \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$):** This function evaluates the quality of an action $a$ in a given state $s$. A higher score indicates a better action. In the implementation, $D$ prioritizes actions resulting in steady forward motion (linear velocity $v \ge 0.15 \text{m/s}$) and classifies DWA's recovery behaviors (slowing down, turning in place, moving backward) as suboptimal, as they result in $v < 0.15 \text{m/s}$.
4. **Streaming Memory Buffer ($B_{stream}$):** A first-in, first-out queue that stores the robot's recent $T$-step trajectory (state-action pairs). This buffer is used to identify suboptimal behaviors and find better alternatives within the robot's recent experience.
5. **Per-environment Memory ($M_k$):** A priority queue that stores exemplar training data (self-generated, high-quality state-action pairs) collected from the current environment $E_k$. Its size is constrained, e.e. $|M_k| \le n/k$, where $n$ is the total memory budget and $k$ is the current environment index.
6. **Global Memory Buffer ($B$):** A collection of all per-environment memories from previous environments ($B = \{M_i\}_{i<k}$). This buffer is used by the continual learning algorithm to prevent catastrophic forgetting.
7. **Self-Correction Algorithm ($A_{correct}$):** Given a suboptimal behavior $(s_p, a_p)$ identified from $B_{stream}$, this algorithm searches within $B_{stream}$ for a state-action pair $(s', a')$ that has a high score ($D(s', a') \ge \eta$) and a state similar to $s_p$. This $(s', a')$ pair serves as a "good" example to learn from to improve performance around $s_p$. Similarity is measured by the negative Euclidean distance between LiDAR readings.
8. **Continual Learning Algorithm ($A_{cl}$):** This algorithm updates $\pi_\theta$ using the exemplar data from $M_k$ while preserving knowledge from previous environments stored in $B$. Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) is adopted as $A_{cl}$.

**B. Learning Pipeline (Algorithm 1):**

The LLfN framework operates in a sequence of $m$ environments $\{E_i\}_{i=1}^m$.

1. **Initialization:** $B$ and $B_{stream}$ are empty, and $\pi_\theta$ is randomly initialized.
2. **Per-Environment Learning (for each $E_k$):**
 - $M_k$ is initialized as empty.
 - **Navigation and Data Collection:** While navigating in $E_k$, the robot executes actions from $\pi_0$. Each state-action pair $(s_t, a_t)$ is added to $B_{stream}$.
 - **Suboptimal Behavior Identification:** The algorithm periodically checks a state-action pair $(s_p, a_p)$ from the midpoint of $B_{stream}$. If its score $D(s_p, a_p)$ falls below a threshold $\eta$ (indicating a suboptimal action, e.g., DWA's recovery behavior), it triggers the self-correction mechanism.
 - **Exemplar Data Selection:** $A_{correct}$ identifies a "good" exemplar state-action pair $(s', a')$ from $B_{stream}$ (i.e., $D(s', a') \ge \eta$ and $s'$ is most similar to $s_p$). This pair is added to $M_k$. If $M_k$ exceeds its capacity ($n/k$), the entry with the lowest similarity score to its own $s_p$ is removed.
 - **Policy Update (Lifelong Learning):** After completing navigation in $E_k$, $\pi_\theta$ is updated using $A_{cl}$ (GEM). GEM minimizes the behavioral cloning loss on $M_k$ while ensuring that the gradients for the current task do not negatively impact the losses on previous tasks (data from $B$). This is achieved by projecting the current gradient onto the feasible space where constraints from previous tasks are satisfied.
 - **Memory Management:** $B$ is updated by shrinking its contents (removing lowest similarity score entries) to accommodate $M_k$, which is then added to $B$. The total memory for $B \cup \{M_k\}$ is constrained to $n$ data points.

**C. Execution:**

During deployment, at each state $s_t$, both $\pi_0(s_t)$ and $\pi_\theta(s_t)$ generate candidate actions ($a_0$ and $\hat{a}$, respectively). The action with the higher score, as determined by $D(s_t, a)$, is executed.

**D. Robot Platform and Implementation Details:**

- **Robot:** Clearpath Jackal (four-wheeled differential-drive UGV).
- **Navigation Stack:** ROS `move_base` with Dijkstra's algorithm for global planning and DWA for local planning (serving as $\pi_0$).
- **State Input:** LiDAR readings (720-dim for simulation, 2095-dim for physical) and local goal (x, y coordinates, 1m away on global path).
- **Action Output:** Linear velocity ($v$) and angular velocity ($\omega$).
- **Scoring Function (D):** Prioritizes DWA actions with $v \ge 0.15 \text{m/s}$ (indicating steady forward motion); DWA recovery behaviors (slowing down, turning, backing up) result in $v < 0.15 \text{m/s}$ and are classified as suboptimal.
- **Neural Network ($\pi_\theta$):** Small network with three hidden layers, each having 64 neurons.
- **Memory Budget:** $B_{stream}$ stores 300 data points. The combined `B` and `M_k` (for exemplar data) stores a total of 300 data points.
- **Computation:** All computation for physical experiments is performed onboard using an Intel Core i5-4570TE CPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates LLfN across simulated and physical experiments to assess its ability to improve navigation performance within a single environment and to adapt to new environments without forgetting previous capabilities.

**A. Simulated Experiments:**

- **Environments:** Three distinct simulated environments (Env. 1: dense obstacles; Env. 2: narrow passage; Env. 3: sharp turn) requiring different navigation behaviors.
- **In-Environment Learning Improvement:** The study incrementally presented training data (divided into 5 segments) from each environment to the learner. Results show that within each environment, LLfN consistently decreased the traversal time with increasing learning experience. This indicates the framework's capability for self-improvement based on collected experience.
- **Cross-Environment Learning and Catastrophic Forgetting:**
 - **Sequential Training:** A baseline where the policy is sequentially trained on Env. 1, then Env. 2, then Env. 3 without mechanisms to prevent forgetting. While Sequential Training demonstrated faster improvement within the current environment compared to LLfN, its performance in previous environments significantly deteriorated. The divergence between the Sequential Training (red line) and LLfN (green line) curves in Figure 4 illustrates catastrophic forgetting: learning in a new environment compromised the robot's ability to navigate previous ones.
 - **LLfN Performance:** LLfN successfully learned to navigate new environments with increasing data while maintaining its performance in previously learned ones. This indicates that the GEM-based continual learning mechanism effectively prevented catastrophic forgetting.
- **Combined Policy:** In both LLfN and Sequential Training, the learned policies were used in conjunction with the initial DWA policy. This setup allowed the learned policy to primarily take over in "red segments" (suboptimal DWA behavior) and avoid repeated mistakes, while the DWA handled the "green segments" (optimal DWA behavior).

**B. Physical Experiments:**

- **Environments:** Three physical environments, designed for sequential traversal, reflecting challenges similar to those in simulation (Figure 1).
- **Baselines:**
 - **DWA:** The initial static DWA planner.
 - **Sequential Training:** Policy sequentially trained on environments 1, 2, then 3.
 - **Individual Models:** Separate, best-performing models trained for each environment (representing an upper bound for Sequential Training in terms of non-forgetting, but requiring multiple models).
- **Performance Metrics:** Traversal time, number of recovery behaviors, and collisions.
- **DWA Baseline:** Exhibited the highest number of recovery behaviors (e.g., 1.8 in Env. 1, 1.4 in Env. 2, 2.2 in Env. 3) and some collisions (0.2 in Env. 2 and 3), confirming its suboptimal performance in specific scenarios.
- **Sequential Training:** Consistent with simulations, this method showed catastrophic forgetting. It performed well in Env. 3 (21.12s), which was the last environment trained, but significantly worse in Env. 1 (39.11s) and Env. 2 (49.41s) compared to DWA or LLfN, with high standard deviations, frequent recovery behaviors, and some collisions. This reinforces the challenge of catastrophic forgetting in real-world deployment.
- **LLfN Performance:** LLfN effectively avoided catastrophic forgetting. It achieved comparable traversal times to Individual Models in Env. 3 (21.80s vs. 21.12s for Individual Models). Notably, LLfN surprisingly outperformed Individual Models in Env. 1 (28.32s vs. 30.17s) and Env. 2 (19.98s vs. 23.41s) in terms of average time. The authors suggest this might be due to a beneficial "backward transfer" ability from gathering more diverse experience across environments, leading to a more robust policy. LLfN also recorded zero recovery behaviors and zero collisions across all environments, indicating improved navigation robustness.
- **Individual Models:** While offering high stability (lowest standard deviation) due to dedicated training for each environment and no forgetting, this approach requires managing multiple models, which might be less efficient than a single lifelong learning policy.

The results from both simulated and physical experiments consistently support the effectiveness of LLfN in enabling self-improvement within environments and preventing catastrophic forgetting across multiple environments, all while operating under resource constraints on a physical robot.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, Lifelong Learning for Navigation (LLfN), that addresses critical challenges in mobile robot autonomy. Its significance lies in its capacity to enable robots to learn and improve their navigation capabilities continuously and adaptively across diverse environments without external human intervention or forgetting previously acquired skills.

The potential impacts of this work include:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** By allowing robots to self-improve based on their own experience, LLfN reduces the reliance on human experts for system adjustment and parameter tuning. This contributes to increased autonomy for mobile robots operating in varied and evolving real-world scenarios, where pre-programmed or statically tuned systems might be insufficient.
- **Improved Robustness and Efficiency:** The ability to learn from suboptimal behaviors and adapt to new environments without forgetting past knowledge can lead to more robust and efficient navigation. Robots equipped with LLfN could avoid repeating mistakes, navigate challenging terrains more effectively, and adapt to changes in their operational environment, reducing mission failures and increasing operational speed. The observed reduction in recovery behaviors and collisions in physical experiments supports this.
- **Practicality for Resource-Constrained Systems:** The implementation of LLfN entirely onboard a physical robot with limited memory and computational resources demonstrates its practical viability. This is crucial for real-world robotic applications where offboard computation or extensive data pre-collection may not be feasible. This makes lifelong learning more accessible for various mobile robot platforms.
- **Advancement in Continual Learning for Robotics:** The application of Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) to a complex, real-time robotic control problem like navigation, especially in a self-supervised context (without expert labels), contributes to the broader field of continual learning. It highlights the potential for these techniques to move beyond static image classification tasks into dynamic, embodied AI applications. The finding of potential "backward transfer" where learning new tasks surprisingly improves performance on old ones (as seen in physical experiments) is an interesting area for further investigation within continual learning theory.
- **Foundation for Future Robot Learning:** LLfN provides a foundation for more advanced forms of robot learning. Future research could extend the framework by developing more sophisticated, learnable scoring functions, incorporating active exploration strategies when the initial policy is severely incompetent, or addressing dynamic obstacles. This work opens avenues for robots to become more capable and versatile over extended periods of operation.

In summary, LLfN offers a practical and effective method for mobile robots to learn continuously and adaptively, overcoming key limitations of both classical and conventional learning-based navigation approaches. Its implications are particularly relevant for deploying intelligent robots in complex, changing environments requiring sustained autonomous operation.
