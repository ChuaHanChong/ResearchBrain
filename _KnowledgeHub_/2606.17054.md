# Human Universal Grasping

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for multi-fingered robot grasping, HUG, leverages a 1-million-frame egocentric human grasp dataset to generate human-like grasps from single RGB-D images. It achieves a 66.7% real-world success rate on a challenging benchmark, outperforming existing methods by over 20 percentage points, and generalizes zero-shot across various robot hands and environments.

## Problem

- Multi-fingered robot hands have limited adaptability and generality for grasping diverse objects in unstructured real-world environments, lagging behind human proficiency.
- Acquiring large-scale, diverse, and robot-relevant grasping data is challenging due to the 'sim-to-real' gap for synthetic data and the labor-intensive nature of teleoperation.
- Current robot learning approaches often require specific training data or retraining for each new robot hand embodiment, hindering generalizability.

## Method

- Assembled 1M-**HUG**S, a 1-million-frame egocentric human grasping dataset with RGB-D and 3D hand poses collected via smart glasses.
- Developed HUG, a flow-matching model fusing RGB and point cloud features to predict 99-dimensional human ==MANO grasp poses== from a single RGB-D image and a user click.
- Established HUG-BENCH, a benchmark with 90 challenging, metric-scale objects reconstructed for both simulated and real-world robot grasping evaluation.

## Results

- HUG achieved a **66.7%** real-world grasping success rate on the challenging HUG-BENCH, outperforming Dex1B by 23 percentage points and CAP by 34 percentage points.
- The framework demonstrated zero-shot generalization, achieving a **62.0%** success rate in complex in-the-wild environments with a new robot and camera system, a minor **4.7** percentage point drop from tabletop performance.
- Ablation studies highlighted the critical importance of 3D geometric loss and the complementary contribution of both RGB and point cloud modalities for robust grasp prediction, with performance scaling with the 1M-HUGS dataset size.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Natural human grasps from egocentric perspectives offer a scalable and generalizable source of data for training dexterous robot grasping policies.
> - The fusion of both visual (RGB) and geometric (point cloud) information is essential for accurate and robust grasp pose prediction, as each modality complements the other's strengths.
> - Model performance, in terms of grasp success and quality, consistently improves with larger scales of human demonstration data, suggesting further benefits from even larger datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The capability of robots to grasp arbitrary objects is a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation, essential for tasks ranging from industrial automation to household assistance. While humans execute grasping effortlessly and with high generality, multi-fingered robot hands currently demonstrate limited adaptability across diverse objects and environments.

Prior research in robotic grasp prediction has largely pursued two main avenues:
- **Synthetic Grasp Generation:** This involves creating grasps in simulation by optimizing analytical objectives (e.g., force-closure), sampling from learned generators or reinforcement learning policies, or reconstructing hands from web images. While these methods can produce large datasets, they frequently encounter a "sim-to-real" gap, where policies learned in simulation do not transfer effectively to real-world robots. Furthermore, they often require retraining for each new robot hand embodiment.
- **Teleoperation:** This approach involves human operators directly controlling a robot to perform grasping tasks, yielding real-world grasp data on the target robot embodiment. However, teleoperation is labor-intensive and challenging to scale for collecting a diverse range of grasps across a wide variety of objects and environmental conditions.

More recent efforts in learning robot behaviors from non-robot datasets have emerged, leveraging advances in motion tracking and hand-object reconstruction. These include using human data with rich 3D annotations, or in-the-wild human datasets that often lack precise 3D signals. The use of smart glasses has been identified as a method to bridge this gap, allowing for the collection of in-the-wild data with stereo depth and 3D hand poses. However, many such endeavors have primarily focused on two-finger grippers, or have necessitated co-training with robot-specific data.

This work addresses the data bottleneck for multi-fingered dexterous grasping by collecting and learning from large-scale, in-the-wild human grasping data. The authors propose that natural human grasps serve as a reliable source for robot learning, offering readily executable and generalizable grasp strategies. This approach leverages recent technological advancements in egocentric sensing (smart glasses for calibrated RGB-D and hand tracking) and anthropomorphic robot hands, alongside learned retargeting techniques that reduce the human-robot morphology gap. The HUG framework positions itself as a system trained purely on human data, applicable across multiple robot embodiments without specific robot training data, aiming to enhance the generality of dexterous robot grasping.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a generalizable framework for multi-fingered robot grasping that can generate diverse, natural grasps for arbitrary objects in real-world scenes. The authors aim to achieve this by exclusively leveraging human grasping data.

The motivation for this approach stems from several observations:
- **Human Proficiency vs. Robot Limitations:** Humans exhibit a high degree of generality and effortlessness in grasping a vast array of objects in diverse environments. Current multi-fingered robot hands, however, remain significantly less versatile, struggling to achieve similar levels of general-purpose grasping.
- **Data Bottleneck in Robotics:** A major impediment to advancing dexterous robot grasping is the scarcity of diverse, real-world grasping data relevant to robots. Traditional methods of data collection, such as synthetic generation or robot teleoperation, present significant limitations, including the "sim-to-real" gap for synthetic data and the tedium and scalability issues for teleoperated data.
- **Naturalness of Human Grasps:** The authors posit that human grasps, which are typically robust and functional, represent a more natural and direct source of data for robot learning than optimizing for every physically valid grasp. By modeling how people naturally grasp objects, the system can potentially learn more reliably executable grasps.
- **Emerging Technologies:** Two technological developments motivate the practicality of this approach:
 - **Egocentric Sensors:** Lightweight smart glasses (e.g., Aria Gen 2) can collect large volumes of egocentric human grasp data, providing synchronized RGB-D and 3D hand tracking, making data collection scalable and less intrusive.
 - **Anthropomorphic Robot Hands and Retargeting:** Advances in anthropomorphic robot hands and learned human-to-robot retargeting techniques have reduced the morphological differences between human and robot hands, making it more feasible to transfer human grasp poses to robots.

Therefore, the key objectives are to:
1. **Collect a large-scale egocentric dataset of human grasps** that captures natural interactions with diverse objects in everyday environments.
2. **Develop a machine learning model (HUG) capable of generating diverse human-like grasps** for any user-specified object from a single RGB-D image. This model should fuse visual (RGB) and geometric (depth/point cloud) information.
3. **Demonstrate the zero-shot retargetability of predicted human grasps** to various robot hands without requiring per-embodiment training.
4. **Establish a standardized benchmark (HUG-BENCH)** with challenging, unseen objects and metric-scale 3D meshes for robust evaluation in both simulation and the real world.
5. **Achieve superior real-world grasping performance** compared to existing state-of-the-art baselines on challenging object sets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HUG framework involves three main components: a large-scale human grasp dataset (1M-HUGS), a flow-matching model for grasp generation, and a new evaluation benchmark (HUG-BENCH).

#### 3.1. Dataset: 1M-HUGS

- **Collection Protocol:** Data was collected using Aria Gen 2 smart glasses, which provide synchronized RGB, stereo grayscale, 6-DoF camera poses, and 3D hand landmarks. During recording, a wearer first observes a static target object for 15-30 seconds without hands visible, then grasps the object with their right hand. This protocol allows for the propagation of the grasp pose back into preceding "no-hand" frames, generating multiple (object-only image, grasp) pairs from diverse viewpoints for a single physical grasp.
- **Curation (aria2mano pipeline):**
 1. **Object Identification:** A vision-language model (Gemini 3 Flash Preview) localizes the grasped object by comparing "before" and "after" grasp frames, identifying points on the object.
 2. **Mask Propagation:** SAM3 is used to segment the object and track its mask across all frames.
 3. **Grasp-Frame Selection:** Heuristics are applied to identify the optimal grasp frame, requiring valid right-hand MANO tracking, low hand motion, fingertip proximity to the object mask, and no visible left hand. The frame with the best MANO quality (geometric mean of fingertip error and anatomy loss) is selected.
 4. **Quality Control:** All recordings undergo human review in a web interface to verify and correct object masks and grasp frame selections.
 5. **Frame Filtering:** Frames are filtered based on criteria such as non-empty object masks, sufficient confident stereo depth pixels (≥ 60%), intersection of projected hand landmarks with the object mask, at least five grasp landmarks within image bounds, and absence of a hand in the current frame. Each surviving frame is cropped and resized to 224x224.
- **Dataset Statistics and Labels:** 1M-HUGS contains 1 million RGB frames and 1 million grayscale frames (from the left stereo camera), totaling 2 million training entries. It covers 6,707 recordings across 41 buildings with approximately 1,500 unique objects. Each entry includes a 224x224 image, camera intrinsics, depth map, object mask, and a MANO hand pose with wrist transformation in the camera frame. A full articulated MANO hand is fitted to the 21 sparse Aria hand landmarks through an optimization procedure incorporating an anatomical validity prior. The MANO shape parameter (β) is fixed to a canonical hand size for consistency across data collectors and for robot retargeting, simplifying the model's prediction task to articulation and placement.

#### 3.2. Grasp Generation Model: HUG

- **Input and Output:** HUG takes a single RGB-D image and a 2D pixel click (u,v) on the target object as input. The 2D click is lifted to a 3D query point (p_q) using its depth and camera intrinsics. The model predicts a 99-dimensional grasp state, comprising 3-dim wrist translation (t), 6-dim global wrist rotation (R_6d), and 90-dim MANO finger joint rotations (θ_6d).
- **Architecture (Flow-Matching Model):**
 1. **Encoders:**
 - **RGB Encoder:** A frozen DINOv2-Base Vision Transformer (ViT) with register tokens encodes the RGB image into 256 patch tokens.
 - **Point Cloud Encoder:** The metric point cloud, back-projected from the depth image, is cropped to a 0.3m radius sphere around the 3D query point. 4096 points are sampled from this crop and fed into a trainable PointNeXt U-Net, which outputs 256 per-region tokens and their XYZ centroids.
 2. **RGB-PC Fusion Transformer:** Point painting is used to fuse the two streams. Each point cloud centroid is projected to the RGB image, its DINOv2 patch feature is bilinearly sampled, concatenated with the corresponding PC token, and projected into a 1024-dim fused token. The 3D query point (p_q) and PC centroids are encoded with random Fourier features. These fused tokens cross-attend to a query token (derived from p_q) and are refined by a 4-layer pre-norm transformer, producing scene-conditioning tokens (s).
 3. **Flow Transformer:** The grasp state (translation, wrist rotation, finger pose) is split into three normalized groups, each projected into a 512-dim token. These tokens pass through 6 DiT blocks, cross-attending to the scene tokens (s) and conditioned on the timestep. Three linear heads decode the output tokens back to their respective dimensions. The velocity is integrated by a flow-matching Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) to produce the predicted grasp state.
- **Training:**
 - **Loss Function:** A combined loss consisting of a velocity-prediction Mean Squared Error (L_v) and an L1 geometric supervision loss (L_3D) on the 3D hand landmarks in the camera frame. The geometric loss is weighted by (1-t) to concentrate supervision on near-clean states.
 - **Training Details:** Trained for 100K steps using AdamW, with a learning rate of 10^-4 and a batch size of 128. Only the PointNeXt encoder, fusion module, and flow transformer are optimized. EMA is maintained from step 50K.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Benchmark: HUG-BENCH

- **Object Set:** HUG-BENCH consists of 90 unseen objects, categorized by five geometric types (cylindrical, spheroidal, prismatic, appendaged, amorphous) and three size bins (small, medium, large). These objects are designed to be challenging, including articulated, very short, or large items.
- **Metric-Scale Simulation Assets (aria2mesh pipeline):**
 1. **3D Object Reconstruction:** Objects are reconstructed from five egocentric Aria Gen 2 views using a modified Multi-view SAM3D pipeline, which incorporates Aria camera intrinsics, extrinsics, and stereo depth.
 2. **Asset Refinement:** Reconstructed meshes are pose-optimized, gravity-aligned, and manually edited in Viser against SLAM point clouds and stereo depth for accurate scale and pose. Meshes are made watertight with Alpha Wrap and decomposed into convex collision parts with CoACD, resulting in physics-ready URDF and MJCF assets.
 3. **Human Grasp Oracle:** For each object, 10 human grasps recorded with Aria Gen 2 are used as a ground-truth "oracle" in simulation.
- **Simulation Environment:** Predicted grasps are evaluated in MuJoCo. A position-actuated MANO right hand executes an open-loop pre-grasp → grasp → lift rollout. The pre-grasp involves offsetting the wrist and opening fingers, followed by linear interpolation to the predicted pose while fingers close with extra flexion. A grasp succeeds if the object is not in contact with the surface after a 0.5m lift.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Success Rate (SR):** Percentage of trials where the object remains grasped after lifting.
 - **Fingertip Contact Error (FC error):** Measures the combined distance of the thumb and the closest supporting finger to the object surface, indicating grasp tightness and quality.
 - Additional penetration metrics (maximum penetration depth, penetration volume, object intersection percentage, miss distance) are also reported for a more nuanced understanding of grasp quality.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Grasping Evaluation

- **Overall Performance:** The full HUG model achieved a 71.5% success rate (SR) and 19.0 mm fingertip contact (FC) error on the validation set, and 73.0% SR with 14.6 mm FC error on the test set. This performance was within approximately 20 percentage points of the human grasp oracle (90.3% val SR, 9.4 mm FC error; 94.0% test SR, 7.4 mm FC error), which serves as an upper bound due to data noise and open-loop execution.
- **Ablation Study (Table 2, Table 7):**
 - **Importance of 3D Loss:** The 3D loss on fingertip placement was identified as the most critical component, with its removal reducing test SR by over 40 points to 32.7% and more than doubling FC error to 35.7 mm.
 - **Point Cloud Crop and Point Painting:** Removing the 0.3m point cloud crop around the query point or the DINO point painting features each resulted in approximately a 10-15 point drop in SR and increased FC error, indicating the importance of dense, semantically-rich spatial context.
 - **Modality Complementarity (Figure 9):**
 - PC-only models showed strong performance (64.2% val SR, 70.7% test SR), but had higher FC error (25.6 mm val, 22.1 mm test) compared to the full model, indicating a lack of semantic grounding. They sometimes grasped non-functional parts of objects (e.g., pineapple leaves) or attached to larger nearby objects when depth was noisy.
 - RGB-only models performed significantly worse (26.8% val SR, 29.7% test SR; 95.4 mm val FC error, 108.6 mm test FC error), often failing to approach the object effectively.
 - The combined RGB+PC model resolved these issues, demonstrating the complementary nature of visual and geometric information for accurate grasp prediction.
- **Data Scaling (Figure 7):** Performance consistently improved with larger training dataset sizes. Test SR increased from 33% at 25K frames to 73% at 1M frames, while FC error decreased from 54.2 mm to 14.6 mm. The learning curves did not saturate at 1M frames, suggesting that the model remains data-bound and could benefit from even more data. Val and test curves tracked closely, indicating generalization to unseen objects without overfitting.

#### 4.2. Real-World Grasping Evaluation

- **Tabletop Experiments (ZED camera, xArm, Ability hand):**
 - HUG achieved a 66.7% overall success rate on the 30 HUG-BENCH test objects, outperforming Dex1B (43.7%) by +23% and CAP (32.7%) by +34%. HUG successfully grasped 28 out of 30 objects at least once.
 - The HUG-BENCH object set was challenging, with low absolute success rates across all methods. HUG demonstrated robustness across various object geometries and sizes. It particularly excelled on large prismatic objects (e.g., storage bin, 10/10 vs. 0/10 for baselines) and objects with complex geometry or handles (e.g., picnic basket 9/10, spray bottle 9/10, easel 8/10), where baselines struggled.
 - Common HUG failures were attributed to hardware limitations of the Ability hand, such as inability to wrap very large objects (e.g., football, wipe dispenser scored 0/10 by all methods).
- **In-the-Wild Experiments (Aria Gen 2, YOR mobile manipulator, WUJI hand):**
 - HUG was deployed in an uncontrolled household environment, transferring to a new robot embodiment (WUJI hand) and camera system (Aria Gen 2) without on-site tuning or per-object adjustments.
 - It achieved a 62.0% success rate in-the-wild, which was only a 4.7 percentage point drop compared to the tabletop setting. This indicates the model's robustness and generalizability across different robot platforms, camera setups, and complex, cluttered environments. HUG successfully grasped 29 out of 30 objects at least once in this setting.
- **Failure Modes (Figure 11):** A consistent distribution of failures was observed across both real-world settings:
 - **Pre-grasp to Grasp:** Most failures occurred during hand closure, either due to contact with the object (42 tabletop, 57 in-the-wild) or the table (8 tabletop, 8 in-the-wild) before fingers settled.
 - **Pre-grasp Missing:** A smaller proportion of failures involved the hand missing or overreaching the object before the pre-grasp phase, often on objects too large to be encompassed.
 - **Post-grasp Slips/Drops:** Some objects slipped during the lift phase (11 tabletop, 15 in-the-wild) or were dropped once raised (8 tabletop, 16 in-the-wild), indicating a need for closed-loop force control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents HUG as a framework that demonstrates the viability of learning dexterous robot grasping exclusively from large-scale, in-the-wild human demonstration data. Its contributions have several significant implications:

- **Novel Data Paradigm:** By introducing 1M-HUGS, a dataset of 1 million egocentric human grasp frames, the work offers a scalable and cost-effective alternative to traditional robot-specific data collection or purely synthetic approaches. This dataset's scale and diversity of objects and environments are expected to accelerate research in data-driven dexterous manipulation.
- **Generalizable Grasping Policies:** HUG's ability to generate human-like grasps from a single RGB-D image and generalize zero-shot to multiple robot embodiments (Ability, WUJI hands) and diverse real-world scenes (uncontrolled households, different stereo cameras) without retraining signifies a step towards more universal robot grasping capabilities. This reduces the burden of domain-specific engineering and data collection for new robot platforms.
- **Standardized Evaluation:** The introduction of HUG-BENCH, a benchmark of 90 challenging, metric-scale objects with paired simulation and real-world evaluation, provides a robust tool for assessing and comparing dexterous grasping methods. Its design, encompassing a wide range of object geometries and sizes, pushes the boundaries beyond typical, more easily graspable objects, fostering the development of more general-purpose solutions.
- **Advancement in Dexterous Manipulation:** HUG's performance, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines on challenging objects, suggests the effectiveness of learning from human motor intelligence for complex manipulation tasks. This work contributes to bridging the gap between human and robot dexterity, enabling robots to handle objects with greater versatility and robustness in unstructured environments.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The open-sourcing of the code, data, benchmark, checkpoints, and a data-to-asset pipeline (aria2mano, aria2mesh) facilitates reproducibility and encourages further research and development in the robotics and computer vision communities. This provides a foundation for future work on human-centric robot learning, dexterous control, and robust perception.

The findings suggest that focusing on natural human grasping behavior as a data source can lead to robot policies that are both effective and highly generalizable, potentially unlocking downstream applications from domestic tasks to complex tool manipulation. While limitations such as open-loop execution and challenges with specific object types remain, the framework lays a groundwork for future advancements in more adaptive and robust robot grasping systems.
