# Mechanical Intelligence-Aware Curriculum Reinforcement Learning for Humanoids with Parallel Actuation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UCLA developed a reinforcement learning framework that explicitly models parallel actuation mechanisms in humanoid robots, such as differential pulleys and five-bar linkages, within a GPU-accelerated simulation environment. This approach enabled the BRUCE humanoid to achieve robust, adaptive locomotion with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, demonstrating superior generalization across diverse real-world surfaces compared to a traditional Model Predictive Controller.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) for humanoid locomotion struggles with accurately modeling complex parallel actuation mechanisms in physics simulators.
- Simplifying parallel linkages to serial chains in simulation leads to inaccurate motion, suboptimal control policies, and the inability to capture inherent mechanical intelligence or kinematic singularities.
- Existing methods for handling closed-chain kinematics are often complex, lack seamless integration with GPU-accelerated RL frameworks, or necessitate additional low-level controllers on hardware, increasing sim-to-real discrepancies.

## Method

- Developed general mathematical formulations and simulation methods for three distinct parallel mechanisms (differential pulley, five-bar, and four-bar linkages) to accurately capture closed-chain kinematics and actuator-level properties in GPU-accelerated MuJoCo (MJX).
- Integrated these high-fidelity mechanical models into a curriculum reinforcement learning framework to train an end-to-end locomotion policy for the BRUCE humanoid robot.
- Incorporated backlash modeling at passive joints using a smooth, symmetric polynomial sigmoid function to modulate constraint impedance, enhancing simulation fidelity and improving sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- The integrated simulation of parallel mechanisms introduced only a **3.4%** increase in per-step simulation time, demonstrating computational efficiency for high-fidelity modeling.
- The RL policy achieved robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a BRUCE humanoid, demonstrating adaptive balancing responses to perturbations and successful in-place stepping on diverse real-world surfaces (synthetic grass, high-friction foam, smooth concrete).
- The learned policy exhibited superior generalization across varied terrains compared to a Model Predictive Controller (MPC) baseline, which only reliably operated on one surface, while also simplifying the control architecture by eliminating the need for on-board kinematic computations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurately simulating complex parallel mechanisms within GPU-accelerated environments introduces minimal computational overhead (3.4% increase in per-step time), making it feasible for large-scale RL training.
> - Explicitly modeling mechanical intelligence, including passive joint compliance and backlash, is crucial for training robust policies that achieve effective zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for humanoids.
> - End-to-end RL policies, trained with high-fidelity mechanical models, can intrinsically learn to leverage a robot's mechanical advantages and handle kinematic properties without requiring explicit kinematic computations during inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) has advanced humanoid robot locomotion, offering methods for complex control policies without explicit system modeling. However, a prevailing challenge in applying RL to humanoids, particularly those with sophisticated mechanical designs like parallel actuation mechanisms, lies in the limitations of physics simulators to accurately model these closed kinematic chains. Traditional approaches often simplify or approximate parallel linkages as serial chains or adopt kinematic joint spaces rather than actuator joint spaces. This simplification leads to several issues:
- **Inaccurate Motion Modeling:** The inherent mechanical intelligence, such as combined motor output power, reduced inertia, and favorable transmission ratios offered by parallel mechanisms, is not fully captured.
- **Suboptimal Policies:** Controllers trained on simplified models cannot exploit the robot's true mechanical properties, potentially leading to inefficient or unstable behaviors.
- **Lack of Singularity Representation:** Approximations often fail to model kinematic singularities, which are critical for the safe and effective operation of parallel mechanisms.
- **Increased System Complexity:** The need for a lower-level controller to handle the actual parallel mechanisms or perform inverse kinematics on hardware introduces additional computational burden and potential for sim-to-real discrepancies.

Prior efforts to address closed-chain kinematics in simulation have involved extending URDF with graph-based pre-processing and constraint embedding or treating closed chains as contact constraints for optimal control. However, these often remain complex or lack seamless integration with GPU-accelerated RL frameworks like Isaac Gym.

This work directly addresses these limitations by proposing general formulations and simulation methods for three specific types of parallel mechanisms (differential pulley, five-bar linkage, and four-bar linkage) within a GPU-accelerated MuJoCo (MJX) environment. By natively simulating these closed-chain constraints, the research aims to preserve the hardware's mechanical nonlinear properties during RL training. This approach distinguishes itself by enabling an end-to-end control policy where actions map directly to physical hardware actuators, thereby eliminating the need for intermediary kinematic computations and facilitating more accurate policy transfer to real robots. The paper benchmarks this RL approach against a Model Predictive Controller (MPC), a common control strategy for humanoids, highlighting its performance and generalization capabilities, particularly in zero-shot real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a reinforcement learning framework that explicitly accounts for the mechanical intelligence embedded in humanoid robots with parallel actuation mechanisms. This involves overcoming the limitations of current simulation tools and control methodologies that often simplify these complex mechanical structures.

The motivation for this work stems from several detailed considerations:

- **Exploiting Mechanical Intelligence:** Parallel mechanisms are known to offer significant advantages in robot design, including higher power output, reduced inertia, and improved transmission ratios, contributing to greater agility and efficiency. Existing RL frameworks often fail to leverage these inherent benefits because they do not accurately model these mechanisms. The authors are motivated to enable learning-based controllers to fully exploit these mechanical properties.
- **Addressing Simulation Inaccuracies:** The simplification of closed kinematic chains into serial approximations in simulators leads to a divergence between the simulated robot dynamics and the real hardware behavior. This mismatch results in training suboptimal control policies and necessitates complex sim-to-real transfer techniques or additional low-level controllers. The objective is to achieve higher simulation fidelity to bridge this sim-to-real gap more effectively.
- **Enabling End-to-End Control:** Current approaches often require separate kinematic or dynamic computations to translate high-level commands into actuator-level control signals for robots with parallel linkages. By natively simulating the closed-chain constraints, the authors aim to allow the RL policy to directly learn mappings from observations to actuator commands, simplifying the control architecture and potentially improving robustness. This direct mapping eliminates the need for explicit forward or inverse kinematics during inference.
- **Faithful Representation of Mechanical Properties:** Beyond general dynamics, specific characteristics like actuator-to-output position and torque mappings, and kinematic singularities, are critical for stable and efficient operation. Simplified models cannot accurately represent these. The objective is to develop simulation formulations that faithfully capture these nonlinear properties, providing richer training signals for the RL agent.
- **Demonstrating Performance Benefits:** The ultimate motivation is to show that policies trained with this mechanical intelligence-aware approach can outperform conventional control methods (like MPC) in terms of robustness, adaptability, and generalization in real-world environments, particularly for zero-shot deployment. This includes demonstrating superior performance on diverse surfaces and in response to disturbances.
- **Advancing Humanoid Robotics:** By integrating detailed mechanical modeling into the RL pipeline, the research aims to pave the way for more sophisticated and capable humanoid robots, fostering a symbiotic relationship between mechanical design and learning-based control.

The main contributions explicitly outlined by the authors are:
1. **Simulation of distinct parallel mechanisms:** Developing formulations for differential pulleys, five-bar linkages, and four-bar linkages in simulation to accurately capture closed-chain kinematics, actuator-level position and torque mappings, and kinematic singularities.
2. **Curriculum RL for end-to-end locomotion:** Training a fully end-to-end locomotion policy for the BRUCE humanoid robot using a curriculum reinforcement learning framework.
3. **Hardware validation and performance comparison:** Conducting hardware validation and comparing the performance of the learned policy against a baseline MPC controller.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research integrates detailed mechanical modeling of parallel mechanisms into a curriculum-based reinforcement learning framework for humanoid locomotion.

**A. Parallel Mechanism and Closed Kinematic Chain Formulation**
The paper provides general mathematical formulations for three distinct parallel mechanisms present in the BRUCE humanoid robot:

1. **Hip Differential Drive Gear Mechanism:** BRUCE's hip uses a cable-driven differential pulley system, which acts as a 2-DoF parallel mechanism. The constraints are modeled as equality constraints between actuated input and passive output joints. Equation (1) expresses the linear relationship between hip roll/pitch joint velocities and the left/right actuator velocities, incorporating gear ratios.
2. **Leg Five-Bar Linkage Mechanism:** This mechanism is modeled by constraining the endpoints of two serial chains to coincide, forming a closed loop. Equations (2) and (3) define the positions of the endpoints (C and D) in 2D, based on link lengths and angles. These are then projected into 3D space using transformation matrices (Equation 4), and the closed-chain constraint is enforced as $\tilde{p}_C \approx \tilde{p}_D$. The authors note the possibility of multiple kinematic solutions and the need for feasible initial configurations.
3. **Four-Bar Linkage Mechanism:** Two approaches are presented:
 - **Polynomial Transmission Ratio Approximation:** This method treats the four-bar linkage as a nonlinear gear. Equation (5) defines the velocity transmission ratio ($\rho_4$) between input and output angles. A polynomial function (Equations 6-8) is used to approximate the nonlinear mapping between input and output angles, with coefficients obtained via least squares regression. This approach does not model singularities.
 - **Closed-loop Kinematic Link Constrained Representation:** Similar to the five-bar linkage, this approach explicitly models the closed loop to capture singularities (e.g., collinear links) that polynomial approximations miss. This is necessary if joint limits do not prevent reaching singular configurations.

**B. Backlash Due to Passive Joints**
The research incorporates modeling of unintended compliance or backlash at passive joints. In MuJoCo (MJX), equality constraints are treated as soft, enforced via a virtual spring-damper system. Equation (9) describes how a smooth, symmetric polynomial sigmoid function, $D(r)$, modulates constraint impedance based on violation $r$. Backlash is modeled by keeping $D(r)$ low within a specified deadband region ($|r| < \epsilon_q$), allowing low-impedance motion, and then smoothly increasing it beyond this threshold.

**C. Reinforcement Learning Formulation**

1. **RL Formulation:** The humanoid locomotion task is formalized as a Markov Decision Process (S, A, P, r, γ).
2. **Action Space:** The action $a_t \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$ is a vector of positional commands for the robot's 16 actuator joints, interpreted as offsets relative to a nominal home configuration $q_{nom}$.
3. **Observation Space:** An aggregated vector $O_t$ is maintained, comprising a history of $H$ observations. Each observation $o_t$ includes the IMU yaw rate ($\dot{\psi}_{IMU,t}$), a projected gravity vector ($g_{proj,t}$) indicating torso tilt, user velocity commands ($C_t$), the difference between current and nominal joint angles ($q_t - q_{nom}$), and the previous action ($a_{t-1}$). The projected gravity vector is computed from an IMU rotation matrix estimated by a Madgwick filter (Equation 12).
4. **Policy and Objective:** The policy $\pi_\theta$ maps aggregated observations to a probability distribution over actions, parameterized by $\theta$. The objective is to maximize the expected cumulative reward (Equation 13).
5. **Training via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** PPO is used for stable policy updates, employing a clipped objective function (Equation 14) to limit policy changes.
6. **Environment Dynamics and Filtering:** The GPU-accelerated MuJoCo (MJX) physics engine simulates robot dynamics, incorporating domain randomization, sensor noise, and latency effects. Control signals and observations are processed with a second-order Butterworth lowpass filter and a deadband filter to mimic hardware latencies and signal smoothing.

**D. Reward Design**
The reward function is multi-component, encouraging stable, efficient, and task-oriented locomotion:
- **Tracking and Motion:** Rewards for following linear and angular velocity commands, penalties for angular velocity in XY and orientation deviations.
- **Control and Smoothness:** Penalties for excessive torque, abrupt action changes, and high-magnitude actions.
- **Gait and Foot Contact:** Rewards for foot air time, penalties for foot slip, and a reward for correct foot phase.
- **Posture and Termination:** Penalties for deviation from nominal pose during standstill and for premature terminations.
Table II details these reward terms. Domain randomization parameters (Table I) are extensively used, including terrain height maps, mass variations, actuator offsets, foot contact errors, CoM translation, observation noise, kicks, IMU perturbation, and latency.

**E. Training Scheme (Curriculum Reinforcement Learning)**
A multi-stage curriculum progressively increases task complexity (Fig. 6):
1. **Stage 1: Upright Posture:** Training to maintain balance with penalties on CoM deviations and body sway.
2. **Stage 2: Small Kicks:** Introducing disturbances to develop balance and recovery, with rewards for stabilization.
3. **Stage 3: Terrain Irregularities:** Training on uneven surfaces with randomized contacts, penalizing slipping or falling.
4. **Final Phase: Dynamic Walking:** Combining rewards from previous stages with task-oriented components for dynamic walking based on random velocity commands.
All stages include domain randomization. Policy evaluation involves sim-to-sim transfer across MJX and MuJoCo backends.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the computational efficiency of parallel mechanism simulation, the effectiveness of the RL policy, and its performance compared to an MPC baseline in both simulation and hardware.

**A. Simulation Efficiency for Parallel Mechanisms**
Benchmarking in MJX with 8,192 parallel environments over 400 timesteps showed that incorporating all three parallel mechanism constraints (4-bar, 5-bar, and differential) introduced only a 3.4% increase in per-step simulation time compared to an unconstrained model. The differential constraints contributed most to this overhead. The Just-In-Time (JIT) compilation time increased by 10.5% with all constraints, but this is a one-time cost deemed insignificant for typical RL training durations. This indicates that integrating high-fidelity parallel mechanism models does not impose a prohibitive computational burden.

**B. Sim-to-Real Transfer: Policy Validation on Hardware**

1. **Experimental Setup:** Policies trained with closed-chain constraints were deployed zero-shot on a BRUCE humanoid robot. The onboard computer handled inference (50 Hz for RL, 500 Hz for MPC baseline). Evaluation was performed on various real-world surfaces: foam, synthetic grass, and smooth concrete.

2. **Locomotion Robustness and Adaptability:**
 - **Standstill Stability:** The RL policy demonstrated adaptive responses to perturbations. For instance, in a sideways disturbance, BRUCE balanced on one leg for 1.07 seconds (Fig. 9), exhibiting a "human-like" balancing behavior. Against a 0.38 m/s forward perturbation from standstill, the robot initially pulled back before taking two forward steps to stabilize its torso, reaching steady-state after 5 seconds (Fig. 10). This highlights the policy's adaptiveness and stability.
 - **Walking:** The RL policy maintained a commanded gait frequency (e.g., 1.9 Hz commanded, 1.91 Hz measured) during straight walks and adjusted its gait frequency and sequence naturally in response to external disturbances. In contrast, the MPC controller required constant stepping and had a fixed gait sequence, even if phase time was adaptable.

3. **Surface Generalization:** The RL policy successfully performed in-place stepping on all tested surfaces: synthetic grass (resistive), high-friction foam, and smooth, low-friction concrete (Table IV). The MPC baseline, however, could only reliably operate on synthetic grass, failing on foam and concrete despite tuning efforts. This suggests superior generalization of the RL policy to varied terrains.

4. **Locomotion Speed:** The RL policy achieved a peak forward walking speed of 0.18 m/s on a flat concrete floor. This speed was 28% lower than the reported MPC maximum of 0.25 m/s, but the RL policy demonstrated operational capability across a broader range of surfaces.

5. **Policy Inference and Computation Cost:** The RL policy ran at 50 Hz, with the neural network requiring 3.2 ms per inference. The closed-chain simulation enabled an end-to-end policy architecture that eliminated the need for separate forward/inverse kinematics or state estimation computations on hardware, reducing CPU consumption and overall system complexity compared to MPC, which runs at 500 Hz, involves online quadratic programming, and requires kinematics.

6. **Ablation Study: Effect of Parallel Mechanism Fidelity on Sim-to-Real Gap:** Policies trained without explicit modeling of passive joint compliance and backlash exhibited brittleness on hardware, often failing during backward tipping events. Including this compliance and backlash in the simulation led to qualitatively improved disturbance response strategies, characterized by earlier footlift and stepping actions. This suggests that accurate modeling of mechanical intelligence, including backlash, contributes to more robust sim-to-real transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for integrating detailed mechanical modeling of parallel mechanisms into reinforcement learning, which holds several significant implications and potential impacts on humanoid robotics.

**A. Benefits in RL Training**
- **Enhanced System Identification:** By accurately simulating parallel mechanisms, the need for complex system identification of passive joints (which often lack sensors) is reduced. The approach simplifies the process by unifying the motor system identification for the entire lower body, as the same motors drive these mechanisms.
- **True End-to-End Policy Learning:** The core benefit is enabling a truly end-to-end RL policy. Aligning the simulation's action space directly with the hardware's actuation space facilitates more seamless policy transfer. This setup allows for learning non-positional commands, such as torque or velocity, without requiring explicit inverse dynamics or Jacobian computations during inference, simplifying the real-time control architecture.
- **Accurate Reward Signals:** Even when using joint positions as control inputs, the reward function's energy minimization terms become more accurate when the mechanical model is faithful to the hardware. Simplified models can lead to inaccurate torque estimations, as minimizing output torques in kinematic joint space does not guarantee a reduction in actual actuator torques for complex parallel linkages.
- **Capture of Kinematic Properties:** The closed-chain simulation explicitly captures actuator-level torque mappings and kinematic singularities (e.g., in four-bar and five-bar linkages). This provides richer, more accurate signals for policy training and evaluation, enabling the agent to learn behaviors that respect the physical limitations and advantageous properties of the hardware.

**B. Limitations**
- **Nonlinear Actuation Joint Limits:** A key limitation is the complex, configuration-dependent nature of joint limits in actuated coordinates for parallel mechanisms. While passive joint limits or self-collision constraints can mitigate this in simulation, the lack of sensing at passive joints makes direct enforcement challenging on hardware without forward kinematics. The current work mitigated this by having the RL policy implicitly learn these limits.
- **Action Scale Considerations:** For position-based policy actions, the action scale for actuators driving parallel mechanisms may require additional consideration due to the nonlinear transmission ratios.
- **Compliant Members:** The current simulation primarily models backlash effects. It does not address large-deformation compliant elements or flexure members often found in advanced legged systems. While the equality constraint formulation can approximate spring-damper behavior for small displacements, comprehensive modeling of such elements would require further extensions.
- **Quantitative Comparison Deferral:** While qualitative gains from closed-chain modeling are observed, a controlled quantitative comparison of RL policies trained with and without simplified serial/kinematic models under identical conditions is deferred to future work. This would provide a more precise measure of the benefit of high-fidelity modeling.

**C. Potential Impact**
This work highlights the importance of "mechanical intelligence" in the context of learning-based control for complex robotic systems. By demonstrating the feasibility and performance benefits of fully simulating parallel mechanisms in an end-to-end RL pipeline, the research opens doors for:
- **Broader Integration of Parallel Mechanisms:** Encouraging the design and deployment of more mechanically sophisticated robots, knowing that their intricate dynamics can be effectively modeled and controlled through RL.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Reducing the sim-to-real gap for complex humanoids, leading to more robust and capable real-world robot behaviors without extensive hardware tuning.
- **Advancements in Humanoid Locomotion:** Contributing to the development of humanoids that can exhibit more adaptive, agile, and energy-efficient locomotion across diverse and challenging terrains, fully leveraging their mechanical advantages.
- **Simplified Control Architectures:** The elimination of complex kinematic calculations on hardware could lead to simpler, more efficient onboard control systems for advanced robots.

In conclusion, this study provides a computational framework and empirical evidence for the advantages of faithfully representing complex mechanical structures in RL training, offering a pathway to harness inherent mechanical intelligence in robotic systems.
