# ManipArena: Comprehensive Real-world Evaluation of Reasoning-Oriented Generalist Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ManipArena introduces a standardized, real-world evaluation framework for reasoning-oriented generalist robot manipulation models, including VLA and world models. It features 20 diverse tasks and systematic out-of-distribution evaluation, revealing that current models achieve a maximum of 42.7% success and exhibit complementary strengths, with no single architecture excelling across all capabilities.

## Problem

- Existing robot evaluation largely relies on simulators, creating a "reality gap" due to uncaptured real-world complexities like perception noise, latency, and contact dynamics.
- Real-world evaluations are fragmented across different robot platforms and custom environments, hindering fair comparison and reproducibility across research groups.
- Current benchmarks often emphasize basic motor skills or task memorization, lacking focus on complex spatial, semantic, and sequential reasoning required for truly generalist agents.

## Method

- ManipArena provides a standardized real-world evaluation framework featuring 20 diverse reasoning-oriented tasks for both tabletop and mobile manipulation.
- It employs a server-side inference architecture, a controlled green-screen environment, and a single bimanual robot platform (X2Robot) to ensure reproducible and fair comparisons.
- The framework integrates systematic diversity design for training data and stratified out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation to rigorously test generalization, alongside rich sensory diagnostics and partial credit scoring for granular analysis.

## Results

- Baseline models achieved a maximum total score of 640.5 out of 1,500 (42.7%) across 15 tabletop tasks, indicating substantial room for improvement in current generalist manipulation capabilities.
- Unified multi-task VLA (OneModel) showed a +109% increase in semantic tasks (e.g., sort_headphone) but a -79% decrease in procedural tasks (e.g., put_items_into_drawer) compared to single-task VLA.
- World Action Models demonstrated stronger spatial invariance and more graceful degradation on compound OOD tasks, but struggled with fine-grained precision, consistently scoring low (e.g., 3/10) on tasks like insert_wireline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A "one-model-for-all-tasks" VLA approach shows multi-task gains in semantic recognition but can lead to significant performance degradation in task-specific procedural reasoning due to catastrophic forgetting.
> - VLA and World Action Models exhibit complementary capabilities: WAMs excel at coarse manipulation and spatial robustness, while VLAs perform better in fine-grained precision and semantic understanding when language explicitly guides.
> - The benchmark highlights that force-sensitive fine manipulation (e.g., pouring, insertion) and complex sequential reasoning remain significant open challenges for current generalist models, as current approaches struggle to achieve high scores.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robotic intelligence, particularly through Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and world models, represents a significant direction in modern artificial intelligence. These models aim to scale learning and robotics to achieve broad generalization across diverse tasks, including manipulation and mobile manipulation.

However, the progress of these models has encountered a notable impediment: the absence of robust and realistic evaluation protocols that genuinely reflect real-world operational scenarios. Existing benchmarks largely reside within simulator environments, such as RLBench, VLABench, and Libero. While these simulators provide essential benefits like controllability and reproducibility, they do not adequately capture the "reality gap." This gap encompasses critical real-world factors such as perception noise, complex contact dynamics, system latency, and hardware-specific constraints. Consequently, a model's success in a simulated environment does not reliably predict its performance in physical deployment.

Furthermore, current real-world evaluations tend to be fragmented. Researchers often employ different robot platforms and custom environments, which complicates fair, horizontal comparisons and hinders the reproducibility of results across different research groups. This diagnostic disparity underscores a need for a standardized evaluation framework capable of bridging the divide between virtual training and physical execution. ManipArena is introduced as a response to these limitations, aiming to provide a unified, realistic, and diagnostically rich platform for assessing generalist robot manipulation in the real world.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective behind the introduction of ManipArena is to overcome the existing limitations in evaluating reasoning-oriented generalist robot manipulation models, particularly VLA and world models, by providing a standardized, realistic, and reproducible evaluation framework. The motivation is rooted in several specific challenges within the robotics research community:

1. **Addressing the Reality Gap**: Current simulator-centric benchmarks overlook critical real-world complexities such as sensor noise, intricate contact physics, system latency, and hardware variability. This leads to a discrepancy between simulated performance and real-world deployment. ManipArena aims to bridge this gap by conducting evaluations in a physical environment that incorporates these real-world factors.
2. **Standardizing Real-World Evaluation**: The fragmented nature of existing real-world evaluations, which often rely on disparate robot platforms and environments, hinders fair comparative analysis and reproducibility. ManipArena seeks to establish a standardized protocol using a consistent robot platform and controlled evaluation environment to enable objective comparisons.
3. **Promoting Reasoning-Oriented Manipulation**: Many existing benchmarks emphasize basic motor skills or task memorization. ManipArena is motivated to push beyond these by focusing on tasks that explicitly demand complex spatial, semantic, and sequential reasoning, requiring models to understand and adapt rather than merely execute memorized trajectories.
4. **Facilitating Multi-Level Generalization Assessment**: Accurately assessing a model's generalization capabilities, especially to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, is crucial. ManipArena aims to enable controlled and systematic testing of generalization across various levels, including visual appearance, spatial configurations, and semantic shifts, moving beyond simple in-domain evaluations.
5. **Extending to Mobile Manipulation**: Most manipulation benchmarks are confined to tabletop scenarios. To reflect broader real-world applications, ManipArena seeks to incorporate long-horizon mobile manipulation tasks that integrate navigation, spatial memory, and sustained whole-body control.
6. **Enhancing Diagnostic Capabilities**: Traditional success/failure metrics provide limited insight into why a model succeeds or fails. ManipArena aims to offer rich sensory diagnostics, including low-level motor signals (currents, velocities), and sub-task scoring to enable granular analysis of model behaviors and pinpoint specific failure modes.
7. **Enabling Scalable Sim-to-Real Research**: While focusing on real-world evaluation, the framework also recognizes the importance of simulation for scalable training and debugging. ManipArena aims to provide synchronized real-to-sim environments to facilitate sim-to-real transfer and diagnostics without compromising physical realism.

By addressing these motivations, ManipArena aims to provide a robust foundation for diagnosing and advancing embodied intelligence systems towards greater generality and real-world applicability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ManipArena is designed based on five core principles to enable standardized, reasoning-intensive, and reproducible evaluation for VLA and world model approaches.

#### 3.1. Competition Format and Evaluation Protocol

ManipArena employs a server-side inference architecture, where participants submit a single HTTP endpoint that accepts observation data (camera images and proprioception) and returns action commands. This design eliminates the need for participants to deploy models on specific hardware, reducing the barrier to entry and ensuring fair comparison by running all trials on identical hardware under controlled conditions. A "one-model-for-all-tasks" rule is enforced, requiring a single unified policy across all tasks to test genuine generalization rather than task-specific overfitting. Each task is evaluated over 10 trials, with a scoring range of 0–10 points per trial based on sub-task completion, leading to a maximum of 100 points per task and a total of 1,500 points across the 15 evaluated tabletop tasks.

#### 3.2. Task Design

The framework comprises 20 tasks, divided into three categories:
- **Execution Reasoning Tasks (10 tasks)**: These tasks emphasize precise motor execution, force control, and bimanual coordination. Examples include `arrange_cup_inverted_triangle` and `insert_wireline`.
- **Semantic Reasoning Tasks (5 tasks)**: These tasks require understanding object categories, attributes, and relationships before acting, challenging models to generalize semantic concepts to novel instances. Examples include `sort_headphone` and `press_button_in_order`.
- **Mobile Manipulation Tasks (5 tasks)**: These involve long-horizon whole-body control, navigation, and spatial memory across an extended workspace. Examples include `put_clothes_in_hamper` and `hang_up_picture`. These mobile tasks are significantly longer, averaging 4.3 times the duration of tabletop tasks.

All tasks are designed to be non-trivial, demanding reasoning beyond simple motor skills. Task selection criteria included diverse manipulation primitives, object types (rigid, articulated, deformable, liquid), unambiguous success criteria, and sufficient difficulty to avoid ceiling performance.

#### 3.3. Controlled Evaluation Environment

All evaluations are conducted within a green-screen enclosed environment, a self-contained booth with uniform chroma-key walls and ceiling, and fixed artificial lighting. This setup provides:
- **Variable Isolation**: Eliminates uncontrolled visual variation, attributing performance differences solely to designed object or spatial changes.
- **Controlled Illumination**: Shields the workspace from external light sources, ensuring consistent visual conditions.
- **Reproducibility and Portability**: The self-contained nature allows for consistent conditions across different times and locations.
- **Future Extensibility**: Enables research into visual robustness by compositing varied backgrounds onto the green screen.

#### 3.4. Systematic Diversity Design

To ensure that high scores reflect genuine generalization, ManipArena systematically injects diversity into the training data. Each task has a diversity guide specifying object variants, color sets, spatial configurations, and compositional permutations. Approximately 500 trajectories per task (10,812 total, ~188 hours) are collected following these guides, with diversity dimensions uniformly distributed. A strict separation between training and test distributions is maintained, ensuring OOD test objects are never present in training data.

Diversity is organized into three hierarchical levels:
1. **Physical Attribute Diversity (Appearance Layer)**: Object variations in material, color, and size (e.g., ceramic mugs vs. paper cups).
2. **Spatial Configuration Diversity (Layout Layer)**: Randomized object positions and orientations (e.g., varying socket or cup positions).
3. **Semantic Composition Diversity (Task Layer)**: For semantic tasks, variations in object combinations, orderings, and category assignments (e.g., permutations of button press sequences).

#### 3.5. Stratified OOD Evaluation Design

Evaluation trials are stratified to measure generalization difficulty. For each task, 10 trials progress from in-domain competence (T1-T4) to visual shifts (T5-T8), and then to either semantic OOD objects or challenging configuration-level generalization (T9-T10). OOD objects are selected to represent varying semantic distances from the training distribution, ranging from near-OOD (e.g., similar grasp strategies) to far-OOD (e.g., radically different form factors or compound changes). This structure enables a complete generalization profile from a single evaluation session.

#### 3.6. Robot Platform

All tasks utilize the X2Robot bimanual system, ensuring a unified embodiment. The tabletop setup consists of four robot units with bimanual 6-DOF arms and three camera views. The mobile setup uses one Quanta X1 mobile robot with bimanual ARX arms, an omnidirectional base, and a height-adjustable lifting column, operating within the green-screen enclosure. This single-embodiment design allows for direct comparison of policy capabilities without hardware confounds.

#### 3.7. Sensor Data and Action Space

ManipArena provides rich sensory signals, including 56-dimensional state and action vectors for tabletop tasks (62D for mobile tasks). These vectors include end-effector poses, joint positions, joint velocities, and joint currents (as a torque proxy). Mobile tasks add dimensions for head rotation, lifting column height, and base velocity. Three synchronized camera views (face, left wrist, right wrist) at 640x480 resolution and 20 fps are also provided. The inclusion of motor current and joint velocity data aims to encourage research into force-aware and dynamics-aware policies.

#### 3.8. Real2Sim

Digitally twinned simulation environments are constructed to be geometrically and visually consistent with the physical setup, enabling scalable offline evaluation and sim-to-real transfer research. The simulation environment is built on IsaacLab and IsaacLab-Arena. Scene reconstruction uses 3D Gaussian Splatting for photorealistic fidelity, and task objects are generated using Hunyuan3D. Crucially, simulation data is trajectory-aligned with real-robot data by replaying recorded real-robot joint trajectories in simulation, facilitating precise sim-to-real gap analysis.

#### 3.9. Evaluation Scoring

Each task is decomposed into ordered sub-tasks, with partial credit scoring applied (0–10 scale). This provides more granular diagnostic information than binary success/failure, indicating at which stage of a task a model may encounter difficulties. For example, a model that grasps an object but fails to pour water would receive partial credit, distinguishing it from a model unable to initiate the task.

#### 3.10. Dataset

The ManipArena training dataset comprises 10,812 teleoperated trajectories across 20 tasks, totaling approximately 188 hours of data at 20 fps. Data was collected using a master-follower bimanual setup across four tabletop robots and one mobile robot. Each trajectory includes three synchronized video streams, proprioceptive state (56D/62D), action labels, and a natural-language instruction. The data is released in LeRobot v2.1 format, designed for compatibility with popular VLA training frameworks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper evaluates three representative baseline models on 15 tabletop tasks: $\pi_{0.5}$-Single (task-specific VLA), $\pi_{0.5}$-OneModel (unified multi-task VLA), and DreamZero (World Action Model). Evaluation of the 5 mobile manipulation tasks is stated as ongoing.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Benchmark Saturation

The benchmark currently remains far from saturated. The highest total score achieved by any model was 640.5 out of 1,500 (42.7%) by OneModel. The median best performance for a single task across all models was 51.5. Four tasks (`pour_water`, `insert_wireline`, `arrange_cup`, `put_stationery`) recorded scores below 30 for all models, indicating that these tasks present significant challenges to current approaches. No single model demonstrated overall dominance, with winning tasks distributed across the baselines, and low pairwise correlation between model performances. This suggests that different models excel in distinct types of tasks.

#### 4.2. Single-Task vs. Multi-Task VLA (π$_{0.5}$-Single vs. OneModel)

A comparison between $\pi_{0.5}$-Single and OneModel, both built on the same architecture, revealed a trade-off:
- **Multi-Task Gains in Semantic Recognition**: OneModel, trained across all 15 tasks, substantially outperformed Single on tasks requiring object-level semantic understanding, such as `sort_headphone` (+109% increase) and `pair_up_items` (+145% increase). This suggests that multi-task training facilitates the development of richer, shared visual representations beneficial for categorical recognition.
- **Multi-Task Costs in Procedural Knowledge Forgetting**: Conversely, OneModel experienced significant performance degradation on tasks requiring task-specific procedural reasoning, such as `press_button_in_order` (-73% decrease) and `put_items_into_drawer` (-79% decrease). This indicates that multi-task training can dilute or overwrite task-specific input-output mappings critical for complex sequential procedures.

#### 4.3. VLA vs. World Action Model (OneModel vs. DreamZero)

A comparison between OneModel (VLA) and DreamZero (WAM) indicated complementary strengths:
- **Coarse vs. Fine Manipulation**: DreamZero excelled at coarse pick-and-place operations, achieving 97.8/100 with 90% success rate on `pick_items_into_basket`. However, it demonstrated limited capability in fine-grained manipulation, often failing at later, precision-demanding sub-steps (e.g., consistently scoring 3/10 on `insert_wireline`, indicating only initial pickup success). OneModel generally performed better in tasks requiring precision (e.g., 87/100 on `put_glasses`).
- **Semantic Understanding**: OneModel generally achieved higher scores on semantic tasks (average 58.9 vs. DreamZero's 32.7). DreamZero was competitive when the language instruction fully specified the task objective (e.g., `sort_headphone`). However, when the model needed to infer action plans from visual cues beyond explicit language (e.g., reading a color-sequence card in `press_button_in_order`), DreamZero's performance significantly declined.
- **Generalization to OOD and Spatial Robustness**:
 - **Compound OOD**: DreamZero exhibited more graceful degradation on compound OOD tasks, such as `sort_headphone` (a -33% drop) compared to OneModel (a -95% drop). This suggests that DreamZero's video-based representations may capture physical affordances more robustly to appearance changes.
 - **Spatial Invariance**: DreamZero demonstrated near-perfect performance on `pick_items_into_basket` despite basket position shifts (only -8% degradation), while OneModel degraded more sharply (-44%). This spatial invariance in DreamZero is hypothesized to arise from its video prediction objective implicitly learning spatial equivariance.
 - **Brittle OOD**: For precision tasks like `put_spoon_to_bowl`, both VLA and WAM models showed sensitivity to spatial configurations, with OOD "generalization" sometimes appearing as accidental alignment with specific configurations rather than true understanding of novel objects.

#### 4.4. Capability Boundary Analysis

A systematic analysis across five core capability dimensions revealed distinct strengths:
- **Coarse Manipulation**: DreamZero was dominant.
- **Precision Manipulation**: OneModel showed stronger performance.
- **Sequential Reasoning**: $\pi_{0.5}$-Single excelled, indicating the benefit of task-specific fine-tuning for procedural knowledge.
- **Semantic Understanding**: OneModel demonstrated leadership, benefiting from cross-task visual representation transfer.
- **Spatial Robustness**: DreamZero exhibited high consistency across spatial configurations.

This analysis indicates that VLA and WAM paradigms learn complementary capabilities, with no single model demonstrating proficiency across all dimensions.

#### 4.5. Diagnostic Value of Partial Credit Scoring

The partial credit scoring system in ManipArena provided valuable diagnostic insights that binary success/failure metrics would not. For instance, DreamZero's consistent partial scores on tasks like `insert_wireline` indicated success in initial sub-steps (e.g., cable pickup) despite overall task failure. This granular scoring mechanism provides actionable signals for identifying specific failure modes and guiding model improvement strategies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ManipArena represents a significant contribution to the field of embodied artificial intelligence by addressing several critical limitations in the evaluation of generalist robot manipulation models. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Standardized Real-World Evaluation**: By providing a standardized framework, ManipArena enables fair and reproducible comparison of robotic policies. This consistency, achieved through a single robot embodiment, a controlled green-screen environment, and uniform evaluation protocols, allows researchers to attribute performance differences to policy capabilities rather than extraneous factors. This is crucial for accelerating progress in a field often hindered by fragmented evaluations.

2. **Bridging the Reality Gap**: The framework's emphasis on real-world execution, incorporating factors like perception noise, complex contact dynamics, and hardware constraints, ensures that evaluated models are tested for their practical deployment readiness. The synchronized Real2Sim environments further support this by offering a platform for scalable experimentation and detailed sim-to-real diagnostics, which can help in systematically identifying and closing the reality gap.

3. **Advancing Reasoning Capabilities**: ManipArena's task design, which prioritizes reasoning-oriented manipulation over simple motor skills or memorization, pushes the boundaries of current robotic intelligence. By requiring semantic understanding, spatial reasoning, and multi-stage bimanual operations, the benchmark encourages the development of more intelligent and adaptive policies.

4. **Promoting Generalization Research**: The systematic design for multi-level generalization, including stratified out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation and diverse training data, provides a robust platform for rigorously testing and diagnosing a model's ability to extrapolate to novel scenarios. This detailed approach can illuminate where and why generalization succeeds or fails, guiding future research into more robust and adaptable agents.

5. **Unveiling Model Complementarities and Limitations**: The baseline evaluations provided valuable diagnostic insights into the strengths and weaknesses of different model paradigms (VLA vs. World Action Models) and training strategies (single-task vs. multi-task). The finding that multi-task VLA training enhances semantic recognition but can hinder procedural memory, and that World Action Models excel at coarse manipulation and spatial robustness but struggle with fine precision, suggests promising avenues for hybrid architectures that leverage complementary capabilities.

6. **Highlighting Open Challenges**: The benchmark clearly identifies force-sensitive fine manipulation (e.g., pouring, insertion) as a significant open challenge for current approaches. By providing rich sensory diagnostics, including low-level motor currents and joint velocities, ManipArena encourages novel research utilizing these signals to develop force-aware and dynamics-aware policies, potentially leading to breakthroughs in dexterity and precise interaction.

7. **Diagnostic Value of Fine-Grained Scoring**: The partial credit scoring system offers a nuanced understanding of model failures, distinguishing between models that cannot initiate a task, those that partially succeed, and those that fail intermittently. This diagnostic granularity is essential for targeted model improvement and debugging.

In summary, ManipArena provides a comprehensive, standardized, and diagnostically rich evaluation framework that is positioned to be a foundational resource for the embodied AI community. It not only allows for fair assessment of current robot learning models but also directs future research towards critical challenges, paving the way for the development of more general, capable, and reliable robotic systems.
