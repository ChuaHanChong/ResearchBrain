# Neurosymbolic Reasoning with Incremental Knowledge for Sample Efficient Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A neurosymbolic hierarchical reinforcement learning framework dynamically updates a high-level symbolic world model with incremental knowledge from a low-level neural controller's execution. This approach significantly enhances sample efficiency in complex, sparse-reward tasks, demonstrating a 30-fold reduction in steps compared to non-incremental methods, and introduces a novel planning algorithm to leverage structural prior knowledge.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and many Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) approaches suffer from prohibitive sample complexity in environments with sparse rewards and long-horizon tasks.
- Existing neurosymbolic HRL methods typically require extensive upfront exploration to construct a complete abstract world model, leading to inefficiency and a 'semantic gap' between high-level plans and low-level execution.
- Traditional incremental planners (like D*) do not inherently consider a belief over possible worlds, limiting their ability to exploit structural prior knowledge, while Bayesian planning methods often assume independence across state-action pairs.

## Method

- A neurosymbolic HRL framework integrates **Incremental Knowledge (InK)**, allowing a high-level symbolic planner to dynamically update its ==abstract world model== based on feedback from a low-level goal-conditioned neural controller.
- The framework employs a ==coordination mechanism== where the high-level planner generates subgoals, the low-level policy executes them, and a monitor observes the execution trace to detect environmental information for world model updates.
- ==Belief World Tree Search (BWTS)== is introduced as a novel high-level planner that performs optimal planning, on average, over a belief set of possible worlds to effectively exploit structural prior knowledge.

## Results

- The InK framework achieved substantial sample efficiency gains over non-InK baselines, demonstrating a **30-fold reduction** in initial goal-reaching steps (e.g., from **2080 to 64.9 steps** in 'Four Rooms') and over **100,000 cumulative steps** saved in complex mazes.
- Belief World Tree Search (BWTS) consistently outperformed D* by reducing expected costs (e.g., **15.7% fewer steps** in worlds with single horizontal walls) through its ability to plan over belief worlds and infer obstacle locations, albeit with increased runtime for symbolic planning.
- In challenging continuous control environments like Ant-Maze U-Room, InK with D* or BWTS significantly reduced the steps required to reach goals (e.g., **1134-1507 steps**) compared to approximately **10,000 steps** for non-InK methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically updating the abstract world model with incrementally acquired knowledge significantly improves sample efficiency by allowing goal-directed behavior from the outset, rather than requiring exhaustive upfront exploration.
> - A tight feedback loop between high-level symbolic planning and low-level neural execution can effectively bridge the 'semantic gap,' ensuring high-level plans are grounded in the actual capabilities and observed environment of the low-level controller.
> - Planning over a belief set of possible worlds (as with BWTS) enables principled exploitation of structural prior knowledge, leading to more efficient information-gathering and goal-reaching strategies compared to planners that ignore or misinterpret such priors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has achieved notable successes in various high-dimensional control problems. However, DRL approaches often encounter significant challenges in environments characterized by sparse rewards and the requirement for long-horizon reasoning. These settings typically lead to prohibitive sample complexity, meaning an excessively large number of interactions with the environment are needed for the agent to learn effective policies.

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) emerged as a paradigm to mitigate these challenges by decomposing complex tasks into multiple levels of abstraction. Most traditional HRL methods employ neural network policies at both high and low levels of abstraction. A more recent direction in HRL involves neurosymbolic approaches, where a symbolic component handles high-level planning and an artificial neural network manages low-level execution. In these neurosymbolic HRL systems, the symbolic high-level typically maintains an abstract world model to facilitate planning, while neural low-level policies execute the resulting subgoals.

Existing neurosymbolic HRL methods, such as SoRB and RGL, commonly operate under a non-incremental knowledge (non-InK) paradigm. This approach necessitates the complete construction of an abstract world model, often through extensive upfront exploration, before any goal-directed behavior or effective planning can commence. This exhaustive exploration, irrespective of the specific task or goal, fundamentally contributes to high sample inefficiency. For instance, a low-level policy might be pretrained in an obstacle-free environment and then deployed in a novel environment with walls, leading to a "semantic gap" where the high-level planner's assumptions of feasibility do not align with the low-level policy's capabilities in the new environment.

In contrast, classical robotics planning, exemplified by techniques like Active SLAM and algorithms such as D\* (Dynamic A\*), frequently adopts an incremental knowledge (InK) approach. In these systems, the abstract world model is dynamically updated during execution as new information (e.g., newly detected obstacles) becomes available, triggering replanning. While Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) also incorporates incremental knowledge by refining task plans based on motion planning feedback, these systems typically assume symbolic components at both the high and low levels. The integration of InK into neurosymbolic HRL, where a neural low-level policy with unknown dynamics interacts with an updatable symbolic high-level, remains an underexplored area. Such an integration could allow for world model updates based on execution feedback, bridging the semantic gap and enabling more adaptive, human-like reasoning in agents.

Furthermore, leveraging structural prior knowledge in these settings presents a challenge. While planning under uncertainty is often framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) or Bayesian Adaptive MDP (BAMDP), these formulations may not adequately capture structural knowledge (e.g., "exactly one wall at an unknown location") that introduces dependencies between states. Existing algorithms like BAMCP often assume independence across state-action pairs, preventing them from effectively exploiting such structural constraints. Incremental replanning algorithms like D\* also do not intrinsically incorporate belief over possible worlds, making them suboptimal when averaged over these possibilities. This work addresses these gaps by proposing a neurosymbolic HRL framework that integrates incremental knowledge and introduces a novel planning algorithm to exploit structural priors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the sample efficiency of Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) agents in environments characterized by sparse rewards and the need for long-horizon reasoning. This is achieved by proposing a novel neurosymbolic HRL framework that integrates **Incremental Knowledge (InK)**.

The motivation for adopting InK stems from several limitations observed in existing DRL and HRL approaches:

1. **High Sample Complexity in Sparse-Reward, Long-Horizon Tasks:** Purely end-to-end DRL methods and many HRL approaches struggle with prohibitive sample complexity in domains where rewards are sparse and goals require extensive sequences of actions. This means agents require an impractical number of environmental interactions to learn effective behaviors.
2. **Inefficiency of Non-Incremental World Model Construction:** Current neurosymbolic HRL methods, such as RGL, typically follow a non-incremental paradigm. They necessitate the upfront and exhaustive exploration of the environment to construct a complete abstract world model before any goal-directed planning or learning begins. This extensive pre-exploration is inefficient, as it requires the agent to acquire a full understanding of the world regardless of the immediate goal.
3. **Semantic Gap between High-Level Abstractions and Low-Level Execution:** In neurosymbolic HRL, low-level controllers are often neural policies with unknown dynamics, sometimes pretrained in simplified environments. This can lead to a "semantic gap" where the high-level symbolic planner assumes actions are feasible that the low-level policy cannot actually execute due to incomplete or inaccurate knowledge of the real environment, stochastic effects, or unforeseen obstacles. This highlights the need for dynamic world model updates.
4. **Reflecting Human-like Reasoning:** Human agents typically operate with partial knowledge, updating their understanding of the world as new information is acquired during interaction, and refining their decisions accordingly. Incorporating InK into HRL aims to better reflect this adaptive learning process, enabling goal-directed behavior from the earliest stages of interaction without requiring full prior environmental knowledge.
5. **Limitations in Exploiting Structural Prior Knowledge:** While prior knowledge can significantly accelerate learning, existing incremental planners like D\* do not inherently consider a belief set of possible worlds. Furthermore, Bayesian planning approaches like BAMCP, while addressing unknown dynamics, often assume independence across state-action pairs. This assumption prevents them from effectively leveraging structural prior knowledge (e.g., "there is exactly one wall at an unknown location") that implies strong dependencies across different parts of the environment.

To address this last limitation, a secondary objective of this work is to develop **Belief World Tree Search (BWTS)**. BWTS is designed to enable optimal planning, on average, over all possible worlds consistent with structural priors, thereby overcoming the limitations of D\* and BAMCP in effectively exploiting such knowledge within the InK setting.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for more sample-efficient and adaptable HRL agents that can reason with partial, incrementally acquired knowledge, and strategically exploit structural prior knowledge in complex, continuous control environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces a neurosymbolic Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) framework that integrates Incremental Knowledge (InK). The methodology consists of a two-level architecture: a high-level symbolic planner and a low-level goal-conditioned neural controller.

#### 3.1. The Incremental Knowledge Setting (Belief World Process)

The framework operates within a "Belief World Process," denoted as $M = (W_0, S, A, (T_W)_{W \in W_0}, C)$.
- $W_0$ is a finite set of possible worlds, where the true environment $W_{true}$ is a member of this set. This $W_0$ encodes any structural prior knowledge about the environment.
- $S \subseteq R^n$ is the continuous state space and $A$ is the continuous action space, common to all possible worlds.
- $T_W: S \times A \times S \rightarrow$ represents the world-dependent transition dynamics.
- $C: S \times A \rightarrow R_{\geq 0}$ is the cost function, where $C(S, a) = 0$ only if the state $S$ is within the goal region $G$.
The agent's policy $\pi$ aims to minimize the cumulative cost $C(\pi)$, which is defined as the average of $C(\pi, W)$ (cost to reach the goal in world $W$ using policy $\pi$) over all worlds $W \in W_0$.
Crucially, the states are fully observable, but the world dynamics ($T_W$) are unknown and discovered incrementally through interaction. Observed history ($\rho$) allows the agent to prune $W_0$ to a compatible belief set $W_\rho$.

#### 3.2. Neurosymbolic Hierarchical RL Architecture for InK

The proposed HRL framework coordinates a symbolic high-level with a neural low-level:
1. **High-Level Symbolic Planner:**
 - **Abstraction:** The continuous state space $S$ and action space $A$ are abstracted into a finite set of symbolic states (vertices $V$) and symbolic actions (edges $A$).
 - **World Model:** The high-level maintains an updatable abstract world model $M$, represented symbolically (e.g., a set of predicates describing obstacles). This model stores the Incremental Knowledge (InK).
 - **Planning:** The symbolic planner (initially D\*, later BWTS) performs planning on $M$ to find an optimal path from the current configuration to the goal $G$. It then selects the first intermediate subgoal $g$ from this plan.
2. **Low-Level Goal-Conditioned Neural Controller:**
 - **Execution:** A goal-conditioned neural policy $\pi_l$ takes the current state $s \in S$ and the high-level subgoal $g$ as input, and executes a sequence of continuous low-level actions in the environment until $g$ is reached or the attempt fails.
 - **Sensor/Monitor:** A monitor `mon` observes the low-level execution trace $\rho$ to detect environmental information, such as newly encountered obstacles.
3. **Coordination and Knowledge Update:**
 - Upon receiving new information `mon($\rho$)` and the resulting state from the low-level, the high-level updates its InK in the abstract world model $M$. This tight feedback loop allows the agent to refine its understanding of the environment dynamically, eliminating the need for a costly upfront exploration phase.

#### 3.3. High-Level Symbolic Planners

- **A\* and Dynamic A\* (D\*):**
 - A\* is a heuristic-guided search algorithm for finding shortest paths in a graph.
 - D\* extends A\* to the InK setting. It plans based on current knowledge and, upon detecting discrepancies (e.g., an unexpected obstacle), updates the graph and replans. D\* is used as the initial incremental planner due to its simplicity in analysis and effectiveness in navigation tasks. However, D\* does not inherently consider a belief set $W_0$ and thus cannot exploit structural prior knowledge effectively.

- **Belief World Tree Search (BWTS):**
 - **Motivation:** To address D\*'s limitation regarding structural prior knowledge and to plan optimally on average over $W_0$.
 - **Tree Structure:** BWTS constructs a tree where nodes are either decision nodes (representing choices of actions, denoted $(v, W)$) or chance nodes (representing outcomes of actions, denoted $(v, a, W)$).
 - A decision node $(v, W)$ has children representing actions $a \in A$.
 - A chance node $(v, a, W)$ has two children (decision nodes $(v, W^-)$ and $(v', W^+)$), corresponding to whether the unobstructed successor $v_a$ is blocked (wall detected, $W^-$) or not (no wall, $W^+$).
 - Leaves are decision nodes $(v, W)$ where $|W|=1$, meaning the world is fully known.
 - **Cost Calculation:** Costs are calculated bottom-up. For a leaf $(v, \{W\})$, cost is the shortest path in $W$. For a chance node, cost is the weighted average of its children's costs. For a decision node, cost is the minimum over actions $a$ of $C(v,a) + C(v,a,W)$. Theorem 1 proves that the policy minimizing these costs is optimal for the InK problem.
 - **BWTS Algorithm (MCTS-like with Modifications):** To handle the intractability of constructing a complete tree for large $W_0$, BWTS adapts Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) stages:
 - **Strategic Rollouts:** Unlike MCTS's random rollouts, BWTS uses a fixed set of $k$ predefined strategies ($\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_k$, e.g., D\* variants, wall-sweeping) to estimate the value $Q(v, W)$ of an unexpanded node. $Q(v, W)$ is the minimum average cost over these strategies across worlds in $W$. This reduces variance and stabilizes backups.
 - **Exploration Bonus (Decision Nodes):** Similar to the UCB1 rule in MCTS, it uses a Lower Confidence Bound (LCB) term to balance exploration and exploitation: $\alpha(v, W) \in \arg \min_{a \in A} \left(Q(v, a, W) - c_e \sqrt{\frac{\ln N(v,W)}{N(v,a,W)}}\right)$.
 - **Selection (Chance Nodes):** Prioritizes children with larger belief sets ($|W|$), as they typically require more exploration.
 - **Expansion and Backpropagation:** Standard MCTS procedures, but Q-values are updated based on strategic rollout estimates and the weighted average/minimum calculations.
 - **Convergence:** Theorem 2 states that the sequence of policies generated by BWTS converges to the optimal policy for the discrete symbolic InK problem, meaning that given sufficient iterations, BWTS will construct the full optimal tree.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation compares the proposed InK framework against a non-InK baseline and analyzes the performance of different symbolic planners within InK.

#### 4.1. InK vs. Non-InK

- **Environments:** Point-Maze (Four Rooms, Medium Maze, Hard Maze) and Ant-Maze U-Room. All low-level policies were pre-trained in obstacle-free environments, consistent with RGL.
- **Baselines:** RGL (a state-of-the-art non-InK neurosymbolic HRL method) was used as the non-InK comparison.
- **Key Metric:** Sample efficiency (number of steps to reach the goal) and runtime.

**Results:**
- **Initial Goal Reaching (from scratch ∅):** InK (using D\* as the high-level planner) demonstrated substantial sample efficiency improvements over RGL. For instance, in "Four Rooms," InK required $64.90 \pm 9.17$ steps, compared to RGL's $2080 \pm 701$ steps, representing a 30-fold reduction. Similar reductions (up to 100-fold) were observed in Medium and Hard Maze. Runtime for InK was also significantly lower (e.g., $0.02s$ vs. $96.48s$ in Four Rooms).
- **After "Training" (cumulative goals):**
 - RGL's "training" involves building a complete abstract world graph (requiring up to 300,000 samples and over 6000s in Hard Maze).
 - InK's "training" involved reaching 75 random goals to prebuild its knowledge base (requiring ~3,000-4,600 samples and less than 1s).
 - After these respective training phases, InK consistently showed better sample efficiency than RGL. InK required approximately 18% fewer samples and was 20 times faster in runtime per goal compared to RGL.
 - **Cumulative Sample Gain:** Over a sequence of 200 goals, InK demonstrated a continuously growing cumulative sample efficiency gain over RGL, exceeding 100,000 environment steps in the Hard Maze scenario. While RGL initially benefits from its upfront learned structure, InK matches its efficiency after approximately 25 goals and then surpasses it.
- **Ant-Maze U-Room:** In this complex, high-dimensional environment (29 dimensions), non-InK methods required approximately 10,000 steps to build a full abstract world graph. In contrast, InK with D\* required 1507 steps, and InK with BWTS required 1134 steps to reach the goal.

#### 4.2. Comparison of Symbolic Planners (D\*, BWTS, BAMCP)

- **Environments:** Synthetic 10x10 grid worlds designed to test the exploitation of structural prior knowledge. Belief sets included worlds with a single horizontal wall ($W_h$), a single vertical wall ($W_v$), or either ($W_{hv}$).
- **Metrics:** Expected number of steps (averaged over belief worlds) and runtime.
- **Planners:** D\* (deterministic), BWTS (with exploration bonus $c_e=1.5$, 200,000 iterations, 10 strategic rollouts), and BAMCP (with varying prior probabilities for wall detection).

**Results:**
- **BWTS vs. D\*:**
 - BWTS consistently achieved lower expected costs. Gains were most significant when structural prior knowledge was highly constraining: 15.7% fewer steps than D\* in $W_h$ and 9.3% in $W_v$. In $W_{hv}$ (more varied worlds), the gain was a smaller 2.2%.
 - BWTS's advantage stemmed from its ability to plan over belief worlds, leading to probing strategies that minimize expected cost by inferring likely obstacle locations (e.g., efficiently finding a doorway without costly backtracking).
 - Runtime for BWTS was significantly higher for these symbolic problems (e.g., $1.05s$ for $W_h$ vs. $0.01s$ for D\*), reflecting the computational cost of exploring belief trees.
- **BAMCP:**
 - BAMCP performed poorly, exhibiting expected costs roughly twice that of D\* and BWTS across all belief sets, with prohibitive runtimes (e.g., $30.88s$ for $W_h$ vs. $1.05s$ for BWTS).
 - This poor performance was attributed to BAMCP's assumption of independence across state-action pairs, which prevents it from effectively leveraging structural knowledge (e.g., if a wall is here, it cannot be there). Additionally, BAMCP's reliance on stochastic rollouts often led to inefficient exploration (e.g., looping trajectories) and unstable planning in open environments.
- **InK-BWTS vs. InK-D\* in Physical Environments:**
 - **Without Prior Knowledge:** When BWTS was used as the symbolic planner in the physical RGL environments without explicitly encoding specific prior knowledge (by generating a subset of $W_{hv}$ for local planning), InK-BWTS was marginally more sample-efficient ($86.97$ steps vs. $87.70$ steps for InK-D\*) but had a significantly longer runtime ($62.56s$ vs. $0.02s$).
 - **With Prior Knowledge:** When InK-BWTS was initialized with a belief set of three specific worlds (4 Rooms, Medium Maze, Hard Maze), it substantially outperformed InK-D\*, halving the number of samples required ($45.97$ steps vs. $87.70$ steps). The initial construction time for BWTS ($2.66s$) was offset by faster subsequent execution times ($0.01s$) across different mazes.

In conclusion, InK (with D\*) provides substantial sample efficiency gains over non-InK methods in continuous control, even without leveraging structural prior knowledge. When structural prior knowledge is available and properly encoded, InK-BWTS further improves sample efficiency, albeit with a trade-off in increased computational time for belief-tree construction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a neurosymbolic Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) framework with Incremental Knowledge (InK) that offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Addressing Sample Inefficiency in HRL:** The core contribution is the substantial improvement in sample efficiency for HRL agents operating in complex environments with sparse rewards and long-horizon planning requirements. By demonstrating gains exceeding 100,000 environment steps over non-InK methods like RGL in continuous control tasks, this work provides a practical pathway to reduce the prohibitive data demands of DRL.
2. **Bridging the Semantic Gap:** The proposed tight coordination between a high-level symbolic planner and a low-level neural controller, with feedback from low-level execution informing high-level world model updates, effectively addresses the semantic gap. This allows the abstract world model to be policy-dependent, ensuring that high-level plans are grounded in the observed capabilities and constraints of the neural low-level.
3. **Human-like Adaptive Reasoning:** The InK paradigm reflects a more adaptive and human-like approach to problem-solving. Instead of requiring exhaustive upfront exploration, agents learn and update their world model incrementally, making decisions with partial knowledge and refining them as new information is acquired. This flexibility is valuable for real-world robotic applications where complete environmental knowledge is rarely available beforehand.
4. **Novel Approach to Structural Prior Knowledge:** The introduction of Belief World Tree Search (BWTS) provides a novel and effective method for exploiting structural prior knowledge in unknown environments. Unlike existing methods like D\* (which ignores such knowledge) or BAMCP (which struggles with dependencies implied by structural priors), BWTS plans optimally on average over a belief set of possible worlds, leading to more efficient, probing strategies. This capability can be crucial in domains where certain structural patterns are known to exist but their specific instantiation is unknown.
5. **Practical Application in Complex Continuous Environments:** The successful application of InK in challenging continuous physical environments, such as the MuJoCo Ant-Maze U-Room, demonstrates its scalability and practicality for high-dimensional control problems beyond simpler grid-world navigation tasks.
6. **Guidance for Planner Selection:** The comparative analysis of D\*, BWTS, and BAMCP provides clear guidance for practitioners. In scenarios without explicit structural prior knowledge, InK-D\* offers a robust and highly sample-efficient solution. When structural prior knowledge is available, InK-BWTS can yield further sample efficiency gains, albeit with increased initial computational cost. This trade-off informs the design choices for specific applications.
7. **Future Research Directions:** The work opens avenues for future research, including extending InK to other domains like manipulation tasks using PDDL-based planners. Furthermore, the identified limitations of BWTS and BAMCP regarding different types of prior knowledge (structural vs. probabilistic) suggest a promising direction for developing unified algorithms (e.g., BAWMCP) that can combine these complementary strengths.

Overall, this research represents a significant step forward in making HRL more sample-efficient, robust, and adaptable, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with neurosymbolic approaches in complex reinforcement learning settings.
