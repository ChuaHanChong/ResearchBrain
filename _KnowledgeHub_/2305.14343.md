# Video Prediction Models as Rewards for Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> VIPER (Video Prediction Rewards) turns a frozen autoregressive video prediction model into a reward function for reinforcement learning. A VQ-GAN plus VideoGPT-style transformer is trained on action-free expert videos; the agent is then rewarded by the model's next-frame log-likelihood plus an entropy/exploration bonus derived from KL matching. It reaches expert-level control on 28 DMC, Atari, and RLBench tasks and generalizes across embodiments.

## Problem

- Prior video-imitation rewards score only the current observation against the expert distribution, so they cannot capture temporally extended, order-dependent behaviors.
- Adversarial imitation methods learn discriminators that mode-collapse on diverse multi-task expert data, latching onto task-irrelevant cues and yielding uninformative reward signals.
- Inverse-dynamics pipelines that relabel action-free video depend on label quality and require expensive generative video rollouts for planning, slowing environment interaction.

## Method

- **VIPER** reward: frozen video model's conditional next-frame log-likelihood over a sliding context window, computed on latent ==VQ codes== rather than pixels for tractability.
- ==KL trajectory-matching== derivation adds an entropy term realized as an exploration bonus (Plan2Explore for DreamerV3, RND for DrQ), weighted by beta, preventing local-optimum collapse.
- Video backbone: per-domain ==VQ-GAN== encoding 64x64 frames to 8x8 or 16x16 codes, plus a task-conditioned ==autoregressive transformer== trained with maximum likelihood on expert demonstrations.

## Results

- Near expert-level control on **15** DMC, **7** Atari, and **6** RLBench tasks without task rewards; beats single-task and multi-task AMP, and exceeds the Task Oracle on sparse-reward RLBench.
- Ablations: beta = **0** collapses policy behavior while equal weighting (beta = **1**) performs best; context length k = **16** beats k = **8** and k = **2** with diminishing returns.
- MaskGIT substitution needs up to **8** forward passes per frame (roughly **8x** slower rewards, **2.5x** slower RL) and underperforms VideoGPT; masking Atari scoreboards markedly stabilizes Pong training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Likelihood-based generative models give stabler, less mode-collapse-prone imitation rewards than adversarial discriminators, and scale naturally with generative-modeling progress.
> - Maximizing likelihood alone is wrong: typical-set matching requires an entropy bonus, so density rewards should always be paired with explicit exploration terms.
> - The reward inherits every bias the video model has: nuisance pixels (scoreboards), high frame rates, and small code budgets all silently corrupt the learning signal.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses one of the most critical and enduring challenges in reinforcement learning (RL): **reward specification**. Designing effective reward signals that allow RL agents to learn complex behaviors is notoriously difficult, time-consuming, and often leads to brittle solutions or undesirable outcomes (e.g., reward hacking). This "reward engineering" bottleneck is a major impediment to developing general-purpose, autonomous decision-making agents.

A promising avenue to overcome this bottleneck is **learning behaviors from observations or demonstrations**, particularly from unlabeled videos, which are abundant (e.g., YouTube). The broader research landscape in this area can be categorized into two main approaches:

- **Behavioral Cloning (BC) with Predicted Actions:** This involves first inferring actions from action-free expert videos using an inverse dynamics model, and then applying standard behavioral cloning or offline RL techniques. While intuitive, this approach heavily depends on the quality of action labels and the diversity of training data, often suffering from distribution shift issues between the expert data and agent rollouts. UniPi, for instance, uses text-conditioned video generation for planning, followed by inverse dynamics for execution, which is computationally expensive due to video model rollouts.
- **Reinforcement Learning with Learned Reward Functions:** Here, a reward function is learned directly from expert videos to guide policy optimization.
 - **Similarity-based Methods:** Early approaches focused on rewarding the similarity between the agent's current observations and the expert data distribution. A major limitation is their focus on static observation-level similarity, failing to capture temporally meaningful behaviors.
 - **Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL):** Methods like Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) train a discriminator to distinguish between expert trajectories and agent trajectories. The policy is then trained to "fool" the discriminator, thereby generating expert-like behavior. While powerful, AIL often suffers from **mode collapse**, where the discriminator finds spurious correlations or the policy only learns a narrow subset of expert behaviors, leading to poor generalization and unstable training. Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) is a prominent example in this category.

**VIPER (Video Prediction Rewards)** positions itself as a novel approach within the "RL with learned reward functions" paradigm, specifically tackling the limitations of existing methods by **leveraging advancements in generative video modeling**. Instead of adversarial training or simple observation-level similarity, VIPER directly uses the **log-likelihoods from a pre-trained autoregressive video prediction model** as an action-free reward signal. This strategy directly addresses:
1. **Temporal Consistency:** Video prediction models inherently capture temporal dynamics and consistency, unlike static observation-level rewards.
2. **Mode Collapse:** Likelihood-based models are generally less susceptible to mode collapse compared to adversarial discriminators, leading to more stable training and potentially better generalization.
3. **Computational Efficiency:** Evaluating likelihoods is significantly faster than performing full video model rollouts required by some inverse dynamics approaches, enabling faster RL training.

By doing so, VIPER aims to provide a more scalable, robust, and generalizable solution for reward specification from unlabeled video data, capitalizing on the rapid progress in generative AI, particularly in high-fidelity video generation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **alleviate the burden of manual reward specification in reinforcement learning** by developing a more scalable and effective method for extracting reward signals from readily available unlabeled videos.

The key motivations driving the development of VIPER are:

- **Addressing the Reward Engineering Bottleneck:** Manual design of reward functions is identified as a "major bottleneck" for developing general decision-making agents. It is laborious, prone to human error, and often leads to sub-optimal or undesirable learned behaviors.
- **Leveraging Abundant Unlabeled Video Data:** The internet provides a vast, unutilized source of information about desired behaviors in the form of videos (e.g., YouTube). The motivation is to unlock this data for RL without requiring explicit action labels or state information.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Prior Video-Based Reward Methods:**
 - **Lack of Temporal Coherence:** Previous observation-level reward methods struggled to capture the sequential, temporally meaningful aspects of behaviors. VIPER aims to inherently measure temporal consistency.
 - **Mode Collapse in Adversarial Methods:** Adversarial imitation learning (AIL) approaches, while powerful, are often unstable and prone to mode collapse, resulting in policies that exhibit only a narrow range of expert behaviors or fail to generalize. VIPER seeks a more stable training process.
 - **Computational Cost of Inverse Dynamics Models:** Approaches relying on inverse dynamics models for action labeling can be computationally expensive, requiring slow video model rollouts. VIPER aims for faster reward computation by directly using likelihoods.
- **Capitalizing on Advances in Generative Modeling:** The rapid progress in video prediction and generative modeling (e.g., autoregressive transformers, diffusion models) provides a strong foundation for accurately modeling complex video distributions. The motivation is to directly harness these powerful models for reward specification.
- **Enabling Generalization:** A key goal is for the learned rewards to generalize not only to novel situations within the training domain but also to entirely new environments or embodiments where no specific expert data is available, moving towards more generalizable reward functions.

In essence, VIPER is motivated by the need for a practical, robust, and scalable solution to reward specification that can learn complex behaviors from the wealth of unlabeled video data, by directly translating the fidelity of video prediction into an effective learning signal for RL agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VIPER (Video Prediction Rewards) is an algorithm that leverages pre-trained video prediction models to generate action-free reward signals for reinforcement learning. The core approach involves a two-stage process: first, training a generative video model on expert demonstrations, and second, using the likelihoods from this model as rewards for an RL agent.

#### 3.1. Video Modeling

The first crucial step is to train a video prediction model capable of computing likelihoods of video sequences.
- **Model Choice:** The paper primarily uses an **autoregressive transformer model** inspired by VideoGPT. This choice is motivated by its ability to model complex multi-modal distributions and its relatively fast likelihood computation.
- **Two-Stage Training:**
 1. **VQ-GAN Training:** A VQ-GAN (Vector Quantized Generative Adversarial Network) is trained to encode individual video frames (x_t) into discrete latent codes (z_t). This compression is critical for handling high-resolution video data and making subsequent transformer training tractable. Different latent sizes (8x8 for DMC, 16x16 for Atari/RLBench) are used based on visual complexity.
 2. **Autoregressive Transformer Training:** The transformer is then trained to model the distribution of these discrete latent codes (z) in an autoregressive manner, maximizing the log-likelihood of a sequence of codes. The objective is `max_theta Σ_t Σ_i log p_theta(z^i_t | z^(1:i-1)_t, z^(1:t-1))`, where `z^i_t` is the i-th code of the t-th frame.
- **Likelihood Approximation:** Since computing the exact conditional likelihoods `p_theta(x_t | x_1:t-1)` for raw pixels is intractable, VIPER uses the conditional likelihoods over the latent codes `p_theta(z_t | z_1:t-1)` as an approximation. The context length `k` for the transformer (e.g., 16 frames for DMC/Atari, 4 frames for RLBench) determines how much past information influences the prediction of the current frame's likelihood.
- **Task Conditioning:** For multi-task video models (e.g., RLBench multi-task), a one-hot task label is incorporated into the model training, allowing it to predict videos conditioned on a specific task.

#### 3.2. Reward Formulation

Given the pre-trained video prediction model, VIPER defines a reward signal for the RL agent.
- **Base VIPER Reward:** The core reward `r_VIPER_t` for a transition `(x_t, a_t, x_t+1)` is the conditional log-likelihood of the next observation `x_t+1` given the previous observations: `r_VIPER_t = ln p_theta(x_t+1 | x_1:t)`. This incentivizes the agent to follow trajectories that are highly probable under the expert video distribution.
- **Addressing Mode-Seeking Behavior:** A key insight from density modeling is that maximizing log-likelihood (mode-seeking) doesn't always lead to sampling typical behaviors from the distribution. The authors acknowledge this by linking the reward formulation to minimizing the KL-divergence between the agent's trajectory distribution `q(x_1:T)` and the expert video model's distribution `p_theta(x_1:T)`.
 - `KL(q(x_1:T) || p(x_1:T)) = E_q[-ln p_theta(x_1:T)] - H(q(x_1:T))`
 - This decomposition reveals that minimizing KL-divergence requires maximizing the cross-entropy term (`-E_q[ln p_theta(x_1:T)]`) *and* maximizing the entropy of the agent's trajectory distribution (`H(q(x_1:T))`).
- **Final Reward Function (KL-Divergence Objective):** To encourage both matching the expert distribution and sufficient exploration, the final reward `r_KL_t` is defined as:
 `r_KL_t = r_VIPER_t + beta * r_expl_t`
 where `beta` is a weighting factor and `r_expl_t` is an **exploration reward term**. This term incentivizes the agent to explore diverse sequences within the high-probability regions of the expert distribution, preventing early convergence to local optima or single modes.
- **Exploration Reward Choices:** VIPER is agnostic to the exploration reward. The paper uses:
 - **Plan2Explore:** A model-based exploration method (for DreamerV3).
 - **Random Network Distillation (RND):** A model-free exploration method (for DrQ).
- **Efficient Likelihood Computation:** For practical implementation, the conditional likelihood is approximated using a sliding window of context frames `r_VIPER_t ≈ ln p_theta(x_t+1 | x_max(1,t-k):t)`. The computation of these likelihoods is batched across multiple environments for efficiency.

#### 3.3. Data Curation

Expert videos are curated from various sources depending on the benchmark:
- **DMC & Atari:** Task Oracle RL agents are trained to convergence with ground truth rewards/state information, and videos from their top episodes are collected.
- **RLBench:** Sampling-based motion planning algorithms with full state information are used to gather demonstrations.

#### 3.4. Integration with RL Algorithms

VIPER is designed to be compatible with any RL algorithm. The paper demonstrates its effectiveness by integrating it with two popular pixel-based RL algorithms: DrQ and DreamerV3. The computed `r_KL_t` reward is provided at each environment step and stored in the replay buffer for policy optimization.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details and Ablations

The paper discusses important practical considerations:
- **Masking Scoreboards (Atari):** Masking task-irrelevant information like scoreboards improved performance, as the video model learns to predict all data, including potentially distracting OOD scores.
- **Frame Subsampling (RLBench):** Training the video model at a reduced frame rate (e.g., subsampling by factor 4) and similarly computing rewards on strided observations helped prevent the model from assigning high likelihoods to stationary trajectories, which was a problem given the high control frequency and slow initial robot movements.
- **Ablation studies** are performed to evaluate the impact of the exploration objective's weight (β), different video model architectures (VideoGPT vs. MaskGIT vs. BYOL), and context length (k) on downstream RL performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate VIPER's effectiveness across a wide range of tasks and its ability to generalize.

#### 4.1. VIPER as an Adequate Learning Signal

- **Reward Alignment:** VIPER's log-likelihood rewards (`r_VIPER`) are shown to correlate positively with ground truth task rewards. Expert trajectories receive high rewards, while random, out-of-distribution (OOD) trajectories consistently receive low rewards. This confirms that the video model provides a meaningful signal.
- **Performance Across Benchmarks:**
 - **DeepMind Control Suite (DMC - 15 tasks):** VIPER achieves near expert-level performance, closely matching the Task Oracle (which uses full state info and dense rewards). Crucially, VIPER significantly outperforms both single-task and multi-task variants of Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), highlighting VIPER's stability and resistance to mode collapse, which AMP struggled with when exposed to diverse data.
 - **Atari (7 tasks):** VIPER approaches the performance of the Task Oracle and outperforms AMP. A critical finding for Atari was that **masking the scoreboard** during video model training dramatically improved downstream RL performance, as the video model otherwise assigned noisy rewards due to its prediction of out-of-distribution scores.
 - **Robot Learning Benchmark (RLBench - 6 tasks):** VIPER surprisingly **outperforms the Task Oracle**. This is attributed to RLBench tasks having very sparse ground truth rewards, making them challenging for standard RL. VIPER provides a dense reward signal derived from expert videos, facilitating learning in these sparse-reward environments. **Subsampling video sequences** during training and reward computation (e.g., by a factor of 4) was beneficial to avoid stationary policies.

#### 4.2. Generalization of Video Prediction Rewards

- **Cross-Embodiment Generalization:** A significant finding is VIPER's ability to generalize to novel scenarios where no expert data was provided for that specific combination of robot arm and task.
 - A single task-conditioned autoregressive video model was trained on demonstrations from two different robot arms (Sawyer and Franka Panda) across 53 tasks (20 overlapping).
 - The video model demonstrated the ability to generate reasonable trajectories for unseen arm/task combinations (e.g., Franka Panda arm solving the saucepan task, for which it had no direct demos).
 - More importantly, VIPER successfully learned a policy for an OOD task (Franka arm, saucepan) without requiring specific demos for that combination, assigning higher likelihoods to expert-like OOD trajectories than random ones. This indicates that the video model learns more generalizable priors that can transfer across embodiments.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies Insights

- **Exploration Objective (β):** The exploration bonus is critical. Without it (β=0), the policy's behavior collapses. An equally weighted exploration term (β=1.0 for Plan2Explore) leads to significantly improved performance, aligning with the KL-divergence objective.
- **Video Model Choice:**
 - **VideoGPT (autoregressive):** Performed best due to its ability to model complex distributions and fast likelihood computation.
 - **MaskGIT:** Performed substantially worse, possibly due to noisier likelihoods from parallel computation and being significantly slower (8x slowdown in reward computation).
 - **BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent):** Performed moderately, but its deterministic recurrent architecture struggled with multi-modality, limiting its ability to capture temporal dynamics accurately and often leading to learned policies that remained stationary.
- **Context Length (k):** Increasing the context length `k` for the video model (how many past frames influence prediction) generally improved performance, with diminishing returns. A very short context length (e.g., k=0, unconditional likelihood) leads to myopic, often stationary, behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work on Video Prediction Rewards (VIPER) makes several significant contributions to the field of reinforcement learning and has substantial potential impact:

1. **Addresses a Core RL Bottleneck:** VIPER directly tackles the fundamental challenge of reward specification, which is a major barrier to applying RL to real-world, complex problems. By enabling reward learning from easily acquirable unlabeled video data, it significantly reduces the human effort and expertise required to set up RL tasks.
2. **Scalability and Data Efficiency:** The reliance on unlabeled videos, which are abundant, offers a highly scalable approach to training RL agents. The demonstrated cross-embodiment generalization suggests that a single video model trained on diverse (but not necessarily exhaustive) expert data could derive rewards for a multitude of tasks and agents, reducing the need for task-specific demonstrations.
3. **Leverages Advances in Generative AI:** VIPER effectively bridges state-of-the-art generative video modeling with reinforcement learning. By translating the powerful capabilities of video prediction models (especially autoregressive transformers) into an effective reward signal, it opens up a new paradigm for reward design that can directly benefit from the rapid advancements in generative AI. This positions VIPER as a "starting point for scalable reward specification from unlabeled videos that will benefit from the rapid advances in generative modeling."
4. **Improved Robustness and Stability:** Unlike adversarial imitation learning methods that frequently suffer from mode collapse and training instability, VIPER's likelihood-based approach offers more stable training and can capture a broader distribution of expert behaviors, leading to more generalizable policies. This is a critical advantage for practical deployment.
5. **Enables Learning in Sparse Reward Environments:** As demonstrated in RLBench, VIPER's ability to provide a dense, temporally consistent reward signal from expert videos can significantly outperform traditional sparse ground truth rewards, making it a valuable tool for challenging tasks where rewards are difficult to define or are only received upon completion.
6. **Towards More Autonomous Agents:** By learning directly from observations without requiring explicit programmatic rewards or expert actions, VIPER moves RL closer to systems that can learn complex skills by simply "watching" desired behaviors, mimicking human learning capabilities more closely.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- Reliance on *in-domain* expert data, which might not always be available in real-world scenarios, though the generalization experiments suggest a path forward.
- Potential for video models to minimize uncertainty in stochastic environments, which could lead to sub-optimal behaviors.
- The sensitivity to hyperparameter choices like VQCode size and context length, which impact reward quality.

Future work suggested by the authors aligns with addressing these limitations and expanding VIPER's capabilities:
- Integrating larger, pre-trained video models (e.g., text-to-video models or video diffusion models) to improve generalization and enable learning from broader, uncurated internet video data or text commands.
- Exploring fine-tuning strategies or human preferences to adapt video prediction rewards.
- Investigating alternative video model architectures (beyond autoregressive) that might better handle multi-modality and temporal dynamics (e.g., probabilistic recurrent state-space models).

In conclusion, VIPER represents a significant step towards practical and scalable reinforcement learning from observation, offering a robust and generalizable framework for learning complex behaviors without the need for laborious reward engineering, ultimately accelerating the development of capable autonomous agents.
