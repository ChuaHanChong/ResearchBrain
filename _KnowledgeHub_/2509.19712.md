# TopoCut: Learning Multi-Step Cutting with Spectral Rewards and Discrete Diffusion Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> The TopoCut framework facilitates robotic agents in performing goal-conditioned multi-step cutting of deformable objects by integrating a high-fidelity simulator, a pose-invariant spectral reward, and a discrete diffusion policy. This approach enables reliable evaluation and robust generalization across various object geometries and cutting goals, outperforming baseline methods in cut quality and accuracy.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation of deformable objects for multi-step cutting is challenging due to complex topological changes and ambiguous cutting outcomes.
- Existing evaluation metrics for cutting tasks are often sensitive to object pose and require explicit alignment, making consistent assessment difficult.
- Learning effective policies for multi-step cutting is hindered by the difficulty in perceiving dense topological changes from sparse or noisy observations.

## Method

- A high-fidelity ==MLS-MPM== simulator with particle-based damage tracking and explicit topology discovery models complex material deformations and cuts.
- A ==pose-invariant spectral reward==, based on intrinsic shape geometry, evaluates cutting quality and guides policy learning without requiring object alignment.
- A dynamics-informed perception model, combined with a conditional ==discrete diffusion policy== (**PDDP**), predicts topological evolution and generates multi-step cutting actions.

## Results

- The spectral reward reliably tracked cutting progress and accurately detected errors in multi-step slicing experiments, unlike traditional metrics which were insensitive to true shape fidelity.
- The framework autonomously generated a large dataset of approximately **5,000** labeled cuts across diverse geometries and goal types (slice, stick, dice) using MPPI planning guided by the spectral reward.
- The **PDDP** policy consistently outperformed baseline methods in cut quality (normalized spectral reward) and accuracy (cut count) across both in-distribution and novel object geometries, achieving up to **85%** test accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Intrinsic shape descriptors derived from spectral analysis provide a robust and continuous reward signal for guiding goal-conditioned cutting policies, independent of object pose.
> - A pretrained dynamics-informed perception module stabilizes policy learning and significantly enhances generalization capabilities to unseen object geometries and tasks.
> - Formulating cutting action prediction as a conditional discrete diffusion process, where actions are inferred from segmented point-wise regions, improves robustness to noise and stability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses the challenges inherent in robotic manipulation of deformable objects, specifically focusing on multi-step cutting. This domain is critical across various applications, including food processing, medical surgery, and manufacturing, where precise segmentation of objects into complex or structured shapes is often required.

Previous work in robotic cutting has explored various simulation environments, ranging from analytical and mesh-based methods to mesh-free approaches like Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) and Material Point Method (MPM). While some advancements have been made in simulating single-material deformable object cutting and incorporating differentiable damage modeling (e.g., DISECT, ROBONINJA, FLUIDLAB), multi-step, goal-conditioned cutting of complex deformable geometries continues to present significant challenges. These challenges include the ambiguity in evaluating cutting outcomes due to incomplete separations, the sensitivity of existing evaluation metrics to pose variations, and the difficulty in perceiving dense topological changes from sparse or noisy observations, which can impede policy learning.

In the realm of perception for deformable objects, research has moved from inferring hidden structures through interactive contacts and analyzing articulation from motion to multimodal sensing and active shape reconstruction. However, predicting future topological states and generating compact, task-relevant embeddings for policy learning from sparse visual input remains an active area of development.

Regarding deformable object manipulation, approaches have historically involved physics-centric models, adaptive online correction pipelines, and imitation learning. More recently, end-to-end dynamics learning combined with sampling-based planners or diffusion policies has gained traction. TopoCut builds upon and extends these efforts by integrating a high-fidelity simulation environment, a novel pose-invariant evaluation metric, and a dynamics-informed perception module with a discrete diffusion policy to address the specific complexities of multi-step topological changes during cutting. The work aims to provide a unified framework that overcomes the limitations of existing methods by focusing on robust topology tracking and generalizable policy learning in a goal-conditioned setting.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robotic agents to segment deformable objects into goal-specified shapes through precise, sequential cutting actions. This overarching goal is motivated by the prevalence of multi-step cutting tasks in real-world applications, such as culinary automation (slicing ingredients), robotic surgery (multi-incision procedures), and industrial processes (multi-pass segmentation), where fine-grained precision, efficiency, safety, and quality are essential.

The authors identify several specific challenges that motivate the development of TopoCut:
- **Complex Topological Behaviors:** Deformable objects undergo significant topological changes during cutting, which are difficult to model and track accurately in a simulation environment. The potential for objects to fail to separate cleanly after a cut complicates the evaluation of outcomes.
- **Difficulty in Perceiving Dense Object States:** Sparse or noisy observations in real-world settings make it challenging for robotic systems to accurately perceive the dense topological state of a deformable object, hindering effective policy learning.
- **Lack of Efficient Evaluation Methods for Cutting Outcomes:** Existing evaluation metrics for cutting outcomes often require explicit alignment and are sensitive to variations in object pose, making consistent and robust assessment challenging. This limits their utility for guiding goal-conditioned policy learning.
- **Need for Goal-Conditioned Multi-Step Cutting:** While progress has been made in robotic cutting for single cuts or fixed trajectories, reliably executing sequences of actions to achieve a specific target shape (goal-conditioned multi-step cutting) remains largely unsolved.

To address these challenges, TopoCut is introduced as a unified framework structured around three core components:
1. **High-fidelity Simulation and Topology Discovery:** To accurately model and track topological changes resulting from sequential cuts.
2. **Pose-invariant Spectral Reward:** To provide a consistent and robust method for evaluating cutting quality and outcomes, independent of object pose or fragment alignment.
3. **Dynamics-informed Policy Learning:** To develop a scalable and generalizable policy that can predict and execute multi-step cutting actions by effectively processing sparse visual input and adapting to topological evolution.

The motivation is to overcome the limitations of prior work by integrating these components into a cohesive system capable of generating, evaluating, and learning complex cutting behaviors for deformable objects.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TopoCut framework integrates a high-fidelity simulation environment, a robust evaluation metric, and a goal-conditioned policy learning pipeline.

#### 3.1. High-fidelity Simulation and Topology Discovery

- **Simulation Environment:** The environment extends FluidLab, utilizing its Moving Least Squares Material Point Method (MLS-MPM) implementation in Taichi. MLS-MPM is selected for its capability to model continuum materials with high fidelity, large deformations, and complex material behaviors. The simulator incorporates cutting-specific features, including compression/stretch-based damage tracking, von Mises plasticity with progressive softening, and surface adherence modeling. Objects are typically represented as multi-material bodies with a plasto-elastic core and a softer von Mises outer skin to facilitate both fracture and flow. A 6-degree-of-freedom (DoF) knife agent performs cutting actions, supported by a teleoperation interface for data collection.
- **Particle-Based Damage Tracking:** The method tracks damage at the particle level within the MLS-MPM framework. A particle's deformation gradient $F_p$ is monitored, and damage is classified if the volumetric Jacobian $J_p = \text{det}(F_p)$ falls below $(1 - \epsilon_c)^m$ or exceeds $(1 + \epsilon_s)^m$, where $\epsilon_c$ and $\epsilon_s$ are critical compression/stretch thresholds and $m$ is a material sensitivity parameter. For von Mises materials, damage also occurs when yielding stress drops below a critical threshold.
- **Topology Discovery:** Upon detecting damaged particles, the knife trajectory is tracked and carved into the object's Signed Distance Field (SDF) to enforce spatial separation. The SDF is defined as $\text{SDF}(x) = \min_{p \in P} \|x - x_p\| - r_p$. The zero-iso-surface is then extracted using Marching Cubes, followed by Laplacian smoothing. The resulting mesh is segmented into connected components. Particles are assigned cluster identities based on proximity to these reconstructed surfaces, maintaining an explicit topological representation throughout the simulation.

#### 3.2. Task Evaluation: Pose-invariant Spectral Reward

- **Pose-Invariant Shape Evaluation via Spectral Analysis:** To evaluate cut fragments against goal shapes without requiring explicit alignment, a spectral reward based on intrinsic shape geometry is introduced. Given a point cloud $X$, a k-nearest neighbor graph is constructed with Gaussian-weighted edges. The combinatorial Laplacian $L = D - W$ (where $D$ is the degree matrix) is then computed. Solving the eigenproblem $L\Phi = \Lambda\Phi$ yields spectral descriptors (eigenvalues $\{\lambda_k\}$ and eigenvectors $\{\phi_k\}$) that are invariant to rigid motions.
- **Spectral Distance and Reward Formulation:** The spectral distance between two shapes $X$ and $Y$ is defined as $d_{\text{spec}}(X, Y) = \alpha\|\Lambda_X - \Lambda_Y\|_2^2 + \beta\|\Phi_X^\top\Phi_X - \Phi_Y^\top\Phi_Y\|_F^2$. This distance is mapped to a reward $R_{\text{spec}}(X, Y) = \kappa \cdot \max(0, C - \gamma d_{\text{spec}}(X, Y))$, where $\gamma$ controls decay, $\kappa$ is a normalization factor, and $C$ is a constant. For multi-fragment objects, rewards are summed over all fragment pairs.
- **Data Generation:** Expert demonstrations are collected using two methods: (i) Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planning, guided by the spectral reward function to sample and re-weight noisy knife trajectories, and (ii) manual 6-DoF teleoperation. Each generated trajectory is replayed in simulation to label particle damage and compute rewards. The dataset consists of tuples (topo$_t$, $a_t$, topo$_{t+1}$, $g$), encompassing diverse geometries, scales, poses, and cutting goals (slice, stick, dice).

#### 3.3. Dynamics-informed Policy Learning

- **Dynamics-informed Perception Model Training:** A perception model $F: (\text{topo}_t, a_t) \rightarrow \text{topo}_{t+1}$ is trained to predict the topological evolution. $\text{topo}_t$ and $a_t$ are represented as particle clouds with cluster labels $f_i$ and binary segmentation masks $g_j$, respectively. The input clouds are downsampled via Farthest Point Sampling (FPS). A topology graph (connecting k-nearest neighbors with the same cluster label) and an action graph (connecting points with the same cut mask) are constructed. A shared perception encoder $\Phi$ independently embeds these graphs, and a Graph Transformer $T$ fuses these embeddings to predict the next topological state. Training uses a combined geometric loss (Chamfer, Earth Mover's, Hausdorff distances) and a Hungarian matching loss on cluster assignments. This module is designed to process raw depth point clouds, facilitating sim2real transfer.
- **PDDP: Particle-based Score-Entropy Discrete Diffusion Policy:** A goal-conditioned behavior cloning (BC) policy is trained using the pretrained perception encoder $\Phi$. The policy takes the current topological observation $\text{topo}_t$, an action history $a_{\text{hist}}$, and a goal point cloud $g$ as input. Point-wise embeddings $z_t = \Phi(X_t, F_t)$ and $z_g = \text{Encoder}(g)$ are computed. Cutting actions are formulated as per-point binary labels $a_t^* \in \{0, 1\}^N$. Inspired by Score-Entropy discrete diffusion, action prediction is modeled as a conditional discrete diffusion process. Clean labels $a_t^*$ are progressively noised into $\tilde{a}_t$, and the policy network $s_\theta$ is trained to predict the score function $\nabla_{\tilde{a}_t} \log q_t(a_t^* | \tilde{a}_t)$, conditioned on action history, current object embedding, and goal embedding. The policy is optimized by minimizing the Denoising Score Entropy (DSE) loss. After denoising, the predicted segmentation identifies cuttable regions, and a Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithm fits a cutting plane to reconstruct the robot's knife pose.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TopoCut involved benchmarking the reliability of the spectral reward and assessing the performance and generalization capabilities of the learned policies.

#### 4.1. Benchmark Evaluation

- **Q1: Reliability of Spectral Reward:** A teleoperated four-step slicing experiment demonstrated the reliability of the spectral reward. The reward steadily increased during successful cuts that matched the target shape and sharply decreased after a deliberately incorrect cut. In contrast, traditional metrics like Chamfer Distance (CD), Earth Mover's Distance (EMD), and Hausdorff Distance (HD) continued to rise even after the erroneous cut, indicating their insensitivity to true shape fidelity or their dependency on object pose. The spectral reward also correctly resumed increasing when a failed cut was corrected, confirming its robustness and sensitivity to geometric improvements. Appendix 7.7 further demonstrated the pose-invariance of the spectral reward across various rotations of ideal stick fragments.
- **Q2: Scalability for Data Generation:** The spectral reward, combined with MPPI planning, facilitated the autonomous generation of a large dataset. This dataset comprised 1,000 trajectories, with approximately five cuts each, spanning three goal types (slice, stick, dice) and ten diverse object geometries. This process yielded a dataset of approximately 5,000 labeled cuts, capturing a wide range of shapes, poses, and cut targets for policy training. The ability of the spectral reward to guide MPPI was also demonstrated in a pyramid cutting task (Appendix 7.2), indicating its generalization to more complex and arbitrary goal shapes.

#### 4.2. Policy Evaluation

- **Experimental Setup:** Policies were evaluated in the MLS-MPM simulator over deterministic rollouts of five sequential cuts per episode. A total of 405 episodes were conducted across nine object geometries (five seen during training, four novel) and three goal types (slice, stick, dice). Object pose (translation, yaw) and scale were randomized to test robustness.
- **Baselines:** The proposed method (PDDP) was compared against a 3D diffusion model (DP3), a standard point cloud diffusion model (3D Diffuser Actor), a heuristic controller, and human teleoperation. Different perception configurations (no perception, frozen pretrained graph encoder Φ, self-attention GraphConv encoder) were also tested.
- **Metrics:** Performance was assessed using two metrics: (1) normalized spectral reward ($\hat{R}$), which quantifies cut quality relative to human teleoperation scores, and (2) cut count ($N_C$), representing the number of correctly segmented and goal-consistent pieces.

- **Q3: Impact of Policy Model and Perception Architecture:** PDDP consistently outperformed all baselines across both in-distribution and out-of-distribution geometries and tasks. PDDP's approach of segmenting point-wise regions and then inferring actions via an SVM (as opposed to direct action prediction) contributed to improved stability and noise tolerance, achieving up to 85% test accuracy. The use of a frozen, pretrained perception encoder was found to stabilize learning and significantly enhance generalization, particularly on unseen geometries. Policies trained without this pretrained encoder tended to overfit and showed degraded performance on novel shapes. The segmentation-based transformer within PDDP also helped maintain spatial consistency, addressing disorganized particle states after poor cuts.

- **Q4: Generalization Across Geometries and Goal Shapes:** The framework demonstrated generalization to unseen objects and goals, maintaining high normalized spectral rewards and accurate cut counts. This generalization capability was attributed to the diverse demonstration data and the pretrained dynamics-informed perception module. The stick task generally yielded the lowest scores across all methods, which was hypothesized to be due to its heavy reliance on global geometry; irregular shapes made clean stick cuts more challenging. Cube-like shapes produced more valid stick and dice cuts compared to irregular or donut-like geometries, especially under the strict reward threshold. These findings emphasize the importance of structured perception and geometry-aware learning for complex cutting tasks.

#### 4.3. Additional Manipulation Tasks (Appendix)

The appendix describes additional tasks such as Cream Writing and Ice-Cream Scooping, demonstrating the extensibility of the MLS-MPM simulator, topology discovery, and spectral reward function to a broader range of deformable object manipulation scenarios beyond multi-step cutting. The Pyramid Cutting Task further highlighted the spectral reward's ability to guide MPPI planning for intricate, non-standard geometric goals, suggesting its adaptability for sophisticated sculpting-like applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TopoCut presents a unified and comprehensive platform designed for multi-step robotic cutting of deformable objects. Its key contributions—a high-fidelity particle simulator with robust topology tracking, a pose-invariant spectral reward for evaluating cut quality, and a dynamics-informed perception module supporting a conditional score-based diffusion policy—address critical challenges in this domain.

The demonstrated capabilities indicate several areas of significance and potential impact:
- **Advancement in Deformable Object Manipulation:** TopoCut provides a framework that explicitly handles complex topological changes, a long-standing challenge in deformable object manipulation. By integrating sophisticated simulation with perception and control, it pushes the boundaries of what autonomous robots can achieve with compliant materials.
- **Robust and Generalizable Policy Learning:** The combination of dynamics-informed perception and a discrete diffusion policy enables the learning of cutting strategies that exhibit strong generalization across diverse object geometries, materials, scales, poses, and cutting goals, including unseen scenarios. This enhances the adaptability and robustness of robotic systems in varied environments.
- **Efficient and Consistent Evaluation:** The introduction of a pose-invariant spectral reward offers a robust, continuous, and efficient method for evaluating cutting outcomes. This overcomes the limitations of traditional, pose-sensitive metrics, enabling consistent assessment and effective guidance for policy learning and data generation.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** TopoCut establishes a new benchmark for robotic cutting, providing tools and methods that can be leveraged by the research community. The modular nature of the framework allows for further extensions in simulation fidelity, perception capabilities, and policy learning algorithms.

The potential impact extends to several real-world applications:
- **Industrial Robotics:** Automation of manufacturing processes involving cutting, such as material processing or component segmentation.
- **Medical Robotics:** Enhancing precision and autonomy in surgical procedures requiring multi-incision or tissue segmentation tasks.
- **Food Processing:** Improving efficiency and consistency in culinary automation, such as slicing and dicing ingredients.

Despite its advancements, the paper acknowledges limitations, including the computational bottleneck of warp mesh reconstruction, the current restriction of evaluation to exact shape matching, and challenges in simulating highly realistic scenarios with stiff materials due to MPM resolution constraints. Addressing these limitations in future work could further amplify TopoCut's impact.
