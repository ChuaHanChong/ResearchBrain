# Torch.manual_seed(3407) is all you need: On the influence of random seeds in deep learning architectures for computer vision

> [!summary] Summary
> David Picard's study empirically quantifies the impact of random seed selection on deep learning model accuracy in computer vision, revealing that performance differences up to 1.82% on CIFAR-10 and approximately 0.5% on ImageNet can be attributed solely to the choice of seed. This work highlights how common experimental practices may lead to inflated reported results by inadvertently selecting "lucky" seeds, advocating for greater statistical rigor in research.

## Problem

- Deep learning experiments involve multiple sources of randomness (initialization, data augmentation, batch sampling, optimization), leading to variability in results.
- It is common practice in deep learning, particularly computer vision, to report model performance based on a single experimental run.
- This practice can lead to an overstatement of performance gains and undermines the statistical validity and reproducibility of reported results.

## Method

- The study empirically investigated the impact of ==random seed== selection by conducting extensive experiments on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet datasets.
- For CIFAR-10, a custom ResNet was trained with up to 10,000 different seeds to characterize the distribution of scores and identify extreme outliers.
- For ImageNet, pre-trained ResNet50 and Vision Transformer models were fine-tuned, with only the classification layer initialized randomly, and tested across 50 seeds to assess variability in large-scale settings.

## Results

- On CIFAR-10, a custom ResNet trained with 10,000 different random seeds showed a range of **1.82%** in final validation accuracy (from **89.01%** to **90.83%**).
- On ImageNet, fine-tuning pre-trained models with 50 different seeds resulted in accuracy ranges of approximately **0.44%** for Supervised ResNet50, **0.37%** for SSL ResNet50, and **0.46%** for SSL ViT.
- The findings suggest that performance differences often deemed significant enough for publication can arise purely from random seed selection, challenging the robustness of many reported deep learning benchmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Even after models converge, seed-dependent performance differences persist and do not diminish, indicating they are intrinsic to the final state.
> - "Black swan" random seeds exist, leading to radically different results (e.g., 1.82% accuracy range on CIFAR-10), a margin often considered a publishable improvement.
> - While pretraining on large datasets reduces seed-induced variability, the remaining differences (e.g., approximately 0.5% on ImageNet) are still widely considered significant in the computer vision community.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a significant and often overlooked issue in the empirical evaluation of deep learning models: the statistical validity and reproducibility of reported results. In the fast-paced field of deep learning, particularly computer vision, it has become common practice to report the performance of models based on a single experimental run. This trend, historically stemming from computational limitations, persists even as computing power increases, leading to a potential overstatement of performance gains.

The broader research landscape acknowledges that deep learning experiments inherently involve multiple sources of randomness, including random initialization of network weights, stochastic data augmentation, random batch sampling, and the stochastic nature of optimization algorithms (e.g., Stochastic Gradient Descent). Previous work, such as that by Mehrer et al. (2020), has shown that these random factors measurably affect the resulting neural networks. More directly related, Bouthillier et al. (2021) conducted a study on accounting for variance in machine learning benchmarks across different tasks, including computer vision. However, their study was limited to smaller datasets and a maximum of 200 runs.

Picard's work extends and reinforces these concerns by focusing specifically on the cumulative effect of all random factors tied to the random seed. By scanning a much larger number of seeds (up to 10,000) and venturing into large-scale datasets (ImageNet) with pre-trained models, the paper aims to provide more robust empirical evidence regarding the extent of variability. It fills a critical gap by systematically quantifying the "noise" introduced by random seeds, questioning the validity of small performance differences often celebrated as significant advancements in publications. The paper implicitly criticizes the "score-driven" culture in deep learning research, where marginal improvements in accuracy can be the primary determinant for publication in highly selective venues, without sufficient scrutiny of their statistical robustness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this paper is to empirically investigate the impact of random seed selection on the accuracy of popular deep learning architectures in computer vision. The author frames this investigation around three specific, clearly articulated questions:

1. **What is the distribution of scores with respect to the choice of seed?** This question aims to characterize the spread and shape of performance outcomes when only the random seed is varied, providing insight into how reliably a single run represents the model's true potential.
2. **Are there black swans, i.e., seeds that produce radically different results?** This probes whether extreme outliers—seeds yielding exceptionally good or bad performance—exist, which could inadvertently be discovered and reported as significant findings.
3. **Does pretraining on larger datasets mitigate variability induced by the choice of seed?** This crucial question explores whether the common practice of using pre-trained models on large datasets, often seen as a way to improve stability and performance, reduces the sensitivity to random seeds.

The motivation behind these questions is rooted in the author's concern regarding the lack of statistical validity in current deep learning publication practices. With the increasing size and complexity of deep learning models and datasets, it has become standard to report only a single experimental run. The author argues that this practice, while understandable given past computational constraints, is problematic because known random factors (initialization, data stochasticity, optimization) introduce variability. The paper posits that researchers, through common trial-and-error practices in hyperparameter tuning and model architecture search, might inadvertently be engaging in an "implicit scan of seeds," thereby increasing the likelihood of discovering and reporting a "lucky" seed's performance rather than the model's average capability. This potentially leads to overstated results and undermines fair comparison among different methods, creating a misleading impression of progress in the field. The author's strong belief is that understanding the influence of these random factors on evaluation metrics is paramount for the scientific integrity of deep learning research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The author adopted a rigorous empirical approach within a predefined computational budget of 1000 V100 GPU hours, split equally between hyperparameter tuning and actual experimentation. All code and results were made publicly available to ensure reproducibility.

**A. CIFAR-10 Experiments:**
To answer the first two questions, experiments were conducted on the CIFAR-10 dataset using a custom 9-layer ResNet architecture, inspired by the DAWN benchmark for fast training.
- **"Short training" setup:** Focused on scanning a massive number of seeds. 10,000 seeds were tested, with each training run lasting 30 seconds. This setup consumed approximately 83 V100 hours.
- **"Long training" setup:** A subset of 500 seeds was trained for a longer duration (1 minute per run) to ensure models reached convergence. This setup, totaling about 8 hours, allowed the author to observe if variability reduced upon convergence.
- **Training Details:** Simple SGD optimizer with momentum and weight decay. Loss function combined cross-entropy with label-smoothing and regular categorical cross-entropy. Learning rate scheduling involved a short linear ramp-up followed by a longer linear ramp-down.

**B. ImageNet Experiments:**
To address the third question regarding the impact of pretraining on large datasets, experiments were performed on ImageNet. Due to the high computational cost, models were not trained from scratch; instead, pre-trained networks were used, with only the classification layer initialized randomly and then the entire network fine-tuned.
- **Architectures Tested:**
 - Supervised ResNet50 (standard PyTorch pre-trained model).
 - Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) ResNet50, pre-trained with DINO.
 - Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) Vision Transformer (ViT), pre-trained with DINO.
- **Seed Count:** 50 seeds were tested for each architecture.
- **Training Details:** Simple SGD with momentum and weight decay. Cosine annealing learning rate scheduling was optimized for decent accuracy within budget. The linear classifier was trained for one epoch, followed by fine-tuning of the entire network.
- **Computational Cost:** Supervised ResNet50 (100 hours total), SSL ResNet50 (155 hours total), SSL ViT (185 hours total), summing to approximately 440 V100 hours for ImageNet experiments.

**C. Limitations (as acknowledged by the author):**
The author explicitly discusses several limitations affecting the generalizability of the conclusions:
- **Sub-optimal Accuracy:** The models' achieved accuracies were not state-of-the-art due to the budget constraints preventing longer training times. The author counters this by noting that CIFAR-10 results are comparable to 2016 SOTA, and ImageNet results are close to original DINO paper benchmarks, which also acknowledged hyperparameter sensitivity.
- **Variability Reduction at SOTA:** It's a possibility that variations might decrease with more accurate architectures or longer, highly optimized training setups, though the author's convergence study on CIFAR-10 suggests otherwise.
- **ImageNet Pretraining Bias:** The ImageNet experiments starting from the same pre-trained model inherently *underestimate* the total variability, as the effect of the seed is limited only to the classification layer initialization and the optimization process, not the entire network's initial state.
- **Limited ImageNet Seeds:** Only 50 seeds were used for ImageNet, which might not fully capture the distribution of results, potentially underestimating the true range of variability.

Despite these limitations, the author argues that the observed variations are still significant relative to what the computer vision community considers a meaningful improvement, making the findings relevant regardless of absolute SOTA performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's findings are presented in response to the three core questions, supported by quantitative data and distribution plots.

**A. Q1: Distribution of scores with respect to the choice of seed?**
- **CIFAR-10 (Long training, 500 seeds):** The distribution of final validation accuracies was found to be mono-modal, fairly concentrated, and "pointy" (leptokurtic). The mean accuracy was 90.70% with a standard deviation of 0.20%. The minimum observed accuracy was 90.14%, and the maximum was 91.41%.
- **Convergence Stability:** Crucially, the analysis of accuracy over epochs (Figure 1) showed that while accuracy converged around epoch 25, the range of variation (minimum to maximum, represented by the light red area) did *not* reduce after convergence. This indicates that the seed-dependent differences in performance are intrinsic to the final converged state, rather than being a transient effect that would disappear with longer training.
- **Implication:** This suggests that even a seemingly small difference of 0.5% in accuracy (e.g., between 90.5% and 91.0%) on CIFAR-10 could be entirely attributable to the random seed choice, rather than a genuine model or method improvement.

**B. Q2: Are there black swans (radically different results)?**
- **CIFAR-10 (Short training, 10,000 seeds):** By scanning a much larger number of seeds, the study found a broader range of results. The mean accuracy was slightly lower at 90.02% (standard deviation 0.23%), likely due to shorter training.
- **Existence of Black Swans:** The most significant finding here was the range of accuracies obtained: from a minimum of 89.01% to a maximum of 90.83%. This constitutes a difference of 1.82% between the worst and best performing seeds.
- **Implication:** The author highlights that a 1.82% difference is "widely considered as significant in the community" and often serves as an argument for publication in selective venues. The study unequivocally demonstrates that such a difference can arise purely from selecting a "lucky" or "cursed" random seed, confirming the existence of "black swans" that can skew reported performance.

**C. Q3: Does pretraining on larger datasets mitigate variability?**
- **ImageNet (50 seeds per model):**
 - **Supervised ResNet50:** Mean 75.48% ± 0.10%; Min 75.25%, Max 75.69% (range: 0.44%).
 - **SSL ResNet50:** Mean 75.15% ± 0.08%; Min 74.98%, Max 75.35% (range: 0.37%).
 - **SSL ViT:** Mean 76.83% ± 0.11%; Min 76.63%, Max 77.09% (range: 0.46%).
- **Mitigation:** Yes, the standard deviations and the maximum-minimum ranges are significantly smaller compared to the CIFAR-10 experiments, indicating that pretraining on larger datasets and fine-tuning does reduce the variability induced by seed choice.
- **Still Significant Variability:** However, the author argues that a ~0.5% difference in accuracy on ImageNet is still "widely considered significant" for the computer vision community.
- **Implication:** While reduced, the variability due to random seeds remains impactful enough to potentially misrepresent actual model improvements. This is particularly concerning given that ImageNet experiments often start from the same pre-trained model, meaning the observed variability is only from the final layer initialization and optimization, not the entire pretraining process. The author expresses undermined confidence in the robustness of recent results, especially when different initializations (e.g., different pre-trained models) or a larger scan of seeds could reveal even greater variability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of David Picard's study have significant implications for the practices and scientific integrity of deep learning research, particularly in computer vision.

**Core Argument and Impact:**
The paper's central argument is that many reported results in computer vision may be overstated due to "implicit seed selection." This occurs not necessarily through deliberate manipulation, but through the common experimental practice of trial-and-error hyperparameter tuning, model architecture search, and the "evolutionary pressure" exerted by peer review, which naturally favors models achieving higher reported scores. Researchers might unknowingly converge on a "lucky" seed that yields a deceptively high performance, leading to claims of significant advancements that are, in fact, artifacts of randomness.

**Impact on Reproducibility and Trust:**
This work directly contributes to the ongoing discussion about the reproducibility crisis in machine learning. If small, publishable performance gains can be explained purely by the choice of a random seed, it undermines trust in reported benchmarks and makes it exceedingly difficult to objectively compare and validate different methods. The title itself, "torch.manual_seed(3407) is all you need," serves as a sardonic critique, implying that a single, fixed seed is emphatically *not* enough for robust scientific claims.

**Recommendations for the Community:**
The author proposes two key actions to address this issue:
1. **More Robust Empirical Studies:** A call for larger-scale, dedicated studies that scan a much wider range of seeds (10,000 to 100,000) on state-of-the-art models trained from scratch on large datasets. Such studies would provide crucial empirical data to establish what constitutes a truly "significant" improvement versus what falls within the expected range of random variability.
2. **Increased Rigor in Experimental Setup for Authors:** A strong recommendation for aspiring authors to proactively conduct "randomness studies" as part of their research. This involves systematically varying random seeds (and ideally, dataset splits) and reporting not just a single score, but the average, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum performance metrics. This practice would provide a more complete and statistically valid picture of a model's true capabilities and the robustness of the reported results.

In conclusion, David Picard's paper serves as a timely and crucial reminder of the importance of statistical rigor in deep learning research. It provides empirical evidence that even seemingly small performance differences, often deemed publishable, can be attributed to random chance rather than genuine methodological advancements. The paper's lasting impact lies in its strong advocacy for more responsible and statistically sound experimental practices, pushing the computer vision community towards greater transparency, reproducibility, and a deeper understanding of the inherent stochasticity in deep learning models.
