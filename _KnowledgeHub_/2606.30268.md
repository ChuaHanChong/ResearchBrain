# ConCent: Contact-Centric Real-to-Sim-to-Real Learning from One Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> ConCent is a real-to-sim-to-real reinforcement learning framework designed for contact-rich robot manipulation that utilizes a single real-world demonstration. It achieves an 80.0% success rate on a challenging shape sorter insertion task by explicitly aligning simulated local contact dynamics with real observations and enforcing demonstrated contact event sequences, alongside a Virtual Collision Penalty (VCP) mechanism that enhances training scalability by 1.3x.

## Problem

- Existing robot manipulation methods require extensive and costly real-world data collection, or struggle with the "sim-to-real gap" in contact-rich tasks.
- Sim-to-real transfer policies often exploit unrealistic contact dynamics in simulation, leading to failures in real-world deployment.
- Manually designing effective reward functions for complex, contact-rich reinforcement learning tasks is difficult and labor-intensive.

## Method

- **ConCent** optimizes simulation ==contact geometry== to precisely match the local contact dynamics observed in a single real-world demonstration.
- It extracts a structured ==contact event sequence== from the demonstration, which serves as a physically validated reward signal for reinforcement learning.
- A ==Virtual Collision Penalty (VCP)== mechanism is introduced to selectively process only task-relevant contacts, significantly reducing computational cost and enabling scalable parallel RL training.

## Results

- ConCent achieved an **80.0%** real-world success rate on a tight-clearance shape sorter insertion task, significantly outperforming ablations (e.g., **20.0%** without contact geometry optimization).
- Contact geometry optimization reduced the mean block position error in simulation from **13.1 mm** to **9.1 mm**, accurately reproducing the demonstrated insertion behavior.
- The Virtual Collision Penalty (VCP) mechanism provided approximately **1.3 times** faster throughput at **256 parallel environments** compared to full collision processing, maintaining stability at scales where traditional methods diverged.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robust sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich tasks relies on accurately reproducing task-relevant local contact dynamics and their temporal sequences, rather than achieving perfect global physics fidelity.
> - A single real-world demonstration can provide sufficient structured physical information to ground simulation and automatically derive a precise, physically plausible reward for RL.
> - Computational efficiency in contact-rich simulations can be drastically improved by prioritizing and fully simulating only essential contact points, while penalizing proximity for non-essential ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation is actively pursuing methods to scale policy learning, with a notable dichotomy between approaches relying on extensive real-world data collection and those leveraging simulation for policy training. While large-scale real-world data collection has demonstrated capabilities, it presents significant challenges regarding data acquisition cost, time, and the need for repeated collection across diverse tasks, scenes, or robot configurations.

In response, sim-to-real policy transfer has emerged as an appealing alternative, aiming to train policies efficiently in simulation and deploy them directly in the real world without additional real-world data. However, a significant obstacle to reliable sim-to-real transfer is the "sim-to-real gap," particularly pronounced in contact-rich manipulation tasks. In these scenarios, subtle inaccuracies in contact dynamics between the simulated and real environments can lead to drastically different object behaviors and task outcomes, causing simulation-trained policies to exploit unrealistic contact modes and fail in reality.

Prior work has attempted to bridge this gap through various strategies. These include physical parameter identification to match global physics properties and domain randomization to make policies robust to variations. While effective in certain contexts, these methods often fall short in contact-rich tasks because success hinges not on global physical parameters, but on precise local contact behavior—specifically, the spatio-temporal pattern of contact events and the local dynamics induced at each contact.

Other real-to-sim approaches focus on improving visual, geometric, and even physics-ready scene reconstruction. However, these generally optimize for scene-level fidelity rather than explicitly identifying and utilizing task-relevant contact structures crucial for policy learning in manipulation. Similarly, systems like RialTo and Digital Cousins construct simulation environments from real scenes, but primarily target scene-level visual or geometric fidelity, not the fine-grained contact interactions.

Some research has explored extracting contact information from demonstrations. Approaches like those that interpret demonstrations as contact modes or constraint sequences, or use differentiable physics to optimize contact points. While philosophically similar, their objectives often lie in compliant execution, planning, or model-based action generation, rather than using inferred contact structure as a direct learning objective for reinforcement learning (RL). ContactGaussian-WM optimizes collision geometry for learned forward prediction within a custom differentiable engine, in contrast to ConCent's focus on general-purpose, non-differentiable simulators.

Demonstration-guided RL and inverse RL also use demonstrations, but often suffer from reward ambiguity or lack physical grounding. More recent methods like X-Sim and Human2Sim2Robot extract dense object pose trajectories as continuous tracking rewards. The limitation of these pose-tracking methods for contact-rich tasks is their contact-agnostic nature; they reward object position regardless of the contact mechanics, which can lead to physically distinct and non-transferable contact modes.

This work addresses the specific gap in contact-centric fidelity. It proposes a framework that shifts the focus from global physics matching or general pose tracking to explicitly learning and reproducing the task-relevant local contact dynamics and contact event sequences observed in a single real-world demonstration. By grounding the simulator in these detailed contact observations, ConCent aims to constrain policy learning to physically plausible interaction regimes, thereby enhancing sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and data-efficient real-to-sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL) framework for contact-rich robot manipulation tasks. The core motivation stems from the limitations of existing approaches to robot learning:

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Real-World Manipulation:** Traditional methods often require vast amounts of high-quality real-world data, which is expensive and time-consuming to collect, especially when tasks, scenes, or robot configurations change. The framework aims to circumvent this by leveraging simulation, requiring only a single real-world demonstration.

2. **Overcoming the Sim-to-Real Gap for Contact-Rich Tasks:** While sim-to-real policy transfer is a promising paradigm, it faces significant challenges, particularly in tasks where interaction is heavily mediated by contact. Minute discrepancies in contact dynamics between simulation and reality can lead to policies that exploit unrealistic behaviors in simulation and fail when deployed in the real world. This work is motivated by the need for policies that are robust to these discrepancies.

3. **Prioritizing Task-Relevant Contact Fidelity:** The authors posit that for successful manipulation, what truly matters is not the faithful reproduction of all physical phenomena or global physics parameters, but rather the accurate reproduction of "contact-centric fidelity." This includes two critical aspects:
 - **Local Contact Dynamics:** How forces and motions evolve at each specific contact point.
 - **Contact Event Sequence:** The precise temporal and spatial pattern of contacts—when, where, and how contacts occur between the robot, object, and environment.
 Existing methods often overlook these nuanced contact aspects, focusing instead on global physics or visual similarity.

4. **Enabling Automatic Reward Design:** Manually designing effective reward functions for RL, especially for complex, contact-rich tasks, is notoriously difficult and labor-intensive. The research aims to automatically extract a structured reward signal from a real demonstration, removing the need for per-task human engineering and promoting broader applicability.

5. **Improving Computational Efficiency of Contact Modeling:** Accurately modeling fine-grained contacts in simulation can be computationally expensive, particularly when objects are represented by numerous primitives. A further objective is to develop a computational optimization that maintains the fidelity of task-relevant contacts while reducing the overall simulation cost, making large-scale RL training practical.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Ground simulation in the physically plausible interaction regimes observed in a single real demonstration.
- Extract a contact event sequence from this demonstration to serve as a structured, physically validated learning objective for RL.
- Optimize object contact geometry in simulation to ensure that local contact dynamics accurately reflect reality.
- Train a closed-loop, contact-rich policy in simulation that is constrained by these real-world validated contact dynamics, preventing the exploitation of unrealistic simulator artifacts.
- Facilitate efficient training through a selective collision processing mechanism.
- Distill the trained policy into a deployable vision-language-action (VLA) model for real-world execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ConCent framework employs a real-to-sim-to-real pipeline that transforms a single real demonstration into a deployable robotic policy. This pipeline comprises three main phases: Real → Sim Contact Optimization, Contact-Centric RL, and Sim → Real Transfer.

#### 3.1. Bridging the Contact Physics Gap via Demonstration-Extracted Contact Events

This phase physically grounds the simulator by extracting a structured description of task-relevant contact events from a real demonstration.

##### 3.1.1. Contact Geometry Optimization

1. **Object Representation**: Objects are approximated as a set of contact primitives (e.g., spheres), which serve as collision geometries within the simulator and possess local physical properties such as position, mass, and friction coefficient.
2. **Data Extraction**: From a single real RGB-D demonstration, object point cloud tracks ($V = \{v_j\}^M_{j=1}$) are extracted over time using tools like SAM2 for segmentation and TAPIR for point tracking.
3. **Optimization Objective**: The goal is to find an optimal set of contact primitives ($C^*$) whose configuration in simulation best explains the observed object point cloud track. This is achieved by minimizing the Chamfer distance between the simulated point cloud track ($f(a_{1:t}; C)$) and the observed real track ($V_t$), where $a_{1:t}$ are the replayed demonstrated actions.
4. **Optimization Process**: A sampling-based evolutionary strategy (ES) is employed for optimization. This iterative process involves replaying the demonstration in simulation and updating the primitive configuration $C$. Crucially, localized deformation masks are used, perturbing only primitives near observed contacts (along with k-nearest neighbors) to preserve global shape while refining local collision surfaces. Perturbations are smoothed using Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernels for spatial coherence. Elite candidates (those minimizing Chamfer distance) are selected, and their mean position is updated via an RBF-smoothed exponential moving average. The resulting $C^*$ defines the contact geometry for subsequent simulation.

##### 3.1.2. Contact Event Sequence Extraction

1. **Replay and Track**: With the optimized contact geometry $C^*$, the real demonstration is replayed in simulation. During this replay, contacts between robot links and contact primitives, as well as between primitives themselves, are tracked at each timestep.
2. **Sequence Definition**: For each contact, the occurrence time and involved entities (robot link or primitive ID) are recorded. This forms the "contact event sequence" $E = (e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_S)$, where $S$ is the number of stages determined by the demonstration's contact-phase transitions (e.g., picking, aligning, inserting). Each event $e_s$ contains the set of robot link and primitive IDs in contact for that stage. This sequence provides a deterministic and structured representation of the physically plausible contact structure from the real demonstration.

#### 3.2. Sim-to-Real Policy Transfer via Contact-Centric RL

This phase uses the extracted contact event sequence to train a reinforcement learning policy.

##### 3.2.1. Contact-Centric Policy Optimization

1. **RL Algorithm and Environment**: The policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) implemented in Brax, a differentiable physics engine for rigid body simulation.
2. **Observation Space**: The observation includes robot state (joint encodings, velocities, end-effector pose, gripper opening), object states (position, 6D rotation for objects O1 and O2), and crucial *per-contact-pair features*. These features include a binary mask for active contacts, contact point coordinates, displacement vector to the target, approach direction, and target surface normal for up to 8 active contact slots.
3. **Action Space**: The action consists of 7-DoF delta-joint commands for the robot arm and a gripper command.
4. **Reward Function**: The total reward $r_t$ is a weighted sum of three terms:
 - **Reaching Term ($r^{\text{reach}}_t$)**: An exponential reward $exp(-\max_i d^{(i)}_t / \sigma)$ that drives active contact pairs together. It uses a two-scale exponential shaping (far and near) to provide dense long-range gradients and sharpen precision near contact. Using the maximum distance across active pairs encourages closing all demonstrated contacts.
 - **Regularization Term ($r^{\text{reg}}_t$)**: Penalizes large actions, high joint velocities, and unnecessary pushing or rotating of manipulated objects, promoting smooth and efficient control.
 - **Virtual Collision Penalty Term ($r^{\text{vcp}}_t$)**: Detailed below.
5. **Exploration Strategy**: To facilitate exploration and robustness, initial states are diversified by sampling poses from the demonstration while preserving the current contact chain, inspired by OmniReset.

##### 3.2.2. Virtual Collision Penalty (VCP): Pruning Collisions for Scalable RL

1. **Problem**: Representing objects with numerous primitives for fine-grained contact dynamics leads to a high computational cost for collision detection and force resolution, dominating training time.
2. **Solution**: The VCP mechanism leverages the extracted contact event sequence $E$. Only the primitives explicitly identified in $E$ as having participated in real contacts are instantiated as full physics bodies in the simulator, undergoing expensive collision detection and contact force computation.
3. **Non-Essential Primitives**: The remaining primitives, which were not observed to participate in real contacts during the demonstration, are omitted from the physics solver entirely. Instead, a distance-based reward penalty is imposed if these non-collidable primitives approach or penetrate target meshes. This distance-based computation is highly parallelizable on GPUs and avoids expensive contact force calculations, allowing for significant speedup and scalability.

#### 3.3. Sim-to-Real Deployment via VLA Distillation

1. **Hybrid Rendering Pipeline**: To bridge the appearance gap between simulation and reality, a photorealistic rendering pipeline is employed.
 - **Objects**: 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is used for rendering objects, as it requires explicit representations compatible with the physics simulator. Primitives for simulation are sampled from the 3DGS model using Farthest Point Sampling.
 - **Robot and Background**: A U-Net-based flow-matching generative model renders the robot and background. This model is conditioned on the current simulation render and a past real image, capturing non-parameterizable appearance factors like shadows and cable deformations. For efficiency, a low-resolution flow matching is combined with a separate super-resolution network. Shadow augmentation is applied to objects to account for interactions not captured by flow matching.
2. **VLA Distillation**: A Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model (FLOWER) is trained for real-world deployment. Synthetic data is generated by running rollouts from the trained RL policy in simulation and applying the hybrid rendering pipeline to produce photorealistic observations. This synthetic data is then used to train the VLA model, enabling deployment without additional real-world data collection for the VLA itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of ConCent was evaluated on a contact-rich shape sorter insertion task, which involves picking a 40 mm block and inserting it into a 42 mm square hole, providing a tight 2 mm clearance. Task success is highly sensitive to contact fidelity during insertion.

#### 4.1. Q1: Real-World Manipulation Performance of ConCent

To assess ConCent's real-world transfer capabilities and the contribution of its components, it was compared against three ablations on 20 distinct initial block and box poses:
- **ConCent**: Full proposed method.
- **w/o Contact Geometry Optimization**: Uses initial, unoptimized contact geometry.
- **w/o Contact-Event Reward**: Replaces the contact-event-based reward with a hand-designed, geometry-based insertion reward.
- **Unconstrained RL**: Removes both contact geometry optimization and uses the hand-designed reward.

All conditions shared the same pick and align policy (100% success), with only the insertion stage ablated.

**Results (Table 1):**
- **ConCent**: Achieved an 80.0% end-to-end insertion success rate (16/20).
- **w/o Contact Geometry Optimization**: 20.0% success (4/20).
- **w/o Contact-Event Reward**: 50.0% success (10/20).
- **Unconstrained RL**: 30.0% success (6/20).

**Interpretation:**
ConCent demonstrated reliable real-world transfer for the precision insertion task. Both contact-centric components, contact geometry optimization and the contact-event reward, were found to be essential. The contact-event reward's effectiveness was substantially enhanced when paired with optimized contact geometry, indicating that accurate contact anchors are crucial for the reward signal. Qualitatively, ConCent allowed the block to remain aligned and slide smoothly into the hole, whereas ablations often led to "wrong contacts" causing the block to jam against the hole edge and fail (Figure 4).

#### 4.2. Q2: How the Two Contact-Centric Components Work

Further analysis investigated the specific contributions of contact geometry optimization and the contact-event reward.

##### 4.2.1. Contact Geometry Optimization Aligns Local Contact Dynamics

- **Methodology**: The block's position error between simulation and real observation was measured throughout the insertion phase, comparing before and after contact geometry optimization (Eq. 1) by replaying recorded robot actions.
- **Results (Figure 5a, b)**: Before optimization, the mean block position error was 13.1 mm, and the block failed to seat in simulation. After optimization, the mean error dropped to 9.1 mm, and the simulation accurately reproduced the demonstrated insertion, with the block seating correctly.
- **Interpretation**: The optimization successfully aligned the simulated local contact dynamics with the real demonstration, specifically for task-relevant relative contact between the block and the hole, which is critical for real-world insertion success.

##### 4.2.2. The Contact-Event Reward Imposes the Demonstrated Contact Schedule

- **Methodology**: The contact schedule was visualized by replaying the demonstration through the optimized contact geometry ($C^*$).
- **Results (Figure 6)**: The replay directly yielded a staged sequence of contact events ($E$), which serves as the precise, demonstration-grounded learning objective.
- **Interpretation**: The contact-event reward constrains the policy to explore physically valid interaction regimes by enforcing this demonstrated contact schedule. This explains why replacing it with a hand-designed reward leads to degraded performance, as such rewards typically cannot capture the exact nuances of the real-world contact schedule.

In conclusion for Q2, the two components are complementary: contact geometry optimization ensures the fidelity of the simulated local contact dynamics, and the contact-event reward directs the policy to reproduce the demonstrated sequence of these faithful contacts.

#### 4.3. Q3: Scalability of VCP

The scalability of the Virtual Collision Penalty (VCP) was tested by comparing its throughput against a "FULL" physical contact model where all 512 surface spheres were active MuJoCo collision geometries. VCP, in contrast, used only 78 primitives identified in $E$ for full physics interaction, handling the rest with an analytic virtual contact penalty.

**Results (Figure 7):**
- **VCP**: Demonstrated steady scaling with the number of parallel environments, achieving approximately 1.3 times faster throughput than FULL at 256 environments. VCP remained stable and effective at all tested scales.
- **FULL**: Was stable only at 256 parallel environments. At 512 or more environments, the simultaneous contacts exceeded the fixed contact buffer capacity of the GPU-batched simulator, leading to dropped contacts and training divergence (observed in 3 out of 3 seeds).

**Interpretation:**
VCP effectively enables collision avoidance learning at a significantly lower computational cost. By selectively applying full collision processing only to task-relevant primitives, it circumvents the limitations of fixed contact buffers in GPU-batched simulators, making large-scale, massively parallel RL training tractable for contact-rich tasks without sacrificing fidelity where it matters.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, ConCent, that demonstrates a novel approach to achieving robust sim-to-real policy transfer for contact-rich robot manipulation tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Data Efficiency**: ConCent offers a data-efficient pathway for robot learning by relying on a single real-world demonstration rather than large-scale real data collection or extensive imitation learning datasets. This significantly reduces the cost and time associated with deploying robotic systems for new tasks.

2. **Addressing the Contact-Rich Sim-to-Real Gap**: The work identifies and explicitly addresses the critical problem of discrepancies in local contact dynamics, which often causes failures in transferring policies trained in simulation to the real world for contact-rich tasks. By optimizing contact geometry to match demonstrated interactions and using the extracted contact event sequence as a structured reward, the framework effectively constrains policy learning to physically plausible and transferable contact regimes.

3. **Automatic Reward Design**: The ability to automatically derive a structured reward signal from a real demonstration eliminates the need for manual, per-task reward engineering, a historically challenging aspect of reinforcement learning. This could accelerate the development and deployment of RL policies for a wider range of complex manipulation tasks.

4. **Computational Scalability**: The introduction of the Virtual Collision Penalty (VCP) is a significant practical contribution. By selectively applying full collision processing only to task-relevant primitives, VCP makes the simulation of fine-grained contact dynamics computationally tractable and scalable, enabling parallel training in environments where traditional full-contact models would fail or be prohibitively expensive.

5. **Focus on Task-Relevant Physics**: The core insight that task success depends on reproducing *task-relevant local contact dynamics* and *contact event sequences*, rather than achieving perfect global physics fidelity, provides a new paradigm for sim-to-real research. This targeted approach may be more efficient and effective for manipulation compared to general-purpose physics or visual matching.

6. **Foundation for Advanced Manipulation**: The inherent manipulator-agnostic nature of contact-centric constraints suggests a promising extension to more complex manipulation scenarios, such as dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands, which is identified as future work.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
- **Generalization Scope**: The current evaluation is confined to a single rigid-body insertion task using a parallel-jaw gripper. Future work is needed to validate the framework's generalization to a wider range of contact-rich tasks, including multi-step assembly, manipulation of deformable objects, or multi-fingered grasping.
- **Efficiency of Optimization**: There is potential for more efficient approaches to contact geometry optimization and in-simulation object representation.
- **VLA Distillation Bottleneck**: The current real-world deployment relies on a VLA distillation strategy that requires upfront rendering and data curation, which could become a bottleneck. Investigating more direct and efficient ways to deploy the trained RL policy in the real world is a recognized area for future research.

Overall, ConCent provides a meaningful step towards making robot manipulation more scalable, robust, and accessible by effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap for tasks requiring precise contact interactions with minimal real-world data input.
