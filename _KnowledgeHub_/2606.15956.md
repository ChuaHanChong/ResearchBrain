# You Don't Need Strong Assumptions: Visual Representation Learning via Temporal Differences

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UIUC and NYU propose Temporal Difference in Vision (TDV), a self-supervised method for learning visual representations from video by predicting future frame embeddings from past frames and learned motion. This approach, which avoids strong inductive biases, demonstrates improved performance on dense spatial tasks like optical flow and stereo depth compared to existing techniques like DINO and iBOT.

## Problem

- Existing self-supervised visual representation learning methods depend on strong inductive biases, such as image augmentations, masking, or contrastive assumptions.
- These strong biases can limit performance scaling as data volume increases, with empirical evidence suggesting weaker biases are more effective at larger scales.
- Many current approaches, by enforcing invariances or focusing on semantic tasks, may inadvertently discard fine-grained spatial and temporal information relevant for dense prediction tasks.

## Method

- Introduces ==Temporal Difference in Vision== (**TDV**), a self-supervised framework that leverages the causal principle of "past predicts future" from video streams.
- Employs a student frame encoder and a motion encoder, conditioned on the current frame, to additively compose a prediction for the next frame's embedding.
- Integrates a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss for temporal prediction with a DINO-like self-distillation loss to prevent representation collapse, without relying on traditional image augmentations or masking.

## Results

- TDV surpasses DINO and iBOT in optical flow estimation, achieving **9.84** EPE (clean) on MPI-Sintel for ViT-S models, outperforming iBOT's **11.31** EPE.
- The method also achieves lower bad pixel rates for stereo depth, with **39.70** bad@1px on SceneFlow (ViT-S) compared to iBOT's **44.91** bad@1px, indicating fewer large correspondence errors.
- TDV provides competitive performance on semantic segmentation tasks, such as **10.54** mIoU on ADE20K (ViT-S), while showing lower performance on strictly semantic classification benchmarks due to its design focus on spatio-temporal properties rather than semantic invariance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal strength of inductive biases in visual representation learning empirically decreases as the dataset size expands, favoring approaches with fewer hard-coded assumptions.
> - The fundamental causal principle of predicting the immediate future from the past offers a weak yet universally valid inductive bias for learning visual representations from video.
> - Factorizing video learning into frame content and motion in the latent space allows for explicit modeling of temporal dynamics, which is crucial for tasks requiring fine-grained spatial and temporal understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions its work within the context of a long-standing trend in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and deep learning: the progressive shift towards methods that rely on fewer or weaker inductive biases. This trend suggests that as computational resources and data availability increase, approaches with minimal hard-coded assumptions tend to achieve superior asymptotic performance.

In the specific domain of visual representation learning, this evolution is evident. The field has progressed from supervised learning, which relies heavily on human-annotated labels, through weakly supervised learning, to the widespread adoption of self-supervised learning (SSL) techniques that eliminate the need for human labels. Examples include early convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for classification, contrastive learning methods like SimCLR and MoCo, and self-distillation approaches such as DINO and iBOT, often combined with Vision Transformers (ViTs). Each step in this progression has generally involved relaxing a stronger inductive bias (e.g., human labels, negative pair assumptions, spatial locality of CNNs).

However, the authors argue that even modern state-of-the-art self-supervised learning methods still incorporate strong inductive biases. These include reliance on image augmentations (like cropping, rotation, color jitter), masking strategies, or contrastive objectives that make assumptions about the dissimilarity of randomly sampled images. The paper empirically supports the claim that the optimal strength of such inductive biases decreases as data scale increases, suggesting these biases could become performance bottlenecks at larger scales.

Temporal Difference in Vision (TDV) is introduced as a novel paradigm for self-supervised learning from video that aims to entirely avoid these prevalent inductive biases. Instead of relying on image-level augmentations or masking, TDV leverages a fundamental causal assumption: that the past precedes and predicts the future. This approach explicitly models temporal changes in video data, contrasting with existing methods that either apply invariances to augmentations (discarding information) or combine temporal signals with other strong biases (e.g., supervision, attention recalibration, augmentation-based invariance). By seeking to learn representations from temporal differences alone, TDV attempts to push the boundary of visual representation learning towards even weaker, domain-agnostic assumptions. The paper also references biological intelligence, where more capable species rely less on innate behaviors (analogous to hardcoded inductive biases) and more on learned behavior, reinforcing the philosophical underpinning of their approach.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel method for visual representation learning that operates with significantly weaker inductive biases compared to existing self-supervised learning approaches. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Observational Trend in AI Progress:** The authors highlight a persistent trend in AI and deep learning: progress has largely been driven by methods that make fewer, weaker assumptions about the data. As computing power and data availability have increased, approaches with minimal inductive biases have consistently outperformed those with stronger, more constrained assumptions. This principle suggests that current strong inductive biases in visual representation learning might become bottlenecks at scale.

2. **Empirical Confirmation of Scaling Bottlenecks:** To further motivate their work, the authors conduct controlled experiments. They evaluate how the optimal strength of inductive biases (using masking ratio as a proxy) changes with data scale (subsets of ImageNet-1k). Their findings indicate that as the amount of training data increases, weaker inductive biases (lower masking ratios) lead to asymptotically better performance. This empirical evidence reinforces the idea that minimizing assumptions is crucial for scaling.

3. **Limitations of Existing Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) Biases:** The paper argues that even state-of-the-art SSL methods, while reducing reliance on human labels, still depend on "strong" inductive biases. These include:
 - **Augmentations:** Such as cropping, rotating, or color jitter, which implicitly assume that transformed views of an image should have invariant representations. This can discard task-relevant information (e.g., exact position, color details).
 - **Masking:** Encouraging similarity between an image and its heavily masked version, potentially collapsing semantically distinct inputs or reducing spatial awareness.
 - **Contrastive Objectives:** Assuming random image pairs are dissimilar, which might not hold true in natural distributions, and requiring large batches of negative samples.
 - **Raw Pixel Reconstruction:** Requiring representations to encode unnecessary detail for perception.
 - **Cross-Modal Alignment:** Tying visual representations to the biases of accompanying modalities (e.g., text emphasizing objects over geometry).
 The authors illustrate these limitations by showing that progressively removing DINO's standard augmentations leads to significant performance degradation and eventual representation collapse.

4. **Search for a Weak, Universal Assumption:** Given the limitations of existing biases, the central motivation becomes finding an alternative assumption that is "exactly correct" rather than "approximately correct," thereby avoiding bottlenecks at scale. The authors propose **causality** – the principle that the past predicts the immediate future – as such a weak and domain-agnostic assumption. This principle is fundamental in physics and is also believed to underlie the success of autoregressive models like Large Language Models.

5. **Leveraging Video for Causal Learning:** Since causality is inherently temporal, the motivation naturally shifts from static image datasets to video data. Videos provide the sequential temporal structure necessary to apply the causal principle effectively, by learning from consecutive frames.

In summary, the key objective is to pioneer a new visual representation learning paradigm, TDV, that bypasses existing strong inductive biases by harnessing the universal principle of causality from video data. The ultimate goal is to lay the groundwork for a more scalable and generalizable approach to representation learning that is less constrained by hand-crafted or domain-specific assumptions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Temporal Difference in Vision (TDV) approach is designed to learn visual representations from video data by leveraging the causal principle that the past predicts the future, while avoiding strong inductive biases common in existing self-supervised learning methods.

#### 3.1. TDV Intuition

The core idea is to train an image encoder and a motion encoder such that the representation of a current frame, when combined with a learned representation of the change between frames, accurately predicts the representation of the subsequent frame. This is conceptually analogous to temporal difference learning in reinforcement learning. The authors argue that unlike augmentations or masking, the causal assumption is "exactly correct" in the sense that next-frame prediction imposes no invariance constraint, thus not forcing the encoder to discard potentially useful information.

#### 3.2. TDV Architecture

The TDV architecture consists of three main components: a student frame encoder, a motion encoder, and a teacher frame encoder.

- **Frame Encoder (f_θ):** This component maps an input RGB frame, `x_t`, into a sequence of token embeddings, `z_t ∈ R^(n×D)`. Here, `n` represents the number of spatial patches plus a `[CLS]` token, and `D` is the embedding dimension. The frame encoder is typically a Vision Transformer (ViT-S or ViT-B).

- **Motion Encoder (m_φ):** This encoder is responsible for capturing the temporal change between consecutive frames. It takes the raw RGB pixel difference, `Δx_t = x_t+1 - x_t`, as input. This `Δx_t` is inherently lower-rank than full frames, as most background pixels remain static. The motion encoder outputs a latent motion shift, `Δz_t`, which represents the change in the representation space. Crucially, `m_φ` is conditioned on the current frame's embedding, `z_t`, via cross-attention. This conditioning helps ground the motion prediction in the semantic context of the current frame, allowing the motion encoder to distinguish between pixel-level changes that carry different semantic meanings based on the visual context.

- **Additive Latent Composition:** The predicted representation of the next frame, `ˆz_t+1`, is obtained by adding the current frame's embedding and the encoded motion: `ˆz_t+1 = z_t + Δz_t`. This additive decomposition explicitly separates the content of a frame from how that content evolves over time.

- **Preventing Collapse (Teacher-Student Framework):** To prevent the model from trivially collapsing all representations to a constant (which would yield near-zero MSE loss but meaningless features), TDV adopts a teacher-student framework inspired by DINO.
 - A "teacher" frame encoder, `f_bar`, is maintained as an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the student frame encoder's parameters.
 - The teacher `f_bar` encodes the *true* next frame, `x_t+1`, to produce the target representation `z_bar_t+1`.
 - Stop-gradients are applied to the teacher's output, `sg(z_bar_t+1)`, ensuring that gradients from the loss function only update the student frame encoder and the motion encoder, not the teacher.

#### 3.3. TDV Training Objective

The training objective combines two losses:

1. **Temporal Prediction Loss (L_mse):** This is a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss that directly enforces the causal principle. It measures the difference between the predicted next-frame embedding `ˆz_t+1` and the teacher-encoded target `sg(z_bar_t+1)`:
 `L_mse = ||ˆz_t+1 - sg(z_bar_t+1)||^2_2`.
 This loss encourages the motion encoder to accurately predict the temporal evolution of representations.

2. **Self-Distillation Loss (L_dino):** This cross-entropy loss, similar to DINO, addresses the collapse problem by encouraging discriminative representations. It operates between the student's and teacher's projection distributions (after passing embeddings through respective projection heads). This loss is applied over *all* tokens ([CLS] token and patch tokens), promoting spatially consistent representations. The teacher's distribution `p_t` is also centered with a running mean to prevent dimensional collapse.
 `L_dino = - Σ_k p^(k)_t log p^(k)_s`, where `k` indexes prototype dimensions.

The total training objective is a weighted combination of these two losses:
`L = λ_mse L_mse + λ_dino L_dino`, where `λ_mse` and `λ_dino` are tunable hyperparameters.

#### 3.4. Pretraining and Evaluation Setup

- **Pretraining Dataset:** SomethingSomethingV2 (SSV2) is used for pretraining all models (TDV, DINO, iBOT) due to its defined motion data.
- **Architectures:** ViT-S and ViT-B are employed across all comparative models.
- **Hyperparameters:** Common hyperparameters (batch size, optimizer, learning rate schedule, EMA momentum) are kept consistent across methods where possible. TDV-specific hyperparameters like `λ_mse` and `λ_dino` are tuned.
- **Data and Temporal Sampling:** TDV samples two temporally adjacent frames from a video clip at a fixed stride. No traditional image augmentations (cropping, color jitter, horizontal flip, masking) are applied to the input frames for TDV, adhering to its core principle of avoiding strong inductive biases.
- **Downstream Evaluations:** The paper focuses on "dorsal stream" tasks that require spatial and temporal understanding, such as semantic segmentation (ADE20K, Cityscapes), optical flow (MPI-Sintel, SceneFlow), and stereo depth (SceneFlow). Semantic benchmarks (ImageNet KNN, SSV2 action recognition) are also reported but are not the primary focus, as TDV is not explicitly designed for semantic invariance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several findings, both in terms of motivating the TDV approach and evaluating its performance against established self-supervised learning (SSL) methods.

#### 4.1. Motivation for Weaker Assumptions

- **Empirical Trend:** Experiments on ImageNet-1k subsets demonstrate a clear trend: as the data scale increases, the optimal strength of inductive biases (represented by masking ratio in iBOT-style pretraining) decreases. Specifically, 50% masking performs best with limited data (0.1% ImageNet), but 30% masking overtakes it at 100% data, with 10% masking showing a similar upward trajectory. This confirms the hypothesis that weaker assumptions become more beneficial at scale.
- **DINO Ablations:** Removing standard DINO augmentations (e.g., local crops, color jitter) progressively degrades performance and eventually causes representation collapse, highlighting the reliance of existing methods on these biases. TDV, by contrast, avoids collapse without these.

#### 4.2. Downstream Task Performance

The evaluations primarily focus on "dense spatial tasks" (semantic segmentation, optical flow, stereo depth) which demand retention of spatial structure and temporal correspondence, properties often suppressed by strong semantic priors but preserved by TDV.

- **Semantic Segmentation (Table 2, Figure 4):**
 - TDV achieves competitive results compared to DINO and iBOT on ADE20K and Cityscapes datasets. For ViT-S, TDV scores 10.54 mIoU on ADE20K (vs. 10.71 DINO) and 37.54 mIoU on Cityscapes (vs. 39.85 DINO). Similar competitive trends are observed for ViT-B.
 - Qualitative results (Figure 4) show TDV produces broader region extents but sometimes slightly worse boundary separation compared to DINO/iBOT. This suggests TDV learns spatially coherent features that segmentation heads can leverage.
 - **Attention Maps (Figure A.1):** TDV's `[CLS]` token attention maps are less object-focused than DINO/iBOT, which is expected given its lack of explicit semantic objectives or local cropping.
 - **PCA Visualizations (Figure B.1):** Patch-level features from TDV show clean, spatially coherent maps that align with object boundaries, often surpassing DINO and iBOT in local structure, supporting its performance on dense prediction tasks.

- **Optical Flow (Table 3, Figure 5):**
 - TDV consistently *outperforms* DINO and iBOT on the Endpoint Error (EPE) metric across MPI-Sintel and SceneFlow datasets for both ViT-S and ViT-B architectures. For ViT-S, TDV achieves 9.84 EPE (clean) and 10.75 EPE (final) on MPI-Sintel, compared to 11.31/11.27 for iBOT and 13.03/12.92 for DINO.
 - This is attributed to TDV explicitly learning how representations evolve between frames, which naturally preserves local motion structure, unlike methods that enforce invariance to augmentations.
 - Qualitative results (Figure 5) show TDV producing locally consistent flow.

- **Stereo Depth (Table 3):**
 - TDV achieves lower "bad" pixel rates (at 0.5px and 1px thresholds) on SceneFlow (final) compared to DINO and iBOT. For ViT-S, TDV's bad@0.5px is 56.89 (vs. 65.51 iBOT) and bad@1px is 39.70 (vs. 44.91 iBOT). This indicates TDV makes significantly fewer large correspondence errors.
 - However, TDV exhibits a slightly higher average disparity error (4.25 for ViT-S vs. 3.50 for iBOT), suggesting it may struggle with precise depth estimation in ambiguous regions where semantic context might be beneficial.

- **Semantic Benchmarks (Table B.1):**
 - On semantic tasks like ImageNet-1k KNN Top-5 accuracy and SSV2 action recognition Top-5 accuracy, TDV lags behind DINO and iBOT. For ViT-B, TDV achieves 17.05% KNN (vs. 40.89% DINO) and 10.10% action recognition (vs. 20.50% DINO).
 - The authors attribute this to TDV's deliberate lack of inductive biases for learning invariances (such as local/global crops), which are designed to enhance semantic understanding in other models.

#### 4.3. TDV Ablation Studies (Table 4)

- **Critical Components:** Removing the motion encoder or the MSE loss causes training to collapse, identifying them as essential for TDV's stability and performance. The motion encoder provides the temporal signal, and the MSE loss supervises this signal.
- **Performance Contributors:** Including the `[CLS]` token in cross-attention for the motion encoder and applying the DINO loss on the `[CLS]` token both contribute meaningfully to performance, suggesting the importance of grounding motion predictions in a global scene representation.
- **Anti-Collapse Mechanisms:** Centering in the teacher's output distribution is found to be more critical than temperature sharpening for preventing dimensional collapse.
- **Positional Encodings:** Standard absolute positional encodings outperform RoPE (Rotary Positional Embedding).

#### 4.4. Limitations Acknowledged by Authors

- TDV does not achieve state-of-the-art across all benchmarks, particularly in semantic tasks.
- Scaling TDV to larger video datasets (Ego4D, FineVideo) or combining multiple datasets did not improve performance, which is attributed to data quality issues (noisy motion, scene cuts) and hyperparameter tuning for SSV2. This suggests further work is needed on data robustness and hyperparameter generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Temporal Difference in Vision (TDV) carries several significant implications and potential impacts for the field of visual representation learning and beyond:

1. **Advancement in Weak Inductive Biases:** TDV represents a notable step in the ongoing quest for self-supervised learning methods with progressively weaker inductive biases. By demonstrating competitive performance on dense spatial tasks without relying on common techniques such as augmentations, masking, contrastive objectives, or pixel-space reconstruction, TDV challenges the necessity of these biases for learning effective representations. This provides empirical evidence for the philosophical argument that as data and compute scale, weaker assumptions are more beneficial.

2. **Foundation for Scalable Representation Learning:** The authors propose that deep learning approaches with weaker inductive biases tend to scale more favorably with increasing compute and data. If this holds true for TDV, it lays a groundwork for future representation learning systems that could achieve stronger asymptotic performance than current methods, which might eventually be bottlenecked by their inherent biases. This could unlock new levels of performance as computational resources continue to grow.

3. **Modality-Agnostic Learning:** A key aspect of TDV is its reliance solely on the principle of causality and temporal consistency, rather than vision-specific techniques. This suggests that the TDV paradigm could be generalized and applied to a wider array of modalities that exhibit strong temporal consistency, such as audio, proprioception (body movement), and touch. This broad applicability could foster cross-modal representation learning with a unified underlying principle.

4. **Efficient Video Encoding and Processing:** TDV's design, which factorizes video content into a frame representation and a motion representation, has practical implications for video processing. Modern video codecs (e.g., MPEG) exploit temporal redundancy by storing keyframes and inter-frame deltas. TDV mirrors this concept in the latent space: after encoding an initial frame with the full frame encoder, subsequent frames can be represented by composing the previous frame's representation with a lightweight motion encoder. This approach could lead to more efficient video encoding and decoding, reducing computational load and storage requirements.

5. **Alignment with Biological Vision Theories:** The methodology of TDV, particularly its MSE objective for next-frame prediction and its decomposition into frame and motion encoders, aligns with several established theories in biological vision, such such as predictive coding and the dorsal/ventral stream dissociation in the primate brain. The additive composition also resonates with the temporal straightening hypothesis. This neuroscientific alignment suggests that TDV might be learning representations in a manner consistent with how biological systems process visual information, potentially offering insights into both artificial and natural intelligence.

In conclusion, TDV contributes a novel, less constrained approach to self-supervised visual representation learning. While acknowledging its current limitations in certain semantic tasks and scaling to highly diverse datasets, its foundational principles suggest significant potential for advancing scalable, generalizable, and efficient AI systems across various modalities.
