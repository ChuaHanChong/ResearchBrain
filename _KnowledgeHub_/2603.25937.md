# Can Vision Foundation Models Navigate? Zero-Shot Real-World Evaluation and Lessons Learned

> [!summary] Summary
> Polytechnique Montreal researchers conducted a comprehensive real-world evaluation of five state-of-the-art visual navigation models across two robot platforms and five diverse environments, revealing systematic limitations in geometric understanding, goal discrimination, and robustness to distribution shifts. The study found that simpler architectures can sometimes match or outperform more complex models, challenging assumptions about architectural sophistication.

## Problem

- Existing evaluation methodologies for Visual Navigation Models (VNMs) predominantly rely on a single metric (success rate), providing limited insight into critical aspects like trajectory quality, collision behavior, and efficiency.
- The extent and limits of VNM generalization to novel or dynamic real-world physical environments, particularly under distribution shifts, remain underexplored with appropriate metrics.
- A lack of understanding exists regarding whether increasing architectural complexity in VNMs (e.g., Transformer- or diffusion-based models) truly translates to improved real-world navigation performance.

## Method

- A comprehensive real-world evaluation setup was implemented, testing five state-of-the-art ==Visual Navigation Models (VNMs)== (**GNM**, **ViNT**, **NoMaD**, **NaviBridger**, **CrossFormer**) across two distinct robotic platforms (tracked and quadruped robots) and five diverse real-world environments, spanning ==feedforward CNN==, ==Transformer decoder==, and ==diffusion policy== backbones.
- An enhanced set of evaluation metrics was introduced, combining path-based metrics (path length, distance to goal, collision occurrence, ==topological node error==) with vision-specific metrics (==LPIPS==, ==DSSIM==, ==PSNR==) for a holistic assessment.
- Robustness to ==distribution shift== was assessed by testing in out-of-distribution environments (e.g., snowy terrain) and under controlled ==visual perturbations== (motion blur, sunflare) applied to topological maps.

## Results

- Tested VNMs exhibited frequent collision failures, with models like CrossFormer, NoMaD, and NaviBridger showing **100%** collision rates in specific trials in the Corridor environment, indicating a fundamental lack of geometric understanding.
- Models struggled with goal discrimination in visually ambiguous environments, for example, NoMaD and ViNT in the Arena environment exhibited high path lengths but consistent failure to reach the intended goal with high average distances to goal (e.g., NoMaD **7.97m**).
- Diffusion-based models demonstrated fragility under distribution shifts, while GNM consistently maintained strong performance in the out-of-distribution Snow environment, achieving the lowest goal distance and robust visual metrics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Architectural complexity alone does not guarantee superior real-world performance; simpler models like GNM often performed comparably or even outperformed more complex Transformer and diffusion-based models in certain scenarios.
> - Current VNM training datasets are likely insufficient in scale and diversity, particularly lacking examples of obstacles, collision recovery, and rare scenarios, which limits geometric reasoning and contributes to goal prediction confusion.
> - Repeated visual features in indoor environments can pose a greater challenge to VNM encoders than certain weather-induced distribution shifts, as indicated by perceptual metrics sometimes improving in outdoor snowy conditions compared to indoors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual Navigation Models (VNMs) represent a significant advancement in robotics, enabling robots to navigate diverse environments using only camera images, without reliance on precomputed maps or expensive sensors. This approach addresses limitations of traditional navigation methods, which typically assume static worlds and struggle with scalability in dynamic or large-scale settings. VNMs leverage large-scale visual demonstration datasets and imitation learning to develop policies that generalize to novel environments.

The field has seen a rapid increase in the complexity and deployment of these models, with architectures evolving from simpler feedforward networks (like GNM) to more sophisticated Transformer-based (ViNT, CrossFormer) and diffusion-based models (NoMaD, NaviBridger). These models are positioned as "foundation models" for visual navigation, promising zero-shot generalization across various robot embodiments and environments, and are seeing deployment in real-world applications, including warehouse automation and space exploration.

Despite this progress and increasing deployment, the existing evaluation methodologies for VNMs are identified as a critical gap. Current evaluations predominantly rely on a single metric: success rate. This metric indicates whether a robot reached its goal but provides limited insight into critical aspects of navigation performance, such as trajectory quality, collision behavior, localization precision, efficiency, or the underlying reasons for success or failure. This gap hinders a comprehensive understanding of how these advanced models perform in diverse real-world conditions, their robustness to environmental changes, and their ability to generalize reliably. This work directly addresses this by proposing and implementing a more exhaustive evaluation framework in real-world settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to provide a comprehensive, real-world evaluation of state-of-the-art Visual Navigation Models (VNMs) that extends beyond the conventional success rate metric. The authors aim to uncover the systematic limitations of current VNMs in practical deployment, thereby bridging the existing evaluation gap in the field.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations:

- **Inadequacy of Current Evaluation Metrics:** The prevailing reliance on success rate as the sole evaluation metric for VNMs is considered insufficient. While success rate indicates whether a robot reached its goal, it fails to reveal crucial performance dimensions such as the quality of the trajectory taken, the frequency of collisions, the precision of localization, the efficiency of navigation, and the underlying reasons for model failures. This limited insight makes it challenging to understand how well models generalize, diagnose failure modes, or predict their performance in novel or dynamic real-world scenarios.
- **Growing Real-World Deployment:** With foundation models for visual navigation increasingly being deployed on physical robots across various applications—from industrial settings to exploratory missions—a thorough understanding of their real-world performance is imperative. Without detailed evaluations, it is difficult to guarantee safety, reliability, and robustness in critical applications.
- **Unclear Generalization Capabilities:** VNMs are often touted for their ability to generalize to novel environments and embodiments through learning from large-scale datasets. However, the extent and limits of this generalization in actual physical environments, particularly under distribution shifts (e.g., changes in lighting, weather, or scene structure), remain underexplored with appropriate metrics.
- **Impact of Architectural Complexity:** The field has seen an evolution towards more complex VNM architectures, including Transformer-based and diffusion-based models. A key motivation is to assess whether this architectural sophistication translates into tangible improvements in real-world navigation performance, particularly concerning geometric understanding, goal discrimination, and robustness, or if other factors like data quality and quantity play a more dominant role.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines two main contributions:

1. **A comprehensive real-world evaluation setup:** This involves testing five state-of-the-art VNMs across two distinct robotic platforms and five diverse real-world environments. The evaluation specifically includes conditions designed to assess robustness, such as familiar settings, controlled distribution shifts (via image perturbations like motion blur and sunflare), and out-of-distribution environments (e.g., snowy terrain). The authors commit to releasing the evaluation dataset to foster reproducible benchmarking.
2. **Introduction of enhanced evaluation metrics:** Beyond success rate, the research integrates a combination of path-based metrics (e.g., path length, distance to goal, collision occurrence, topological node error) and vision-specific metrics (e.g., LPIPS, DSSIM, PSNR for goal image recognition). These metrics are intended to provide a more holistic assessment of trajectory quality, collision behavior, and the models' understanding of goal states.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to provide concrete insights into the current capabilities and fundamental limitations of VNMs, guiding future research toward more robust and reliable visual navigation systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involved a multi-faceted real-world evaluation of five state-of-the-art Visual Navigation Models (VNMs) across diverse environments and robot platforms, utilizing an expanded set of evaluation metrics.

#### 3.1. Models Evaluated

Five prominent VNMs were selected for evaluation:
- **GNM:** Utilizes MobileNetv2 for image and distance encoding, employing fully connected layers as its backbone, and an action space shared across robot embodiments. It outputs a trajectory of waypoints and temporal distance.
- **ViNT:** Builds on GNM, using EfficientNet-B0 for encoding. It replaces GNM's fully connected layers with a Transformer decoder, incorporating a goal token for fusing current observation and goal image features. It outputs a single next waypoint and temporal distance.
- **NoMaD:** Extends ViNT by incorporating a diffusion process for multi-modal action prediction and goal masking for both directed and exploratory navigation. It outputs a trajectory of waypoints.
- **NaviBridger:** Further refines NoMaD by integrating a learned action prior using a conditional variational autoencoder within a diffusion model framework. It outputs a trajectory of waypoints.
- **CrossFormer:** Employs a ResNet-26 encoder for images and ViNT for distance prediction. It introduces embodiment-specific Transformer heads to enhance scalability across diverse robot types. It outputs a single next waypoint.

All models were deployed using their open-source pretrained weights, exported to ONNX for optimized onboard inference.

#### 3.2. Robot Platforms

The evaluation utilized two distinct robotic platforms:
- **Bunker:** A tracked mobile robot. This robot lacks an inherent collision avoidance mechanism, providing a direct assessment of the VNM's collision avoidance capabilities.
- **Spot:** A quadruped robot. Spot possesses an in-built controller that provides collision prevention, allowing for an isolated assessment of visual navigation performance without direct geometric failure from the VNM.

#### 3.3. Real-World Environments

Five real-world environments were chosen to represent varying levels of difficulty and types of visual features:
- **Corridor:** A short-horizon, straight-line indoor trajectory (3.749m) towards a cardboard box. This served as a baseline, relatively simple environment.
- **Stairs:** An ascending staircase trajectory (6.247m) featuring two visually identical staircases. This environment tested robustness to perceptually similar features and the model's ability to generalize in repetitive visual conditions.
- **Office Loop:** A long-horizon, full loop of an office environment (27.960m) characterized by clutter (chairs, desks, computers). This challenged long-horizon navigation and obstacle avoidance.
- **Arena:** A doorway navigation task (20.051m) requiring the robot to exit one door and enter a specific third door. This environment assessed visual discrimination among multiple visually similar openings.
- **Snow:** An outdoor snowy parking lot (18.964m). This represented a significant out-of-distribution (OOD) shift, evaluating model robustness and generalization under adverse environmental conditions.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Setup

- **Data Collection:** Reference trajectories were manually recorded for each environment by piloting the robot. RGB images were captured from an onboard fisheye camera, and poses were obtained from SLAM-LVI-SAM (LiDAR+IMU) or SuperOdometry (LiDAR-only). These images were sampled at fixed intervals to construct a linear topological map.
- **Navigation Strategy:** During autonomous deployment, the VNMs navigated by following this topological map. A closest-node belief was maintained, and a sequence of subgoals was computed via graph search over the map, connecting the current closest node to the final goal node. The VNM incrementally updated its active subgoal.
- **Onboard Processing:** All models were deployed on Nvidia AGX Xavier/Orin, performing inference onboard.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Metrics

The evaluation employed a comprehensive set of metrics categorized into vision-based and navigation-based measurements:

- **Vision-based Metrics:** These assessed the perceptual similarity between the robot's final observation and the goal image.
 - **LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity):** Lower is better.
 - **DSSIM (Structural Dissimilarity Index Measure):** Lower is better.
 - **PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio):** Higher is better.
- **Navigation Metrics:** These quantified aspects of path quality, safety, and goal achievement.
 - **Path Length:** Total distance traveled by the robot.
 - **Distance to Goal:** Final Euclidean distance from the robot's position to the true goal.
 - **Collision Occurrence:** A binary indicator marking a trial as a collision if the robot made contact at any point, leading to immediate termination.
 - **Goal Prediction:** The count of trials where the model explicitly believed it had reached the goal, irrespective of its actual physical position.
 - **Topological Node Error:** The averaged absolute difference between the model’s closest node belief and the ground truth node at each trajectory position.

#### 3.6. Robustness Assessment (Visual Perturbations)

To further evaluate robustness to distribution shift, the Corridor environment was tested under two visual perturbation variants:
- **Motion Blur:** Applied to the topological map images, simulating fast motion.
- **Sunflare:** Applied to the topological map images, replicating adverse lighting conditions.
RGB observations during trials remained unmodified, focusing on the impact of map perturbations rather than real-time image processing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation revealed systematic limitations across the tested Visual Navigation Models (VNMs), challenging the notion that architectural complexity alone guarantees robust real-world performance.

#### 4.1. Collision Hotspots: Limited Geometric Understanding

- **Frequent Collisions:** Despite architectural advancements, including Transformer- and diffusion-based models, a consistent failure in collision avoidance was observed. In the Corridor environment, CrossFormer, NoMaD, and NaviBridger exhibited 100% collision rates in their trials (3/3 and 2/3 collisions, respectively), indicating a fundamental lack of geometric understanding. Even in familiar indoor settings like the Office Loop, models frequently collided with static obstacles such as door frames, desks, chairs, and pillars (e.g., NaviBridger 9/10, GNM 5/10, ViNT 1/10 collisions).
- **Contrast in Performance:** Simpler models like GNM and ViNT demonstrated better collision avoidance in the Corridor, achieving 0/3 collisions and successfully reaching the goal in all trials.
- **Underlying Issues:** These collision failures suggest two primary issues: insufficient collision and recovery examples within the training datasets, and an absence of explicit geometric reasoning capabilities in current vision-based architectures. This implies that learned visual features alone do not reliably encode obstacle geometry.

#### 4.2. Precision vs. Completion: Analyzing Prediction Patterns

- **Goal Discrimination Challenges:** Models struggled to discriminate between visually similar locations, leading to goal prediction failures and trajectory deviations.
 - In the Stairs environment, GNM, ViNT, and NoMaD consistently identified the goal and followed the reference trajectory. NoMaD showed improved goal recognition compared to Corridor, achieving 3/3 successful goal recognition with lower distance-to-goal.
 - In the Arena environment, which featured multiple visually similar doorways, NoMaD and ViNT achieved high path lengths, indicating strong trajectory following, but exhibited consistent failure to reach the intended goal, resulting in higher average distances to goal (e.g., NoMaD 7.97m, ViNT 3.09m). This suggests difficulty in precisely identifying the correct goal among distractors. CrossFormer and NaviBridger also showed severe failure to reach the goal, with average goal distances exceeding 10 meters and path lengths disproportionately long relative to the 20-meter reference trajectory.
- **Encoder Impact:** GNM, with its simpler MobileNetV2 encoder, sometimes achieved more consistent goal prediction compared to models using the EfficientNet-B0 encoder (ViNT, NoMaD, NaviBridger, CrossFormer). This suggests that the quality of visual encoding, particularly in distinguishing subtle semantic features, may be more critical than architectural complexity for accurate goal understanding in repetitive environments.

#### 4.3. From Pixels to Waypoints: The Role of Visual Encoding

- **Perceptual Similarity Metrics:** Vision-based metrics (LPIPS, DSSIM, PSNR) were used to assess how well models recognized goal-relevant features.
 - In familiar environments (e.g., Arena), GNM generally achieved the lowest LPIPS and DSSIM, and highest PSNR, indicating a closer match to reference goal images. ViNT, NoMaD, NaviBridger, and CrossFormer, sharing common encoder elements, exhibited similar scores across these metrics, suggesting they struggled to discriminate across visually similar rooms despite recognizing doorway semantics.
 - In the out-of-distribution Snow environment, GNM again maintained the lowest LPIPS and DSSIM scores, implying accurate goal prediction even under novel conditions.
- **Outdoor Performance Nuance:** Surprisingly, ViNT, NoMaD, and NaviBridger showed some improvement in perceptual metrics in the outdoor Snow environment compared to their indoor performance. This observation suggests that outdoor environments with more distinct spatial structure might reduce the visual ambiguity that hinders indoor navigation, though weather and lighting variations were noted as potential confounding factors.

#### 4.4. Generalization Under Distribution Shift

- **Fragility of Diffusion Models:** Diffusion-based models demonstrated fragility under distribution shifts. While GNM and ViNT achieved relatively reliable navigation under controlled visual perturbations (motion blur, sunflare) in the Corridor environment (e.g., ViNT achieving 3/3 goal prediction with sunflare), other models were more susceptible.
- **Snow Environment Performance:** In the Snow environment, GNM maintained strong performance, achieving the lowest goal distance and robust visual metrics. NaviBridger showed closer trajectory following, but ViNT and NoMaD suffered from false goal predictions, leading to premature termination. This reinforces that architectural complexity does not inherently guarantee robustness to environmental variations.

#### 4.5. Lessons Learned (Summary of Insights)

The analysis distilled several key insights:
- **Simpler architectures can be competitive:** GNM, despite its MobileNetV2 encoder and simpler architecture, consistently matched or slightly outperformed more complex Transformer and diffusion-based models on perceptual metrics and in novel environments like Snow.
- **Data quantity and composition are crucial:** The observed performance parity and specific failure modes (e.g., collisions, goal confusion) suggest that current training datasets may be insufficient in scale and diversity for complex models to fully realize their representational capacity. Specifically, a lack of obstacle, recovery, and rare scenario examples limits geometric reasoning, while repetitive features in training data may contribute to goal prediction confusion.
- **Repeated features pose a significant challenge:** Perceptual metrics sometimes surprisingly improved in the Snow environment compared to indoor settings, implying that repetitive visual features in indoor environments might challenge encoders more than certain weather-induced distribution shifts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a foundational contribution to the field of visual navigation in robotics by shifting the paradigm of model evaluation from simplistic success rates to a comprehensive, multi-metric, real-world assessment. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Elevating Evaluation Standards:** The most immediate impact is the establishment of a more rigorous and informative evaluation framework for Visual Navigation Models (VNMs). By advocating for and implementing a suite of metrics that includes path quality, collision behavior, goal prediction, topological node errors, and vision-based perceptual similarity, the work provides a benchmark that captures a richer understanding of VNM performance beyond mere goal achievement. This will encourage future research to adopt similarly comprehensive evaluation protocols, leading to more robust and reliable models.
- **Identifying Critical Limitations:** The systematic uncovering of three key limitations—frequent collisions due to limited geometric understanding, goal prediction failures in visually ambiguous environments, and degradation under distribution shifts—provides crucial insights for the robotics community. These findings highlight fundamental challenges that need to be addressed before VNMs can be reliably deployed in safety-critical or complex real-world applications.
- **Informing Architectural and Data Design:** The observation that simpler architectures can perform comparably to, or even outperform, more complex Transformer and diffusion-based models in certain scenarios has significant implications. It suggests that architectural complexity alone does not guarantee superior real-world performance and points to the critical roles of training data quantity, quality, and composition. This will likely steer future research towards focusing not only on novel architectures but also on designing more comprehensive datasets that include rare scenarios, collision examples, and diverse environmental conditions, as well as considering how models learn to interpret geometric properties.
- **Guiding Future Research Directions:** The lessons learned directly inform future research. The need for integrating explicit geometric reasoning, developing strategies for handling visually repetitive features, and enhancing robustness through methods like test-time adaptation or improved data augmentation are highlighted. The suggestion that data composition matters for collision avoidance and handling perceptual similarities can inspire new data collection strategies and curriculum learning approaches.
- **Promoting Reproducibility and Open Science:** The commitment to publicly releasing the evaluation codebase and dataset is a substantial contribution to open science. This will enable other researchers to reproduce the benchmarks, test new models against a standardized real-world setup, and directly compare their advancements, thereby accelerating progress in the field.
- **Enhancing Real-World Robot Deployment:** By identifying specific failure modes and conditions under which VNMs struggle, the research contributes to making real-world robot deployment safer and more predictable. Understanding these limitations is essential for developing supplementary safety mechanisms (e.g., reactive planners, collision avoidance modules) or for defining the operational envelopes where current VNMs can be reliably used.

In summary, this work provides a critical reality check for the burgeoning field of vision foundation models in navigation. It moves beyond theoretical promises to empirical validation, offering actionable insights that will guide the development of the next generation of visual navigation systems to be more capable, reliable, and deployable in complex human environments.
