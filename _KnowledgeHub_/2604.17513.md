# FLASH: Fast Learning via GPU-Accelerated Simulation for High-Fidelity Deformable Manipulation in Minutes

> [!summary] Summary
> A GPU-native simulation framework named FLASH was developed to enable fast, high-fidelity deformable manipulation by co-optimizing its solver and rendering for parallel execution. This platform accelerates robot policy learning to minutes, achieving robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with high success rates, such as 85.8% for continuous dual-arm towel folding on physical robots.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation of deformable objects is challenging due to their high degrees of freedom and complex, contact-rich dynamics.
- Learning-based approaches for these tasks demand vast datasets, which are costly and inefficient to collect in real-world experiments.
- Current simulation platforms struggle to provide both high physical fidelity and sufficient computational throughput, impeding scalable policy learning and reliable sim-to-real transfer for soft materials.

## Method

- Developed **FLASH**, a ==GPU-native simulation framework== with ==co-optimized solver-level and rendering-level design==, using a lightweight physics system that ==approximates the Schur complement== (`Z ≈ JM⁻¹Jᵀ + E`) for sparsity and efficiency in contact-rich dynamics.
- Constructed a high-fidelity ==digital twin== through geometric modeling, ==physical calibration (system identification)==, and ==perception augmentation== to bridge the sim-to-real gap.
- Employed a ==teacher-student distillation== framework with ==hierarchical finite-state machines==, ==DAgger==, and extensive ==domain randomization== for robust policy learning.

## Results

- FLASH achieves real-time simulation at **30 frames per second** for contact-rich deformable scenes with over **3 million degrees of freedom** on a single GPU, enabling a **100-300x** end-to-end training speedup.
- Policies trained in as little as **50 minutes** for single-arm towel folding demonstrated robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, with an **85.8% success rate** over a one-hour continuous dual-arm towel folding task and **70%** for T-shirt folding on physical robots.
- Cross-simulator comparisons highlight FLASH's superior fidelity for garment folding, producing behaviors that closely match real-world observations and surpass other GPU-capable simulators in accuracy and stability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Achieving scalable and high-fidelity deformable object simulation requires a fundamental redesign of the physics engine for GPU-native parallelism, rather than adapting conventional solvers.
> - Approximating the Schur complement in the contact solver significantly improves computational efficiency and sparsity for contact-rich deformable dynamics without compromising numerical stability.
> - Robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for deformable manipulation can be enabled by meticulously aligning geometric, physical, and visual aspects of a digital twin, complemented by comprehensive perceptual augmentation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deformable object manipulation is a recognized challenge in robotics, with implications across domestic, healthcare, and industrial sectors. The high degrees of freedom and complex dynamics inherent to soft materials distinguish these tasks from rigid-body manipulation, making generalization difficult. Learning-based robotic methods offer a promising approach to such complex problems; however, they typically require extensive datasets, often involving millions of environmental interactions to achieve robust performance. Generating such large volumes of diverse interaction data through real-world experiments is often inefficient, costly, and may not provide sufficient physical variability for policy generalization.

This context places significant demands on simulation platforms, requiring them to deliver both high physical fidelity for realistic behavior and high computational throughput for scalable data generation. Existing simulation frameworks encounter difficulties in simultaneously satisfying these two requirements. CPU-based physics engines, while capable of high precision, often lack the scalability necessary for large-scale parallel learning. Previous GPU-based approaches, such as Isaac Sim, Genesis, and Newton, have advanced simulation speed, but they often face limitations due to architectural constraints, including insufficient solver-level parallelism and inefficient memory access patterns. These limitations prevent them from fully leveraging the computational potential of modern GPU architectures for contact-rich deformable dynamics.

The challenge of transferring policies trained in simulation to real-world robots, known as the sim-to-real gap, remains significant. This gap encompasses both dynamic discrepancies and perceptual misalignments. While techniques like domain randomization and algorithmic supervisors have been explored to bridge this gap, and various control problem simplifications or alternative task formulations have been proposed, benchmarks indicate that current simulators frequently struggle to accurately replicate dynamic fabric behaviors. This often necessitates complex real-to-sim parameter tuning or the learning of residual dynamics. On the perceptual front, efforts have focused on abstracting visual differences using semantic keypoints or enhancing simulation fidelity through methods like mesh-attached neural fields.

FLASH is presented as an advancement within this landscape by specifically addressing the need for a simulation platform that provides both fast and realistic deformable object simulation. The work aims to enable high-throughput learning of deployable policies with robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, thereby reducing reliance on real-world data collection for contact-rich deformable manipulation tasks. It seeks to achieve this by fundamentally redesigning the physics engine for GPU parallelism, rather than adapting conventional solvers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop FLASH, a GPU-native simulation framework designed for contact-rich deformable manipulation, which facilitates rapid, large-scale learning of robot policies. The framework integrates high-fidelity simulation, efficient rendering, and scalable policy learning capabilities.

The motivation for this development stems from several factors:
- **Complexity of Deformable Manipulation:** Deformable objects possess high degrees of freedom and exhibit complex dynamics, particularly in contact-rich scenarios, making them challenging for robots to manipulate.
- **Data Hunger of Learning-Based Methods:** While learning-based approaches show promise for these complex tasks, they inherently require extensive interaction data, often millions of samples, which are impractical and expensive to gather in the physical world.
- **Limitations of Existing Simulators:** Current simulation platforms do not adequately meet the dual requirements of high physical fidelity and high computational throughput for deformable objects:
 - Traditional CPU-based, high-precision physics engines are too slow for large-scale parallel learning.
 - Prior GPU-based simulators often face architectural limitations, such as insufficient solver-level parallelism and inefficient memory access patterns, hindering their ability to fully exploit GPU computational capabilities for the demanding calculations involved in continuously changing geometries, numerous vertices, and contact constraints of soft materials. This leads to difficulties in achieving the necessary accuracy, speed, and stability for interactive learning.
- **Need for Efficient Sim-to-Real Transfer:** There is a critical need for policies trained purely in simulation to transfer effectively to real-world robots without extensive fine-tuning. This requires a simulation environment that accurately models physical interactions and provides realistic perceptual data.
- **Acceleration of Robot Learning:** The overarching goal is to accelerate the robot policy learning process, reducing training times from potentially days or hours to minutes, thereby making learning-based deformable manipulation more accessible and practical for deployment.

FLASH aims to provide a solution by creating a robust and scalable platform that enables rapid, simulation-only training of policies that are directly deployable on physical robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FLASH framework adopts an approach centered on co-optimizing solver-level and rendering-level designs specifically for GPU parallelism. This is proposed as a method to achieve scalable learning for deformable manipulation.

**Simulation Core (Sec. IV-B):**
The physics simulation core builds upon a GPU-friendly local-global pipeline previously established for hyperelastic simulation with accurate frictional contact. A key challenge in applying this to robotic manipulation, especially with soft materials, is the emergence of numerous time-dependent contact constraints (e.g., in cloth folding). These constraints significantly expand the dimensionality of the Lagrange multiplier space, increasing both computational cost and numerical difficulty. Specifically, the inverse of the system matrix (`A⁻¹`) and the associated Schur complement (`Z`) can become fully dense, forming a performance bottleneck.

To address this, FLASH introduces a lightweight simulation system that approximates the Schur complement `Z = JA⁻¹Jᵀ + E` with `Z ≈ JM⁻¹Jᵀ + E`. This approximation shifts the contact metric towards an inertia-dominated inverse, thereby reducing global contact coupling while retaining the implicit treatment of elastic forces through projective dynamics. This design choice is posited to improve the sparsity of the Schur complement, which is crucial for computational efficiency, particularly in contact-rich scenarios where elastic coupling typically dominates system behavior and contact responses are largely governed by inertia and local geometry. The approximation maintains sparsity throughout the non-smooth Newton iterations, enabling scalable simulation.

For multi-environment support, FLASH uses a block-diagonal system matrix and stacked primal variables to represent multiple parallel environments within a single large-scale sparse system. This structure ensures that all core operations are expressed using standard sparse primitives, allowing underlying numerical libraries to manage hardware parallelism, load balancing, and scheduling automatically. The computational cost is designed to scale linearly with the number of environments and active contact constraints. The physics engine is developed natively for GPU architectures, incorporating optimized collision handling and memory layouts, rather than porting conventional single-instruction-multiple-data (SIMD) solvers.

**Real-to-Sim Transfer and Rendering (Sec. IV-B.4 and IX):**
To bridge the simulation-reality gap, FLASH constructs a high-fidelity digital twin that aligns geometry, physics, and visuals.
- **Geometric Modeling:** Simple objects like square towels are procedurally generated, while complex garments like T-shirts are reconstructed from 3D scans or existing synthetic datasets.
- **Physical Calibration (System Identification):** Material properties such as Poisson's ratio and Young's modulus are calibrated. This involves recording a "corner lift" interaction with a real robot using a depth camera to capture ground truth geometry. The exact end-effector trajectory is then replayed in simulation, and a grid search is performed over material parameters to minimize the geometric alignment error (average point-to-mesh distance) between real and simulated states.
- **Visual Rendering and Perception Augmentation:** Simulated depth images, including robot self-occlusion, are generated and spatially aligned with real-world data via hand-eye calibration. To account for real-world sensor noise and segmentation imperfections, the simulation pipeline introduces synthetic perturbations such as random blockout, boundary jitter, and kinematic-based self-occlusion. This approach aims to produce augmented observations that closely match the real-world distribution. The system employs a versatile ray-casting pipeline supporting both GPU (NVIDIA OptiX) and CPU (Intel Embree) backends for generating depth maps, instance segmentation masks, and point clouds efficiently.

**Policy Learning (Sec. IV-C and X):**
FLASH utilizes a teacher-student distillation framework.
- **Teacher Synthesis:** Teachers are synthesized using privileged state information and heuristic rules organized into hierarchical finite-state machines. These teachers command end-effectors to grasp and transport specific keypoints to target locations, including reactive recovery behaviors for grasp failures.
- **Student Policy:** The behaviors learned by the teacher are distilled into deployable student policies. These policies are conditioned on a stacked history of proprioceptive and visual observations. The student architecture uses a convolutional neural network (CNN) for perception and multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) for other components, also being trained to reconstruct state variables.
- **Domain Randomization:** Extensive domain randomization is applied during the distillation process to enhance sim-to-real robustness. This includes variations in cloth dynamics parameters, initial cloth and end-effector states, proprioceptive observations (with calibrated noise), and perceptual observations (camera pose jitter, edge noise, random occlusions). Training uses DAgger, with mean-absolute error (MAE) loss for position delta, log-probability loss for gripper commands, and an auxiliary mean-squared error (MSE) loss for state reconstruction.

**Sim-to-Real Transfer (Sec. IV-D and XI):**
Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is achieved by aligning observation and action spaces.
- **Perception:** A real-time pipeline combining YOLOv8 and SAM 2 is used to segment the target object from raw depth streams, isolating the object and filtering background noise to provide clean visual input matching simulation.
- **Control:** The policy predicts end-effector actions (position delta and gripper open/close). Cartesian targets are converted to joint positions using a numerical Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver and executed by a high-frequency low-level controller. For dual-arm setups, the policy outputs 8-dimensional actions. Deployment on real robots (Airbot Play, AdamU) utilizes asynchronous multi-threading on heterogeneous computing platforms, with policies exported as ONNX models for efficient inference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings related to FLASH's simulation performance, cross-simulator comparison, learning efficiency, and real-robot deployment.

**Simulation Performance:**
- FLASH achieves real-time simulation rates of 30 frames per second (FPS) for contact-rich deformable scenes with over 3 million degrees of freedom (DoF) on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. This performance reportedly translates to a 100–300 times end-to-end training speedup compared to real-time.
- **Friction and Contact Fidelity:** Experiments demonstrated the simulator's ability to distinguish friction coefficients with 0.001 precision (Fig. 3a) and accurately simulate complex interactions such as cloth-chair collisions (Fig. 3b) and soft grippers grasping soft objects via friction (Fig. 3c).

**Cross-Simulator Comparison (T-shirt Dual-Sleeve Folding):**
- **Fidelity Comparison (Fig. 4a):** When compared against other GPU-capable simulators (Genesis, Isaac Sim, Newton) and real-world execution for a T-shirt dual-sleeve folding task, FLASH produced results that most closely matched the real behavior. This included smooth, symmetric folds, stable resting configurations, and accurate frictional sticking without spurious sliding or post-release motion.
 - **Genesis (PBD):** Exhibited large elastic snapping and poor frictional stability, leading to persistent sliding and failure to achieve a stable fold. It also demonstrated numerical instability in multi-environment settings, preventing meaningful scaling.
 - **Isaac Sim (FEM):** Showed difficulty in coupling stiff in-plane stretching with compliant bending, resulting in shear-like distortions and excessive wrinkling. While exhibiting strong parallel efficiency, its simulated garment behavior was physically inaccurate.
 - **Newton (VBD):** Produced strong baseline results but was overly stiff in bending, preventing the folded sleeve from settling flush and causing gradual elastic unfolding. It was consistently slower than FLASH.
- The comparison indicated that realistic garment folding requires both accurate frictional contact resolution and balanced stretch-bend coupling, capabilities that FLASH demonstrated effectively.
- **Parameter Ablation (Fig. 4b):** Varying motion speed, bending stiffness, and solver fidelity in FLASH produced predictable changes in garment behavior, such as tighter folds with increased gripper speed or increased elastic rebound with higher bending stiffness. Even in an extreme low-iteration solver regime, the simulation remained numerically stable and maintained a robust frictional solution, demonstrating interpretable degradation rather than unstable artifacts.

**Massively Parallel Throughput (Table II):**
- FLASH demonstrated competitive parallel throughput. For 256 parallel environments, FLASH achieved a per-step time of 185.10 ms. In comparison, Isaac Sim, while faster at 47.81 ms/step, produced physically inaccurate behavior. Newton was slower at 480.64 ms/step, and Genesis failed due to numerical instability in multi-environment settings. FLASH was concluded to offer a favorable fidelity/throughput trade-off for garment folding tasks.

**Learning Results in Simulation (Fig. 5):**
- Policies were trained for various cloth-folding tasks, including single-arm and dual-arm towel folding, T-shirt folding, and shorts folding.
- Training times on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 ranged from 50 minutes for single-arm towel folding to 150 minutes for dual-arm towel folding, and 600 minutes (10 hours) for T-shirt and shorts folding.

**Real-Robot Experiments (Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer):**
- **Towel Folding (Airbot Play):** A policy trained in 50 minutes demonstrated robust performance on a physical desktop manipulator. It tolerated significant placement uncertainty (±8 cm translation, arbitrary rotation) and exhibited reactive recovery behaviors against dynamic disturbances, such as re-attempting missed grasps or adapting to human interference (Fig. 6).
- **Long-Duration Continuous Evaluation (AdamU):** A continuous one-hour evaluation (106 trials) of dual-arm towel folding on a humanoid robot (AdamU) achieved an 85.8% success rate (91/106), defined as aligning the towel corner within 5 cm of the goal within 40 seconds. The policy consistently converged to the target configuration despite initial variance and accumulated deformations (Fig. 7c). Inference latency remained stable throughout the operation.
- **Folding Shorts and T-shirts (AdamU):** The framework demonstrated generalization to garments with different topologies.
 - Folding shorts achieved a 60% success rate (12/20) and qualitatively showed successful sim-to-real transfer (Fig. 7a).
 - Folding T-shirts, a longer-horizon task, achieved a 70% success rate (35/50) and closely matched simulated behavior in the real world (Fig. 7b).

**Failure Modes:**
- **Perception Bottleneck:** Identified as the primary source of real-world failures, attributed to depth sensor noise on thin fabrics and self-occlusions leading to grasp misalignment and imprecise final geometry.
- **Hardware Abstraction Gap:** The use of a simplified binary grasp model without accounting for low-level motor dynamics (e.g., actuation delays, backlash) introduced tracking deviations. The absence of tactile feedback also limited the system's ability to correct these deviations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of FLASH represents a notable advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for deformable objects, and has several potential impacts:

- **Accelerated Robot Learning:** FLASH significantly reduces the time required to train policies for complex deformable manipulation tasks, from potentially hours or days to minutes. This substantial acceleration makes learning-based approaches more practical, scalable, and accessible for researchers and practitioners, lowering the barrier to entry for developing solutions to challenging real-world problems.
- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer Capabilities:** The framework demonstrates robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, allowing policies trained entirely in simulation to be deployed directly on physical robots without real-world fine-tuning or demonstrations. This achievement addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics by providing a practical alternative to labor-intensive and costly real-world data collection, thereby accelerating the deployment of robotic solutions in diverse environments.
- **High-Fidelity and High-Throughput Simulation:** FLASH addresses a critical gap in existing simulators by offering a GPU-native physics engine that simultaneously provides high physical fidelity (especially for contact, friction, and elastic coupling) and high computational throughput. This combination is crucial for generating physically meaningful interaction data that is essential for training reliable manipulation policies, moving beyond simulators that compromise realism for speed or vice versa.
- **Scalability for Data-Hungry Algorithms:** The architecture, designed from the ground up for massive GPU parallelism, enables the simultaneous simulation of numerous environments. This inherent scalability is a key enabler for data-hungry machine learning algorithms, allowing for the generation of vast and diverse datasets required for robust policy generalization and domain randomization.
- **Generality Across Manipulation Tasks:** The framework's ability to support various deformable objects (cloth, volumetric materials) and different learning settings without requiring task-specific simulator redesign suggests broad applicability. This generality can foster innovation across a wider range of deformable manipulation challenges.
- **Practical Applications in Robotics:** The demonstrated capabilities in complex tasks like garment folding highlight the potential for FLASH to enable advancements in domestic services, healthcare (e.g., patient dressing), and industrial automation (e.g., handling fabrics or soft goods), where robust deformable object manipulation is essential.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a robust foundation for future research. Identified limitations, such as CPU-GPU transfer overhead and the need for improved learning formulations or supervision signals for longer-horizon tasks, provide clear directions for further optimization and innovation in both simulation technology and robot learning algorithms.
