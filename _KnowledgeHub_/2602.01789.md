# RFS: Reinforcement Learning with Residual Flow Steering for Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces Residual Flow Steering (RFS), a data-efficient reinforcement learning framework that adapts pretrained generative policies for dexterous manipulation. RFS integrates latent noise steering for global behavioral modulation with residual actions for local refinement, achieving an average success rate of 0.861 across six simulated tasks and enabling robust real-world generalization to unseen objects with limited human corrective data.

## Problem

- Policies pretrained via imitation learning often lack robustness and generalization capabilities for successful deployment in diverse, real-world conditions, especially for dexterous manipulation.
- Many established reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms are incompatible with expressive generative policy architectures (e.g., diffusion and flow models) that represent multimodal action distributions.
- Existing policy adaptation methods typically focus on either local corrections (residual policies) or global behavioral shifts (latent modulation), failing to efficiently combine both for comprehensive policy improvement.

## Method

- The authors propose **RFS** (Residual Flow Steering), a unified ==policy modulation framework== that jointly learns an ==initial latent noise variable== ($a_0$) for global steering and a ==residual action== ($a_r$) for local adjustments.
- **RFS** uses the latent noise $a_0$ to steer a pretrained ==flow-matching base policy==, generating a base action, to which the learned residual action $a_r$ is then added.
- The framework is implemented with a ==sim-to-reality pipeline==, involving online ==PPO== fine-tuning in simulation and offline ==TD3+BC== fine-tuning in the real world using limited human-corrected data.

## Results

- **RFS** achieved an average success rate of **0.861** across six simulated dexterous manipulation tasks, outperforming various baselines including Diffusion/Flow RL (e.g., DPPO: **0.178**), offline RL (e.g., IQL: **0.457**), and other policy adaptation methods.
- In real-world experiments, RFS significantly improved generalization to unseen objects, achieving **74.0%** grasping and **70.0%** pick-and-place success with only 50 human corrections, compared to zero-shot performance (**30.0%** grasping, **40.0%** pick-and-place) and supervised fine-tuning baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the combined RFS approach consistently outperformed individual components (e.g., Residual RL or DSRL) in both simulation and real-world settings, demonstrating the synergistic benefits of joint local and global policy modulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unifying latent-noise steering (global adaptation) with residual actions (local refinement) on top of flow-matching policies significantly enhances policy robustness and generalization compared to using either method in isolation.
> - For effective offline reinforcement learning adaptation of modulated policies, conditioning the critic on the final executed action (a=ab+ara = a_b + a_ra=ab+ar) is crucial for stability and learning efficacy.
> - Simulation pretraining provides a broad coverage of behaviors and perturbations, which, when combined with a small set of real-world human corrections via RFS, leads to substantially greater improvements and generalization than fine-tuning policies trained solely on real data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly dexterous manipulation, increasingly leverages imitation learning (IL) from human demonstrations to train policies for complex sequential decision-making tasks. Recent advancements in generative models, such as diffusion models and flow matching, have enabled the representation of multimodal action distributions, improving the expressiveness of learned behaviors. These generative imitation learning approaches can capture the inherent multimodality of human actions, making them suitable for complex manipulation tasks.

Despite their initial success, policies pretrained via imitation learning often exhibit limitations in generalization to novel test-time scenarios or out-of-distribution states. This necessitates additional fine-tuning or adaptation to achieve robust, production-level performance upon deployment. Traditional supervised fine-tuning methods, while effective for some decision-making systems, typically require high-quality, curated expert data, which can be expensive and difficult to obtain in robotics.

Reinforcement learning (RL) and offline reinforcement learning (offline RL) offer an alternative framework for policy improvement by optimizing reward objectives, potentially utilizing cheaper, suboptimal, or self-collected data. However, combining imitation learning pretraining with RL fine-tuning presents challenges. Many existing RL algorithms, designed for policies with closed-form likelihoods or simple reparameterization, are often incompatible with expressive generative architectures like diffusion and flow models. Furthermore, a central challenge in policy adaptation is maintaining the benefits of global exploration learned during pretraining while enabling rapid correction of local execution errors.

Prior work in policy adaptation can be broadly categorized into output modulation and input modulation. Output modulation methods, such as residual policy learning, train a small residual policy to correct the actions of a pretrained base policy. While effective for local refinements and error correction, these methods typically struggle to induce significant global behavioral changes, especially when the base policy is limited. Conversely, input modulation methods, like diffusion steering (DSRL), modulate the initial latent noise of generative policies to influence high-level behavior. While enabling global adaptation, DSRL is often constrained to the demonstration manifold, limiting its ability to explore off-distribution states or perform fine-grained corrections.

This work addresses the limitations of both pure imitation learning and existing adaptation strategies by proposing a unified framework that combines their complementary strengths. It seeks to bridge the gap between expressive generative policy representations and robust, data-efficient RL fine-tuning, specifically for high-dimensional dexterous manipulation tasks, by enabling both local and global policy adjustments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-efficient reinforcement learning framework that can effectively adapt pretrained generative policies for robust performance in dexterous manipulation tasks, both in simulation and the real world. The motivation stems from several identified limitations in current robot learning paradigms:

1. **Limited Generalization of Imitation Learning Policies:** Policies pretrained through imitation learning, even those leveraging expressive generative models like flow matching, often lack the robustness and generalization capabilities required for successful deployment in diverse, real-world conditions. They tend to fail in scenarios that deviate from the training demonstration distribution, highlighting a need for subsequent adaptation.
2. **Incompatibility of RL with Generative Architectures:** While RL offers a principled way to fine-tune policies, many established RL algorithms are not directly compatible with non-differentiable generative models or models that lack closed-form likelihoods, such as diffusion and flow models. This makes it challenging to apply standard RL fine-tuning to these expressive policy representations.
3. **Balancing Local Refinement and Global Exploration:** Existing policy adaptation methods tend to focus on either local corrections or global behavioral shifts, but rarely both simultaneously and efficiently.
 - **Residual Policy Learning (Output Modulation):** This approach excels at making precise, local adjustments to a base policy's actions, correcting minor errors. However, it struggles to steer the policy towards fundamentally different modes of behavior or to recover from significant deviations from the demonstration manifold.
 - **Diffusion Steering (Input Modulation):** This method modulates the latent space of generative policies, enabling more global, semantic changes in behavior. However, its effectiveness is often limited to the learned demonstration manifold, potentially restricting fine-grained control and exploration in off-distribution states.
4. **Data Efficiency for Real-World Adaptation:** Deploying and fine-tuning robotic policies in the real world is resource-intensive due to the cost and scarcity of expert human interaction data. There is a need for adaptation frameworks that can leverage limited real-world data effectively to bridge the sim-to-real gap and improve robustness.

To address these motivations, the authors propose Residual Flow Steering (RFS) with the following specific objectives:
- To introduce a unified policy modulation framework that combines latent-noise steering (for global adaptation) with residual actions (for local refinement) on top of pretrained flow-matching base policies.
- To enable efficient learning in high-dexterity tasks by preserving the broad exploration benefits of pretraining while allowing for rapid correction of local execution errors.
- To demonstrate the effectiveness of RFS in simulation for generating robust behaviors and in real-world settings for data-efficient fine-tuning, including successful transfer to novel objects and conditions.
- To identify the key design choices and contributions of RFS through comprehensive experimental evaluations and ablations in both simulated and real-world manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is Residual Flow Steering (RFS), a unified framework for adapting pretrained generative policies using reinforcement learning. RFS operates by jointly optimizing a residual action and a latent noise distribution, enabling both local refinement and global exploration.

#### 3.1. Overview of Residual Flow Steering (RFS)

RFS modulates a pretrained flow-matching policy by learning a combined policy $\pi_{RFS}(a_0, a_r | s)$ that outputs both an initial latent noise variable $a_0$ and a residual action $a_r$. The latent variable $a_0$ is used to steer the pretrained flow-matching policy (denoted as $Des(s, a_0, v_\theta)$) to produce a base action $a_b$. The residual action $a_r$ is then added to $a_b$ to yield the final executed action $a = a_b + a_r$. This design allows for global shifts in behavior via $a_0$ and precise local corrections via $a_r$.

#### 3.2. Background: Flow Matching

Flow matching is a generative modeling technique that learns a time-dependent velocity field $v_\theta(x_t, t)$ to transport a simple base distribution $p_0$ to a complex target distribution $p_1$. The training objective minimizes the squared difference between the predicted velocity and a straight-path target velocity, conditioned on interpolated samples $x_t = (1-t)x_0 + tx_1$. For policy learning, this becomes a conditional objective $L(\theta) = E_{a_0 \sim p_0, (s,a) \sim D, t \sim U} \|v_\theta(a_t, t, s) - (a - a_0)\|^2$, where $a_0$ is initial noise, $(s,a)$ is a state-action pair from a demonstration dataset $D$, and $a_t$ is an interpolation between $a_0$ and $a$. Actions are sampled by integrating the learned velocity field $v_\theta(a_t, t, s)$ from an initial noise $a_0$.

#### 3.3. Policy Modulation Paradigms

RFS builds upon and unifies two distinct policy modulation paradigms:
- **Output Modulation (Residual RL):** In residual reinforcement learning, a base policy $\pi_\theta$ generates an action $a_b$, and a separate residual policy $\pi_r$ learns an additive correction $a_r$. The final action is $a = a_b + a_r$. This is effective for local refinements but limited in inducing global behavioral changes.
- **Input Modulation (Diffusion Steering, DSRL):** Diffusion steering adapts generative policies by learning a policy over the initial latent noise $a_0$ instead of sampling it from a fixed distribution. The action is then generated by the base generative model conditioned on this learned $a_0$. This allows for global behavioral modulation but can be constrained to the demonstration manifold.

#### 3.4. RFS: Joint Local and Global Adaptation

RFS unifies these two mechanisms. The RFS policy $\pi_{RFS}(a_0, a_r | s)$ jointly determines the latent noise $a_0$ for global steering and the residual action $a_r$ for local adjustments. The base action $a_b$ is generated by the pretrained flow-matching policy $v_\theta$ using $a_0$, and the final action is $a = a_b + a_r$. The overall objective is to maximize the expected discounted return through an RL algorithm compatible with this modulated action space.

#### 3.5. Instantiation for Dexterous Manipulation

RFS is instantiated through a sim-to-reality pipeline for dexterous manipulation tasks.

#### 3.5.1. Simulation Training Pipeline

1. **Data Generation:** A small set of VR-teleoperated demonstrations is collected in simulation using IsaacLab for various dexterous manipulation tasks (e.g., grasping, pick-and-place, stacking).
2. **Base Policy Training:** A generative base policy $v_\theta$ is pretrained using these demonstrations via the flow-matching objective.
3. **Online RFS Fine-tuning:** The RFS policy $\pi_{RFS}$ is fine-tuned on top of this base policy using online reinforcement learning with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). This stage optimizes for task success and stability in simulation, leveraging privileged low-level state information. External disturbances are applied to enhance robustness.
4. **Policy Distillation:** The trained RFS policy (which operates with state-based observations) is then distilled into a point-cloud-conditioned visuomotor policy via a student-teacher framework. This process aims to improve sim-to-real transfer by using visual observations.

#### 3.5.2. Real-World Fine-tuning Pipeline (Offline RFS)

1. **Zero-Shot Deployment and Human Correction:** The distilled visuomotor policy is deployed in the real world on a Franka arm with a LEAP hand. When the policy encounters execution failures (e.g., unstable grasps, misplacement), a human operator provides corrective actions using a SpaceMouse. These corrections are defined as bounded deltas from the base policy output, computing $a_r = a_{human} - a_b$.
2. **Offline Data Collection:** This process generates a dataset $D_{RFS}$ consisting of human-corrected transitions $((o, s), (a_0, a_r), (o', s'), r)$, where $(o,s)$ are observations and state, $(a_0, a_r)$ are the RFS policy outputs, and $r$ is the reward.
3. **Offline RFS Fine-tuning:** The $\pi_{RFS}(a_0, a_r | o_{pc}, s_{pro})$ policy is then trained using offline reinforcement learning, specifically TD3+BC (Twin-Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient with Behavior Cloning regularization).
 - **Critic Update:** The critic $Q_\phi(o, s, a)$ is updated using a standard TD-learning objective. A key design choice, empirically justified, is to condition the critic on the *final executed action* $a = a_b + a_r$ rather than its decomposed components.
 - **Actor Update:** The actor maximizes the critic output while applying a behavior cloning (BC) regularizer on the residual actions $a_r$, encouraging them to stay close to the human corrections.
The reward function in the real world is programmatically defined, utilizing SAM2 for object tracking and centroid extraction, and includes terms for object-to-palm distance and lifting height.

The use of point clouds as visual observations is further enhanced by collecting simulation data from multiple camera viewpoints, calibrating to the robot base frame, and injecting random noise into camera extrinsics and point clouds during training to improve robustness to real-world variations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate RFS across dexterous manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world settings, focusing on the efficacy of residual refinement, the impact of design choices, and the role of latent-noise modulation.

#### 4.1. Simulation Performance

RFS was evaluated on six simulated tasks: grasping, pick-and-place, non-prehensile push-to-grasp, long-horizon packing, and high-precision stacking and pouring.

#### 4.1.1. Comparison to Baselines

RFS consistently outperformed a wide range of baselines across all tasks.
- **Diffusion/Flow RL Fine-tuning (DPPO, ReinFlow):** These methods generally underperformed, with success rates often below 0.50. DPPO had limited success (average 0.178), and ReinFlow showed modest improvements but struggled on complex tasks. RFS, by restricting RL updates to initial noise and residual terms, demonstrated more stable learning and achieved success rates at least 0.35 higher across all tasks.
- **Offline-to-Online RL (IQL, AWAC, Flow Q-Learning):** IQL was the strongest among these, achieving moderate success on some tasks (e.g., 0.69 grasp, 0.56 pick-and-place) but struggled with long-horizon (packing 0.20) and non-prehensile (push-to-grasp 0.26) tasks. AWAC and Flow Q-Learning performed poorly on multiple tasks. RFS consistently achieved higher success rates by leveraging base-policy pretraining for directed exploration.
- **RL with Demonstrations (RLPD, IBRL):** RLPD performed well on grasping (0.54) and pick-and-place (0.76) but failed on long-horizon or high-precision tasks. IBRL had limited success across tasks. RFS consistently improved over the base policy, which itself had only moderate success (average 0.25).

#### 4.1.2. Ablation Studies in Simulation

- **Residual RL methods (Policy Decorator, ResiP):** These methods achieved moderate success (e.g., grasping ~0.60, pick-and-place ~0.50) but struggled with sequential and high-precision tasks (packing 0.40, push-to-grasp 0.15, stacking 0.30, pouring 0.20). Their gains were primarily local.
- **DSRL (Diffusion Steering RL):** DSRL was a strong baseline, achieving respectable performance on grasping (0.732), pick-and-place (0.692), and packing (0.639). However, it performed poorly on stacking (0.135) and pouring (0.268), indicating limitations in adapting to challenging or out-of-distribution states due to its constraint to the demonstration distribution.
- **Overall RFS Performance:** By jointly modulating input and output, RFS achieved the highest success rates across all tasks, with an average success rate of 0.861, significantly outperforming all baselines and ablations. For instance, RFS achieved 0.899 (Grasping), 0.939 (Pick & Place), 0.781 (Packing), 0.721 (Push-to-Grasp), 0.951 (Stacking), and 0.873 (Pouring).

#### 4.2. Real-World Performance

RFS was evaluated for offline fine-tuning on real-world grasping and pick-and-place tasks using both seen and unseen objects. The Franka-Leap Hand system was used, and 50 human corrective demonstrations were collected for fine-tuning.

#### 4.2.1. Sim-to-Real Transfer Challenges

Zero-shot transfer of the simulation-trained policy performed poorly (43.3% grasping, 50.0% pick-and-place on seen objects; 30.0% grasp, 40.0% pick-and-place on unseen objects), exhibiting common failure modes such as translational/rotational offsets, insufficient finger closure, and mistimed actions. This underscored a significant sim-to-real gap.

#### 4.2.2. Comparison to Supervised Fine-Tuning

- **Co-training:** Showed substantial improvement on seen objects (83.3% grasping, 60.0% pick-and-place), but generalization to unseen objects remained limited (47.5% grasp, 46.0% pick-and-place).
- **BC Fine-tuning:** Performed worse than co-training (73.3% grasping, 40.0% pick-and-place on seen objects; 35.0% grasp, 23.0% pick-and-place on unseen objects).
The noisy and inconsistent nature of human-collected data often led to instability and failures, particularly with novel objects.

#### 4.2.3. Impact of Critic Design Choices

Ablations on critic architectures in the offline RL setting revealed that conditioning the critic on the *final executed action* ($Q(a_b + a_r, o)$) was critical for stable and effective offline RL adaptation. Alternative approaches like $Q(a_r, o)$ or $Q([a_r, a_b], o)$ resulted in zero success on both grasping and pick-and-place.

#### 4.2.4. Ablation Studies in Real World

- **Residual RL (only $a_r$):** Achieved 80.0% grasping and 50.0% pick-and-place on seen objects; 45.0% grasp and 36.0% pick-and-place on unseen objects.
- **DSRL (only $a_0$):** Achieved 70.0% grasping and 80.0% pick-and-place on seen objects; 48.0% grasp and 60.0% pick-and-place on unseen objects.
- **Full RFS:** The full RFS model ($a_0$ and $a_r$) achieved the strongest performance. On seen objects, RFS achieved 80.0% grasping and 90.0% pick-and-place. Crucially, RFS demonstrated superior generalization to *unseen objects*, achieving 74.0% grasping and 70.0% pick-and-place success. Qualitatively, latent-noise steering (global exploration) and residual actions (local refinement) synergized, making their combination more effective than either component alone.

#### 4.2.5. Benefits of Simulation Pretraining

Fine-tuning RFS on a policy trained *solely* on real demonstrations yielded only marginal gains. In contrast, simulation pretraining provided broad coverage of grasp poses and perturbations, enabling adaptation behaviors that were not learnable from limited real-world data alone. Fine-tuning a simulation-pretrained policy with a small number of real demonstrations led to substantially larger improvements and stronger generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Residual Flow Steering (RFS) represents a contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly for dexterous manipulation. By unifying latent noise steering and residual action corrections, RFS provides a framework for robust and data-efficient adaptation of pretrained generative policies.

The significance of this work lies in several aspects:
- **Enhanced Policy Generalization and Robustness:** RFS directly addresses the challenge of limited generalization in imitation learning policies. By combining mechanisms for both global behavioral modulation and local error correction, it enables policies to adapt to novel scenarios and recover from execution errors that lie off the initial demonstration manifold. This is crucial for deploying robots in complex and variable real-world environments.
- **Compatibility with Expressive Generative Models:** RFS demonstrates a method for integrating reinforcement learning with advanced generative models like flow matching, which are powerful for representing multimodal action distributions but traditionally difficult to fine-tune with standard RL. This opens avenues for leveraging the expressiveness of generative models without sacrificing adaptability.
- **Data Efficiency in Real-World Adaptation:** The sim-to-real pipeline, particularly the offline RFS fine-tuning stage with human-corrected data, offers a data-efficient approach to bridge the simulation-reality gap. Utilizing a small set of human corrections to refine a simulation-pretrained policy significantly improves real-world performance and generalization to unseen objects, reducing the dependency on extensive real-world expert data.
- **Improved Dexterous Manipulation Capabilities:** The empirical results in both simulation and the real world showcase RFS's ability to achieve high success rates across a range of dexterous manipulation tasks, including precise and long-horizon operations. This indicates a potential for RFS to enable robots to perform more complex and skillful manipulation tasks.

Potential impacts of this research include:
- **Advancement of Sim-to-Real Transfer:** RFS provides a robust strategy for transferring policies from simulation to the real world, which is a persistent challenge in robotics. Its data-efficient real-world fine-tuning capabilities could accelerate the development and deployment of robotic systems.
- **Foundation for More Capable Robots:** By allowing robots to learn complex behaviors from demonstrations and then efficiently adapt to new conditions, RFS could contribute to the development of more autonomous and versatile robotic manipulators.
- **Inspiration for Future Robot Learning Architectures:** The unified policy modulation framework presented by RFS could inspire further research into hybrid learning architectures that combine the strengths of different learning paradigms for optimal performance.

Despite these contributions, the authors identify limitations and directions for future work. The current reliance on point cloud observations provides limited semantic and contextual understanding, which could hinder performance in cluttered or semantically rich environments requiring higher-level reasoning. Additionally, the real-world adaptation is currently restricted to offline fine-tuning, preventing online policy updates in response to dynamically changing conditions. Addressing these limitations, possibly through integration with vision-language models for semantic understanding and enabling online adaptation, represents promising avenues for further research to enhance the responsiveness and robustness of RFS in real-world deployment.
