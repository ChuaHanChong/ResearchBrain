# Mendel Gödel Machine: Recursive Self-Improving Coding Agents via Comparative Evolution

> [!summary] Summary
> MGM (Mendel Gödel Machine) is an archive-based self-improving coding agent that conditions each self-edit on comparative evidence rather than one failure trajectory: reaction-norm mutation compares an agent's trajectories across tasks, cross-lineage hybridization compares two lineages on a shared task, and a failed-task pool manufactures overlap. It lifts a 35B-backbone scaffold on Polyglot from 50.8% to 93.3%.

## Problem

- Prior self-improving agents derive each self-modification from a single recent failure trajectory on one task, ignoring comparative signals accumulated in the growing archive.
- Existing work improves only archive storage and the sampling policy; the self-modification operator itself, where the agent rewrites its own code, stays underexplored.
- Single-trajectory evidence leaves diagnostic uncertainty: task-specific accidents cannot be separated from genuine genotype-level scaffold defects, so edits search a large candidate set.

## Method

- **MGM**'s ==reaction-norm mutation== edits an agent using two of its own trajectories on different tasks, exposing recurring rather than accidental failure modes once enough evaluations accumulate.
- ==Cross-lineage hybridization== edits a failing target agent using a reference agent's trajectory on the same task, extracting a transferable behavioral trait instead of splicing source files.
- A ==failed-task pool== reweights task sampling toward known-failure tasks to create cross-lineage overlap; ==Thompson sampling== then picks among eligible operators under configurable weights.

## Results

- Under a matched **200**-evaluation budget, MGM raises Polyglot-60 from **50.8%** to **93.2%** versus HGM's **77.9%**, and SWE-bench Verified-60 from **68.3%** to **78.3%** versus **73.3%**.
- Frozen Polyglot-evolved scaffolds reach **26.7%** on SWE-bench Pro and **55.0%** Multilingual, while HGM regresses to **13.3%**; swapping in a stronger backbone gives **96.9%** on full Polyglot.
- Ablations cut Polyglot-60 accuracy to **79.7%** without reaction-norm mutation and **74.6%** without hybridization, at comparable wall-clock hours and per-operator token cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Better evidence, not a stronger editor, drives self-improvement: comparative trajectories shrink the candidate-defect set and raise fix probability at zero extra evaluation cost.
> - Contrastive cross-lineage evidence yields workflow-level scaffold changes that transfer to unseen benchmarks and stronger backbones, whereas single-trajectory evolution can overfit and regress.
> - The gain is history-dependent: with a small archive or sparse task overlap the method degenerates to single-trajectory baselines, and good evidence still cannot guarantee a good edit.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The concept of artificial intelligence systems recursively improving themselves has been a long-standing vision in the field, tracing back to early ideas like Schmidhuber's work on self-referential learning (1987) and the mathematically rigorous Gödel Machine framework (Schmidhuber, 2003; 2007). Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) and the development of specialized coding agents have initiated empirical realizations of this vision.

Prior research has demonstrated various aspects of self-improving agents. For instance, agents equipped with file-editing tools have shown capabilities in autonomously refactoring their own codebase to enhance performance (Robeyns et al., 2025). This iterative improvement process has been reframed as open-ended evolution, where an expanding archive of agent variants is maintained and sampled to seed subsequent self-modifications (Zhang et al., 2026a). Further refinements have focused on optimizing sampling policies by leveraging the aggregated performance signals of agents' descendants to guide evaluation and expansion operations (Wang et al., 2026).

However, a notable gap identified in this landscape is the underexploration of the self-modification process itself. Existing solutions typically condition each self-modification step on a single failure trajectory of an agent on one task. This approach potentially overlooks comparative signals available within the agent's growing archive of past attempts and evaluations. The Mendel Gödel Machine (MGM) is introduced to address this specific limitation by enhancing the self-modification mechanism through the utilization of rich comparative evidence, thereby aiming to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of agent evolution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the observation that current self-improving coding agents, despite their advancements, rely on a limited form of diagnostic evidence for self-modification. Typically, an agent modifies its source code based on a single failure trajectory encountered during a task. This approach, while functional, is recognized as potentially suboptimal because it neglects the broader context and comparative signals present in the agent's accumulating history of interactions with various tasks.

The authors identify two specific types of comparative signals that are largely underutilized in existing frameworks:
1. **Reaction Norms:** When an agent is evaluated across multiple tasks, its performance profile (a pattern of successes and failures) forms a "reaction norm." This pattern can provide more robust diagnostic evidence, allowing for a better distinction between inherent, genotype-level defects and transient, task-specific errors. Recurring failure modes across diverse tasks are more indicative of fundamental weaknesses in the agent's design.
2. **Cross-Lineage Comparisons:** When multiple agents, possibly from different evolutionary lineages, attempt the same task, their diverse trajectories and outcomes can offer valuable contrasting evidence. By comparing a failing agent's trajectory with a successful one (or even two failing ones to identify complementary weaknesses), transferable behavioral traits can be identified and leveraged. This comparative analysis is intended to facilitate targeted ability transfer and reduce redundant exploratory modifications.

Building on these insights, the key objectives of the Mendel Gödel Machine (MGM) are:
- To integrate these comparative signals into the self-modification process, moving beyond single-trajectory-based editing.
- To design and implement new self-modification operators (reaction-norm mutation and cross-lineage hybridization) that effectively utilize these comparative evidences.
- To theoretically demonstrate that these new strategies lead to faster and more effective convergence in self-improvement compared to single-trajectory baselines.
- To empirically validate MGM's performance, efficiency, and generalizability on challenging software engineering benchmarks.
- To explore whether MGM can discover reusable, workflow-level improvements that support robust coding-agent performance and transfer across different tasks and underlying large language models.

By addressing the diagnostic bottleneck in self-modification, the work aims to enhance the scalability and efficacy of recursive self-improving coding agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MGM is an archive-based self-improvement framework that extends previous work like the Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) and the Huxley Gödel Machine (HGM). It maintains an archive of agent variants (genotypes) and their evaluation outcomes (phenotypes) as a lineage tree. The core innovation of MGM lies in refining the *expansion operator*, which is responsible for self-modifying an agent's source code, by introducing three specialized sub-operators based on the type of diagnostic evidence utilized.

#### 3.1. Mendelian Self-Modification Operators

MGM organizes self-modification through controlled inheritance mechanisms, inspired by Mendelian genetics, to isolate heritable effects through comparative analysis.

1. **Clonal Mutation (Φ_CM_):** This is the standard single-agent, single-trajectory self-improvement operator, akin to existing methods. It is applied when an agent (`a_i`) has failed a task (`τ`), and only a single failure trajectory `{(φ(a_i, τ), r(a_i, τ))}` is available as evidence. The agent modifies itself to address this specific failure. This operator ensures that the search can proceed even with limited comparative evidence.

2. **Reaction-Norm Mutation (Φ_RM_):** This operator leverages multiple trajectories from the *same agent* across different tasks. It becomes available when an agent has been evaluated on a sufficient number of distinct tasks (`|S_i| ≥ m_RM`), including a target failed task (`τ_t`) and at least one other reference task (`τ_r`). The evidence `{(φ(a_i, τ_t), r(a_i, τ_t)), (φ(a_i, τ_r), r(a_i, τ_r))}` allows the agent to identify recurring or inconsistent behavioral patterns, which can indicate a more general, genotype-level weakness rather than an isolated task-specific error. The agent is then prompted to implement a general improvement.

3. **Cross-Lineage Hybridization (Φ_CH_):** This operator compares trajectories of *different agents* (from distinct lineages) on the *same task*. It is eligible when two agents (`a_i`, `a_j`) have both attempted a shared task (`τ_t`) that is not solved by both. The evidence `{(φ(a_t, τ_t), r(a_t, τ_t)), (φ(a_r, τ_t), r(a_r, τ_t))}` is used, where `a_t` is the target agent to be improved and `a_r` is a reference agent. If the reference agent `a_r` succeeded on `τ_t` while `a_t` failed, the successful trajectory provides corrective signals to guide `a_t`'s modification. If both fail, the comparison helps identify complementary failure modes. The resulting child is attached to the primary lineage (the failing agent, or the higher-utility agent if both fail). This process aims to transfer useful behavioral traits across lineages without directly splicing source code.

#### 3.2. Sampling Tasks and Operators

MGM enhances the selection process for both tasks and self-modification operators:
- **Failed-Task Pool:** A global pool (`P_t`) is maintained, containing tasks that have caused failures in any previously evaluated agent. This pool is used to prioritize future evaluations.
- **Task Sampling:** When an agent is selected for evaluation, tasks not yet attempted by that agent are chosen, with a higher weight (`β_fail`) assigned to tasks in `P_t`. This strategy increases the diagnostic value of evaluations and fosters overlap across lineages, facilitating `Φ_CH`.
- **Operator Selection:** MGM uses HGM's Thompson sampling to decide between a `φ`-evaluation or a `Φ`-expansion. If an expansion is chosen, MGM first identifies which of its three operators (`Φ_CM`, `Φ_RM`, `Φ_CH`) are eligible based on the available evidence in the archive. An eligible operator is then sampled based on configurable weights (`λ_CM`, `λ_RM`, `λ_CH`).

#### 3.3. Theoretical Justification and Simulation

To rigorously evaluate the design, an additive fitness landscape model was developed. In this model, an agent's genotype is a binary vector, and a task requires a subset of its loci to be correct. A failed task reveals that at least one required locus is incorrect. Self-modification operators construct a "candidate set" of loci that might explain the failure. The effective fix probability (`p_σ_f`) depends on the density of truly incorrect loci within this candidate set.

**Proposition 1:** The paper theoretically demonstrates that `Φ_RM` and `Φ_CH` have a strictly higher effective fix probability than `Φ_CM`. This is because `Φ_RM` compresses the candidate set by identifying shared defects across multiple failures of the same agent. `Φ_CH` further refines the candidate set by using a contrasting successful reference trajectory to filter out non-causal task-relevant loci. This "diagnostic compression" reduces uncertainty, leading to more targeted and effective modifications.

**Monte Carlo Simulation:** Controlled simulations using this model were conducted across various initial difficulties (`d_0`) and fix-probability advantage ratios (`ρ`). This setup allowed isolating the impact of diagnostic quality on self-improvement, showing that MGM yields faster and better convergence compared to single-trajectory baselines, especially when `ρ > 1.0`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several key findings, supported by theoretical analysis, controlled simulations, and empirical experiments on software engineering benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Theoretical and Simulation Results

- **Diagnostic Advantage:** The theoretical model, an additive fitness landscape, indicated that MGM’s comparative operators (Reaction-norm Mutation, Φ_RM_, and Cross-lineage Hybridization, Φ_CH_) could achieve a strictly higher effective fix probability than Clonal Mutation (Φ_CM_). This is attributed to their ability to "compress" the set of candidate loci explaining a failure, reducing diagnostic uncertainty by leveraging comparative evidence.
- **Simulated Performance:** Controlled Monte Carlo simulations confirmed the theoretical advantage. MGM consistently outperformed single-trajectory baselines (DGM, HGM) across a range of initial task difficulties and diagnostic-advantage ratios (ρ). The performance gains in terms of faster convergence and lower final error were directly linked to higher ρ values, suggesting that improved diagnostic quality translates into more efficient self-improvement. When ρ was set to 1.0 (no diagnostic advantage), MGM's performance aligned with HGM, affirming that the observed gains were due to the comparative evidence.

#### 4.2. Empirical Performance and Efficiency

- **Benchmark Performance:** Experiments on SWE-bench Verified-60 and Polyglot-60, using Qwen3.6-35B-A3B as the backbone LLM and matched computational budgets (200 evaluations, 24 expansions), demonstrated MGM's superior performance:
 - On SWE-bench Verified-60, MGM improved the initial agent's accuracy from 68.3% to 78.3% (+10.0 percentage points), surpassing HGM's improvement to 73.3% (+5.0 points).
 - On Polyglot-60, MGM significantly increased accuracy from 50.8% to 93.2% (+42.4 points), outperforming HGM's 77.9% (+27.1 points).
- **Efficiency:** MGM achieved these performance gains within comparable wall-clock times and token consumption for all self-modification operators, indicating that the improvements were not due to increased resource expenditure but rather to the quality of diagnostic information.
- **Full Polyglot Evaluation:** The best MGM-evolved agent, using Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, achieved 93.3% accuracy on the complete 225-task Polyglot benchmark, solving 210 out of 225 tasks. This sustained high performance across multiple programming languages (C++, Go, Java, JavaScript, Python, Rust) suggests that MGM discovers reusable, language-agnostic workflow improvements.

#### 4.3. Generalization Capabilities

- **Cross-Benchmark Generalization:** Scaffolds evolved by MGM on Polyglot-60 showed positive transfer to unseen tasks on SWE-bench Pro and SWE-bench Multilingual.
 - MGM achieved 26.7% on SWE-bench Pro (+10.0 points over initial), while HGM saw a slight decrease (-3.4 points).
 - On SWE-bench Multilingual, MGM reached 55.0% (+13.3 points), outperforming HGM (+1.6 points).
 - These results suggest that MGM fosters the development of scaffold changes that generalize beyond the training benchmark's specific failure patterns, promoting reusable software engineering workflows.
- **Cross-Model Scaffold Transfer:** MGM-evolved scaffolds from SWE-bench Verified-60 (using Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) maintained their effectiveness when transferred to different backbone LLMs (DeepSeek-V4-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Pro). MGM consistently provided a larger performance boost across these models compared to HGM. For instance, with DeepSeek-V4-Pro, MGM achieved 75.0% accuracy compared to HGM's 70.0%. A transferred MGM scaffold from Qwen-evolved Polyglot to DeepSeek-V4-Pro achieved 96.9% on Polyglot. This indicates that MGM's improvements are embedded in the agent scaffold itself, rather than being specific to the original LLM backbone.
- **Qualitative Analysis:** Visualizations of evolved skills indicated that MGM's changes tend to concentrate around robust, workflow-level capabilities such as "exact test-contract extraction, API-contract adherence, and pre-implementation verification," which are broadly applicable across coding tasks.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

- An ablation study on Polyglot-60 revealed the individual contributions of MGM’s comparative operators.
 - Removing Reaction-norm Mutation (w/o Φ_RM_) led to a degradation in accuracy from 93.2% to 79.7%.
 - Removing Cross-lineage Hybridization (w/o Φ_CH_) resulted in a further drop to 74.6%.
 - This indicates that both Φ_RM_ and Φ_CH_ contribute significantly to MGM’s performance, with Φ_CH_ appearing particularly critical, suggesting its role in preserving and reusing useful evolutionary information across lineages.

#### 4.5. Impact of Backbone LLM Reasoning Ability

- An additional experiment using a larger, coding-specialized LLM (Qwen3-Coder-Next-80B-A3B) for evolution yielded lower performance for both MGM and HGM compared to the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B backbone, despite Qwen3-Coder-Next's larger size. This finding suggests that a backbone LLM's general reasoning and failure-diagnosis abilities are more critical for effective self-improvement than its raw coding specialization or parameter count. Stronger reasoning enables more accurate diagnoses from trajectories, leading to higher-quality, reusable scaffold modifications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Mendel Gödel Machine (MGM) represents an advancement in the field of self-improving coding agents by introducing a method for recursive self-modification that systematically leverages comparative evidence from an agent's expanding archive of past attempts.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Enhanced Self-Modification:** MGM shifts the focus of self-improvement from solely optimizing archive management and agent sampling to fundamentally enhancing the diagnostic quality of the self-modification process. By incorporating reaction-norm mutation and cross-lineage hybridization, it utilizes rich comparative signals (across tasks for the same agent, and across agents for the same task) that were previously underutilized.
- **Improved Performance and Efficiency:** The consistent and substantial performance gains demonstrated on challenging benchmarks like SWE-bench and Polyglot, achieved within comparable computational budgets, indicate that MGM facilitates more effective and efficient self-improvement.
- **Stronger Generalizability:** A critical contribution is the observed generalizability of MGM-evolved agent scaffolds across different software engineering benchmarks (SWE-bench Pro, SWE-bench Multilingual) and diverse backbone LLMs. This suggests that MGM discovers reusable, workflow-level improvements rather than merely generating task-specific patches, which is vital for practical agent development.
- **Theoretical Foundation:** The theoretical model and controlled simulations provide a formal understanding of how comparative evidence can reduce diagnostic uncertainty and accelerate the convergence of self-improvement processes.
- **Scalable Development Path:** The capability to evolve robust and transferable agent scaffolds using existing evaluation trajectories and, potentially, less resource-intensive backbone models, points towards a more scalable and compute-efficient approach for developing future self-improving agents.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

MGM's approach could contribute to the development of more autonomous and capable software engineering agents that can adapt and improve over time with less human intervention. By discovering generalizable coding workflows, it may reduce the need for extensive manual prompt engineering and agent design. This could accelerate progress in automated software development, maintenance, and bug fixing.

### 6. Limitations and Ethical Considerations

The authors acknowledge several limitations, including the high computational cost of self-improving agent experiments, which restricts extensive hyperparameter sweeps and independent evolution seeds. MGM's performance is also history-dependent, requiring sufficient accumulated comparative evidence to realize its full potential. Furthermore, while MGM enhances diagnostic evidence, the quality of the resulting self-edit still relies on the underlying LLM-based editor.

Ethical considerations highlight the dual-use implications of self-improving coding agents, emphasizing the risk of generating malicious software or exploiting vulnerabilities if not deployed within strictly sandboxed environments. The potential for inheriting biases from pretraining data and benchmarks is also noted, stressing the need for explicit safety and fairness reviews for any deployment beyond controlled research settings.
