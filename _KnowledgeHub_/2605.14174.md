# Safety-Constrained Reinforcement Learning with Post-Training Reachability Verification for Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces VIA, a framework combining CVaR-constrained reinforcement learning with post-training neural network reachability verification to train robust and formally verifiable robot navigation policies. The method achieved a 98.3% success rate and 1.7% collision rate in simulation, demonstrating that policies trained with risk-sensitive objectives maintain greater safety margins and are more amenable to formal verification under observation uncertainty, even exhibiting consistent safety rates during sim-to-real transfer.

## Problem

- Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) policies often overlook 'tail-risk' behaviors in safety-critical robot navigation, as average reward/cost metrics are insufficient for quantifying robustness under perception uncertainty.
- Existing safe RL methods, particularly those based on expectation-constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs), provide only statistical safety guarantees and do not explicitly control for high-consequence, rare unsafe outcomes.
- Formally verifying learned robot policies is challenging due to their complexity, and often relies on analytical models or differentiable safety functions, which are hard to obtain for general neural network controllers.

## Method

- A two-phase framework, **VIA**, was developed, integrating ==CVaR-constrained off-policy optimization== within a ==TD3== architecture for training risk-sensitive navigation policies.
- A ==distributional cost critic== with a ==noncrossing quantile logit network== and an ==adaptive Value-at-Risk (VaR)== update mechanism were used to stably estimate and constrain ==Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)== on cumulative costs.
- A ==post-training safety verification phase== employs ==POLAR reachability analysis== to formally compute guaranteed action bounds under bounded observation uncertainty, defining a ==Safety Rate== metric to quantify policy robustness.

## Results

- VIA achieved superior performance with a **98.3%** success rate and **1.7%** collision rate in simulation, outperforming unconstrained and expectation-constrained baselines in both task completion and safety.
- Reachability verification showed VIA policies maintaining a **99.6%** 'Safety (Succ.)' rate, significantly higher than other methods, indicating larger safety margins and better verifiability despite sometimes having a slightly higher average empirical cost.
- The trained VIA policy successfully transferred to a physical Clearpath Jackal robot, maintaining consistent overall safety rates (**99.1%** in sim vs. **99.2%** in real) and similar reachable set widths for actions, demonstrating practical applicability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - CVaR-constrained training promotes more conservative and tightly bounded action distributions, making learned policies inherently more amenable to formal reachability verification compared to unconstrained or expectation-constrained methods.
> - Reachability verification uncovers safety risks that are not adequately captured by traditional average cost metrics, as policies with similar average costs can have vastly different robustness margins under uncertainty.
> - In robot navigation, increasing CVaR confidence (focusing on more extreme tail risks) aligns with better task performance, indicating that avoiding worst-case scenarios also leads to higher success rates and greater safety.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The safe deployment of autonomous mobile robots in complex and uncertain environments is a critical challenge in robotics. Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) methods, which typically optimize for average reward or minimize average cumulative cost, often overlook "tail-risk" behaviors—rare but high-consequence events that can lead to catastrophic failures like collisions. These conventional metrics provide statistical insights into nominal performance but fail to quantify robustness under perception uncertainty, such as sensor noise.

Existing approaches to safe RL primarily fall into two categories:
- **Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs):** These methods formulate safety as a constraint on cumulative costs during training. Many approaches enforce these constraints in expectation, which can limit the overall frequency of unsafe events but does not explicitly control for high-consequence outcomes that occur in the tail of the cost distribution. Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) methods address this by constraining the expected cost beyond a chosen quantile, focusing on the worst-case fraction of outcomes. However, existing CVaR-based RL methods often rely on distributional assumptions, may suffer from sample inefficiency due to on-policy updates, or provide only statistical guarantees.
- **Formal Verification and Runtime Enforcement:** Methods such as Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability, and shielding mechanisms aim to provide stronger safety guarantees. These approaches typically operate at runtime, constraining or overriding unsafe actions based on closed-loop system dynamics. A common limitation is their reliance on known analytical models or differentiable safety functions, which can be challenging to obtain for complex learned policies. Neural network reachability verification tools, such as Polynomial Arithmetic for Reachability (POLAR), offer a complementary approach by computing guaranteed bounds on a trained network's outputs given interval input perturbations. This allows for direct assessment of whether a policy's action set remains within prescribed safety margins, a form of *post-training* safety assessment.

The presented work integrates CVaR-constrained policy optimization with post-training neural network reachability verification. It addresses the limitation that while CVaR can shape policies towards risk-sensitivity, it still offers only statistical guarantees. Simultaneously, it tackles the challenge that unconstrained or expectation-only policies often produce broad, less conservative action distributions, which makes them difficult to formally verify using reachability analysis. By explicitly combining risk-sensitive training (CVaR) with formal verification (POLAR), this research aims to produce policies that are not only statistically safe but also formally verifiable under bounded observation uncertainty, bridging a critical gap in the development of robust and reliable autonomous navigation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework for training robot navigation policies that are both robust to high-consequence tail-risk events and formally verifiable under bounded perception uncertainty. This objective is motivated by several identified shortcomings in current safe reinforcement learning and verification methodologies:

1. **Inadequacy of Average Cost Metrics:** Standard RL policies are often evaluated based on average reward or average cumulative cost. The authors argue that these metrics are insufficient for safety-critical applications because they can mask dangerous "tail-risk" behaviors. As illustrated by the paper's introductory figure, two policies might achieve similar average performance (e.g., reaching a goal without nominal collision) but exhibit vastly different latent collision risks when considering the full range of possible actions under bounded observation uncertainty. This discrepancy highlights the need for more comprehensive safety assessments.
2. **Limitations of Expectation-Based Safety Constraints:** While constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) provide a principled way to enforce safety during training, most existing methods enforce constraints only on the *expected* cumulative cost. This approach limits the overall frequency of unsafe events but provides inadequate control over rare, high-consequence outcomes. The motivation here is to move beyond average-case guarantees to address worst-case scenarios explicitly.
3. **Need for Risk-Sensitive Policy Training:** To address the limitations of expectation-based constraints, the authors motivate the use of Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). CVaR focuses on the expected cost over the tail of the distribution beyond a chosen quantile, thereby encouraging policies that are sensitive to and robust against high-cost events, rather than merely performing well on average.
4. **Bridging Statistical Training Signals with Formal Guarantees:** A fundamental limitation of both expectation-based and CVaR-based training approaches is that they provide only *statistical* safety signals. A policy deemed safe by training metrics might still produce unsafe actions when deployed under real-world observation perturbations. This motivates the integration of formal verification methods, which can reason over the *set* of all possible policy outputs under bounded uncertainty, providing stronger, non-statistical guarantees.
5. **Enhancing Verifiability through Training:** Unconstrained or expectation-constrained policies often learn broader, less conservative action distributions, which can result in reachable sets that frequently encroach on unsafe regions, making formal verification challenging or impossible. The authors hypothesize that CVaR-constrained training, by promoting more conservative and tightly bounded action distributions, can make policies more amenable to formal reachability verification.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives are:
- To design a CVaR-constrained optimization framework integrated into an off-policy TD3 architecture, incorporating a distributional cost critic and an adaptive VaR update mechanism for stable tail-risk estimation.
- To develop a post-training safety verification framework using POLAR reachability analysis, combined with layered safety boundaries, to quantify policy robustness under bounded observation uncertainty via a "safety rate" metric.
- To demonstrate that policies trained with CVaR constraints exhibit larger safety margins and are more amenable to formal reachability verification compared to unconstrained or expectation-constrained baselines.
- To validate the proposed approach in simulation and demonstrate successful sim-to-real transfer on a physical robot.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named VIA (Verification-Informed safe reinforcement learning for Autonomous navigation), operates in two distinct phases: a training phase involving CVaR-constrained off-policy optimization, and a post-training verification phase utilizing reachability analysis.

**Phase I: CVaR-Constrained Off-Policy Training**

The training phase aims to develop a risk-sensitive navigation policy by maximizing expected reward subject to a CVaR constraint on cumulative costs. It is built upon the Twin-Delayed DDPG (TD3) architecture, which is an off-policy algorithm known for its sample efficiency.

1. **Problem Formulation:** The problem is framed as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) where the policy `π` maximizes expected cumulative reward `J_R(π)` subject to `CVaR_α(C_π) ≤ b`. The cost function `C(s_t, a_t)` is binary, set to 1 if the minimum distance to an obstacle `d_min(s_t)` falls below a `d_danger` threshold (0.5m), and 0 otherwise. A `d_col` threshold (0.4m) is used for episode termination.

2. **State Augmentation for CVaR Estimation:** To allow the policy to be sensitive to accumulated costs, the state space is augmented. The agent observes `s_bar_t = (s_t, e_t)`, where `e_t` is a dynamic cost budget. `e_0` is initialized to `u` (an adaptive parameter approximating the α-quantile of cumulative cost), and `e_t+1 = (e_t - C(s_t, a_t))/γ` tracks the remaining budget, enabling the cost critic to estimate the conditional cost distribution.

3. **Distributional Cost Critic:** To estimate CVaR, which depends on the tail of the cost distribution, a distributional cost critic `Q_C_ϕ` is employed. This critic uses a noncrossing quantile logit network that outputs multiple quantiles `{q_i}` to prevent quantile crossing (where `q_i > q_j` for `i < j`). The network learns to estimate the full cost distribution. From these quantiles, the CVaR-related cost value `V_C_hat(s_bar_t, a_t)` is computed as the conditional mean of predicted quantiles that exceed the current budget `e_t`. The critic is trained using a Huber quantile regression loss to ensure accurate quantile estimates.

4. **VaR Parameter Adaptive Update:** A key challenge in off-policy CVaR optimization is stably estimating the Value-at-Risk (VaR) quantile `u`. VIA uses a gradient-free adaptive update mechanism for `u`. `u_0` is initialized as the empirical α-quantile from a warm-up phase. In subsequent epochs, `u` is updated based on the empirical exceedance probability `P_hat(C ≥ u_k)` of observed episodic costs, driving `u` towards the true α-quantile. This method is compatible with off-policy learning as it does not rely on direct critic evaluations for the VaR update.

5. **CVaR-Constrained Policy Optimization:** The policy `π` is optimized using Lagrangian relaxation. The actor's objective combines maximizing the expected reward (from the standard TD3 reward critic `Q_R_ψ`) with minimizing a Lagrangian penalty term related to the CVaR constraint. The actor loss is `L_π = -E[Q_R_ψ(s_bar, π(s_bar))] + λ ⋅ E[V_C_hat(s_bar, π(s_bar))]`. The Lagrange multiplier `λ` is updated based on the empirical constraint slack `(b - u_k - C_bar_k)`, where `C_bar_k` is the average episodic cost of the current epoch, offering a stable constraint signal for off-policy updates.

**Phase II: Post-Training Safety Verification**

After the policy is trained, its robustness and safety margins are formally assessed using reachability analysis under bounded observation uncertainty.

1. **POLAR Reachability Analysis:** The POLAR (Polynomial Arithmetic for Reachability) tool is used. POLAR propagates Taylor Models (TMs) through the neural network. A TM represents an uncertain variable as a polynomial over symbolic variables plus an interval remainder. Given a state `s` and a bounded observation uncertainty `ϵ`, the perturbed input `s_tilde = s + ϵ ⋅ z` (where `z` is a symbolic variable in `[-1, 1]`) is propagated through the network layers. Linear transformations and activation functions (approximated via Bernstein polynomials) are applied, ultimately yielding an action reachable set `A(s, ϵ) = [a_lower, a_upper]` that provides guaranteed bounds on all possible actions the policy might output under the given uncertainty. During verification, the augmented state's `e_t` component is fixed to the converged VaR parameter `u`.

2. **Safety Verification Criterion:** To determine if a state `s` is "verified safe," actions are sampled from the computed reachable set `A(s, ϵ)`. Specifically, nine combinations covering the bounds and midpoint of the 2D action space (linear and angular velocity) are tested. For each sampled action, the robot's motion is simulated over a control interval `∆t` using a differential-drive kinematic model. The robot's swept area at each time step is checked against the obstacle map. A state `s` is declared `Safe(s)` if *no collision* (`d_min > d_col`) is detected across all nine sampled actions and their resulting trajectories.

3. **Safety Rate Metric:** Based on the per-state safety assessment, a "Safety Rate" metric is defined as the proportion of evaluated states along recorded trajectories that satisfy the `Safe(s)` criterion. This metric quantifies the policy's robustness margin under observation uncertainty, offering a verification-informed safety measure beyond empirical collision rates.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted in a Gazebo-based simulation environment (10m x 10m arena with 8 obstacles) using a TurtleBot3 robot with LiDAR. The state space is 25-dimensional (LiDAR, goal info, previous actions), and the action space is 2-dimensional (linear/angular velocity). Safety thresholds are `d_col = 0.4m` and `d_danger = 0.5m`. Training involves 100 epochs, 70 episodes per epoch, `γ = 0.99`, and a CVaR risk level `α = 0.9` with cost threshold `b = 10`. POLAR verification uses an observation error `ϵ = 0.01`. Performance is evaluated using Success Rate, Collision Rate, Average Cost, and the three variants of Safety Rate (over successful, collision, and all episodes). Baselines include unconstrained RL (TD3, TD7), expectation-constrained RL (SAC-Lagrangian, RCPO), and CVaR-constrained RL (WCSAC, CVaR-CPO), all adapted to the TD3 backbone.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments addressed four primary questions regarding the performance of the VIA framework in robot navigation, its safety verification capabilities, the influence of CVaR confidence levels, and its transferability to real-world robots.

**Q1: CVaR-Based Risk Formulation vs. Baselines**
The results demonstrate that the CVaR-based risk formulation significantly improves navigation and safety performance. VIA achieved a success rate of 98.3% and a collision rate of 1.7%, which were the highest success rate and lowest collision rate, respectively, among all compared methods. In comparison, unconstrained methods (TD3, TD7) had lower success rates and higher collision rates. Expectation-constrained methods like SAC-Lagrangian exhibited the highest collision rate at 36.0%, indicating their inability to adequately address tail risks. CVaR-based baselines (WCSAC, CVaR-CPO) also performed better than expectation-constrained methods, but VIA surpassed them in both task performance and safety verification rates.

**Q2: Reachability Verification for Uncaptured Risks**
A key finding is that average cost metrics alone are insufficient indicators of safety, and reachability verification can reveal safety risks not captured by these metrics. RCPO achieved the lowest average cost (1.17) among constrained methods, which would typically suggest it is the safest. However, VIA, despite a slightly higher average cost (1.52), demonstrated superior reachability-based safety. VIA achieved 99.6% Safety (Succ.) and 94.4% Safety (Coll.), compared to RCPO's 98.5% and 91.7%, respectively. This indicates that VIA policies maintain larger safety margins from obstacles across evaluated states, making them more amenable to formal verification. This divergence between average cost rankings and reachability-based safety rankings highlights that reachability verification captures robustness to bounded observation uncertainty, a risk aspect missed by empirical cost metrics alone.

**Q3: Effect of CVaR Confidence Level (α)**
An ablation study on the CVaR confidence level `α` revealed that increasing `α` (which makes the policy more conservative by focusing on a smaller, more extreme tail of the cost distribution) consistently improved both task performance and safety verification rates. For `α = 0.1`, VIA achieved an 86.0% success rate and 98.7% overall safety. For `α = 0.5`, these increased to 90.0% success and 97.6% overall safety. At `α = 0.9`, the policy achieved its best performance with a 98.3% success rate and 99.1% overall safety. This suggests that in robot navigation, avoiding worst-case scenarios and achieving task completion are aligned objectives. Policies trained with higher `α` levels produced more consistent paths and narrower reachable action sets, indicating greater conservatism and robustness.

**Q4: Sim-to-Real Transfer Validation**
The trained VIA policy (`α = 0.9`) was deployed on a physical Clearpath Jackal robot to validate sim-to-real transfer. While there was a slight decrease in task-level performance in the real world (success rate dropped from 98.3% to 90.0%, and collision rate increased from 1.7% to 8.0%), reflecting the inherent challenges of physical deployment (e.g., sensor noise, actuation delays), the overall safety rate remained consistent. The overall safety rate was 99.1% in simulation and 99.2% in the real world. Furthermore, the reachable set widths for linear and angular velocity remained nearly identical across both domains (e.g., linear velocity width: 0.019 m/s in sim vs. 0.020 m/s in real). This consistency suggests that VIA's conservative, CVaR-constrained policy maintains its verified safety margins even under real-world perturbations, demonstrating the practical applicability of the approach.

In summary, VIA achieves high success and low collision rates by focusing on tail-risk events through CVaR constraints. More importantly, it demonstrates that formal reachability verification provides a distinct and critical measure of safety that is not captured by conventional empirical cost metrics. Policies shaped by CVaR constraints inherently yield tighter action reachable sets, which makes them more amenable to robust safety verification.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of safe autonomous systems, particularly in robot navigation, by addressing key limitations of existing reinforcement learning and verification methodologies.

**Bridging Statistical and Formal Safety Guarantees:** The most notable significance of VIA lies in its integration of statistical risk-sensitive training (CVaR) with formal, post-training reachability verification. This framework directly tackles the gap where statistically safe policies might still be unsafe under deployment uncertainties, and formally verifiable policies are often difficult to obtain from complex learned controllers. By designing a training process that explicitly promotes verifiability, VIA offers a pathway to policies with stronger, more comprehensive safety assurances.

**Enhanced Safety for Autonomous Robot Navigation:** The demonstrated ability of VIA to achieve high success rates while maintaining low collision rates and high verification rates under bounded observation uncertainty is critical for the reliable deployment of mobile robots in real-world, cluttered environments. This approach provides a method for developing navigation policies that are not only robust to sensor noise and unmodeled disturbances but also demonstrably so through formal methods.

**Refined Safety Metrics and Evaluation:** The introduction of a reachability-based "safety rate" metric offers a more nuanced and rigorous way to quantify policy safety compared to traditional empirical collision rates or average costs. This metric assesses the policy's inherent robustness margin, providing insights into its behavior under the full range of potential observation perturbations, rather than just observed failures. This can lead to more trustworthy and predictive safety evaluations.

**Novelty in Off-Policy CVaR Optimization:** The development of a stable, off-policy CVaR-constrained optimization framework, particularly through the adaptive VaR update mechanism and noncrossing quantile networks, advances the state of the art in applying CVaR to practical RL problems. This contributes to more sample-efficient learning of risk-sensitive behaviors.

**Practical Validation and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful deployment on a physical Clearpath Jackal robot, maintaining consistent safety rates despite a slight degradation in task performance, highlights the practical relevance and transferability of the proposed methodology. This makes VIA a promising candidate for real-world applications where safety is paramount.

**Future Research Directions:** The paper also implicitly outlines important avenues for future research. The current limitation regarding compact network architectures for Taylor Model arithmetic suggests a need for advancements in scalable verification methods (e.g., GPU-accelerated techniques, network compression). Furthermore, the prospect of integrating verification signals directly into the training loop, rather than solely as a post-training step, represents a frontier for developing truly closed-loop safe reinforcement learning systems that can continuously refine their safety guarantees. This work thus serves as a foundational step towards more robust, verifiable, and ultimately safer AI systems for robotics.
