# Plug-and-Play Label Map Diffusion for Universal Goal-Oriented Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> A plug-and-play diffusion framework named PLMD enhances goal-oriented navigation in partially observed environments by completing Bird's-Eye-View (BEV) label maps with obstacle-guided semantic predictions. It significantly improves success rates and path efficiency across ObjectNav, Instance-ImageNav, and Multi-Robot ObjectNav tasks, outperforming prior map completion methods.

## Problem

- Goal-Oriented Navigation (GON) methods often rely on complete environmental maps, performing poorly in partially observed or unexplored settings.
- Existing generative models for map completion struggle with structural consistency, leading to physically implausible predictions like room boundary drift and semantic hallucinations in Bird's-Eye-View (BEV) maps.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) based GON methods frequently exhibit low sample efficiency and difficulty in capturing fine-grained semantic context for effective long-term planning.

## Method

- A Plug-and-Play Label Map Diffusion (PLMD) framework based on Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) generates complete obstacle and semantic label maps from partial observations.
- PLMD employs two coupled diffusion networks, where a semantic map network is explicitly conditioned on an obstacle map network, using Spatially-Adaptive DEnormalization (SPADE) to integrate structural priors.
- The framework is designed as a plug-and-play module that provides restored label maps to existing map-dependent **Goal-Oriented** Navigation (GON) strategies, enhancing goal localization and planning without re-training the base navigators.

## Results

- PLMD significantly improves Goal-Oriented Navigation (GON) performance, achieving a **5.4%** increase in Success Rate (SR) for ObjectNav over SGM, a **7%** SR increase for Instance-ImageNav over IEVE, and state-of-the-art SR and SPL in Multi-Robot ObjectNav.
- The framework generates more accurate and complete map predictions, evidenced by a superior PSNR of **34.284** compared to **29.895** for IR-SDE and **31.486** for StrDiffusion on the MRON task.
- Ablation studies confirm the critical role of obstacle map priors and the combined label map representation, with their removal leading to substantial drops in SR and PSNR, validating the design choices.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly incorporating obstacle map priors into the semantic denoising process is crucial for generating geometrically sound and semantically consistent map completions, preventing issues like boundary drift.
> - Operating at a 'label-level' by combining both obstacle and semantic information provides richer contextual environmental relationships for navigation systems.
> - A modular, plug-and-play map completion system can universally enhance diverse existing Goal-Oriented Navigation strategies across ObjectNav, Instance-ImageNav, and Multi-Robot ObjectNav tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Goal-Oriented Navigation (GON) in embodied vision tasks requires robots to locate a specified goal, such as an object category or an instance image, within an initially unexplored environment. A significant challenge in GON is the need for robots to build an understanding of their surroundings, often through constructing a Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) map, while simultaneously localizing an unobserved goal.

Existing GON approaches typically fall into two categories: end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) methods and modular approaches. RL-based methods directly learn goal-directed exploration policies but often suffer from low sample efficiency and difficulty in capturing fine-grained semantic context. Modular methods, on the other hand, construct semantic or 3D maps to infer plausible goal locations by leveraging semantic relationships. However, a common limitation of these map-based modular methods is their reliance on complete environmental maps. When operating in unexplored or partially observed environments, these methods become less reliable for reasoning about unobserved map regions, potentially leading to inefficient exploration or failure to localize the goal.

Attempts to address incomplete map visibility have included employing generative models for map completion. Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) have shown promise in semantic generation and image inpainting, providing a mechanism to complete missing parts of semantic maps. Previous work has explored constraining diffusion models to learn statistical distribution patterns of objects in semantic maps to generate goal locations more effectively. However, these methods often focus on correlations between scene object semantics, potentially overlooking the structural consistency between known and unknown obstacle layouts. This can lead to issues such as room boundary drift and semantic hallucinations in unobserved regions, especially in BEV maps characterized by large free spaces and sparse object pixels.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing the problem of incomplete BEV semantic maps in GON. It builds upon the capabilities of diffusion models for map completion but introduces explicit guidance from obstacle information. By integrating obstacle priors into the semantic denoising process, the proposed method aims to mitigate inconsistencies and generate more geometrically plausible and semantically coherent completions, thereby enhancing universal goal-oriented navigation in partially observed settings. The work differentiates itself by focusing on a "label-level" map completion that explicitly encodes both obstacles and semantic categories, rather than solely pixel-level semantic predictions, and by designing a "plug-and-play" module compatible with existing navigation strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance of map-based Goal-Oriented Navigation (GON) strategies, particularly in environments where the robot operates with partially observed or incomplete Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) maps. The authors aim to overcome limitations in existing map-based methods, specifically:

1. **Reliance on Complete Maps:** Current map-based GON methods often assume or require a complete understanding of the environment. In real-world scenarios, robots start in unknown environments and build maps incrementally, leaving large portions unobserved. This incompleteness hinders effective long-term planning and goal localization.
2. **Inconsistent Semantic Association:** While generative models like Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) have been explored for map completion, they often focus predominantly on semantic correlations between objects. This approach can neglect the underlying structural consistency of the environment, leading to physically implausible predictions such as drifting room boundaries or semantic hallucinations in unobserved regions. BEV maps, with their sparse object pixels and extensive free space, exacerbate this issue when only semantic associations are considered.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes the following key objectives:

- **Develop a novel map completion diffusion model:** The model, termed Plug-and-Play Label Map Diffusion (PLMD), is designed to generate obstacle and semantic labels for unobserved regions. This enables goal localization even in partially observed environments.
- **Integrate structural consistency:** A core objective is to leverage the structural consistency between known and unknown obstacle layouts. This involves incorporating obstacle priors into the semantic denoising process to ensure geometrically sound and semantically consistent map completions, preventing issues like boundary drift.
- **Operate at label granularity:** The system aims to construct a "Label Map" that explicitly encodes both obstacles and scene object categories. This multi-level representation is intended to provide navigation strategies with richer contextual environmental relationships (e.g., interactions between objects and potential obstacles).
- **Ensure plug-and-play compatibility:** PLMD is designed as an auxiliary module that can be seamlessly integrated with existing map-dependent GON strategies without requiring their re-training. This allows for broad applicability across different navigation frameworks.
- **Demonstrate universal applicability:** The research aims to show that PLMD can effectively assist in various GON tasks, including ObjectNav (ON), Instance-ImageNav (IIN), and Multi-Robot ObjectNav (MRON), across different realistic 3D environments.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to improve the accuracy and efficiency of goal localization and navigation in previously unseen and partially explored indoor environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Plug-and-Play Label Map Diffusion (PLMD) framework is designed to complete semantic and obstacle maps for unexplored regions, thereby assisting existing Goal-Oriented Navigation (GON) strategies. The methodology encompasses label map construction, a diffusion-based training process, and a strategy for navigation utilizing the predicted maps.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Label Map Construction

At each timestep, the robot captures RGB-D observations and pose information. Depth images and semantic labels are projected into 3D space. Points within a predefined height range are marked as occupied, discretized into a voxel occupancy grid, and integrated to form an egocentric map. This egocentric representation is transformed to a geocentric frame using the robot's pose and fused with global semantic labels. The output is a map $M_t \in \mathbb{R}^{(n+4) \times H \times W}$, where $n$ is the number of semantic classes, plus channels for occupancy, free space, and robot position.

The core of PLMD's map representation is the **Label Map**, which merges an **Obstacle Map** (from occupancy and free-space info) and a **Semantic Map**. These are rendered using fixed color palettes for visualization. The challenge addressed is that these label maps are only partial observations, necessitating inference about unobserved regions.

#### 3.2. Label Map Diffusion Policy

PLMD utilizes a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) framework with a Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) formulation to reconstruct complete visualized label maps from masked observations.

**Label Map Data Collection:**
To train PLMD, a dedicated dataset of label map observation pairs is collected. A robot, using a Frontier Exploration Strategy (FBE), navigates from multiple initial positions in various indoor environments (HM3D v0.1 and MP3D). Incomplete label maps are stored every 25 navigation steps. The fully explored semantic map ($s_{gt}$) and obstacle map ($c_{gt}$) at the final step ($F$) form the ground truth. An observation pair $O_{label} = \{(s_{gt}, s_m_t, c_{gt}, c_m_t)\}$ includes the full ground truth maps and their masked versions ($s_m_t$, $c_m_t$), where masks ($m$) indicate unobserved regions. This process generates a dataset $A$ of 238,800 processed observation pairs, split 8:2 for training and validation.

**Diffusion Training Process:**
Given a full semantic map ($s_{gt}$), a full obstacle map ($c_{gt}$), and a binary mask ($m$), masked inputs $s_m = m \odot s_{gt}$ and $c_m = m \odot c_{gt}$ are created. The training involves two coupled diffusion networks:

1. **Obstacle Map Network ($G_{\phi}$):** This network learns to complete the obstacle map. The diffusion process for the obstacle map state $c_{\tau}$ (from $c_0 = c_{gt}$ to $c_T = \mu_c + G$, where $\mu_c = c_m$) is defined by an SDE. The reverse denoising process estimates the score functions $\nabla_c \log p_{\tau}(c)$ by training a conditional time-dependent neural network $G_{\phi}(c_{\tau}, \mu_c, \tau)$. It optimizes $c_{\tau-1}^*$ by minimizing the negative log-likelihood, leading to an objective function $L_{\alpha}(\phi)$. This network is pre-trained to obtain robust obstacle map priors.
2. **Semantic Map Network ($\tilde{G}_{\phi}$):** This network learns to complete the semantic map, *conditioned* on the refined obstacle map from $G_{\phi}$. The semantic denoising process operates similarly to the obstacle map network but takes $s_{\tau}$ and the *intermediate refined obstacle map $c_{\tau-1}$* as conditions: $\tilde{G}_{\phi}(s_{\tau}, c_{\tau-1}, \tau)$. The objective is to find the optimal reversed semantic state $s_{\tau-1}^*$ by minimizing a negative log-likelihood, resulting in objective function $L_{\zeta}(\phi)$.

Crucially, $\tilde{G}_{\phi}$ integrates obstacle map information via an **obstacle-aware feature modulation network**. As shown in Figure 2(b), this network uses Spatially-Adaptive DEnormalization (SPADE) residual blocks. For a feature map $f_{\tau}^k$, it's modulated as $\hat{f}_{\tau}^k = W_{\gamma}^{(k)}(c_{\tau-1})f_{\tau}^k + b_{\beta}^{(k)}(c_{\tau-1})$, where $W_{\gamma}^{(k)}$ and $b_{\beta}^{(k)}$ are scaling and biasing parameters derived from the obstacle map $c_{\tau-1}$. This mechanism provides stable structural constraints during early semantic diffusion steps, preventing physically implausible room geometries. After pre-training, $G_{\phi}$ is frozen during $\tilde{G}_{\phi}$ training.

#### 3.3. Navigation with Predicted Label Map

The trained PLMD acts as a plug-and-play auxiliary module for any embodied navigation strategy.

**Label Map Restored:**
For a single robot, its current RGB-D view, pose, and goal are used to construct visualized semantic ($S_{vt}$) and obstacle ($C_{vt}$) map vectors. These maps are cropped to local semantic ($s_t$) and obstacle ($c_t$) maps and their masked versions ($s_m_t$, $c_m_t$).
The trained $G_{\phi}$ initializes $c_{\tau t}$ with noise and iteratively denoises it over $T$ steps to produce the final obstacle prediction $c_{0t}$. Similarly, $s_{\tau t}$ is initialized with noise and denoised by $\tilde{G}_{\phi}$, which leverages multi-scale obstacle priors from $G_{\phi}$'s intermediate outputs $c_{\tau-1 t}$ using SPADE blocks. The outputs $c_{0t}$ and $s_{0t}$ are upscaled and converted into predicted obstacle map vector $C_t^P$ and semantic map vector $S_t^P$. Their concatenation forms the predicted label map $L_t^P = [S_t^P, C_t^P]$. This $L_t^P$ serves as a high-level prediction for goal-candidate proposal and planning guidance, not replacing observed map evidence.

**Localization Strategy:**
Before the navigation strategy executes, goal pixel patches are searched in $L_t^P$. HDBSCAN clustering is applied to goal pixel coordinates to identify potential navigation goals (pixel clusters matching the goal color). A composite score is calculated for each cluster center based on cluster density (50% weight), cluster size (40% weight), and distance to the starting point (10% weight). The center with the highest score is selected as the long-term goal. If no valid clustering is found, the navigation strategy executes its default exploration. A local navigation strategy (e.g., Fast Marching Method) is then used to reach the selected goal. PLMD continuously generates predicted maps based on the updated label map until a candidate goal is identified or the navigation strategy locates the goal.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates PLMD across three Goal-Oriented Navigation (GON) tasks: ObjectNav (ON), Instance-ImageNav (IIN), and Multi-Robot ObjectNav (MRON), using Habitat-Matterport3D (HM3D v0.1, v0.2) and Matterport3D (MP3D) datasets. Performance is measured by Success Rate (SR), Path Length-Weighted Success Rate (SPL), and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) for map completion quality.

#### 4.1. Plug-in Performance with Existing Methods

PLMD is demonstrated to integrate seamlessly with mainstream navigation strategies.
- **ObjectNav (ON):** When combined with OpenFMNav on HM3D v0.1, PLMD-assisted navigation achieves an SR of 0.656 and SPL of 0.333, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art SGM (0.602 SR, 0.308 SPL) by 5.4% in SR. When evaluated without an RL policy, it still achieves a high SR.
- **Instance-ImageNav (IIN):** On HM3D v0.2, PLMD combined with RL shows a significant improvement over IEVE (0.776 SR, 0.283 SPL vs. 0.702 SR, 0.252 SPL), demonstrating a 7% increase in SR. This suggests that more complete label maps better leverage RL's planning potential.
- **Multi-Robot ObjectNav (MRON):** PLMD achieves state-of-the-art performance in both centralized (Co-NavGPT) and decentralized (MCoCoNav) MRON tasks on HM3D v0.2 and MP3D. For instance, on HM3D v0.2, PLMD combined with MCoCoNav achieves 0.762 SR and 0.406 SPL, surpassing MCoCoNav's standalone performance (0.634 SR, 0.297 SPL) and Co-NavGPT (0.661 SR, 0.331 SPL).
- **Ground Truth Comparison:** Experiments comparing PLMD-predicted label maps with ground-truth label maps (which represent perfect semantic and obstacle distributions) show that the ground-truth maps lead to even higher SR and SPL (e.g., 0.742 SR / 0.425 SPL for ON on HM3D v0.2), further validating the importance of accurate map completion.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Different Diffusion Methods

PLMD's efficacy in generating unknown map regions is compared against IR-SDE and StrDiffusion on the MRON task (Table 2, Figure 3).
- IR-SDE directly encodes semantic maps, while StrDiffusion uses structural sparsity priors. Neither explicitly incorporates obstacle map priors during denoising or operates on a combined label map.
- PLMD achieves higher SR (0.762 vs. 0.698/0.729), SPL (0.406 vs. 0.370/0.374), and PSNR (34.284 vs. 29.895/31.486). This indicates PLMD produces more accurate and complete map predictions, especially for obstacles, which other methods struggle with. The authors attribute this to PLMD's explicit modeling of obstacle structure as a prior.

#### 4.3. Discussion of PLMD Execution Frequency

Evaluations across ON, IIN, and MRON demonstrate that initiating PLMD after 100 navigation steps and repeating every 50 steps thereafter is generally optimal (Diffuse@ for ON/MRON, Diffuse@ for IIN, where numbers represent global steps for starting and repetition, respectively). This strategy enhances SR and SPL while reducing the average number of navigation steps. The results suggest that prediction should begin after sufficient environmental data is collected and maps should be refreshed periodically.

#### 4.4. Evaluations of Open-Vocabulary Goal

To test generalization, PLMD was retrained with Grounded SAM for open-vocabulary segmentation (PLMD$^\dagger$). In MRON experiments on HM3D v0.2 with unseen categories (lamp, toy car, microwave), PLMD$^\dagger$ achieved 0.354 SR and 0.268 SPL, outperforming the original PLMD (0.323 SR, 0.225 SPL) and baselines. This indicates the architecture's transferability to open-vocabulary tasks, though with increased computational overhead.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies on HM3D v0.2 confirm the contributions of various PLMD components (Table 3):
- **Impact of Network $G_{\phi}$ (Obstacle map prior):** Removing $G_{\phi}$ (i.e., not using obstacle map as a prior) significantly decreases SR across tasks (e.g., 4.8% for MRON) and reduces PSNR by 3.847, confirming the importance of obstacle priors.
- **Effect of Obstacle Map:** Eliminating the entire obstacle map component leads to even more substantial performance drops (e.g., 4.5% SR for MRON), highlighting its critical role.
- **Impact of Clustering:** The clustering mechanism for the localization strategy is particularly crucial for IIN, where its absence causes a 1.9% SR decline. Its effect is smaller but still noticeable for ON and MRON.
- **Image Replacement:** Directly replacing the observed semantic map with diffusion-generated outputs causes performance drops (e.g., 3.1% SR for MRON) but still shows PLMD's plug-and-play compatibility. This indicates the benefit of retaining observed regions and using PLMD for unobserved areas.

Further analyses from the appendices:
- **Cross-dataset validation (Appendix F):** PLMD trained on MP3D shows comparable performance to HM3D v0.1, confirming robustness to different environments.
- **Memorization and Data Leakage Check (Appendix G):** Nearest-neighbor retrieval of training targets performs substantially worse than PLMD, suggesting PLMD learns to generalize structural constraints rather than memorizing layouts.
- **Computational Efficiency (Appendix H, I):** While PLMD involves multiple iterations, its FLOPs (34.5G) are lower than some baselines (e.g., PONI at 46.6G, LLM-based OpenFMNav at 276.9G). Inference time accounts for 10-20% of total navigation time, deemed acceptable. Ablations on diffusion steps (Appendix N) show a trade-off, with T=100 offering a good balance between performance and speed.
- **Network Design Choices (Appendix K):** SPADE is preferred over CNN and Attention fusion modules for its balance of performance and efficiency.
- **Cluster Weight Distributions (Appendix J):** The chosen weighting (0.5 density / 0.4 size / 0.1 distance) for selecting goal candidates consistently yields optimal performance.
- **Failure Cases (Appendix O):** Analysis reveals that Localization Errors (due to inaccurate segmentation or incomplete maps) are the primary cause of navigation failures, which PLMD effectively mitigates by generating complete label maps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Plug-and-Play Label Map Diffusion (PLMD) approach presents a method for enhancing Goal-Oriented Navigation (GON) in partially observed environments. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

1. **Addressing Incomplete Map Challenges:** PLMD directly confronts a core limitation of many map-based navigation strategies: their reliance on complete environmental maps. By generating obstacle and semantic labels for unobserved regions, PLMD enables robots to localize goals more effectively even when operating in initially unfamiliar or partially explored spaces. This contributes to bridging the gap between simulated environments (often with full maps) and real-world deployment.

2. **Enhanced Semantic and Structural Consistency:** The method introduces an obstacle-guided denoising process, leveraging structural priors from obstacle maps to constrain semantic predictions. This helps mitigate issues like room boundary drift and semantic hallucinations that can arise from relying solely on semantic correlations. The explicit modeling of obstacles ensures more geometrically plausible and consistent map completions, which is crucial for reliable navigation and planning.

3. **Label-Level Map Completion:** Unlike methods that focus solely on pixel-level semantic predictions, PLMD operates at the granularity of combined obstacle and semantic labels. This "Label Map" representation provides richer, multi-level contextual information, allowing navigation strategies to better understand environmental relationships, such as the interaction between objects and potential obstacles.

4. **Plug-and-Play Universal Compatibility:** A notable design aspect is PLMD's ability to seamlessly integrate with existing, map-dependent GON strategies without requiring re-training of the navigation policy itself. This modularity means PLMD can augment a wide range of current and future navigation frameworks (e.g., those based on RL, SL, LLMs), making it a versatile tool for the embodied AI community. Its demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across diverse tasks (ObjectNav, Instance-ImageNav, Multi-Robot ObjectNav) and datasets (HM3D, MP3D) supports its claim of universal applicability.

5. **Improved Navigation Performance and Efficiency:** The experimental results show consistent and notable improvements in navigation metrics (SR, SPL) across various tasks and baselines when PLMD is integrated. By providing more complete and accurate map information, PLMD reduces the decision-making burden on modular navigation strategies, leading to more efficient exploration and goal localization, often with fewer navigation steps. The demonstrated adaptability to open-vocabulary goals further extends its potential utility.

The potential impact of PLMD extends to the advancement of robust embodied AI systems capable of operating autonomously in complex, unknown indoor environments. By providing a more reliable mechanism for environmental understanding, it could facilitate the development of more capable service robots, autonomous exploration systems, and intelligent agents that interact with human environments.

However, the authors acknowledge that the model does not guarantee perfect prediction, and errors could lead to incorrect robot actions with potential safety implications. Future work could focus on further efficiency optimizations, enhancing real-time deployment capabilities, and extending the framework to handle more dynamic or outdoor environments, while ensuring rigorous verification processes for practical applications.
