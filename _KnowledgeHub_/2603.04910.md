# VPWEM: Non-Markovian Visuomotor Policy with Working and Episodic Memory

> [!summary] Summary
> VPWEM augments diffusion policies with working memory (a sliding window of recent observations) and episodic memory (a fixed-size compressed summary of out-of-window observations), produced by a Transformer-based compressor trained jointly with the policy. It beats diffusion-policy and VLA baselines by over 20% on memory-intensive manipulation tasks and 5% on mobile manipulation, at near-constant per-step compute.

## Problem

- Existing visuomotor policies condition on single-step or short-context histories (2-10 frames), failing on non-Markovian tasks needing long-term temporal dependencies.
- Enlarging the context window scales attention cost quadratically, making training expensive and violating real-time inference constraints on robots.
- Naively conditioning on longer history causes causal confusion and copycat problems, where policies overfit nuisance correlations and fail under distribution shift.

## Method

- ==Working memory==: a FIFO sliding-window cache of recent per-frame multi-modal (RGBD plus proprioceptive) observation embeddings feeding the policy directly.
- **Contextual memory compressor**: a Transformer with ==self-attention== over cached summary embeddings and ==cross-attention== over cached out-of-window observations, recursively producing fixed-size episodic memory.
- Joint end-to-end training with ==gradient-detached caches==: compressor and diffusion noise predictor optimized together under the ==behavior-cloning denoising loss==, decoupling memory from single-frame encoding.

## Results

- Outperforms diffusion-policy and VLA baselines (Octo, OpenVLA, pi-0, CronusVLA, MemoryVLA) by over **20%** on memory-intensive MIKASA tasks and **5%** on average on MoMaRT mobile manipulation.
- On MoMaRT unload-suboptimal task, DP-VPWEM reaches **58.3%** success versus DP-PTP's best **49.6%**, with lower GPU memory and near-constant compute across context lengths.
- Real-robot deployment raises success rate from **37%** to **67%** over diffusion policy with only **72ms** to **79ms** latency increase per action chunk.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Compressing history into a small fixed-size memory beats simply widening the context window, which degrades performance and blows up compute past a length.
> - Detaching cache embeddings from the backward pass lets long-horizon memory propagate through learned summaries alone, cutting overfitting and memory cost.
> - A modular external memory compressor, decoupled from single-frame perception, transfers to unseen but related tasks without retraining the encoder.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control has significantly benefited from imitation learning, enabling robots to acquire complex behaviors from human demonstrations. However, a prevalent limitation in many existing visuomotor policies is their reliance on either single-step observations or short-context historical data. This approach typically involves fixed-size context windows, where observations older than the window length are discarded. Such policies often struggle with non-Markovian tasks, which necessitate understanding and leveraging long-term temporal dependencies that extend beyond immediate observations. These tasks are common in real-world scenarios due to factors like sensor limitations, environmental stochasticity, and multi-stage, long-horizon structures.

Attempts to address this by simply enlarging the context window face significant challenges. The computational and memory costs associated with mechanisms like self-attention, which scales quadratically with sequence length, become prohibitive for long histories. Moreover, merely expanding the context can lead to performance degradation, attributed to issues like "causal confusion" from nuisance correlations or the "copycat problem," where agents replicate previous actions based on irrelevant historical cues. This can result in catastrophic failures under distribution shifts and violate real-time constraints critical for robotic systems.

Within this landscape, several lines of research have emerged to incorporate long-term memory into robot policies. Some approaches utilize recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or state space models (SSMs) to maintain belief-like internal states, with methods like Mamba Imitation Learning (MaIL) attempting to improve efficiency within short contexts. Other works explore direct use of full observation-action histories for autoregressive prediction, though this also faces scalability issues with increasing sequence length. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have begun to integrate memory through multi-frame prediction, feature compression, retrieval mechanisms for relevant memory entries, or selection of historical keyframes, sometimes leveraging large vision-language models (VLMs) for summarization.

Beyond robotics, the concept of managing long-term context has been explored in natural language processing and video understanding, employing techniques such as extrapolation methods for positional embeddings, sparse attention, and various memory-augmentation strategies (e.g., k-nearest neighbor retrieval, learnable memory tokens, and recurrent architectures).

This paper aims to bridge the gap between the recognized need for long-term memory in non-Markovian robotic tasks and the practical limitations of existing methods. It draws inspiration from neuroscience, specifically the human brain's hippocampus-cortex system, which continuously converts working memories into long-term storage, enabling lifelong knowledge retention despite limited capacity. The proposed method seeks to integrate a similar mechanism into visuomotor policies, offering a solution that maintains bounded computational and memory costs while enabling robust performance in tasks requiring extensive temporal reasoning. This positions the work as an advancement in developing more capable and biologically-inspired architectures for imitation learning in complex, real-world robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a visuomotor policy capable of effectively handling non-Markovian robotic tasks that require long-term memory, while maintaining computational and memory efficiency suitable for robotic systems.

The motivation for this work stems from several observed limitations and challenges in current imitation learning approaches for robotics:

1. **Inadequacy for Non-Markovian Tasks:** Existing visuomotor policies predominantly rely on single-step observations or short-context histories (e.g., 2 to 10 frames). This limited temporal scope makes them inherently insufficient for tasks where critical information for decision-making is distributed over long periods or past events. Real-world robotic tasks, characterized by partial observability, environmental stochasticity, and multi-stage structures, frequently exhibit non-Markovian properties.
2. **Computational and Memory Overheads of Naive Long Context:** Simply increasing the context window size to capture longer histories leads to substantial computational complexity (e.g., O(L^2) for self-attention) and high memory requirements. This renders training prohibitively expensive and introduces significant inference latency, making such approaches impractical for real-time robotic deployment.
3. **Performance Degradation from Unfiltered Long Context:** Naively conditioning policies on extended histories without careful design can paradoxically degrade performance. This issue is attributed to "causal confusion" arising from nuisance correlations in the history and the "copycat problem," where the agent inappropriately replicates past actions or develops dependencies on irrelevant historical variables, leading to failures under distribution shifts.
4. **Biological Inspiration:** Human cognition effectively solves long-horizon tasks by compressing important past experiences into long-term memories and selectively recalling them. The paper explicitly cites the hippocampus's role in converting working memories to long-term storage as inspiration, suggesting that a similar mechanism could address the challenges in robotic policies.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes VPWEM, a non-Markovian visuomotor policy. The core objectives are:

- **Develop a memory mechanism analogous to the human brain:** Introduce a system with both short-term "working memory" and a "long-term episodic memory" to capture both immediate and distant historical information.
- **Design an efficient memory compressor:** Create a Transformer-based contextual memory compressor that recursively condenses out-of-window observations into a fixed number of episodic memory embeddings. This aims to maintain long-term context with bounded computational and memory costs, preventing the scalability issues of naive long-context models.
- **Integrate memory with diffusion policies:** Instantiate this memory mechanism within diffusion policies, which are effective for modeling multi-modal action distributions, to leverage both short-term and episode-wide information for robust action generation.
- **Joint Optimization for Task Relevance:** Train the memory compressor jointly with the policy using a behavior cloning objective, encouraging it to extract task-relevant information while minimizing sensitivity to nuisance correlations.
- **Demonstrate Empirical Superiority:** Provide experimental evidence that the proposed framework outperforms state-of-the-art baselines on memory-intensive manipulation tasks and maintains competitive performance on more Markovian tasks.

In summary, the work aims to overcome the limitations of short-context policies by introducing an efficient, biologically-inspired memory architecture, thereby enabling robots to tackle complex, long-horizon tasks requiring deep temporal understanding while adhering to practical resource constraints.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VPWEM (Non-Markovian Visuomotor Policy with Working and Episodic Memory) integrates a memory system into diffusion policies to handle long-term dependencies in non-Markovian tasks. The approach combines a sliding window for recent observations (working memory) with a Transformer-based contextual memory compressor for historical observations (episodic memory).

**Problem Formulation:**
The robotic task is framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The goal is to learn a policy $\pi_{\theta}(a_t|h_t)$ that predicts actions based on the full observation history $h_t = o_{\leq t} = (o_0, \dots, o_t)$, departing from common practices that limit the context to a fixed window $L$, i.e., $\pi_{\theta}(a_t|o_{t-L:t})$. The policy is learned from an expert demonstration dataset $D = \{\tau_n\}_{n=1}^N$.

**Architecture Overview:**
VPWEM augments diffusion policies with two memory components:
1. **Working Memory:** A sliding window of recent observation embeddings.
2. **Episodic Memory:** Fixed-size summary embeddings derived from out-of-window observations by a contextual memory compressor.

These two types of memory provide complementary conditioning signals for the action generation process, which is based on a diffusion model.

**A. Working Memory:**
A multi-modal encoder processes raw observations (e.g., multi-view RGBD images $I_{n_c,t}$ and proprioceptive states $o_{P,t}$). Visual features $o_{I,t}$ and proprioceptive features are concatenated and passed through an MLP to obtain a joint feature vector $o_t \in \mathbb{R}^D$ for each single frame.
The working memory $w_t$ is defined as the sequence of observation embeddings within a predefined sliding window: $w_t \triangleq o_{t-L:t} = \text{concat}(o_{t-L+1}, \dots, o_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times D}$. This window is managed using a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) scheme, balancing recent context with computational efficiency.

**B. Episodic Memory:**
To address the limitations of the fixed-size working memory, a Transformer-based contextual memory compressor is introduced to consolidate information from observations $o_{\leq t-L}$ that have exited the working memory window.
When an observation embedding $o_{\tau}$ moves out of the window, a positional embedding PE($\tau$) is added to it, forming $f_{\tau} = o_{\tau} + \text{PE}(\tau)$. This embedding is then pushed into an "out-of-window observation cache" $C_f$, which maintains a maximum size $S$ (e.g., using FIFO).

The contextual memory compressor, structured as a Transformer encoder, processes this historical information. It consists of $N$ blocks, each containing:
- **Self-attention layer:** Interacts with past summary embeddings.
- **Cross-attention layer:** Interacts with observation embeddings from $C_f$.
- **Feed-forward network:** For non-linear transformations.
Layer normalization and residual connections are applied.

The compression process is formalized as:
1. **Initial Queries:** For the first layer, queries $q_{1,\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times D}$ are trainable model parameters $q$. $M$ represents the number of episodic memory embeddings.
2. **Summary Cache:** A summary cache $C_q$ is maintained per block, storing query embeddings $q_{n,\tau}$ from previous timesteps.
3. **Self-Attention:** $q_{n,\tau}$ attends to the concatenated summary cache $\bar{C}_q = \text{concat}(C_q)$ to summarize past memories ($x_{1,n} = q_{n,\tau} + \text{attn}(q_{n,\tau}Q_s, \bar{C}_q K_s, \bar{C}_q V_s)$).
4. **Cross-Attention:** $x_{1,n}$ then attends to the concatenated observation cache $\bar{C}_f = \text{concat}(C_f)$, capturing long-term dependencies across frames ($x_{2,n} = x_{1,n} + \text{attn}(x_1 Q_c, \bar{C}_f K_c, \bar{C}_f V_c)$).
5. **Feed-Forward:** The output is processed by an MLP ($q_{n+1,\tau} = x_{2,n} + \text{MLP}(x_{2,n})$), becoming queries for the next block.
6. **Episodic Memory Output:** The output of the final layer, $q_{N,\tau}$, is projected to yield the fixed-size episodic memory $e_{\tau} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times D}$.

**C. Action Generation with Working and Episodic Memory:**
VPWEM employs a diffusion model for action generation. At each timestep $t$, the policy generates an action chunk $a_{t:t+H}$ conditioned on both the working memory $w_t$ and the episodic memory $e_{\tau}$.
The diffusion backbone iteratively denoises a sequence starting from an initial Gaussian noise sample $a_K \sim N(0, I)$, producing the final action chunk $a_0$.

**Training Process:**
- **Data Sampling:** Randomly samples $(o_{\leq t}, a_{t:t+H})$ from the demonstration dataset. Unlike fixed-context methods, VPWEM uses full trajectory history, leading to varying input lengths.
- **Batching:** Samples are sorted and grouped by similar sequence lengths within a mini-batch, with padding applied to the beginning of episodes for parallel training.
- **Memory Compressor Input:** To increase robustness, trajectories are divided into segments, and one frame is randomly selected from each segment to construct the input for the contextual memory compressor.
- **Gradient Detachment:** Features $f_{\tau}$ and query embeddings $q_{n,\tau}$ are detached from the computational graph before being stored in caches. This prevents backpropagation through time, ensuring historical information propagates solely via summary embeddings, reducing memory and mitigating overfitting.
- **Denoising Objective:** A neural network $\epsilon_{\theta}$ (a Transformer decoder) is trained to predict the noise $\epsilon$ from a noisy action $a_k$, conditioned on working memory $w_t$, episodic memory $e_{\tau}$, and the denoising timestep $k$. The objective is a standard mean squared error loss:
 $L = E_{(o,a_0),\epsilon} ||\epsilon - \epsilon_{\theta}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_k}a_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_k}\epsilon, w_t, e_{\tau}, k)||^2_2$.
 The memory compressor is optimized jointly with the behavior cloning loss.

**Inference Process:**
- **Cache Maintenance:** A queue of observation embeddings is maintained. New frames are encoded by the multi-modal encoder.
- **Memory Update:** Observations within the context window form the working memory. Observations that move out of the window are passed to the trained contextual memory compressor to update the episodic memory.
- **Action Prediction:** The combined long-term episodic memory and short-term working memory condition the denoising network to iteratively transform a noise sequence $a_K$ into the robot action $a_0$ over $K$ steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of VPWEM involved extensive experiments across three robotic benchmarks and a real-world validation. The results are compared against various state-of-the-art imitation learning baselines.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Benchmarks:**
 - **MIKASA (Memory-Intensive Skills Assessment Suite for Agents):** Two memory-intensive tabletop manipulation tasks, `ShellGameTouch-v0` (memorizing ball position) and `RememberColor3-v0` (remembering cube color).
 - **MoMaRT (Mobile Manipulation RoboTurk):** Five long-horizon, inherently non-Markovian mobile manipulation tasks in a simulated kitchen (e.g., `Table Setup from Dishwasher/Dresser`, `Table Cleanup to Dishwasher/Sink`).
 - **Robomimic:** Two tabletop manipulation tasks, `Square` and `Transport`, considered roughly Markovian. A non-Markovian `Long-Horizon Square` task from was also used, requiring memory of an initial state to identify the goal.
- **Baselines:** Recurrent Neural Network Policy (RNN), Diffusion Policy (DP), Diffusion Policy with Past Token Prediction (DP-PTP), Mamba Imitation Learning (MaIL) with Diffusion Policy. Additionally, performances of VLA models like Octo, OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, SpatialVLA, CronusVLA, MemoryVLA, BPP, and MemER are reported from prior work for MIKASA.
- **Implementation:** CleanDiffuser framework, NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, three random seeds for statistical significance.

**Main Results:**

1. **Performance on Memory-Intensive Tasks (MIKASA):**
 - VPWEM demonstrated substantial improvements on the MIKASA benchmark (Figure 5). It outperformed fixed-context-window baselines and various state-of-the-art VLA models by more than 20% on average in `ShellGameTouch-v0` and `RememberColor3-v0`.
 - The paper attributes this to the compressed episodic memory providing sufficient long-term information, highlighting that mere scaling of policies with single-frame conditioning is insufficient for such non-Markovian tasks.
 - Language-based (MemER) and image-based (BPP) summaries were noted to preserve information for `ShellGameTouch-v0` but fell short on `Long-Horizon Square`, indicating a limitation in capturing very long-range temporal dependencies.

2. **Performance on Long-Horizon Mobile Manipulation (MoMaRT):**
 - VPWEM achieved an average 5% improvement over baselines on the MoMaRT benchmark (Figure 4).
 - The proposed memory mechanism consistently enhanced the performance of both DP and MaIL baselines when integrated.

3. **Performance on Markovian Tasks (Robomimic):**
 - On the largely Markovian Robomimic benchmark (Figure 4), VPWEM performed on par with existing baselines, suggesting that its memory mechanisms do not degrade performance in contexts where long-term memory is less critical.

4. **Computational Efficiency (Table II):**
 - Compared to DP-PTP with increasing context lengths, VPWEM (specifically DP-VPWEM) maintained a more favorable balance of performance and efficiency.
 - While DP-PTP's training and inference times increased substantially with context length, its success rate only marginally improved or even degraded at longer contexts (e.g., 128).
 - DP-VPWEM introduced a lightweight memory module (approx. 2.24M parameters) with modest computational and storage overhead, yet achieved a higher success rate (58.3%) than all DP-PTP variants evaluated on the `unload-suboptimal` task.
 - VLM-based baselines like BPP and MemER had significantly higher latency (around 0.9s per query) compared to VPWEM's compressor.

5. **Generalization:**
 - The compressor, trained on `ShellGameTouch`, demonstrated generalization by achieving 90% and 63% success rates on unseen but related tasks, `ShellGamePush` and `ShellGamePick`, respectively. This suggests the jointly trained compressor can transfer learned memory representations.

6. **Key Information Probe:**
 - An experiment on `Long-Horizon Square` (Figure 6b) showed that episodic memory preserved significantly more initial-state information than working memory throughout an episode, indicated by a 20x lower validation MSE.
 - Even after 400 steps, episodic memory maintained a 4x lower loss compared to working memory, supporting the hypothesis that it effectively captures long-horizon information. More episodic memory embeddings slightly improved performance at later timesteps.

**Ablation Studies (Long-Horizon Square Task - Figure 6a):**

- **Context Length (Observation Horizon):** Baselines (DP, MaIL) struggled, with DP's performance degrading at longer contexts. VPWEM variants, even L1S1 (one long-term, one short-term embedding), significantly outperformed baselines. Performance generally improved with more long-term or short-term embeddings but could degrade if too many were used (L2S2 was chosen for main experiments).
- **Number of Layers in Compressor:** Deeper compressors (more layers) generally improved performance, indicating better summarization of information, up to saturation. This suggested that the gain was from the memory mechanism rather than just increased parameters.
- **Maximum Cache Size:** Performance improved with increasing cache size but saturated around 8, suggesting that the compressor could distill necessary information into a small number of embeddings.
- **Cache Management Method:** FIFO performed similarly to K-Means and AdjSim, with only random eviction showing inferior performance. FIFO was selected for its simplicity.

**Real-World Experiments:**
- VPWEM was deployed on an AgileX Cobot Magic platform for a memory-demanding tabletop task (returning packing tape to its original plate).
- DP-VPWEM improved the success rate from 37% to 67% over DP, with only a marginal increase in latency (72 ms to 79 ms per action chunk), demonstrating practical effectiveness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of VPWEM represents an advancement in addressing the long-standing challenge of long-term memory in visuomotor policies for non-Markovian robotic tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Capability for Non-Markovian Tasks:** VPWEM's core contribution is its ability to effectively capture and utilize long-term temporal dependencies, which are critical for tasks involving partial observability, multi-stage objectives, or events separated by significant time horizons. This enables robots to tackle more complex and realistic manipulation and mobile manipulation scenarios that current short-context policies struggle with. The demonstrated substantial performance improvements on memory-intensive benchmarks like MIKASA and MoMaRT corroborate this.

2. **Computational and Memory Efficiency:** A key strength of VPWEM is its design for bounded computational and storage costs. By employing a contextual memory compressor that condenses out-of-window observations into a fixed number of episodic memory embeddings, the framework avoids the quadratic scaling issues associated with naively increasing context windows. This efficiency is crucial for real-time robotic deployment, where latency and resource constraints are significant factors. The comparison with DP-PTP highlighted VPWEM's ability to achieve higher success rates with modest overhead.

3. **Robustness and Generalization:** The joint optimization of the memory compressor with the behavior cloning loss encourages the compressor to extract task-relevant information while implicitly reducing sensitivity to nuisance correlations, thereby mitigating issues like causal confusion and the copycat problem. The demonstrated generalization capabilities of the compressor to unseen, related tasks indicate its potential for broader applicability and adaptability.

4. **Modularity and Extensibility:** The design of VPWEM, with its decoupled single-frame encoder and external memory compressor, offers a modular framework. This allows for straightforward integration with various existing and future visuomotor policies, including different diffusion policy backbones (as shown with DP and MaIL) and potentially other VLA models, by providing compact episodic memory embeddings as additional context. This modularity could accelerate research and development in robot learning by enabling researchers to easily augment their policies with robust long-term memory.

5. **Biologically Inspired Design:** Drawing inspiration from the human brain's hippocampus-cortex system for memory consolidation provides a neuroscientific basis for the model's architecture. This interdisciplinary approach can lead to more robust and intelligent robotic systems, potentially inspiring further biologically-plausible designs in artificial intelligence.

6. **Real-World Applicability:** The successful deployment on a real-world robot platform for a memory-demanding task, showing significant performance gains with only a small increase in latency, validates the practical utility and robustness of VPWEM in physical environments. This real-robot validation is critical for transitioning research findings into tangible robotic capabilities.

In conclusion, VPWEM offers a principled and efficient solution for endowing visuomotor policies with long-term memory. Its potential impact lies in enabling robots to perform more complex, adaptable, and robust behaviors in real-world settings, thereby expanding the scope of tasks that can be reliably solved through imitation learning. Future work on integrating VPWEM into broader architectures, incorporating auxiliary objectives, and deploying it on a wider range of complex real-world tasks could further amplify its impact on the field of robotics.
