# Reward Is Enough: LLMs Are In-Context Reinforcement Learners

> [!summary] Summary
> Large Language Models exhibit in-context reinforcement learning at inference time, improving performance by optimizing scalar reward signals. A minimalist prompting framework achieved a 90% success rate on Game of 24 and 10-20 point gains on Olympiad-level math problems, surpassing self-revision baselines.

## Problem

- Enabling Large Language Models (LLMs) to continuously improve and adapt to novel tasks during inference without expensive retraining (test-time scaling).
- Limitations in existing self-improvement methods for LLMs, where search-based approaches rely on external heuristics and supervised in-context learning requires unavailable expert demonstrations.
- Previous in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) research was largely restricted to simplified environments, failing to demonstrate LLMs' intrinsic RL capacity on complex, language-based tasks.

## Method

- A multi-round "==ICRL prompting==" framework that uses the LLM as a policy, generating responses based on task descriptions, meta-instructions, and a historical experience buffer.
- Numerical scalar reward feedback is provided for each generated response, serving as the sole learning signal, with sources varying from rule-based functions to LLM self-evaluations.
- An experience buffer stores past state-action-reward tuples, which are concatenated into future prompts, allowing the LLM to learn from its own generated experience in-context.

## Results

- ICRL prompting surpassed baselines like Best-of-N, Self-Refine, and Reflexion, achieving a **90%** success rate on Game of 24 and demonstrating sustained performance gains on Creative Writing and ScienceWorld.
- Substantial performance improvements (10-20 points) were observed over competitive methods on Olympiad-level mathematics benchmarks, applicable across various LLM architectures and context lengths.
- Mechanistic analysis revealed reward-aware attention heads in Qwen3-**32B**, with **29.1%** of heads correlating with reward labels, providing internal evidence of how LLMs process reward signals.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement learning behavior, characterized by continuous performance improvement from scalar reward signals, emerges intrinsically within LLMs during inference time.
> - Even LLM-generated scalar rewards are effective in driving in-context learning, offering a scalable path for self-improvement without external human feedback.
> - The framework fosters genuine learning from external signals, enabling performance gains on tasks outside the model's pre-trained knowledge, rather than merely performing a search.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of enabling Large Language Models (LLMs) to improve their performance during inference time, a capability referred to as test-time scaling. This area of research aims to allow LLMs to act as more effective agents on novel tasks without requiring costly retraining.

Existing approaches to LLM self-improvement at inference time broadly fall into two categories: search and learning. Search-based methods, such as Best-of-N, Tree of Thoughts (ToT), Graph of Thoughts (GoT), Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS, and Intelligent Go-Explore, leverage computational scaling for performance enhancement. However, these methods often rely on externally engineered components, such as heuristics or complex memory management, rather than fully utilizing the model's intrinsic learning ability.

On the learning side, In-Context Learning (ICL) in supervised settings has been explored. However, ICL typically requires expert demonstrations, which are not easily scalable or readily available during inference, thereby limiting its applicability for test-time scaling. This necessitates approaches where LLMs can learn from their own generated experience for continual self-improvement.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) presents a paradigm for self-improvement independent of human knowledge, with notable successes in simulated environments and during LLM training. However, its application to inference-time self-improvement for LLMs, especially in diverse, open-ended tasks where natural language serves as the action space, has been less explored. Previous work in In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL) has largely been restricted to bandit problems or small-scale simulated environments, and often requires artificial interventions, leaving LLMs uncompetitive with algorithmic baselines in these contexts.

This paper bridges a gap by demonstrating that RL can emerge purely during the inference phase of LLMs on complex, language-based tasks. It distinguishes itself from existing self-revision methods like Self-Refine and Reflexion, which depend on natural language self-revision and are susceptible to accumulating hallucinated feedback. Instead, this work proposes a minimal framework that relies solely on numerical scalar rewards, aligning with the "reward hypothesis" and "reward is enough" principles, to elicit genuine reinforcement learning behavior from LLMs in-context. This approach aims to provide a general-purpose initial policy (the LLM) with the capacity for continual self-improvement through RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to demonstrate that reinforcement learning emerges as an intrinsic capability of Large Language Models during their inference time. This phenomenon is termed "in-context Reinforcement Learning" (ICRL).

The motivation behind pursuing this objective is multifaceted:

- **Enabling Test-Time Scaling for LLMs:** For LLMs to function as effective and adaptable agents in real-world scenarios, they must possess the ability to improve their performance and adapt to novel tasks during inference, without requiring extensive retraining. This capability, known as test-time scaling, is crucial for practical deployment in dynamic environments.
- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Self-Improvement Methods:**
 - **Search-based methods:** While successful, methods like Tree of Thoughts often rely on external search algorithms and heuristics. The authors seek a mechanism that leverages the LLM's intrinsic learning capabilities rather than external computation and control.
 - **Supervised In-Context Learning (ICL):** ICL requires expert demonstrations (ground-truth labels), which are difficult and costly to scale during inference time, limiting its utility for continuous self-improvement in novel situations.
 - **Training-time RL:** While RL has shown success in training LLMs, this often involves significant computational cost and is not suitable for on-the-fly adaptation to new tasks encountered in the "big world" setting, where environments are vastly more complex than the agent's prior training data.
- **Bridging the Gap to Real-World, Language-Based Tasks:** Previous ICRL research was predominantly confined to bandit problems or simulated environments. A key motivation is to extend ICRL to diverse, open-ended tasks where natural language is the primary action space, such as creative writing, scientific experimentation, and complex mathematics. This aligns with the vision of LLMs acting as general-purpose initial policies that can continually refine their actions.
- **Exploring the "Reward is Enough" Hypothesis:** The research is inspired by the reward hypothesis, which posits that goals and purposes can be understood as the maximization of an expected cumulative scalar reward. By showing that LLMs can optimize scalar reward signals in-context, the authors aim to provide empirical support for this hypothesis within the context of LLM behavior.
- **Simplicity and Isolation of Emergent Capability:** A critical motivation is to introduce a minimal prompting framework that deliberately excludes auxiliary mechanisms (e.g., textual gradients, prioritized experience replay, sampling-based heuristics, additional engineered modules). This minimal design is intended to isolate and reveal the LLM's intrinsic capacity for ICRL, ensuring that observed performance gains are attributable to emergent RL capabilities rather than external scaffolding.

In essence, the paper aims to demonstrate that LLMs, through a simple multi-round prompting mechanism and scalar reward feedback, can exhibit behavior analogous to reinforcement learning, thereby enabling continuous self-improvement during inference on a variety of complex, language-based tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the "ICRL prompting" framework, a multi-round prompting approach designed to elicit in-context reinforcement learning during the inference time of LLMs.

**LLM as the Policy:**
The LLM, denoted as $\pi_\theta$, functions as the policy network responsible for generating responses. For each task, a natural language description, $s_{task}$, is provided. At the beginning of each "episode" (or round), an initial prompt $S_0$ is constructed. This prompt concatenates the task description, meta-instructions ($s_{ICRL}$), and a history of the LLM's previous attempts along with their associated rewards, stored in an experience buffer. The LLM then generates a response based on this contextualized prompt.

**Reward Function:**
A numerical scalar reward feedback is provided for each state ($S_t$) in an episode. This reward can be:
- **Sparse:** Only a final reward ($R_T$) at the episode's conclusion is non-zero (e.g., Creative Writing, Game of 24, where a solution is either correct or incorrect).
- **Dense:** Rewards can be provided at intermediate steps (e.g., ScienceWorld, where subgoals are rewarded).
The source of these rewards can vary:
- **Rule-based:** Programmatically determined (e.g., SymPy for Game of 24 ground truth).
- **Learned separately:** From a distinct reward model.
- **LLM self-evaluation:** The same LLM (prompted differently) can act as a judge to provide reward scores (e.g., Game of 24, Creative Writing coherence scores).
A key aspect is that this scalar reward is the *only* feedback provided, explicitly tagged with "Reward:" to inform the LLM of its significance. The framework posits that even LLM-generated self-evaluations can drive improvement due to the hypothesis that evaluation is often easier than generation.

**Memory for Experience (Experience Buffer $B$):**
An experience buffer, $B$, stores the LLM's past responses and their corresponding rewards for the given task. In each subsequent episode, this historical context is incorporated into the initial prompt $S_0$. The underlying hypothesis is that the pre-trained LLM possesses an innate ICRL ability, which can be activated by providing a sufficiently rich history of state-action-reward tuples. The context length is limited by the LLM's maximum context window.

**ICRL Instructions ($s_{ICRL}$):**
To facilitate inference-time RL, natural language instructions ($s_{ICRL}$) are included in the initial prompt $S_0$. Three types of instructions are explored:
- **Exploration instruction:** Directs the LLM to generate a response that is explicitly different from all prior attempts in the context.
- **Exploitation instruction:** Asks the LLM to produce the best possible response based on previous attempts that yielded the highest rewards.
- **Exploration or Exploitation instruction:** Prompts the LLM to decide autonomously whether to explore or exploit.

Two strategies for applying these instructions are implemented:
- **ICRL Preset:** Alternates between exploration and exploitation instructions based on the episode number (even for exploration, odd for exploitation).
- **ICRL Autonomous:** Consistently provides the "exploration or exploitation" instruction, allowing the LLM to make the strategic choice.

**Design Principle: Minimality:**
A core design principle is minimality. The framework deliberately excludes complex mechanisms like textual gradients, prioritized experience replay, sampling-based heuristics, or additional engineered modules. This ensures that observed performance gains are attributed to the LLM's emergent RL capacity, aligning with the "reward hypothesis" and the "reward is enough" principle.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is evaluated across diverse benchmarks:
- **Game of 24:** Given four numbers, generate an arithmetic expression equaling 24. GPT-4.1 is used, and rewards are generated by GPT-4.1 itself, scoring solution likelihood (0-3). SymPy verifies ground truth (r*).
- **Creative Writing:** Generate four coherent paragraphs, each ending with a specific sentence. GPT-4.1 is used for policy and reward generation (1-10 coherence score against a reference). Evaluation uses Length-Controlled Alpaca-Eval 2 for pairwise win rates (r*).
- **ScienceWorld:** Interactive text-based science experiment tasks. GPT-4.1 mini is the policy. Environment-provided sparse rewards are used for both r and r*.
- **Olympiad-level Mathematics (AIME, HMMT):** Solve challenging math problems. Open-source models (Qwen3-32B, Llama-4 Maverick, Phi-4) are tested. A learned reward model (Su et al., 2025) provides dense signals, and ground truth checks verify answers.

**Baselines for Comparison:**
The ICRL prompting framework is compared against:
- CoT-only and Long-CoT style prompting.
- Best-of-N (often using ground truth reward for selection).
- Self-Refine (relies on verbal self-feedback, no numerical rewards).
- Reflexion (generates verbal reflections based on rewards).
- ReAct (for ScienceWorld).

**Ablation Studies and Analysis:**
To understand the framework's components, ablation studies are conducted by varying key aspects:
- **Zero Rewards:** All rewards set to 0.
- **Short Context:** Buffer limited to the 3 most recent episodes.
- **Exploration Only:** Only exploration instructions, no reward signal.
- **Exploitation Only:** Always exploitation instructions, with reward signal.
- **No ICRL Instruction:** $s_{ICRL}$ is removed.
Additional analyses include context length analysis (using Qwen3-32B at 8k, 16k, 32k tokens) and a mechanistic analysis of reward-aware attention heads in Qwen3-32B on the creative writing task. A separate task of generating abstracts for unseen arXiv papers is designed to differentiate learning from searching within parametric knowledge.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings, indicating the emergence of in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) in LLMs and the effectiveness of the ICRL prompting framework.

### 5. Emergence of Reinforcement Learning in LLM Inference Time

The primary finding is that LLMs can optimize scalar reward signals during inference. This is evidenced by a consistent improvement in response quality as the context (concatenation of prior responses and rewards) grows across episodes. This behavior, where the LLM effectively learns from experience without parameter updates, demonstrates an emergent RL capability. The paper presents this as a "duck test" for inference-time RL.

### 6. Superior Performance on Diverse Benchmarks

ICRL prompting consistently outperformed strong baselines, including Self-Refine and Reflexion, across various tasks:
- **Game of 24:** ICRL Preset achieved a 90% success rate, and ICRL Autonomous 84%, significantly higher than Best-of-N (49%), Self-Refine (47%), and Reflexion (44%) after 50 trials. This demonstrates reward maximization and effective exploration-exploitation.
- **Creative Writing:** ICRL methods showed higher length-controlled win rates against baselines (e.g., 59.48% vs Reflexion, 86.32% vs Self-Refine). ICRL exhibited sustained improvement over 100 trials, whereas Self-Refine's performance plateaued and then declined, possibly due to context growth issues.
- **ScienceWorld:** ICRL methods demonstrated steady improvements in mean return, eventually outperforming baselines by approximately 20% over 35 trials, even with dense, sparse environment rewards.
- **Olympiad-level Mathematics (AIME, HMMT):** ICRL consistently showed substantial performance gains (10-20 points) over Self-Refine and Reflexion across various open-source models (Qwen3-32B, Llama-4 Maverick, Phi-4) and different context lengths (8k, 16k, 32k). This indicates broad applicability and robustness of ICRL.

### 7. Effectiveness of Self-Generated Rewards

Notably, performance improvements were observed even when the scalar reward signals were generated by the LLM itself (e.g., GPT-4.1 evaluating its own Game of 24 steps or creative writing coherence). This highlights a promising paradigm for test-time scaling where external human feedback or complex reward models are not strictly necessary.

### 8. Insights from Ablation Studies

The ablation studies confirmed the critical role of reward signals and context:
- **Zero Rewards:** Setting all rewards to zero resulted in a significant performance drop, affirming that the scalar reward signal is essential for improvement.
- **Short Context:** Limiting the context to only the three most recent episodes led to a performance decrease, indicating that a growing and sufficiently long context of experiences is beneficial for ICRL.
- **Exploration Only (without reward signal):** This method performed substantially worse than ICRL, suggesting that the observed gains are not merely due to diverse sampling (exploration) and subsequent selection of the best seen response (like Best-of-N), but rather genuine learning from rewards to generate novel, better responses.
- **Exploitation Only (with reward signal):** This method performed competitively with the full ICRL framework, demonstrating the robustness of the reward-driven improvement process.
- **No ICRL Instruction:** Removing meta-instructions also reduced performance, emphasizing their role in guiding the LLM's in-context learning process.

### 9. Robustness Across Context Lengths and Models

ICRL consistently surpassed Self-Refine and Reflexion across varying context lengths (8k, 16k, 32k) on Creative Writing and AIME benchmarks, indicating its computational efficiency and scalability with longer contexts. Its effectiveness across different model architectures (GPT-4.1, Qwen3-32B, Llama-4 Maverick, Phi-4) further supports its broad applicability.

### 10. Evidence for True Learning vs. Search

On the "Unseen Paper Abstract Generation" task, which involved generating abstracts for papers published after the model's training cutoff (i.e., information not in its parametric knowledge), ICRL continued to improve ROUGE-Recall scores over 200 iterations. In contrast, search methods (Best-of-N) and self-correction methods (Self-Refine, Reflexion) quickly plateaued. This finding suggests that ICRL can genuinely learn from external reward signals to uncover unseen information, rather than merely performing a sophisticated search within its pre-trained knowledge.

### 11. Mechanistic Evidence: Reward-Aware Attention Heads

A mechanistic analysis of Qwen3-32B on the creative writing task revealed that 29.1% of attention heads across the last 32 layers showed statistically significant correlations between their attention patterns and the reward labels. Some heads focused on high-reward responses (successes), while others attended more to low-reward responses (failures). This pattern is consistent with classical RL algorithms that learn from both successful and unsuccessful experiences, providing internal evidence for how LLMs process reward signals. Reward sensitivity was observed to be higher in earlier layers.

### 12. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of artificial intelligence, particularly concerning Large Language Models and agent development:

**1. Paradigm Shift in LLM Self-Improvement:** The most significant contribution is the empirical demonstration that reinforcement learning is an emergent capability of LLMs during inference time. This "in-context RL" (ICRL) introduces a new paradigm for test-time scaling, moving beyond relying solely on search or supervised in-context learning, and enabling LLMs to improve purely from scalar reward signals.

**2. Minimalist and Effective Framework:** The ICRL prompting framework is designed for minimality, relying only on scalar rewards and simple meta-instructions. This contrasts with many existing methods that require complex engineered modules, textual gradients, or sophisticated memory management. The effectiveness of this minimal design suggests that the intrinsic RL capability is quite robust and does not necessitate extensive external scaffolding.

**3. Superiority Over Self-Revision Methods:** The consistent outperformance of ICRL prompting over established self-revision methods like Self-Refine and Reflexion is noteworthy. This suggests that scalar reward signals, even when generated by the LLM itself, can be a more effective form of feedback for driving self-improvement than verbal, natural language reflections, which can be prone to hallucination or less precise guidance.

**4. Enhanced LLM Agency and Adaptability:** By enabling LLMs to learn and adapt from their own experiences in-context, this work paves the way for more autonomous agents. Such agents could continually explore, adapt to novel situations, and refine their policies in open-ended, real-world environments without requiring re-training for every new task or scenario. This could significantly broaden the applicability of LLMs in dynamic and complex domains.

**5. Scalability with Self-Generated Feedback:** The finding that ICRL improves performance even when reward signals are generated by the same LLM is impactful. This eliminates the dependency on human-labeled data or external reward models for continuous improvement, suggesting a scalable mechanism for enhancing LLM performance during deployment.

**6. Deeper Understanding of LLM Mechanics:** The mechanistic analysis revealing reward-aware attention heads provides insights into how LLMs internally process and leverage reward signals. This suggests that LLMs are not merely pattern matchers but can develop internal representations that differentiate successful and unsuccessful outcomes, learning from both. This could inform future model architectures and training objectives.

**7. Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for future research, including:
 - Investigating how training-time interventions can further enhance the emergent ICRL capabilities of LLMs.
 - Exploring more sophisticated in-context RL algorithms.
 - Applying ICRL to an even wider range of real-world, interactive tasks.

In summary, this research positions LLMs as powerful in-context reinforcement learners, demonstrating an emergent capability that could fundamentally change how autonomous agents learn and adapt, leading to more intelligent and self-improving AI systems.
