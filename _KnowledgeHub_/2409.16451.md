# ARCH: Hierarchical Hybrid Learning for Long-Horizon Contact-Rich Robotic Assembly

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford, MIT, University of Michigan, and Autodesk Research introduced ARCH, a hierarchical hybrid learning framework that enables robots to perform complex, multi-stage assembly tasks involving contact with high precision. The framework achieved a 55% success rate on a real-world assembly task with 9 objects using only 10 demonstrations and demonstrated generalization to 8 unseen objects.

## Problem

- Automating complex, long-horizon robotic assembly tasks that involve delicate physical contact and demand high precision.
- Existing end-to-end learning methods require vast amounts of data, while reinforcement learning is sample inefficient for long sequences.
- Traditional task and motion planning (TAMP) relies on labor-intensive hand-designed components and struggles with dynamic environments.

## Method

- Developed **ARCH**, a hierarchical framework decomposing complex assembly into low-level primitives and a high-level orchestrating policy.
- Implemented low-level primitives using a hybrid approach: ==motion planning== for free-space movements and ==reinforcement learning== for contact-rich operations like insertion.
- Trained the high-level policy using ==imitation learning== with a ==Diffusion Transformer== from a small number of human demonstrations, where continuous parameters are automatically derived.

## Results

- Demonstrated a **55%** success rate (SR) and **0.51** Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) on a real-world 9-object FMB assembly task, outperforming other hierarchical and end-to-end baselines.
- Exhibited strong generalization capabilities by successfully assembling 8 novel objects, achieving up to **80%** SR for some unseen parts without additional training.
- Achieved high performance and robustness across tasks using only **10** human demonstrations for the high-level policy, showing high data efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining model-based motion planning with reinforcement learning for low-level primitives effectively handles both free-space movements and delicate contact-rich interactions.
> - A hierarchical structure allows a high-level policy to compose primitives from limited demonstrations, overcoming the data inefficiency of end-to-end learning for long-horizon tasks.
> - Abstracting continuous parameter inputs during data collection simplifies human demonstrations, enabling efficient learning without requiring direct teleoperation for precise control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic assembly is a critical area for industrial automation, aiming to enhance precision, consistency, and efficiency in manufacturing. However, **long-horizon, contact-rich, and high-precision assembly tasks** remain a significant challenge. These tasks require robots to perform complex sequences of actions, often involving delicate physical interactions with objects, and maintain high accuracy over extended periods, while also being adaptable to variations in components and scenarios.

Existing approaches to robotic manipulation and assembly face several limitations:
- **End-to-to-end Imitation Learning (IL):** While promising for learning complex behaviors from human demonstrations, it typically requires vast amounts of expert data, which is costly and time-consuming to collect, especially for high-precision tasks. Moreover, E2E IL policies often struggle with the fine-grained accuracy demanded by assembly.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** RL has shown success in learning specific high-precision operations (e.g., insertion). However, it suffers from severe sample inefficiency, making it impractical for long-horizon tasks where rewards are sparse and the action space is vast. Training in such scenarios can lead to exploding sampling spaces and convergence issues.
- **Task and Motion Planning (TAMP):** TAMP methods leverage hybrid symbolic-continuous reasoning for long-horizon problems, offering strong capabilities for resolving temporally dependent constraints. However, they typically rely on hand-designed symbolic transition functions and continuous parameter samplers, making them labor-intensive to develop and less adaptable to dynamic environments or failures. They also tend to be computationally expensive.
- **Hierarchical Approaches:** Many researchers have adopted a "divide-and-conquer" mindset, decomposing complex tasks into simpler, reusable subtasks or skills. While hierarchical methods offer benefits in managing complexity, existing approaches often still require significant demonstration data for both high-level policies and low-level skills (e.g., MimicPlay, Luo et al.), or struggle with the robustness and adaptability needed for contact-rich interactions.

ARCH positions itself by directly addressing these limitations. It bridges the gap between the generalizability of learning-based methods and the precision/efficiency requirements of industrial assembly. By adopting a **hybrid hierarchical learning approach** that intelligently combines model-based methods (for routine motions) and reinforcement learning (for contact-rich interactions) at the low level, and data-efficient imitation learning for high-level sequencing, ARCH aims to offer a robust, precise, and data-efficient solution that can generalize to novel objects and long-horizon tasks, thereby pushing the frontier of robotic automation in manufacturing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable **generalizable, long-horizon, high-precision robotic assembly in contact-rich settings** from limited demonstrations. The authors aim to develop a robotic system capable of tackling complex multi-stage assembly tasks that typically challenge existing robotic automation solutions.

The key motivations driving the development of ARCH are:
- **Bridging the Gap in Robotic Automation:** Current industrial robotic systems are largely engineered for specific tasks and struggle to adapt to variations in assembly scenarios or component geometries. There is a pressing need for more flexible and adaptable systems.
- **Overcoming Data Scarcity in Learning:** End-to-end imitation learning, while powerful, demands prohibitively large datasets of expert demonstrations, making it impractical for many real-world industrial deployments. ARCH seeks to achieve high performance with only a handful of demonstrations.
- **Addressing Sample Inefficiency in RL:** Reinforcement learning excels at learning specific, precise behaviors but falters in long-horizon tasks due to the challenges of sparse rewards and an exponentially growing state-action space, leading to extreme sample inefficiency.
- **Mastering Contact-Rich and High-Precision Operations:** Assembly tasks inherently involve delicate physical contact and require very high precision. Traditional methods often struggle with robustly handling these interactions, while learning-based methods may lack the necessary precision without extensive tuning or data.
- **Enabling Generalization:** The goal is to develop a system that can generalize to unseen objects and new scenarios within the same task category, moving beyond highly specialized, rigid robotic solutions.

By introducing ARCH, the authors are motivated to create a more sophisticated learning and object interaction capability that goes beyond traditional and current learning-based approaches, paving the way for more versatile and autonomous robotic assembly in manufacturing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach (Adaptive Robotic Compositional Hierarchy - ARCH)

ARCH proposes a hierarchical modular approach modeled as a parameterized-action Markov decision process (PAMDP). This framework decomposes the complex long-horizon assembly problem into two distinct levels: a low-level library of parameterized primitive skills and a high-level policy that orchestrates these primitives.

#### 3.1. Low-level Primitive Library: Modeling Basic Skills with Motion Planning and Reinforcement Learning

The low-level library consists of fundamental, adaptable primitives essential for assembly tasks. These primitives are implemented using a **hybrid strategy**, leveraging both classical **motion planning (MP)** algorithms and **reinforcement learning (RL)** policies. This hybrid design allows the system to capitalize on the efficiency and reliability of model-based methods for predictable free-space movements, while utilizing the adaptability of RL for complex, contact-rich interactions.
- **Motion Planning (MP) based Primitives:**
 - **GRASP(o, p_g):** Moves the end-effector to a pre-grasp pose `p_g` for object `o` and closes the gripper.
 - **PLACE(o, p_p):** Moves the end-effector to a pre-place pose `p_p` for object `o` and opens the gripper.
 - **MOVE(g_m):** Moves the end-effector to a specified goal pose `g_m`.
 These primitives utilize a motion planner (e.g., lazyPRM) for execution, benefiting from their robustness in collision-free trajectory generation.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) based Primitives:**
 - **INSERT(o, g):** Takes a grasped object `o` and inserts it into a goal pose `g`. This is implemented using an RL policy (PPO) trained in simulation (IsaacLab). The choice of RL for insertion is motivated by the inherent inaccuracies of pose estimation and the complex, contact-rich dynamics involved in successful insertion. The RL policy learns to navigate these uncertainties by observing end-effector, force-torque, and relative goal pose information, and outputs end-effector velocities to minimize the distance to the goal. Domain randomization is used during training to facilitate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

Each primitive is parameterized (e.g., with an object index or a 6-DoF pose), making them flexible and adaptable to varying task contexts.

#### 3.2. High-level Policy: Composing Primitives via Imitation

The high-level policy, $\pi_\theta(a, x|s)$, is responsible for selecting the appropriate primitive `a` from the low-level library and instantiating its parameters `x` given the current state `s`. This policy is trained via **imitation learning (IL)** from a small number of human demonstrations.
- **Architecture:** A **Diffusion Transformer (DiT)** serves as the backbone of the high-level policy. DiT is chosen for its strong capabilities in handling sequential data, which is suitable for processing a history of states and actions.
- **Inputs:** The policy takes object pose information (from a pose estimator) and robot proprioceptive state as input.
- **Outputs:** It produces a categorical distribution over the available low-level primitives (primitive scores) and the continuous action parameters for the selected primitive.
- **Learning:** The policy is supervised using cross-entropy loss for primitive selection and Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss for parameter prediction, aiming to maximize the likelihood of expert actions.

#### 3.3. Data Collection

A distinguishing feature of ARCH is its data collection strategy for the high-level IL policy, designed for **data efficiency without teleoperation**. Instead of continuous teleoperation, a human demonstrator provides high-level guidance by:
1. **Selecting a primitive index:** Chooses which basic skill (e.g., GRASP, INSERT) to execute.
2. **Identifying the object index:** Specifies which object the primitive should act upon.
The continuous parameters (e.g., pre-grasp pose, insertion goal pose) are **automatically derived** from the pose estimation module and pre-defined offsets, rather than being manually input by the demonstrator. This abstraction significantly reduces the burden of data collection, especially for high-precision tasks. The dataset includes both "successful" trials and "recovery" trials where operators demonstrate corrective actions after failures, enhancing the learned policy's robustness.

#### 3.4. Pose Estimation

Accurate object pose estimation is critical for both the high-level policy and the low-level primitives. ARCH integrates **CPPF++**, a category-level pose estimation method adapted for instance-level use with known CAD models. To further enhance precision, a post-optimization procedure is applied using the **one-way Chamfer distance** and iterative optimization in the tangent space of the Lie group. This ensures that the estimated 6D poses (rotation and translation) are highly accurate, which is fundamental for successful high-precision grasping and insertion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors extensively evaluated ARCH in both simulation and real-world robotic manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against various baselines.

#### 4.1. Task Description and Setup

- **Workcell:** UR10e robotic arm, Robotiq 2F-85 gripper, Zivid RGB-D camera. Simulation platform: IsaacLab.
- **Tasks:** Three long-horizon assembly tasks, involving 4 to 9 objects, were used:
 - **FMB Multi-peg Assembly (real robot):** 9 distinct objects to be grasped, reoriented (if needed), and inserted into a board.
 - **5-part Beam Assembly (simulation):** Assembling two legs into a base, then two feet onto the legs.
 - **9-part Stool Assembly (simulation):** More challenging, involving four legs into a base, then four feet onto the legs.
- **Evaluation:** 20 trials per task, with objects initialized randomly in a grasp region.

#### 4.2. Baselines

- **End-to-end (E2E) IL:** Diffusion Policy (DP), trained via teleoperated demonstrations.
- **End-to-end (E2E) RL:** PPO, trained in simulation.
- **Hierarchical Baselines:**
 - **MimicPlay:** Adapted to use expert demonstrations, learning high-level latent plans and low-level visuomotor control.
 - **Luo et al.:** Adapted hierarchical imitation learning approach where the entire low-level primitive library is also learned via imitation.

#### 4.3. Evaluation Metrics

- **Success Rate (SR%):** Percentage of successful long-horizon task completions.
- **Success weighted by Path Length (SPL):** A measure of efficiency, considering the shortest path vs. actual path taken for successful trials.
- **% Grasped:** Success rate of the GRASP primitive.
- **% Inserted:** Success rate of the INSERT primitive.

#### 4.4. Comparative Performance Analysis

- **Superiority over Baselines:** ARCH demonstrated significantly higher success rates and SPL scores across all tasks compared to E2E and hierarchical baselines (Table 1).
 - **FMB (real):** ARCH achieved 55% SR (0.51 SPL), substantially outperforming MimicPlay (20% SR, 0.16 SPL) and Luo et al. (25% SR, 0.19 SPL).
 - **Simulated Tasks:** The performance gap was even more pronounced in the more challenging Beam and Stool Assembly tasks. For Stool Assembly, ARCH achieved 45% SR (0.44 SPL), while MimicPlay and Luo et al. only reached 10% and 20% respectively.
- **Limitations of Baselines:**
 - E2E RL (PPO) failed completely (0% SR) on long-horizon assembly due to sparse rewards and sample inefficiency.
 - E2E IL (Diffusion Policy) also failed (0% SR), highlighting its struggles with high-precision demands and the need for massive datasets not available under the 10-demonstration limit.
 - Hierarchical baselines performed better than E2E methods but struggled with contact-rich steps like insertion, primarily due to the limited number of demonstrations and potentially less robust low-level primitive implementations.
- **ARCH's Advantages:** The high performance of ARCH is attributed to its hierarchical framework and the hybrid MP-RL design of its low-level library, balancing precision, flexibility, and efficiency. Failures in ARCH were mainly due to pose estimation inaccuracies and limitations in the RL policies.

#### 4.5. Generalization to Unseen Objects

- ARCH was trained exclusively on the Hexagon object for both RL primitive and high-level policy. Experiments showed that ARCH **generalized remarkably well to 8 unseen objects** with varying shapes in the FMB Assembly task (Table 2), without any fine-tuning. For instance, it achieved 80% SR for the "3Prong" object and 75% for "Circle".
- Some objects with narrow edges (SquareCircle, DoubleSquare) presented challenges during grasping, and certain shapes (Star, SquareCircle, Arch) were more challenging for pose estimation, leading to slightly lower success rates for those specific objects.

#### 4.6. Data Efficiency

- A crucial finding is ARCH's **exceptional data efficiency**. It achieved high success rates with **merely 10 demonstrations** for the high-level policy, which is a significant advantage over data-hungry E2E methods and even some hierarchical baselines that typically require much more data. This makes ARCH practically applicable in real-world scenarios where data collection is a bottleneck.

#### 4.7. Robustness

- The high-level policy demonstrated robustness through **failure recovery**. For example, if a grasp primitive failed, the policy automatically triggered another grasp attempt using updated pose estimates. This inherent ability to retry and self-correct contributed to ARCH's higher overall success rate.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ARCH framework presents a significant advancement in robotic manipulation, particularly for the challenging domain of long-horizon, contact-rich, and high-precision robotic assembly. Its contributions have several important implications:

- **Enabling Advanced Industrial Automation:** ARCH directly addresses critical bottlenecks in manufacturing, paving the way for more autonomous and flexible assembly lines. By enabling robots to handle variations in components and perform complex sequences of actions with high precision and robustness, it can significantly reduce manual labor, increase efficiency, and lower costs in industries such as electronics, automotive, and general manufacturing.
- **Practical Data Efficiency:** The ability to learn complex, long-horizon tasks from a handful of demonstrations (without the need for costly and tedious teleoperation) is a major breakthrough. This substantially lowers the barrier to entry for deploying advanced robotic systems in diverse industrial settings where large-scale data collection is impractical.
- **Robustness through Hybrid Learning:** The hybrid design of the low-level primitive library, combining the reliability of model-based motion planning for free-space movements with the adaptability of reinforcement learning for contact-rich interactions, provides a robust and flexible solution. This approach intelligently leverages the strengths of different control paradigms.
- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** ARCH's ability to generalize to unseen objects after training on a single object demonstrates a crucial step towards truly versatile robotic systems. This reduces the need for extensive re-training for every new component or slight design variation, a common limitation in current industrial robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The modular nature of ARCH and its clear identification of current limitations (e.g., cross-task generalization, fully automated object identification, scalability of the primitive library) provide clear directions for future research. This framework can serve as a strong base for integrating more advanced perception modules (e.g., vision-language models), expanding the primitive library, and developing policies that can transfer across fundamentally different tasks.

In conclusion, ARCH offers a highly practical and effective solution to a long-standing challenge in robotics. Its blend of hierarchical learning, hybrid primitive implementation, and data-efficient training makes it a compelling candidate for real-world deployment, pushing the boundaries of what autonomous robotic assembly systems can achieve.
