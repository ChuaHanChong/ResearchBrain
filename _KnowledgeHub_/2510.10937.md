# Neutral Agent-based Adversarial Policy Learning against Deep Reinforcement Learning in Multi-party Open Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Chinese Academy of Sciences developed a neutral agent-based adversarial policy learning approach to attack Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents in multi-party open systems. This method enables adversarial agents to learn policies to induce task failures in DRL victims without direct interaction or full access to environmental states, reducing victim winning rates to as low as 0% in various scenarios.

## Problem

- Existing adversarial attacks against DRL agents typically assume either full environmental control or direct interaction between attacker and victim, which is impractical in complex multi-party open systems.
- Attackers in multi-party open systems face challenges like inaccessible victim reward functions and operating under partial observability, hindering effective adversarial reward design and computation.
- The complexity of multi-party open systems, with varying numbers of agents and party affiliations, limits the applicability of current attack methods.

## Method

- A neutral agent-based adversarial policy learning framework is introduced, allowing bystander agents to learn adversarial policies that indirectly influence victim DRL agents within a Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP).
- A novel adversary reward function is designed based on observable 'failure paths' (e.g., victim damage, task delay) and a weighted sum of their impacts, circumventing the need for private victim reward access.
- An LSTM-based estimation model is proposed to calculate step-wise adversarial rewards from partial observations, trained by matching episode-end total ground truth rewards, addressing the challenge of partial observability.

## Results

- The method reduced victim DRL agent winning rates from 95-100% (no attack) to 0-10% in Starcraft II scenarios and from 72-100% to 12-28% in Highway-env tasks, demonstrating broad effectiveness.
- The estimation-based reward model outperformed traditional models in attack effectiveness and exhibited significantly faster training convergence, for example, requiring 2 million episodes versus 18 million for the traditional model on the '8m' map.
- The proposed attack proved robust against simple retraining of victim agents and was largely unaffected by existing defense mechanisms like environment-manipulation based (CAMP) and game-theoretic (PATROL) defenses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Neutral agents, even without direct interaction or full environmental control, can effectively learn and deploy adversarial policies to degrade the performance of DRL victim agents in complex multi-party environments.
> - Adversarial rewards can be effectively constructed from observable 'failure paths,' removing the dependency on private victim reward functions and making attacks feasible in black-box scenarios.
> - Partial observability for reward computation can be effectively managed using an estimation-based model, allowing adversarial agents to learn robust policies in realistic, complex systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), which integrates deep neural networks (DNNs) into the Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework, has demonstrated significant capabilities in various complex decision-making domains, including strategic games, robotics, and autonomous driving. However, this integration also exposes DRL systems to vulnerabilities against adversarial attacks, a known weakness of DNNs.

Existing research on adversarial attacks against DRL generally falls into two categories:
1. **Environment Manipulation-based Methods**: These techniques involve directly altering the environment with which a victim DRL agent interacts, thereby perturbing its observations and inducing sub-optimal or dangerous behaviors. Examples include adding subtle noise to inputs or observations.
2. **Adversarial Policy Learning-based Methods**: This approach involves deploying a separate adversarial agent that interacts with the victim agent. The adversarial agent learns a policy to mislead the victim, often by observing its behaviors and inferring its policy.

While these existing methods have demonstrated effectiveness in various scenarios, their applicability in "multi-party open systems" is limited. Multi-party open systems, characterized by a potentially variable number of agents organized into multiple parties (which can collaborate, compete, or remain neutral), are more complex than the single-agent or two-agent environments typically studied. The limitations of current attack methods in such systems are identified as:
- **Impractical Assumption of Full Control**: Environment manipulation methods often assume an attacker has full control over the environment or victim agents, which is generally not feasible in real-world, complex systems due to the resources required for system compromise.
- **Dependence on Direct Interactions**: Adversarial policy learning methods frequently necessitate direct interaction (e.g., competition or cooperation) between the adversarial agent and the victim agent. In open multi-party systems, such direct interaction opportunities may not always be available or sustained.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing an adversarial policy learning approach tailored for multi-party open systems, aiming to extend the practicality and generality of DRL attacks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an adversarial policy learning approach that can mislead well-trained DRL victim agents within multi-party open systems. This approach specifically aims to overcome the identified limitations of existing methods by:
- Not requiring direct interactions between the adversarial agents and the victim agents.
- Not necessitating full control over the victim agents' environments.

The motivation stems from the observation that agents in multi-party open systems can exist in "neutral roles" – acting as bystanders that do not directly participate in interactions with other agents but can still observe and indirectly influence them through the shared environment. The core idea is to repurpose these neutral agents to learn adversarial policies.

The implementation of this neutral agent-based adversarial policy learning faces two significant technical challenges:

1. **Adversary Reward Design in Non-Zero-Sum, Private Reward Systems**: In many multi-party open systems, tasks are often non-zero-sum, and the reward functions of victim agents are typically private and inaccessible to an attacker. This renders traditional adversary reward designs (e.g., taking the negative of the victim's reward, which is common in zero-sum competitive settings) ineffective or impractical. The objective is to design an adversary reward that effectively incentivizes actions leading to victim task failures without requiring access to the victim's internal reward signals.

2. **Reward Computation under Partial Observability**: Existing adversary reward computation methods often assume access to the global state of the environment, providing complete information for accurate reward estimation at each step. However, in multi-party open systems, agents typically operate under "partial observability," meaning they have limited perception distance or region and cannot access the complete global state. This makes precise, step-wise reward computation challenging for adversarial agents. The objective is to develop a method for calculating adversarial rewards that can function effectively with only partial observations.

By addressing these challenges, the authors aim to enable general and effective adversarial attacks against DRL in realistic, complex multi-party open environments, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of DRL system security and robustness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a neutral agent-based adversarial policy learning approach to attack DRL agents in multi-party open systems. This approach is formulated within a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) framework, specifically adapting the QMIX algorithm.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Assumptions

The multi-party open system is modeled as a Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP), denoted by $M = \langle (N_{\alpha}, N_v), S, (A_{\alpha}, A_v), P, (R_{\alpha}, R_v), \gamma \rangle$.
- $N_{\alpha}$ and $N_v$ represent the sets of adversarial and victim agents, respectively.
- $S$ is the global state set.
- $A_{\alpha}$ and $A_v$ are the joint action sets for adversarial and victim agents.
- $P$ is the joint state transition function.
- $R_i$ is the reward function for agent $i \in \{\alpha, v\}$.
- $\gamma$ is the discount rate.

A key assumption is that only adversarial agents adapt their policies, while victim agents and other third-party agents follow fixed, pre-trained policies. This simplifies the problem for adversarial agents into a single-party Dec-POMDP, $M_{\alpha} = \langle N_{\alpha}, S, A_{\alpha}, P_{\alpha}, R_{\alpha}, \gamma \rangle$, where $P_{\alpha}$ and $S$ are not explicitly available. Adversarial agents only receive their individual partial observations.

#### 3.2. Reward Function Design

To address the challenge of inaccessible victim rewards and non-zero-sum tasks, the research proposes a novel adversary reward function based on "failure paths" of victim agents. Instead of directly negating victim rewards, the approach identifies observable state-action sequences that lead to victim task failures.
- **Failure Paths**: Common failure paths, such as "victim damage" (potential harm to victim agents) and "task delay" (obstacles to task completion), are introduced. The design is extensible, allowing for the incorporation of additional task-specific failure paths.
- **Weighted Sum**: The reward for an individual adversarial agent $i$ is defined as a weighted sum of the impacts caused by different failure paths ($R_i^j$). A manually defined weight vector $W_j$ assigns importance to each failure path. The reward $R_i^{\alpha}$ for an adversarial agent $i$ is calculated as $R_i^{\alpha} = W \times (R_i)^{\top}$.
- The total adversarial reward $R_{tot}^w$ is similarly computed as $W \times (R_{tot})^{\top}$, where $R_{tot}^j$ is a vector of total impacts for each failure path.

#### 3.3. Objective Function Building

The adversarial agents are trained to maximize their cumulative rewards using a MARL algorithm. QMIX is chosen as an example due to its prevalence and effectiveness in MARL tasks.
- The objective function for the adversarial multi-agent party is to maximize the total Q-value function: $J(\theta) = maximum_{A_{adv}} Q_{tot}^{adv}(S, A)$.
- Individual Q-values ($Q_{\pi_i}^{\alpha}$) for each adversarial agent $i$ are reformulated using the newly designed reward function: $Q_{\pi_i}^{\alpha}(S, A_{\alpha_i}) = R_{\alpha_i}(S, A_{\alpha_i}) + \gamma \sum_{S'} P(S'|S, A_{\alpha_i})V_{\pi_i}^{\alpha}(S')$.
- Proposition 1 states that if only adversarial agents adapt policies, the state transition depends solely on the adversarial joint policy, simplifying the Q-value calculation.
- Proposition 2 establishes that the long-horizon expected return shares the same weighted changes as the short-term reward function, ensuring consistency in reward signal aggregation over time.
- The total Q-value is then approximated using the QMIX mixing network, and adversarial agents are trained by minimizing the QMIX loss function, which compares the current Q-value approximation with the target Q-value derived from the designed rewards.

#### 3.4. Reward Calculation under Partial Observability

To address the challenge of partial observability, two methods for reward calculation are considered:
1. **Rule-based Reward Calculation**: This method is used as a baseline or ground truth. It assigns reward signals based on mission completion status at the end of an episode (0 if completed, high reward if failed), or immediate rewards based on failure path progression. This method assumes access to global state information, which is impractical in many real-world settings.
2. **Estimation-based Reward Model**: To overcome the impracticality of global state access, a neural network based on Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is proposed.
 - The LSTM network takes partial observations of adversarial agents as input at each step and outputs an estimated immediate reward.
 - Since global state is unavailable for step-wise ground truth, the model is trained by comparing the sum of estimated rewards over an entire episode with the ground truth total reward calculated by the rule-based method at the episode's conclusion.
 - The loss function for the reward model is $L_{R-model}(\theta) = (R_{tot}^{GT} - \sum_{i=1}^l M_i(obs))^2$, where $M$ is the LSTM network, $l$ is episode length, and $obs$ refers to partial observations. This enables the model to learn to estimate step-wise rewards from partial observations, providing informative feedback for policy optimization.

The overall algorithm integrates the adversarial Q-networks, the mixing network, and the LSTM-based reward estimator, allowing for end-to-end training of adversarial policies that can mislead victim agents without direct interaction or global state knowledge.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the proposed method was conducted on the Starcraft Multi-agent Challenge (SMAC) platform (based on Starcraft II) and the autonomous driving simulation platform Highway-env.

#### 4.1. RQ1: Generalization Effectiveness of the Neutral Agent-based Method

The method demonstrated generalizability across diverse task settings representing various circumstances in multi-party open systems (single-agent, two-agent competitive, multi-agent cooperative, and multi-party open).
- On Starcraft II maps, victim winning rates were reduced from at least 95-100% (no attack) to 0-10% (under attack) across "1m" (0.04), "1c_vs_30zg" (0.1), "8m" (0.08), and "MMM" (0.0).
- In Highway-env scenarios, winning rates declined from 72-100% to 12-28% for "highway\_M" (0.12) and "intersection\_S" (0.28). These results indicate the method's capability to launch effective attacks across a variety of scenarios.

#### 4.2. RQ2: Performance of the Estimation-based Reward Model

The estimation-based reward model was compared with a rule-based model (assuming global state access) and a traditional model (negative of victim reward).
- **Attack Effectiveness**: The estimation-based model generally outperformed the traditional model in reducing victim winning rates. For example, on "8m" map, it achieved an 80% decline versus 20% for the traditional model, and on "highway" scenario, 90% versus 25%. On some tasks like "6h\_vs\_8z", both the estimation-based and rule-based models achieved similar effectiveness (90% decline).
- **Training Efficiency**: The estimation-based model demonstrated significantly faster convergence. For instance, on the "8m" map with two adversarial agents, it required only 2 million episodes for training compared to 18 million episodes for the traditional reward model. These findings suggest that the estimation-based reward model is more general and efficient for complex environments with partial observability.

#### 4.3. RQ3: Influence of Varying Numbers of Adversarial Agents

Experiments showed that increasing the number of adversarial agents generally led to more effective attacks.
- Multi-agent collaborative attacks were observed to be more effective than single-agent methods across various maps ("C", "D", "E").
- The more adversarial agents deployed, the more pronounced the attack effectiveness, although this was noted to potentially increase detection risk.

#### 4.4. RQ4: Effectiveness in Various Difficulty Level Tasks

The complexity of the victim's task influenced both the training efficiency of adversarial agents and the attack effectiveness.
- Adversarial agents required longer training periods for easier victim tasks (e.g., Map C).
- Training was more efficient for medium (Map D) and difficult (Map E) victim tasks.
- Adversarial agent training was also more stable in more difficult tasks (Map E compared to Map D). This indicates that victim agents performing more difficult tasks may exhibit higher vulnerability to the proposed attack method.

#### 4.5. RQ5: Effectiveness Against Countermeasures

The research explored the robustness of the attack against potential defense mechanisms.
- **Simple Retraining**: Retraining the victim agents' policy in the presence of adversarial agents (Map D) did not lead to convergence for the victim and significantly degraded their performance even without an active attack. This suggests that simple retraining is not an effective countermeasure.
- **Existing Defense Methods**:
 - Environment-manipulation based defenses like CAMP were found to be ineffective against adversarial policy training methods.
 - Game-theoretic defenses like PATROL, while effective in specific two-agent zero-sum competitive environments with prior knowledge of the attacker, were not broadly applicable to the general multi-party open systems or against re-trained attackers under modified adversarial density, showing a collapse in robustness.
- The conclusion drawn is that the proposed attack method is challenging to defend against using existing techniques due to its characteristics such as unpredictability, difficulty in detection, and variable number of adversarial agents.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of adversarial attacks against Deep Reinforcement Learning, particularly by extending the scope to multi-party open systems, which represent a more realistic and complex class of environments compared to those typically studied.

The primary significance lies in its **novelty and practicality**:
- It is identified as the first effort to utilize neutral agent-based adversarial policy learning to attack DRL in multi-party open systems. This introduces a new threat vector that does not rely on direct interaction with victim agents or the ability to fully control or manipulate their environments, thereby enhancing its real-world applicability.
- The proposed methodology addresses critical practical challenges:
 - **Inaccessible Victim Rewards**: By redesigning the reward function based on observable "failure paths" and assigning importance weights, the approach circumvents the need for prior knowledge of victim agents' private reward functions, making attacks feasible in black-box scenarios.
 - **Partial Observability**: The introduction of an estimation-based reward model using LSTM networks allows adversarial agents to operate effectively with only partial observations, overcoming the impractical assumption of global state access in complex open environments.

The **generalizability** of the method across diverse DRL task environments (single-agent, two-agent competitive, multi-agent cooperative, and general multi-party open systems) implies a broad potential impact on the security and robustness of various DRL applications, including autonomous driving, robotics, and strategic AI systems. The demonstrated difficulty in defending against these attacks with existing countermeasures underscores a critical vulnerability in current DRL system designs.

The findings contribute to a deeper understanding of DRL robustness, prompting the development of more resilient DRL algorithms and defense strategies for multi-agent systems. The research opens avenues for future work, including investigating the transferability of adversarial policies across different DRL algorithms and extending the adversarial policy learning paradigm to non-DRL sequential decision-making tasks, which could broaden the scope of this attack methodology further.
