# When More is Less: Understanding Chain-of-Thought Length in LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper demonstrates that the relationship between Chain-of-Thought (CoT) length and Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning accuracy is non-monotonic, typically forming an inverted U-shaped curve with an optimal length. More capable LLMs exhibit a 'simplicity bias,' achieving peak performance with shorter, more efficient CoTs, a behavior also promoted during Reinforcement Learning training.

## Problem

- A prevailing intuition suggests that longer, more detailed Chain-of-Thought (CoT) processes consistently lead to superior performance in Large Language Models (LLMs).
- Recent observations challenge this 'longer is always better' paradigm, noting instances where excessively long CoTs might degrade performance, termed 'overthinking'.
- There was a lack of a systematic and rigorous understanding of how CoT length influences reasoning accuracy and how optimal length scales with model capability and task difficulty.

## Method

- The research employs a multi-faceted approach combining real-world **LLM** evaluations (Qwen2.5, Llama3.1) on datasets like MATH and MMLU STEM.
- Controlled synthetic arithmetic experiments with GPT-2 models are used to systematically vary CoT length and granularity, allowing for fine-grained analysis of scaling laws.
- A formal theoretical model is established to explain the observed inverted U-shaped curve, the scaling laws of optimal CoT length, and the emergence of simplicity bias in RL training.

## Results

- Optimal CoT length consistently decreases with model capability (e.g., from 14 steps for a **1.5B** model to 4 steps for a **72B** model on the MATH dataset) and increases with task difficulty.
- Excessive CoT length leads to substantial performance degradation, with a **72B** model showing up to a **40%** accuracy drop compared to its optimal CoT length.
- Training smaller models with optimal-length CoT data significantly boosts performance, and an adaptive 'Length-Filtered Vote' at inference time consistently outperforms vanilla majority vote on the GPQA dataset.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The relationship between Chain-of-Thought (CoT) length and LLM reasoning accuracy typically follows an inverted U-shaped curve, indicating the existence of an optimal length.
> - More capable LLMs exhibit a 'simplicity bias,' achieving peak performance with shorter CoTs, while harder tasks consistently require longer optimal CoTs.
> - Reinforcement Learning (RL) training naturally drives models towards optimal, often shorter, CoT lengths, aligning with the simplicity bias.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing and artificial intelligence, demonstrating remarkable capabilities in complex reasoning tasks. A pivotal technique enabling this success is Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, introduced by Wei et al. (2022). CoT allows LLMs to break down intricate problems into a sequence of explicit intermediate steps, mimicking human-like deliberation. This "divide-and-conquer" strategy has been widely adopted and extended, giving rise to numerous variants such as least-to-most prompting, Tree of Thoughts, and various divide-and-conquer methods, all fundamentally relying on the decomposition principle.

A prevailing intuition, reinforced by some early research, has been that longer and more detailed CoT processes generally lead to superior performance, especially for challenging tasks. The assumption is that more steps equate to more thorough deliberation and thus higher accuracy. Consequently, much of the research and development in CoT has focused on techniques to elicit longer, more comprehensive reasoning chains or to scale test-time computation through extended CoT.

However, recent observations have begun to challenge this "longer is always better" paradigm, noting instances where concise CoTs can be effective or where excessively long CoTs might even degrade performance—a phenomenon often termed "overthinking." This paper directly addresses this emerging skepticism, positioning itself as a foundational study that rigorously investigates the relationship between CoT length and reasoning accuracy. It directly challenges the simplistic notion that more steps automatically yield better results, proposing instead an "inverted U-shaped curve" where performance initially improves with CoT length but eventually declines.

By providing comprehensive evidence from real-world LLMs, controlled synthetic experiments, and formal theoretical analysis, this work offers a mechanistic understanding of the optimal CoT length. It delves into the scaling behaviors of this optimum with respect to model capability and task difficulty, revealing a crucial "simplicity bias" where more capable models prefer shorter, more efficient reasoning paths. This insight is significant as it provides a principled explanation for the "overthinking" phenomenon observed in some LLMs and bridges it with broader machine learning principles like Occam's Razor, which advocates for simpler explanations.

Furthermore, the paper's findings on the emergence of simplicity bias during Reinforcement Learning (RL) training for LLMs contribute to the ongoing discourse on how RL shapes model behavior beyond mere performance optimization. Its practical applications, such as training with optimally-lengthed CoT data and introducing "Length-Filtered Vote" at inference, directly inform current LLM development practices, moving beyond heuristic CoT generation towards adaptive, calibrated reasoning strategies. This work thus marks a significant step towards developing more efficient, effective, and human-aligned LLMs by emphasizing the importance of adaptive and optimally-calibrated reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from a fundamental question arising in the field of large language models: Does reasoning performance consistently improve as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) processes grow longer and longer? This question directly challenges the common intuition that more detailed and extensive intermediate reasoning steps are always beneficial for complex problem-solving.

The paper sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

1. **Challenge the "Longer is Better" Notion:** Empirically demonstrate that the relationship between CoT length and task accuracy typically follows an inverted U-shaped curve. This implies the existence of an optimal CoT length, where performance peaks before deteriorating due to excessive length (overthinking) or insufficient decomposition (underthinking).
2. **Uncover Scaling Behaviors of Optimal CoT Length:** Investigate how the optimal CoT length varies with crucial factors:
 - **Task Difficulty:** Determine if harder tasks necessitate longer or shorter optimal CoTs.
 - **Model Capability:** Explore how the reasoning prowess of an LLM influences its ideal CoT length. This objective aims to expose what the authors term an "inherent simplicity bias" in more capable models.
3. **Analyze Simplicity Bias in Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Observe and understand if and how RL training influences the length of generated CoTs, particularly in relation to the hypothesized simplicity bias.
4. **Establish a Theoretical Framework:** Develop a simple yet formal theoretical model to explain the empirically observed phenomena, including the inverted U-shaped curve, the existence of an optimal CoT length, its scaling laws, and the emergence of simplicity bias during RL.
5. **Translate Understanding into Practical Guidelines:** Leverage the findings to propose and demonstrate practical strategies for optimizing LLM reasoning performance, specifically in terms of:
 - Improving training data curation by aligning CoT lengths with optimal performance.
 - Developing length-aware inference-time strategies.

Ultimately, the paper aims to provide both a principled understanding of the "overthinking" phenomenon in LLMs and actionable guidelines for calibrating CoT generation, moving towards adaptive CoTs that are tailored to task complexity and model capabilities, thereby enabling optimal reasoning performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multi-faceted approach, combining real-world observations, controlled synthetic experiments, and theoretical analysis to rigorously investigate the relationship between CoT length and LLM reasoning performance.

#### 3.1. Real-World Observations

- **Model Assessment:** The study utilizes the **Qwen2.5 series of Instruct models** (ranging from 1.5B to 72B parameters) and **Llama3.1 series** (8B and 70B), representing a spectrum of model capabilities.
- **Task Datasets:**
 - **MATH dataset (Level 5):** Composed of challenging competition mathematics problems, used to assess how model capability interacts with CoT length.
 - **MMLU STEM dataset:** Used for additional validation of the observations.
 - **LeetCode-2K dataset:** Employed to monitor the evolution of CoT length during Reinforcement Learning (RL) training.
- **CoT Generation and Length Measurement:** For each question, 60 solutions with varied CoT lengths are generated using in-context examples (8-shots) across three complexity levels. CoT length is determined by the number of intermediate reasoning steps, identified by line breaks, rather than token count, to reflect reasoning complexity.
- **Experiments:**
 - **Model Capability vs. Optimal CoT Length:** Evaluate different sized models on a fixed set of problems to see if optimal length changes with model scale.
 - **Task Difficulty vs. Optimal CoT Length:** For a fixed model, analyze the correlation between problem difficulty (measured as `1 - accuracy`) and the optimal CoT length.
 - **Impact of Excessively Long CoTs:** Compare the accuracy achieved by optimal-length CoTs versus the longest generated CoTs.
 - **RL Simplicity Bias:** Monitor average CoT length and accuracy of a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model trained with GRPO on LeetCode-2K, observing changes over training steps.

#### 3.2. Controlled Experiments (Synthetic Arithmetic Tasks)

To overcome the uncontrolled variables in real-world CoTs, a synthetic experimental setup is designed:

- **Dataset Design:** Simple arithmetic problems consisting only of sequences of addition operations. Task difficulty (`T`) is defined by the total number of operators. The key innovation is the ability to generate multiple valid CoT solutions for a given `T`, differing in their length (`N`) and granularity (number of operators per step `t`). This allows systematic variation of `N` and `t` for a fixed `T`.
- **Model and Training:** GPT-2 models of varying depths (number of layers) are trained. Model depth serves as a controlled proxy for model capability. Models are trained on automatically synthesized CoT solutions with varying `T` and `N`. During testing, models can be guided to produce a specific CoT length using control tokens or allowed to choose their preferred length.
- **Experiments:** These experiments aim to replicate real-world observations in a controlled setting and provide fine-grained analysis of scaling laws, including:
 - Replication of the inverted U-shaped performance curve.
 - Scaling of optimal CoT length with `T` (task difficulty).
 - Scaling of optimal CoT length with model capability (number of layers).
 - Observation of per-step difficulty (`t`) and its impact.
 - Replication of RL's simplicity bias using a PPO-trained GPT-2 model on the synthetic task.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Analysis

A simple mathematical model is established to formally explain the observed phenomena:

- **CoT Process Probability:** The model formalizes the probability of an N-step CoT leading to a correct final answer, `A(N)`. It considers two primary error sources in each step:
 - `σ(T)`: Error rate of generating the correct sub-question (positively correlated with total task difficulty `T`).
 - `E(N, M, T)`: Error rate of answering the sub-question (dependent on `N` for decomposition, model capability `M`, and `T`).
- **Error Assumptions:** Initially, linear error functions are assumed for simplicity (`σ(T) = T/C`, `E(N, M, T) = T/(NM)`). These assumptions are later generalized to broader scenarios with monotonic and convex error functions.
- **Derivations:**
 - Proof of the inverted U-shaped curve for `A(N)`.
 - Derivation of a closed-form expression for the optimal CoT length `N*(M, T)` using the Lambert W function.
 - Formal proof of the scaling laws: `N*` increases with `T` and decreases with `M`.
- **RL Simplicity Bias Explanation:** RL training is modeled as a stateless bandit problem where each CoT length `N_i` yields a reward with probability `A(N_i)`. It is proven that gradient ascent on the policy will converge to a deterministic policy that selects the CoT length maximizing `A(N)`.

#### 3.4. Practical Applications

Based on the theoretical and empirical insights, two practical applications are demonstrated:

- **Training with Optimal-Length CoT Data:** Comparing models trained on datasets containing either mixed-length CoT solutions or predominantly optimal-length CoT solutions (for specific model sizes and task difficulties).
- **Adaptive Length-Filtered Vote at Inference Time:** Proposing a modified majority voting strategy that selectively considers or weights answers from CoTs whose lengths fall within a proper range, identified by lower prediction uncertainty (Shannon entropy). This is evaluated against vanilla majority vote on the GPQA dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings across its multi-pronged investigative approach, consistently supporting the central argument that "longer is not always better" for Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning.

#### 4.1. The Inverted U-shaped Performance Curve

- **Consistent Observation:** Both real-world LLMs (Qwen2.5-GPT2) and synthetic arithmetic experiments demonstrate that task accuracy, when plotted against CoT length, typically follows an inverted U-shaped curve. This means performance initially improves as CoTs provide necessary decomposition but eventually deteriorates if CoTs become excessively long (due to error accumulation) or too short (insufficient decomposition). This clearly indicates the existence of an optimal CoT length.

#### 4.2. Scaling Behaviors of Optimal CoT Length

The study uncovers crucial scaling laws for this optimal length:

- **Optimal Length Increases with Task Difficulty:** For both real-world (MATH, MMLU STEM datasets) and synthetic tasks, harder problems consistently require longer optimal CoTs. This indicates that more complex problems genuinely benefit from more extended decomposition steps to achieve peak performance.
- **Optimal Length Decreases with Model Capability (Simplicity Bias):** Across the Qwen2.5 and Llama3.1 series, more capable (larger) models achieve their peak performance with *shorter* optimal CoTs. This is a key finding, revealing an "inherent simplicity bias" where stronger models can condense more complex reasoning into fewer, more efficient steps. For example, the optimal length shifts from 14 steps for a 1.5B model to 4 steps for a 72B model on the MATH dataset.
- **Excessive Length Degradation:** There is a substantial performance gap between optimal-length CoTs and excessively long CoTs, particularly for highly capable models. For a 72B model, this gap can be as large as 40% accuracy, highlighting the significant penalty of "overthinking."
- **Harder Tasks Favor Harder Sub-tasks (Synthetic Data):** In the controlled synthetic setup, as tasks become more challenging, optimal performance is achieved not just by increasing the number of steps, but also by increasing the complexity of each sub-task within a step (i.e., higher optimal operators per step). This suggests that fixed-layer Transformers might be suboptimal for dynamic per-step computation.

#### 4.3. Simplicity Bias in Reinforcement Learning (RL)

- **RL Drives Towards Shorter CoTs:** Contrary to some intuitions, RL training (using GRPO on LeetCode-2K for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and PPO on synthetic data for GPT-2) leads models to gravitate towards *shorter* CoT lengths as training progresses and model accuracy improves. This demonstrates that RL implicitly calibrates the model's CoT generation policy towards the optimal length regime, aligning with the simplicity bias.

#### 4.4. Theoretical Validation

- **Formal Proof of Optimal Length:** The simple theoretical model formally proves the existence of the inverted U-shaped curve and derives a closed-form expression for the optimal CoT length (`N*`).
- **Theoretical Proof of Scaling Laws:** The theory mathematically confirms the empirical scaling laws: `N*` increases monotonically with task difficulty and decreases monotonically with model capability, providing a unified explanation for the observations, including RL's simplicity bias.

#### 4.5. Practical Applications and Benefits

- **Training with Optimal-Length CoT Data:** Experiments show that training smaller models with CoT data explicitly tailored to their optimal lengths significantly boosts performance. A smaller (6-layer) model trained on optimal-length data can even outperform a larger (9-layer) model trained on mixed-length data, highlighting the critical impact of suitable training data curation.
- **Adaptive Length-Filtered Vote at Inference:** A proposed inference strategy, "Length-Filtered Vote," which leverages uncertainty (Shannon entropy) to select CoT lengths within an optimal range for majority voting, consistently outperforms vanilla majority vote on the GPQA dataset, demonstrating performance improvements and robustness.

In summary, the main findings robustly establish the non-monotonic relationship between CoT length and accuracy, unveil the critical role of model capability and task difficulty in determining optimal CoT length, provide a theoretical foundation for these phenomena, and offer concrete strategies for enhancing LLM reasoning through informed CoT calibration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a profound re-evaluation of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning in Large Language Models (LLMs), fundamentally challenging the pervasive, yet often unexamined, assumption that "longer is always better." Its significance and potential impact span both fundamental understanding of LLM capabilities and practical guidelines for their development and deployment.

#### 5.1. Principled Understanding of LLM Reasoning

- **Demystifying "Overthinking":** The paper provides a systematic and empirically validated explanation for the "overthinking" phenomenon, where LLMs generate excessively long and counterproductive reasoning chains. By demonstrating the inverted U-shaped curve of CoT length vs. accuracy, it offers a crucial insight into the trade-off between task decomposition and error accumulation, providing a nuanced understanding beyond simple heuristics.
- **Revealing Simplicity Bias:** The discovery and theoretical formalization of the "simplicity bias"—that more capable models gravitate towards shorter, more efficient reasoning paths—is a key contribution. This aligns LLM behavior with principles like Occam's Razor, suggesting an intrinsic preference for parsimonious solutions as models mature. This insight could guide future architectural designs or training objectives that explicitly encourage efficiency.
- **Theoretical Foundation for CoT Dynamics:** Establishing a simple yet powerful theoretical model that formally proves the existence of optimal CoT length, its scaling laws, and the emergence of simplicity bias in RL provides a much-needed analytical framework. This moves the understanding of CoT beyond empirical observation to a more rigorous, first-principles basis, paving the way for more predictable and controllable reasoning behaviors.

#### 5.2. Practical Guidelines for LLM Development and Deployment

The findings offer direct and actionable implications for improving LLM performance:

- **Adaptive CoT Calibration:** The most immediate impact is the shift from a "one-size-fits-all" approach to CoT generation towards adaptive calibration. LLMs should no longer blindly strive for maximum CoT length. Instead, they should be designed to dynamically choose the "right amount of thought" tailored to the specific task complexity and their own inherent capabilities. This can lead to more efficient computation without sacrificing accuracy.
- **Improved Training Data Curation:** The demonstration that training with *optimally-lengthed* CoT data significantly boosts performance, even enabling smaller models to outperform larger ones trained on mixed-length data, is a game-changer for data engineering. It suggests that future CoT datasets should be carefully curated, potentially with model-specific optimal lengths in mind, rather than simply collecting diverse or excessively long reasoning chains. This could lead to more effective fine-tuning and distillation processes.
- **Enhanced Inference Strategies:** The "Length-Filtered Vote" method showcases a practical way to leverage the understanding of optimal CoT length at inference time. By selectively weighting or filtering responses based on their CoT length and associated uncertainty, models can make more robust and accurate final predictions, moving beyond naive majority voting. This implies a future where inference pipelines are more intelligent and length-aware.
- **Rethinking RL for Reasoning:** The observation that RL naturally guides models towards optimal (often shorter) CoT lengths provides a fresh perspective on the benefits of RL in LLM training. It suggests that RL can act as a "calibrator," helping models learn more efficient reasoning behaviors even if their initial pre-training or supervised fine-tuning data is suboptimal in terms of CoT length. This could inform the design of more effective RL objectives and reward functions.
- **Efficient Resource Utilization:** By advocating for optimal, rather than maximal, CoT lengths, the research implicitly promotes more efficient use of computational resources during inference. Shorter, more potent CoTs translate to fewer tokens generated, reducing inference latency and operational costs, which is a critical consideration for large-scale LLM deployment.

In conclusion, "When More is Less" is a pivotal study that transforms our understanding of CoT reasoning. By scientifically disproving the "longer is better" fallacy and offering a principled framework for optimal CoT length, it provides invaluable insights for developing LLMs that are not only more capable but also more efficient, nuanced, and adaptively intelligent in their reasoning processes.
