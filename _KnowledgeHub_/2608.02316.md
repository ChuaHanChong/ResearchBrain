# Residual-Based Adaptive Kalman Filtering for Legged Robot State Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Bremen and DFKI GmbH developed adaptive Kalman filtering techniques to enhance legged robot state estimation, achieving an average 15% reduction in position RMSE by dynamically adjusting measurement noise covariance based on filter residuals. This self-contained approach operates without requiring additional sensors or extensive manual tuning.

## Problem

- Conventional Kalman filters used in legged robotics rely on fixed process and measurement noise parameters, which fail to adapt to time-varying disturbances like foot slippage or terrain changes.
- Inaccurate high-rate state estimates, crucial for model-based control, result from IMU drift, kinematic errors, and uncertainties in ground contact detection, hindering stable and efficient locomotion.
- Existing adaptive methods often require manual tuning, depend on additional sensor modalities (e.g., foot force), or have not been systematically compared for IMU and leg kinematics fusion in this specific form.

## Method

- An online ==noise covariance adaptation== strategy was implemented within an **Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF)** framework, focusing on dynamically adjusting the process noise (Q) and measurement noise (R) matrices.
- A ==filter innovation and residual-based== method was introduced to adapt Q and R at each iteration by leveraging internal filter quantities, designed to operate without additional sensor data or extensive manual tuning.
- The proposed method was systematically compared against a baseline approach that adapts noise covariances based on ==IMU transient disturbances and foot force data== using real-world quadruped robot experiments across diverse environments.

## Results

- Adapting only the measurement noise (R) covariance led to an average **15%** reduction in position RMSE relative to a fixed-tuned InEKF, with adapting process noise (Q) showing negligible additional benefit.
- The proposed filter residual-based R adaptation achieved a mean position RMSE improvement of **16.08%** indoors and **14.89%** outdoors, outperforming the baseline foot force method in overall indoor performance and yaw orientation error reduction.
- A comprehensive open-source dataset, covering over **1200 meters** of quadruped locomotion with motion capture and high-accuracy GNSS/RTK ground truth, was released to support future research.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Legged robot state estimation performance is significantly more sensitive to the accurate modeling of measurement uncertainty (leg odometry) than to process noise (IMU), particularly due to varying contact conditions.
> - Adapting the measurement noise covariance (R) based on filter residuals provides robust and substantial performance improvements, offering a self-contained solution without needing additional sensor modalities or extensive manual tuning.
> - Adaptive Kalman filtering, especially for R, can significantly reduce position drift and improve heading estimates across different gaits and terrains, showcasing its practical applicability for robust locomotion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged robots operate as highly dynamic systems, frequently required to navigate and adapt to unstructured, human-centered environments. Achieving stable and efficient locomotion in such challenging conditions necessitates robust and adaptable control frameworks. Model-based control approaches, which are widely employed in this domain, critically depend on accurate, high-rate estimates of the robot's floating-base state.

However, proprioceptive state estimation for legged robots presents inherent challenges due to various sources of uncertainty. These include drift in Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) and kinematic errors arising from foot slippage, compliant ground contact, or uncertainties in contact detection. Kalman filter (KF)-based estimators are a common tool for fusing data from multiple sensors to enhance state estimation accuracy. A significant limitation of conventional KF-based estimators is their reliance on fixed process and measurement noise characteristics. These static parameters render them unable to adapt to time-varying disturbances that affect sensor measurements, such as foot slippage, impact-induced IMU disturbances, and variations in terrain or gait patterns.

Prior research has addressed aspects of contact uncertainty in legged robot state estimation. Some approaches improve robustness by rejecting unreliable leg kinematic measurements based on metrics like Mahalanobis distance or L2 norm of estimated foot velocity, often requiring manual tuning of thresholds. Others assign fixed, high covariances to slipping measurements instead of outright discarding them, or employ robust cost functions within the InEKF. Probabilistic methods have been developed to estimate contact probability by fusing planned gait phases with measured foot height and contact forces. A notable baseline, the probabilistic contact estimation framework, scales leg kinematics measurements and adapts measurement noise covariance by combining leg contribution consistency and impact forces, but requires accurate foot force measurements and manual tuning. Learning-based methods have also emerged, utilizing neural networks to weight leg contributions or learn continuous contact velocity covariances, though their performance and generalization can be platform-specific.

In the broader context of adaptive Kalman filtering, several works propose online adaptation of noise parameters for position and orientation tracking, particularly for integrated inertial navigation and global positioning systems (INS/GPS). These methods often use filter innovation and residual to adapt process noise covariance (Q), measurement noise covariance (R), or both, without requiring additional parameter tuning. Some extensions include scaled adaptation algorithms or the introduction of forgetting factors. While some studies have explored using innovation covariance to adapt specific components of Q, such as foot-slip in quadruped state estimation, the potential of using innovation/residual-based approaches to adapt the measurement noise covariance (R) in legged robot state estimation has not been extensively explored or systematically compared in this specific form for IMU and leg kinematics fusion.

This work addresses the gap by proposing and evaluating a novel application of an online innovation and residual-based noise adaptation technique within an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) for proprioceptive legged robot state estimation. It also conducts a direct comparison with a foot force-based covariance adaptation method, aiming to provide a solution that is robust to varying contact conditions without requiring additional sensor modalities or extensive manual tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the inherent limitations of conventional Kalman filter-based state estimators in legged robotics. While these filters are widely used for sensor fusion, their assumption of fixed process and measurement noise parameters (Q and R) poses a challenge. Legged robots operate in highly dynamic and unpredictable environments, where gaits, terrain properties, and contact conditions (e.g., foot slippage, impacts) vary significantly over time. Fixed noise parameters are unable to accurately reflect these time-varying uncertainties, leading to suboptimal or degraded state estimation performance. This inadequacy directly impacts the performance of model-based control frameworks, which rely on precise, high-rate state estimates for stable and efficient locomotion.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop an Online Noise Covariance Adaptation Strategy:** The primary objective is to propose and implement a method for online adaptation of the process noise covariance matrix (Q) and the measurement noise covariance matrix (R) within an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) framework. This adaptation aims to enable the state estimator to cope with variable noise characteristics caused by the repetitive making and breaking of leg contact with the ground, as well as other dynamic perturbations.

2. **Introduce a Filter Innovation and Residual-Based Adaptation Method:** A specific objective is to apply and extend an innovation and residual-based covariance adaptation technique for Kalman filtering, which has not been previously explored in this form for legged robot state estimation. This method is designed to adjust Q and R at every iteration by leveraging internal filter quantities (innovation and residual), ideally without requiring additional sensor data or extensive manual tuning.

3. **Conduct a Systematic Comparison with a Baseline Approach:** A crucial objective is to systematically compare the proposed innovation and residual-based adaptation method against an existing foot force-based covariance adaptation approach. This comparison seeks to evaluate the relative performance and practical advantages of both techniques under real-world conditions.

4. **Perform an Ablation Study on Q and R Adaptation:** The research aims to conduct an ablation study to assess the individual and combined impact of adapting Q and R. This objective seeks to determine whether adapting both matrices simultaneously provides additional benefits or if adapting only one (e.g., R, corresponding to leg kinematics) is sufficient for significant performance improvement.

5. **Validate on Real-World Data and Diverse Environments:** A practical objective is to validate the proposed methods using real-world data collected from a quadruped robot (Unitree Go2) performing various gaits (trotting, adaptive) in different indoor (flat, stones) and outdoor (sandy, rocky) environments. This extensive validation aims to demonstrate the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed approach across diverse operational scenarios.

6. **Release a Quadruped State Estimation Dataset:** To contribute to the broader research community, an objective is to release an open-source dataset recorded indoors with motion capture ground truth and outdoors with high-accuracy GNSS/RTK ground truth, covering a substantial distance of over 1200 meters.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the development and evaluation of online noise covariance adaptation strategies within an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) for legged robot state estimation.

#### 3.1. State Estimation Framework

The InEKF, chosen for its convergence properties and reduced sensitivity to linearization errors, forms the foundation of the state estimator. A left-invariant, world-centric formulation is adopted, aligning with the world-frame representation of the robot's state and contact-aided leg kinematics. The state matrix **X** includes the robot's floating-base orientation (**R**), velocity (**v**), position (**p**) in the world frame, and the world-frame positions of feet in contact (**p**^c^~i~). Additionally, IMU gyroscope and accelerometer biases (**θ**) are estimated. The estimation error (**ξ**) is defined on the Lie algebra, and the state error covariance matrix (**P**) represents the uncertainty of the state estimate.

#### 3.1.1. Prediction Step

The prediction step propagates the state estimate on the Lie group and its covariance on the Lie algebra. IMU sensor measurements (angular velocity **~ω** and linear acceleration **~a**) are used as input, modeled with additive zero-mean Gaussian noise. The process noise covariance matrix **Q** is defined as a diagonal matrix, comprising variances for gyroscope, accelerometer, foot slip, and IMU bias. The discrete-time state propagation is achieved via forward Euler integration, with orientation updated on the Lie group SO(3). The predicted covariance **P**^-^ is calculated using the linearized state transition matrix **A** and the process noise Jacobian **G**.

#### 3.1.2. Update Step

The update step incorporates leg kinematics obtained from joint encoder measurements when a foot is in ground contact. The foot position in the body frame (**~p**^B^~c~^i^) is modeled with additive Gaussian measurement noise. The measurement noise covariance matrix **R** is a diagonal matrix containing variances of the leg kinematics measurements along the Cartesian axes. This approach models foot position uncertainty directly, rather than propagating joint encoder uncertainties. The Kalman gain (**K**) is derived from the state and measurement noise covariance matrices to fuse IMU predictions with leg kinematics updates.

#### 3.2. Covariance Matrix Adaptation

The core of the methodology involves online adaptation of the diagonal elements of the **Q** and **R** matrices to maintain their symmetric positive definite (SPD) property and prevent filter divergence. Two distinct adaptation methods are proposed and evaluated:

#### 3.2.1. Adaptation based on Filter Innovation and Residual (Proposed Method)

This novel approach leverages internal Kalman filter quantities: the innovation (**ν**^-^), defined as the difference between the sensor measurement and the predicted measurement, and the residual (**ν**^+^), the difference between the sensor measurement and the updated measurement. For each contact leg, these quantities are computed, and their covariances are obtained over a sliding window of size M (empirically set to 1 second).

- **Process Noise Covariance Matrix Q Adaptation:** Based on the innovation covariance, the process noise covariance **Q** is scaled by a factor (**α**) derived from the trace of the innovation covariance and the measurement covariance **R**, normalized by the state covariance **P** and measurement Jacobian **H**. This adaptation primarily affects the IMU gyroscope and accelerometer noise parameters, using the average **α** over all contacting legs.
- **Measurement Noise Covariance Matrix R Adaptation:** This adaptation utilizes the residual covariance. The residual formulation is chosen for its property of preserving the SPD nature of **R**. To maintain frame consistency, **R** is rotated from the body to the world frame using the updated filter state's orientation. The **R** matrix is adapted individually for each leg in contact.

#### 3.2.2. Adaptation based on IMU and Foot Force Data (Baseline Method)

This baseline approach uses proprioceptive sensor data for online adaptation, drawing on prior work.

- **Process Noise Covariance Matrix Q Adaptation:** Transient disturbances in IMU measurements during ground contact events are detected by monitoring variations in consecutive gyroscope and accelerometer readings. The IMU noise parameters (**σ**^g^ and **σ**^a^) are increased during these impact events by a configurable gain (**α**^1^), using initial nominal values.
- **Measurement Noise Covariance Matrix R Adaptation:** For each contacting leg, **R** is adapted based on the consistency of leg kinematic updates and the difference in foot forces. This involves an initial leg kinematics standard deviation (**σ**^l^~0~), the standard deviation of leg odometry measurements, and the absolute temporal difference of the z-axis foot force (**f**^z^). This adaptation uses tunable parameters (**α**^2^, **α**^3^) and is applied individually for each leg.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Robotic Platform:** A Unitree Go2 quadruped robot was used, equipped with a 6-DOF IMU, joint encoders, and foot force/torque (F/T) sensors. The robot's control architecture includes a gait sequencer, model predictive controller (MPC), and whole-body controller (WBC).
- **Datasets:** Extensive datasets were collected in both indoor and outdoor environments.
 - **Indoor (Lab):** 9 trajectories (680 m) with trotting and adaptive gaits on flat and stone terrains. Ground truth was provided by a Vicon motion capture system at 100 Hz. Initial robot yaw was aligned with Vicon for ground truth.
 - **Outdoor (Field):** 18 trajectories (530 m) with trotting and adaptive walking on sandy and rocky terrain (Vulcano Island). Position ground truth was obtained from an Emlid GNSS RTK receiver (cm-level accuracy) at 10 Hz. Robot yaw was aligned using the Kabsch algorithm from the initial 2 meters of each trajectory.
 - IMU and joint encoder data were recorded at 1000 Hz for both datasets. All data was released open-source.

#### 3.4. Parameter Tuning

- **Fixed-tuned InEKF Optimization:** Initial noise parameters for the fixed-tuned InEKF, and as starting values for adaptation, were optimized using a "gold-standard" method. This involves calculating **Q** from the covariance of differences between propagated and ground-truth states, and **R** from the covariance of errors between leg odometry measurements and motion capture-derived displacements during foot contact. Optimization was performed on indoor trotting and adaptive trajectories (L1, L5), and parameters were transferred to the outdoor dataset.
- **Window Size for Innovation and Residual:** An optimal window size of 1 second was empirically determined for the proposed adaptation method.
- **Scale Factors for Baseline Approach:** Hyperparameters for the IMU and foot force-based method (α1, α2, α3) were selected via grid search on the indoor dataset.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Metrics

Performance was assessed using:
- **Position Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)** improvement (RMSEp) relative to the fixed-tuned InEKF, expressed as a percentage. Higher values indicate better performance.
- **Drift per Distance Traveled (DDT)**, calculated as the absolute difference between final estimated and ground-truth positions along each axis, normalized by the total trajectory length.
- **Yaw orientation error (RMSE**^yaw^**)** for the indoor dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the performance of two adaptive Kalman filtering approaches against a fixed-tuned InEKF for legged robot state estimation, with a particular focus on the adaptation of process noise (Q) and measurement noise (R) covariance matrices.

#### 4.1. Joint Q and R Adaptation Ablation Study

An initial ablation study investigated the impact of adapting Q, R, or both simultaneously.
- Adapting the **Q** matrix alone, representing IMU uncertainty, showed negligible effect on state estimation performance in both indoor and outdoor scenarios for both adaptation methods.
- In contrast, adapting the **R** matrix, representing leg kinematics uncertainty, consistently outperformed the fixed-tuned InEKF, achieving a mean position RMSE reduction of approximately 15% across datasets. The median RMSE improvement was slightly lower for the outdoor dataset, attributed to increased terrain variability.
- Jointly adapting both **Q** and **R** yielded performance comparable to adapting **R** alone.
- **Conclusion:** The findings suggest that the state estimation performance for quadruped robots is more sensitive to the modeling of measurement uncertainty (leg odometry) than to the process noise (IMU). Consequently, adapting the **R** covariance matrix alone was deemed sufficient and formed the focus of subsequent detailed analysis.

#### 4.2. R Adaptation Performance: Filter Residual vs. Foot Force

A detailed comparison of the proposed filter residual-based **R** adaptation method against the foot force-based baseline method yielded the following:

- **Overall Performance (RMSEp):**
 - **Indoors:** The filter residual method demonstrated a higher overall mean RMSEp improvement (16.08%) compared to the foot force method (13.39%) relative to the fixed-tuned InEKF.
 - **Outdoors:** The foot force method achieved a slightly higher overall mean RMSEp improvement (16.39%) than the filter residual method (14.89%).
- **Yaw Orientation Error (RMSE**^yaw^**):** For the indoor dataset, the filter residual method resulted in a larger overall reduction in yaw RMSE (0.64 rad) compared to the foot force method (0.71 rad) and the fixed-tuned InEKF (0.77 rad). This indicates improved heading estimation.
- **Drift per Distance Traveled (DDT):** The filter residual-based adaptation generally achieved a larger reduction in drift across x and y axes for both datasets. Specifically, for indoor data, it showed better x and y DDT. For outdoor data, it demonstrated better DDT across all x, y, and z axes compared to the foot force method. The z-axis drift was negative indoors (flat floor) and positive outdoors (rocky terrain), suggesting different error sources (modeling errors/flexibility vs. unstable contact).
- **Gait-Specific Performance:**
 - **Trotting Gait:** Adaptation significantly improved results by up to 25%. The proposed filter residual method outperformed the baseline foot force approach on indoor trotting (23.68% vs 15.83% RMSEp improvement). On outdoor trotting, the foot force method slightly edged out the filter residual method (24.87% vs 20.44% RMSEp improvement).
 - **Adaptive Gait:** Improvements were more modest, around 8%. On indoor adaptive gait, foot force adaptation performed better (8.51% vs 0.88% RMSEp improvement). Conversely, on outdoor adaptive gait, the filter residual method showed better performance (7.95% vs 5.79% RMSEp improvement).
- **Trajectory Visualization:** Visual inspection of trajectories (e.g., indoor L7, outdoor F10) showed that adaptation significantly reduced z-axis drift and improved heading estimates, leading to better alignment with ground truth, particularly with the filter residual method on L7.
- **Adaptation Factor Behavior:** Both adaptation methods effectively captured the contact-driven structure in leg kinematic measurements, as indicated by the **R** matrix adaptation factor plots. The foot-force-based adaptation exhibited slightly more regular patterns, but the residual-based method followed a comparable trend.

#### 4.3. Dataset Release

The research includes the release of a comprehensive open-source quadruped dataset, covering a total distance of 1200 meters from both indoor and outdoor locomotion experiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of legged robotics by addressing the critical challenge of robust state estimation in dynamic and unstructured environments. The proposed online noise covariance adaptation for Kalman filters, particularly focusing on the measurement noise covariance (R), offers several implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced State Estimation Accuracy:** The demonstrated improvements in state estimation accuracy, with an average of 15% overall and up to 25% during trotting gaits, signify a notable advancement. More accurate and reliable state estimates are fundamental for model-based control frameworks, enabling legged robots to achieve more stable, efficient, and dynamic locomotion, especially when traversing complex terrains or performing agile maneuvers.

2. **Increased Robustness to Environmental Variability:** By adapting the noise characteristics online, the state estimator becomes less reliant on fixed, manually tuned parameters. This inherent adaptability allows the robot to maintain performance across varying gaits, contact conditions (e.g., slippery surfaces, uneven ground), and environmental disturbances (e.g., impacts, vibrations), which are common in real-world applications such as search-and-rescue, industrial inspection, or planetary exploration.

3. **Reduced Sensor Requirements and Manual Tuning:** A key advantage of the proposed filter innovation and residual-based adaptation method is its self-contained nature. Unlike the baseline approach, it does not require additional sensor data, such as foot force measurements, which can be prone to noise or failure. Furthermore, it largely obviates the need for extensive manual tuning of noise parameters, making it more practical and less labor-intensive for deployment on real robot platforms. This simplifies the development and commissioning process for legged robot systems.

4. **Insights into Noise Modeling in Legged Robotics:** The ablation study's finding that measurement noise (R) adaptation has a significantly greater impact than process noise (Q) adaptation provides valuable insight. It suggests that modeling the time-varying uncertainty in leg odometry, primarily influenced by contact conditions and foot slippage, is more critical for robust state estimation in proprioceptive legged systems than solely adjusting IMU-related process noise. This understanding can guide future research in designing more effective state estimators.

5. **Foundation for Advanced Control and Autonomy:** Improved state estimation directly supports the development of more sophisticated control algorithms and higher levels of autonomy. With more trustworthy state information, control systems can make more informed decisions, leading to better trajectory tracking, disturbance rejection, and ultimately, enhanced operational capabilities for legged robots in demanding scenarios.

6. **Open-Source Dataset Contribution:** The release of a comprehensive, real-world quadruped dataset, covering diverse indoor and outdoor locomotion over a substantial distance, serves as a valuable resource for the robotics community. This dataset facilitates benchmarking of new state estimation algorithms, promotes reproducibility, and accelerates research and development in legged robot locomotion and perception.

7. **Practicality over Complex Learning Methods:** The reliance on classical adaptive Kalman filter methods, emphasizing speed, stability, and interpretability, positions this work as a practical alternative to some learning-based approaches which may incur higher computational demands, suffer from explainability issues, or exhibit poor generalization across different robot platforms. This is particularly relevant for safety-critical applications where predictability and reliability are paramount.
