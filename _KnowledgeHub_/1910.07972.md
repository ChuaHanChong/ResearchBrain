# Adaptive Curriculum Generation from Demonstrations for Sim-to-Real Visuomotor Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Adaptive Curriculum Generation from Demonstrations (ACGD) trains sparse-reward visuomotor RL policies by adaptively tuning task difficulty: it controls where episodes are initialized along ten demonstration trajectories and jointly scales simulation and domain-randomization parameters to hold success rates inside a target band. Policies trained in PyBullet transfer zero-shot to a real KUKA arm, reaching 85% pick-and-stow and 60% block stacking.

## Problem

- Sparse-reward manipulation defeats random exploration, and reward shaping is task-specific, needs manual tuning, and biases policies toward satisfying the surrogate reward.
- Behavior cloning needs hundreds or thousands of expert demonstrations, suffers compounding error, and copies possibly suboptimal demonstrator actions rather than improving on them.
- Prior reverse curricula sample start states uniformly or linearly backwards, ignoring bottleneck sub-tasks; heavy domain randomization applied from the start slows or blocks convergence.

## Method

- ==Reverse-trajectory sampling== initializes episodes near the end of ten demonstrations and moves earlier, mixed with regular randomized resets via a success-rate-and-progress-dependent probability, using only visited states, not actions.
- Task and environment parameters (bounciness, gripper speed, camera pose, colors, brightness, dynamics) are unified as difficulty knobs scaled linearly by coefficients for demonstration and regular resets.
- The **ACGD** adaptive controller increments or decrements each difficulty coefficient by a fixed step to keep measured success rates within a target interval, with **PPO** as the on-policy learner.

## Results

- In simulation ACGD reaches **94%** pick-and-stow and **91%** stacking; behavior cloning gets **23%**, while sparse-reward RL, shaped-reward RL, and BC-initialized RL fail to complete either task.
- On stacking, the adaptive curriculum exceeds uniform and linear reverse-curriculum baselines by over **20** percentage points final success rate, with lower variance across five random seeds.
- Zero-shot real-robot transfer gives **85%** pick-and-stow and **60%** stacking on 2.5 cm blocks; ablations show constant maximum difficulty and image-only observations both degrade learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating domain randomization as a curriculum variable rather than a fixed setting resolves the tension between transfer robustness and training convergence.
> - Using demonstration states as reset points, discarding the demonstrated actions, keeps the method robust to suboptimal demos: learned policies ran roughly twice as fast as humans.
> - Keeping success probability inside a moderate band automatically allocates training time to bottleneck phases; the band's exact width mattered little across tested settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) presents a paradigm for developing autonomous agents capable of solving complex tasks, particularly in robotics. For continuous visuomotor control, learning directly from raw image inputs has the potential to eliminate the need for laborious manual design of multi-stage manipulation and computer vision pipelines, offering increased robustness in challenging, semi-structured environments involving contacts, non-rigid objects, or clutter.

However, a significant challenge in applying deep RL to physical robots is the exploration problem, especially in scenarios with sparse rewards. When rewards are infrequent, random exploration by an RL agent is often insufficient to discover successful action sequences. Furthermore, random exploration on a physical robot can pose safety risks. Existing approaches to address sparse rewards include:
- **Reward Shaping:** Involves manually designing dense reward functions that provide continuous feedback, guiding the agent towards the goal. While effective, reward shaping is typically task-specific, requires expert knowledge, and can potentially bias the learned policies towards the shaped rewards rather than the ultimate task goal.
- **Behavior Cloning (BC):** Utilizes expert demonstration trajectories to pre-train a policy through supervised learning. BC can guide an RL algorithm to initial rewards, but often requires a substantial volume of demonstrations and can suffer from compounding errors during execution. Some methods combine BC with RL, either hierarchically or as an initialization step.
- **Curriculum Learning:** Involves structuring the learning process by progressively increasing task difficulty, starting with simpler subtasks. Early work demonstrated benefits for supervised learning. In RL, methods include reverse curricula, which start from goal states and gradually expand the initial state distribution. However, many existing curriculum learning approaches use fixed schedules or primarily focus on initial state sampling, not adapting to the agent's learning progress or incorporating other aspects of task difficulty.

Another critical area for transferring RL policies to the real world is the "sim-to-real" gap. Training in simulation offers advantages such as accelerated learning, reduced human involvement, enhanced safety, and access to internal simulator states. Domain randomization (DR) is a technique used to bridge this gap by randomizing simulation parameters (e.g., visual textures, lighting, physics properties) to increase the policy's robustness and transferability to diverse real-world conditions. While effective, applying excessive domain randomization from the outset can hinder or slow down the initial learning process.

This paper positions its proposed method, Adaptive Curriculum Generation from Demonstrations (ACGD), as an approach that addresses the challenges of sparse rewards and sim-to-real transfer by leveraging a small number of demonstrations within an adaptive curriculum framework. ACGD extends curriculum learning by not only controlling the sampling of initial states from demonstrations but also by dynamically adjusting the degree of domain randomization. This integrated approach aims to enable robust learning of visuomotor policies in simulation that can transfer directly to physical robots without further fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective method for training visuomotor control policies for robotic manipulation tasks in the presence of sparse rewards, facilitating their transfer from simulation to the real world. The authors identify several motivations for this work, rooted in the limitations of existing reinforcement learning techniques for real-world robotics:

1. **Overcoming Sparse Rewards and Exploration Challenges:** A central motivation is to mitigate the exploration problem inherent in RL algorithms when rewards are sparse. In complex robotic manipulation tasks, randomly exploring the action space is unlikely to yield rewards, hindering policy learning. This issue is exacerbated in physical robots where random actions can be unsafe or time-consuming.
2. **Reducing Reliance on Reward Shaping:** Traditional reward shaping, while providing dense feedback, requires extensive manual engineering, is task-specific, and can introduce biases. The authors aim for a more general and automated approach that does not necessitate hand-crafted reward functions, thereby aligning with the goal of RL as a general solution method.
3. **Minimizing Expert Demonstration Requirements:** Behavior cloning approaches often demand a substantial volume of expert demonstrations to achieve robust performance. The authors seek a method that can effectively utilize a limited number of demonstrations (e.g., around a dozen) to guide the learning process without requiring extensive data collection.
4. **Enabling Adaptive Task Difficulty Scaling:** Conventional curriculum learning often follows predefined, linear schedules for increasing task difficulty. This can be inefficient if the agent learns faster or slower than anticipated, potentially leading to wasted training time on mastered subtasks or encountering overly difficult tasks too soon, which prevents learning. The motivation is to create a curriculum that dynamically adapts to the learner's current performance, maintaining a suitable level of challenge.
5. **Integrating Domain Randomization into Curriculum Learning:** While domain randomization is crucial for sim-to-real transfer, applying it uniformly and maximally from the beginning of training can impede learning convergence. The authors are motivated to incorporate the degree of domain randomization as a parameter of task difficulty within the curriculum, allowing it to be gradually increased as the policy matures. This aims to ensure that policies are sufficiently robust for real-world transfer without compromising early training stability.
6. **Achieving Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The ultimate motivation is to develop policies trained entirely in simulation that can be directly deployed on physical robots without any real-world fine-tuning or adaptation. This would significantly reduce the cost, time, and safety concerns associated with real-world robot learning.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Develop an adaptive curriculum generation method for reinforcement learning with sparse rewards using a limited number of demonstrations.
- Automate the scaling of task difficulty by controlling both initial state sampling from demonstrations and the degree of domain randomization.
- Demonstrate successful zero-shot transfer of visuomotor control policies from simulation to real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Adaptive Curriculum Generation from Demonstrations (ACGD), combines curriculum learning, demonstration data, and adaptive domain randomization within a reinforcement learning framework.

**Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** The core policy optimization is performed using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an on-policy RL algorithm. PPO was selected due to its suitability for environments where parameters, and thus the task, change gradually over time.

**Policy Network Architecture:**
- The observation space comprises an 84x84 pixel RGB camera image (from an eye-in-hand camera) and a proprioceptive state vector.
- The proprioceptive state vector includes gripper height, gripper rotation angle, gripper finger opening width, and normalized remaining timesteps.
- The network processes the RGB image using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN).
- The output of the CNN is concatenated with the proprioceptive state vector.
- This combined feature vector is then fed into a shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) that branches into two heads:
 - **Policy Head:** Outputs the mean and standard deviation of a 5-dimensional continuous action space (for a diagonal Gaussian distribution). The actions include Cartesian offsets in x, y, z, yaw rotation, and a binary gripper action (open/close).
 - **Value Head:** Outputs a single scalar value representing the value function.

**Adaptive Curriculum Generation from Demonstrations (ACGD):**
ACGD adaptively controls task difficulty using two primary mechanisms: reverse-trajectory sampling from demonstrations and adaptive scaling of environment/domain randomization parameters.

1. **Reverse-Trajectory Sampling:**
 - To bootstrap exploration in sparse reward settings, episodes are initialized from states within a small set of manually recorded demonstration trajectories (10 demonstrations were used for each task).
 - Initially, states are sampled from the end of the demonstration trajectories, close to the goal, making the task easier.
 - As training progresses and the agent learns, initial states are progressively sampled from earlier points in the trajectories, increasing task difficulty.
 - To prevent the policy from becoming biased towards the specific states in the demonstrations and to encourage generalization, "regular resets" are mixed with "demonstration resets." Regular resets involve randomly initializing the environment states within a broader distribution.
 - The probability of sampling a regular reset is dynamically adjusted based on the success rate of regular reset episodes and the overall training progress, using a linear mixing function. The approach does not require the actions from the demonstrations, only the visited states, which is stated to improve robustness to suboptimal demonstrations.

2. **Task and Environment Parameter Adaptation (Adaptive Domain Randomization):**
 - Beyond initial state proximity to the goal, task difficulty is influenced by various environment parameters (H), such as object bounciness, gripper speed, camera properties (e.g., field of view, position), and visual attributes (e.g., block/table color, image brightness/contrast/hue/saturation/sharpness, blur).
 - At the beginning of training, these parameters are set to their "easiest" configurations (e.g., minimal object bounciness, minimal randomization).
 - During training, the variance of the sampling distributions for these parameters is linearly scaled, effectively increasing the degree of domain randomization and task difficulty over time. This gradual increase aims to prevent an excessive level of randomization from hindering initial learning.
 - A distinction is made between parameters that can be randomized during demonstration resets ($H_d$) and additional parameters that can be randomized during regular resets ($H_a$), as some configurations are pre-determined by the demonstration when sampling from them.

3. **Adaptive Difficulty Control Mechanism:**
 - The core of ACGD is to maintain the success rate within a predefined target interval [α, β] (empirically set to [0.4, 0.6]).
 - Two separate difficulty coefficients, $\delta_d$ (for demonstration resets) and $\delta_r$ (for regular resets), both ranging from 0 (easiest) to 1 (most difficult), control the scaling of task parameters and sampling locations.
 - If the observed success rate for a given reset type (demonstration or regular) falls below α, its corresponding $\delta$ is decreased by a small increment $\epsilon$.
 - If the success rate exceeds β, its $\delta$ is increased by $\epsilon$.
 - This adaptive adjustment ensures the agent continually learns at an appropriate difficulty level, dedicating more time to challenging parts of the task.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Tasks:** Two robotic manipulation tasks were used: pick-and-stow (picking a block and placing it in a box) and block stacking (stacking one small block on another).
- **Robot System:** A KUKA iiwa manipulator equipped with a WSG-50 two-finger gripper and an Intel SR300 camera for an eye-in-hand view.
- **Simulation:** PyBullet Physics simulator was used, with efforts to align simulation and real-world setups.
- **Training:** Policies were trained for 10^7 timesteps across 8 parallel environments, taking approximately 11 hours on a system with one Titan X GPU and 16 CPU cores.
- **Evaluation:** Policy performance was evaluated on the maximum task difficulty to ensure comparability across methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented ACGD as a method for learning visuomotor control policies in simulated environments for zero-shot transfer to real robots. The findings indicate the effectiveness of this approach across two manipulation tasks: pick-and-stow and block stacking.

**Performance in Simulation:**

1. **Enabling Learning for Sparse Reward Tasks:**
 - For the **pick-and-stow task**, ACGD achieved an average final success rate of 94%. In contrast, baseline methods like standard PPO with sparse rewards or even shaped rewards were unable to reliably complete the task. Behavior Cloning (BC) alone showed a 23% success rate but lacked consistency. Using BC as an initialization for PPO with sparse rewards resulted in catastrophic forgetting.
 - For the more complex **block stacking task**, which involves long episode lengths and precision, none of the curriculum-free baselines (sparse RL, shaped RL, BC, or BC initialized RL) were able to solve the task.

2. **Outperforming Other Curriculum Baselines:**
 - In the block stacking task, ACGD outperformed uniform and linear reverse curriculum learning variants. ACGD demonstrated faster learning and achieved a final success rate more than 20% higher than linear curriculum learning, while also exhibiting less variance across different random seeds, suggesting improved learning stability.
 - The experiments indicated the importance of mixing demonstration resets with regular, randomized environment resets, especially when demonstration data is limited. Linear curricula that included regular resets outperformed those relying solely on demonstration-sampled initial states.

3. **Efficiency of Learned Policies:**
 - ACGD learned significantly more efficient solutions than the human demonstrations it was trained on. For the block stacking task, manual demonstration episodes averaged 164 ± 17 transitions, whereas the learned policy achieved the task in 73 ± 15 transitions, approximately twice as fast.

4. **Impact of Adaptive Task and Domain Randomization Parameters:**
 - Ablation studies demonstrated that adaptively changing task parameters (H) and domain randomization during training improved both training speed and final performance.
 - Training with adaptively increased difficulty for these parameters outperformed a constant maximum difficulty setting.
 - Using separate difficulty coefficients ($\delta_d$ and $\delta_r$) for demonstration resets and regular resets performed better than sharing a single $\delta$ across both types of resets.

**Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:**

1. **Successful Real-World Deployment:**
 - Policies trained exclusively in simulation using ACGD were transferred to a physical KUKA iiwa robot without any additional real-world fine-tuning.
 - For the **pick-and-stow task**, a success rate of 85% (17 out of 20 trials) was observed on the real robot.
 - For the **block stacking task**, a success rate of 60% (12 out of 20 trials) was achieved on the real robot using small 2.5cm wooden blocks, a task described as requiring high precision.
 - The learned policies operated in a closed-loop manner, implicitly re-attempting failed actions (e.g., re-grasping a dropped block) within the episode time limit, which contributes to the observed success rates.
 - In contrast, policies learned with non-adaptive curriculum baselines did not achieve successful sim-to-real transfer.

2. **Comparison to Related Work in Real-World Transfer:**
 - The reported 60% success rate for block stacking with small, rigid blocks compares favorably to prior work (e.g., Zhu et al.), which achieved 35% success with larger, deformable foam blocks that are generally considered easier to grasp and stack.

**Additional Experiments (Appendix):**

1. **Input Modality:** Including proprioceptive features (gripper height, angle, width, remaining timesteps) alongside the camera image significantly outperformed learning from images alone, indicating the value of this additional state information.
2. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The reward interval [α, β] of [0.4, 0.6] was identified as optimal, though other tested intervals also yielded good performance. The difficulty increment $\epsilon = 0.002$ also showed slightly superior performance compared to other values, but variations did not drastically alter training curves.

In summary, ACGD demonstrates that adaptively controlling task difficulty through a combination of sparse demonstration sampling and dynamic domain randomization can enable efficient learning of robust visuomotor policies in simulation, leading to notable zero-shot transfer capabilities for complex robotic manipulation tasks in the real world.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Adaptive Curriculum Generation from Demonstrations (ACGD) method presents several significant contributions and holds potential for broad impact in the field of robotic reinforcement learning:

1. **Addressing Core RL Challenges:** ACGD directly tackles two fundamental obstacles in deploying deep reinforcement learning for robotics: the problem of sparse rewards and the exploration dilemma. By adaptively managing task difficulty, the method enables learning in scenarios where traditional RL algorithms struggle to find any reward.
2. **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** The approach demonstrates effectiveness with a limited number of expert demonstrations (approximately 10 per task). This reduces the significant burden of data collection often associated with imitation learning or behavior cloning methods, making RL more practical for a wider range of robotic tasks.
3. **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A key achievement is the successful zero-shot transfer of visuomotor policies from simulation to physical robots. This capability is critical for reducing development costs, time, and safety risks in robotics. The adaptive scaling of domain randomization within the curriculum is shown to be instrumental in training policies that are sufficiently robust to handle the discrepancies between simulated and real-world environments.
4. **Automation of Curriculum Design:** Unlike static or linearly scheduled curricula, ACGD adaptively adjusts task difficulty based on the agent's real-time learning progress. This reduces the need for manual tuning of curriculum schedules, making the learning process more autonomous and efficient. The unified treatment of demonstration sampling and domain randomization as difficulty parameters offers a streamlined approach to curriculum generation.
5. **Improved Policy Efficiency:** The learned policies demonstrated superior efficiency (e.g., faster task completion) compared to human demonstrations. This indicates that the method not only enables learning but can also optimize for performance beyond the demonstrated behavior, which is a desirable characteristic for autonomous systems.
6. **Broader Applicability:** The method's general principles, such as adaptive difficulty scaling and combining demonstration sampling with domain randomization, are not inherently task-specific. This suggests that ACGD could be applicable to a variety of other complex, multi-step manipulation tasks requiring visuomotor control.
7. **Advancement in Fine Manipulation:** The successful transfer of policies for tasks like block stacking with small blocks underscores the method's ability to handle high-precision, contact-rich interactions. This capability is valuable for applications in manufacturing, assembly, and service robotics where delicate and accurate object manipulation is required.

The potential impact of this work lies in facilitating the wider adoption of deep reinforcement learning for real-world robotic systems. By offering a more robust, data-efficient, and automated training paradigm that bridges the sim-to-real gap, ACGD contributes to the development of more versatile and capable autonomous robots for a range of industrial and research applications.
