# Is the Future Compatible? Diagnosing Dynamic Consistency in World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This diagnostic study defines action-state consistency for World Action Models: how closely the future a model predicts matches the observation actually reached by executing its predicted actions, measured in latent space. Consistency separates successful from failed rollouts in Cosmos-Policy and LingBot-VA and tracks learned value trends. It also drives a training-free consensus best-of-N selector that lifts RoboTwin 2.0 success from 90.2% to 93.0%.

## Problem

- World Action Model and VLA evaluations score downstream success, predicted reward or generated video quality, but never isolate whether predicted futures are faithful to the action-conditioned transition.
- Imagined futures can look plausible yet be dynamically incompatible with the actions they condition on, misrepresenting contact or object motion, and no existing metric exposes this failure.
- Choosing among sampled candidate futures at test time relies on learned value heads, reward models or verifiers, which many World Action Models lack and which need extra training.

## Method

- ==Action-state consistency== score: exp(-alpha times ==MSE==) between predicted and realized future observations in ==VAE latent space==, z-scored per task, studied on joint-prediction **Cosmos-Policy** and ==inverse-dynamics== **LingBot-VA**.
- ==Background-collapse== diagnosis: ==latent change magnitude== across the prediction horizon explains misaligned tasks, where stalled failures predict near-static scenes that are trivially easy to forecast and therefore deceptively consistent.
- **Consistency-Consensus** selection: sample N=8 candidate futures, average them into a ==consensus future==, and pick the single branch closest to it; **Consistency-Exploring** executes every branch from a reset as an upper bound.

## Results

- On consistency-aligned tasks, logistic regression on normalized episode consistency predicts success versus failure with AUC **0.77** for Cosmos-Policy and **0.88** for LingBot-VA, with Cohen's d of **0.76** and **0.99**.
- On RoboCasa, Consistency-Consensus lifts Cosmos-Policy from **66.6%** to **67.3%**, nearly matching value-head selection at **67.4%**; Consistency-Exploring, which executes every branch, reaches **68.0%** as the upper bound.
- On RoboTwin 2.0, consensus selection lifts LingBot-VA from **90.2%** to **93.0%**; success rises steadily as candidates grow from **1** to **8**, and scoring adds only about **0.7 ms**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Judge world models by whether imagined futures match the transitions their actions actually produce, not by visual realism alone; that agreement carries value-like, decision-relevant signal without a value head.
> - Prediction-error signals are confounded by scene motion: stalled or static failures are easy to predict, so consistency-based failure detectors need a motion-magnitude check to avoid rewarding collapse.
> - Agreement among a model's own sampled futures is a cheap, training-free ranking signal; picking one branch beats averaging actions that live on SO(3)/SE(3) or encode distinct motion hypotheses.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control has seen significant advancements with the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which facilitate direct mapping from visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. These models, often leveraging large-scale vision-language pretraining and robot demonstration data, offer a general interface for instruction-conditioned manipulation across diverse tasks. However, VLA models primarily focus on direct action prediction and do not inherently represent the future consequences of an action, making it difficult to assess the feasibility or task alignment of predicted commands.

World Action Models (WAMs) address this limitation by extending VLA models to include future prediction capabilities. WAMs model not only the next action but also the future state induced by that action, thereby enabling anticipation of object motion, contact, scene evolution, and the consequences of robot behavior prior to or during execution. This capability is crucial for informed decision-making and planning in complex robotic tasks.

Despite the advancement of WAMs in generating plausible future observations, the reliability of these predicted futures as a basis for decision-making has remained a subject of investigation. Specifically, prior research has largely focused on metrics such as downstream task success, predicted reward, or the visual quality of generated futures. However, a gap exists in systematically evaluating whether the predicted future is dynamically compatible with the action it purports to model, and whether it faithfully represents the underlying environment dynamics.

This paper introduces "action-state consistency" as a previously under-examined reliability axis for WAMs. It distinguishes itself from existing WAM and VLA studies, which have progressed in areas like policy learning, action-conditioned future prediction, verifier-based selection, reward estimation, and value-based planning, by focusing on the internal dynamic alignment between actions and their predicted or realized future states. By investigating this internal consistency, the work aims to provide a diagnostic tool for WAM reliability and a practical signal for value-free planning, thereby contributing to the development of more trustworthy and robust robotic policies. It connects to broader efforts in world models (e.g., Dreamer, World Models) by focusing on action-conditioned predictions and to test-time scaling methods for robot control by proposing a novel, model-agnostic scoring signal for candidate action selection.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation of this research is to address a perceived gap in the evaluation and utilization of World Action Models (WAMs): the reliability of their imagined futures. While WAMs can generate visually plausible predictions of future observations and actions, it is not consistently clear whether these predictions are dynamically compatible with the action sequences they claim to model, or if they are truly physically faithful and decision-relevant. The authors identify that a model might produce a visually convincing future that does not accurately reflect the physical transition induced by a given action, potentially leading to unreliable decision-making.

This leads to the introduction of **action-state consistency** as a critical, yet under-examined, reliability property for WAMs. Action-state consistency is defined as the alignment between predicted actions and the induced state transitions. A strong alignment indicates that the model effectively captures action-conditioned dynamics, while a mismatch suggests potential failures in action conditioning, such as misrepresenting contact, object motion, or the consequences of selected actions.

The paper outlines three key objectives to investigate action-state consistency:

1. **Characterizing Action-State Consistency in WAMs:** The first objective is to determine if action-state consistency can be systematically measured across different WAM formulations (joint-prediction and inverse-dynamics models) and whether it correlates with task success. This involves analyzing if successful robot rollouts exhibit distinct consistency scores compared to failed ones, and if this distinction can serve as a predictive signal for task outcomes.
2. **Identifying When Consistency Becomes Unreliable (Boundary Conditions):** The second objective is to uncover specific conditions under which action-state consistency may cease to be a reliable indicator of task success. This includes identifying failure modes where, despite task failure, predicted futures might still appear consistent, thereby providing misleading reliability signals. A particular focus is placed on the phenomenon termed "background collapse."
3. **Using Consistency as a Value-Free Test-Time Selection Signal:** The third objective explores the practical utility of action-state consistency beyond diagnosis. Given that many WAMs may not provide an explicit value head for reward prediction, the motivation is to investigate if consistency, as an intrinsic measure of dynamic alignment, can serve as a proxy for utility and guide test-time action selection. This aims to provide a model-agnostic strategy for improving task success without requiring additional training or reward modeling.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to establish action-state consistency as both a diagnostic tool for evaluating WAM reliability and a practical signal for value-free planning, thereby enhancing the trustworthiness and effectiveness of robotic agents that rely on imagined futures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research systematically investigates action-state consistency across two representative World Action Model (WAM) formulations: joint-prediction models and inverse-dynamics models.

**WAM Formulations Studied:**
- **Joint-Prediction WAMs:** These models, exemplified by Cosmos-Policy, directly parameterize the joint distribution of future observations and actions. They simultaneously generate future observations ($o_{t+1:t+\Delta}$) and actions ($a_{t+1:t+\Delta}$) given past observations, proprioceptive state, and task instruction. Consistency is implicitly enforced through a shared latent representation.
- **Inverse-Dynamics WAMs:** These models, exemplified by LingBot-VA, decompose the joint distribution into two components: a world model predicting future observations and an inverse dynamics model inferring actions required to realize those predicted states. This separation makes the action-outcome alignment an explicit modeling objective.

The paper uses Cosmos-Policy (pretrained on RoboCasa) and LingBot-VA (pretrained on RoboTwin 2.0) as the primary models for empirical study.

**Measuring Action-State Consistency:**
Action-state consistency ($c_t$) is defined as a scalar measure of how closely predicted future observations ($\hat{o}_{t+\Delta}$) match the real observations ($o_{t+\Delta}$) obtained by executing the predicted actions in the environment. It is formally expressed as:
$c_t(\hat{o}_{t+\Delta}, o_{t+\Delta}) = \exp(-\alpha \cdot d(\hat{o}_{t+\Delta}, o_{t+\Delta}))$
where $\alpha$ is a scaling term (set to 0.1) and $d(\cdot, \cdot)$ is a distance function. The distance metric used is Mean Squared Error (MSE) computed in the latent space prior to VAE decoding. This choice aims to capture structural agreement in the decision-relevant feature space, rather than being sensitive to minor pixel-level variations.

**Characterizing Action-State Consistency (Section 3):**
1. **Emergence and Separability (RQ1):**
 - To compare consistency across tasks with varying score ranges, episode-level consistency scores are normalized into z-scores within each task.
 - Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) plots and Cohen's d effect sizes are used to statistically compare normalized consistency distributions for successful versus failed trajectories across different models and tasks.
 - A logistic regression classifier, trained on normalized consistency scores with 5-fold cross-validation, is used to predict task success or failure. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and Area Under the Curve (AUC) values quantify the predictive power of consistency.
2. **Boundary Conditions (RQ2):**
 - The study identifies "background collapse" as a failure mode where low-dynamics failed trajectories become nearly static or collapse towards background-like predictions, leading to deceptively high consistency scores despite task failure.
 - To quantify this, **latent change magnitude ($\Delta z_t$)** is introduced, defined as the distance between latent representations at consecutive states ($d(z_t, z_{t+\Delta})$). This metric is used to correlate the amount of scene motion with consistency scores.
 - Analysis involves comparing latent change trajectories across "consistency-aligned" (success has higher consistency) and "consistency-misaligned" (failure has higher consistency) tasks, further partitioned by success and failure outcomes.
3. **Alignment with Utility (RQ3):**
 - Using Cosmos-Policy, which provides explicit value prediction outputs, the research compares the temporal evolution of consistency scores with predicted value estimates.
 - The "success minus failure" gap for both metrics is analyzed to determine if consistency trends similarly to value predictions, suggesting its potential as a utility signal.

**Consistency-Guided Test-Time Selection (Section 4):**
The paper proposes two strategies for using consistency as a test-time selection signal, addressing the challenge of sparse rewards and the absence of explicit value heads in some WAMs:

1. **Selection by Environment Exploring (Consistency-Exploring):**
 - At each decision step, the WAM samples $N$ candidate action sequences and their predicted future observations.
 - Each candidate action is *executed* from the same initial state in the environment, and the *real* resulting observation is recorded.
 - An action-state consistency score is computed for each branch by comparing its predicted future observation with the *real* observed future.
 - The branch with the highest consistency score is selected and its action is executed. This strategy serves as an upper bound for the potential of consistency-guided selection but requires environment resets and multiple executions.

2. **Selection by Future Consensus (Consistency-Consensus):**
 - This deployable strategy avoids multiple environment executions. The WAM samples $N$ candidate action sequences and their *predicted* future observations.
 - A **consensus future** ($\bar{o}_{t+\Delta}$) is computed by averaging all $N$ predicted future observations.
 - Each candidate branch is then scored by how consistent its *own predicted future* is with this *consensus future*.
 - The branch whose predicted future is most consistent with the consensus is selected (winner-takes-all strategy). This approach relies on the assumption that the consensus mean provides a better approximation of the true future than any single prediction. The use of winner-takes-all over weighted averaging of actions is justified by the non-Euclidean nature of many robot actions and the potential for blurring distinct motion hypotheses.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on RoboCasa (24 kitchen manipulation tasks, single arm) with Cosmos-Policy and RoboTwin 2.0 (50 bimanual manipulation tasks, dual arm) with LingBot-VA. Default test-time scaling uses $N=8$ candidate rollouts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding action-state consistency in WAMs:

**Characterizing Action-State Consistency:**
- **Emergence and Separability:** WAMs exhibit measurable action-state consistency that systematically separates successful and failed rollouts. Successful episodes consistently showed higher normalized consistency scores than failed ones.
 - Quantitatively, the effect sizes (Cohen's d) were 0.76 for Cosmos-Policy and 0.99 for LingBot-VA, indicating a substantial separation between success and failure distributions.
 - A logistic regression classifier, using only normalized consistency scores, predicted task success with AUCs of 0.77 for Cosmos-Policy and 0.88 for LingBot-VA in aligned task settings. This suggests that consistency provides a strong predictive signal for task outcomes.
- **Boundary Conditions (Background Collapse):** The study identified "background collapse" as a condition where consistency becomes unreliable as a sole indicator of task success. In certain tasks, failed trajectories could unexpectedly obtain high consistency scores.
 - Background collapse occurs when low-dynamics failed trajectories lead to static or background-like predictions that are trivially accurate, despite the task not being completed.
 - This phenomenon is explained by a negative correlation between latent change magnitude ($\Delta z_t$) and consistency scores. Smaller latent changes (indicating less motion) tended to receive higher consistency scores. Misaligned failed tasks exhibited a rapid and severe drop in latent change over time, supporting the background collapse explanation.
- **Alignment with Utility:** Consistency was found to align with learned value predictions, suggesting it captures information correlated with downstream utility.
 - On Cosmos-Policy, a similar positive gap between successful and failed rollouts was observed for both predicted value and consistency scores over time. This indicates that successful trajectories generally have both higher predicted values and higher consistency. This finding supports consistency's potential as a decision-making signal when an explicit value head is absent.

**Consistency-Guided Test-Time Selection:**
The proposed consistency-guided test-time selection strategies demonstrated improvements in task success rates without additional training or reward modeling.

- **RoboCasa Results (Cosmos-Policy):**
 - The reimplemented Cosmos-Policy baseline achieved a 66.6% average success rate.
 - Value-Prediction (using the model's explicit value head) improved this to 67.4%.
 - Consistency-Consensus (the deployable strategy) achieved 67.3%, nearly matching value-based selection.
 - Consistency-Exploring (the upper-bound strategy) achieved the highest success rate of 68.0%.
- **RoboTwin 2.0 Results (LingBot-VA):**
 - The reimplemented LingBot-VA baseline achieved a 90.2% average success rate.
 - Consistency-Consensus improved this to 93.0%. This result is particularly relevant as RoboTwin 2.0 does not provide a state-saving API, making Consistency-Consensus the practical choice.
- **Scaling with Number of Candidates:** Increasing the number of sampled candidates ($N$) from 1 to 8 further improved success rates for both models using the Consistency-Consensus strategy (e.g., from 66.6% to 67.3% for Cosmos-Policy; from 90.2% to 93.0% for LingBot-VA). This suggests that consistency provides an effective ranking signal that benefits from increased exploration of candidate futures.
- **Mitigating Background Collapse:** Consistency-guided selection helped mitigate background collapse by preserving task-relevant dynamics in later execution stages. This indicates that better early-stage action selection can reduce the likelihood of trajectories entering collapsed prediction regimes.
- **Winner-Takes-All vs. Weighted Averaging:** The study found that a winner-takes-all approach for selecting actions based on consensus scores outperformed weighted averaging of actions, supporting the preservation of coherent motion hypotheses over blending potentially incompatible action intentions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic control and world modeling by establishing **action-state consistency** as a significant and previously under-examined reliability axis for World Action Models (WAMs). The findings suggest that evaluating WAMs based on whether their imagined futures are dynamically compatible with the actions they represent, beyond mere visual plausibility or downstream reward, is crucial for developing trustworthy robotic systems.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:
- **Novel Diagnostic Tool:** It introduces and characterizes action-state consistency as a novel diagnostic for WAM reliability. This adds a new dimension to WAM evaluation, complementing existing metrics like task reward, visual prediction quality, and learned value estimates. By understanding when and why WAMs produce inconsistent predictions (e.g., through "background collapse"), researchers can better diagnose model limitations and target improvements.
- **Value-Free Planning Signal:** The demonstration that consistency correlates with task utility and can guide test-time selection without explicit reward modeling or value functions is a practical advancement. Many WAMs do not include a dedicated value head, making value-based planning difficult. The proposed Consistency-Consensus strategy offers a model-agnostic, deployable solution to improve policy performance by leveraging the internal coherence of predicted futures. This can broaden the applicability of test-time scaling techniques to a wider range of WAM architectures.
- **Enhanced Robot Trustworthiness:** By providing a mechanism to assess the dynamic faithfulness of imagined consequences, this work paves the way for "consistency-aware" WAMs. Such models would not only predict actions and their outcomes but also explicitly signal the reliability of these predicted outcomes before acting. This explicit reasoning about trust in imagined futures could lead to more robust, safer, and ultimately more trustworthy autonomous robotic policies, particularly in scenarios requiring proactive decision-making and error detection.
- **Insights into Model Failure Modes:** The identification and characterization of "background collapse" provide valuable insights into a common failure mode in generative world models. Understanding that low-dynamics, failed trajectories can become deceptively consistent highlights a critical challenge in relying solely on visual prediction accuracy. This finding suggests future research directions in training-time interventions or robust early-stage action selection to mitigate such collapses.

The potential impact of this research is in fostering the development of more intelligent and reliable robots. By enabling robots to critically assess the dynamic compatibility of their imagined futures, this work contributes to building systems that can better reason about the consequences of their actions, leading to more robust performance in complex, real-world manipulation tasks.
