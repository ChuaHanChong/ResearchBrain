# DemoBridge: A Simulation-in-the-Loop Toolkit for Single-View Human Demonstration Retargeting

> [!summary] Summary
> DemoBridge is a toolkit converting single-view RGB-stereo human hand demonstrations into physics-validated robot-arm trajectories. A unified collision-aware trajectory optimizer jointly handles grasp selection, whole-arm and grasped-object collision, and demonstrated-path fidelity, seeded by a global route; an Isaac Sim loop validates each phase and backtracks on failure. It reaches the final place stage on 6-7 of 10 real demonstrations per task, far above reimplemented baselines.

## Problem

- Teleoperation, multi-camera rigs and mocap gloves instrument the demonstrator, raising cost and confining data collection to controlled environments rather than passive in-place recording.
- Closest retargeting systems reach demonstrated poses one at a time by per-frame inverse kinematics or keyframe interpolation, never planning the whole arm against surrounding scene geometry.
- Single-view hand keypoints and object poses jitter, drift, and are occluded exactly at contact, so the demonstrated reference is inaccurate where grasp precision matters most.

## Method

- A unified ==trajectory optimizer== over joint trajectories minimizes tracking, attachment, smoothness, collision and joint-limit terms on ==PyRoKi==, with augmented-Lagrangian hard constraints and phase-specific binary masks gating fixed-size collision primitives.
- ==Global routing== seeds the local descent into a collision-free homotopy class: seat inverse kinematics for the terminal pose, then a collision-aware sweep of the demonstrated reference, or ==RRT-Connect== when no demonstration path exists.
- A ==simulation-in-the-loop coordinator== sequences rest, grasp, transport and release phases in Isaac Sim, committing only validated phases and backtracking through ranked antipodal grasps; events come from ==MANO==-denoised hand-object co-motion, annotation-free.

## Results

- On 50 held-out synthetic scenes with a reference path, the multi-stage planner reaches **4.6mm** endpoint error and nDTW **0.78** with one collision, versus trajopt at 11.8mm, nDTW 0.11, seven collisions.
- Goal-only, the multi-stage planner attains roughly **3mm** error and **98%** success with zero collisions on both base and attach splits, against trajopt's 9.1-10.1mm and 12-26% success.
- On real demonstrations it reaches the place stage **6/10, 7/10 and 7/10** across mug-on-plate, cup-stack and pour-milk, while reimplemented YOTO and RoboWheel place at most 2/10.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Local trajectory descent cannot escape its seed's homotopy class, so a global routing stage, not a better objective, is what removes clutter collisions.
> - Reproducing a human's exact grasp pose is the wrong target; re-seating the grasp from an alternative feasible approach recovers demonstrations that pose-faithful retargeting drops.
> - Sharing one simulator between planning and validation makes the committed trajectory both a correctness check and a reusable policy-learning rollout, at no extra cost.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation learning often relies on demonstrations to acquire policies. A significant challenge in this area is the collection of such demonstrations at scale. Traditional methods for collecting robot manipulation data typically involve instrumenting the demonstrator or the robot, which imposes constraints on the data collection process.

Common approaches include:
- **Teleoperation:** Directly controlling the robot, which demands exclusive access to the platform and can disrupt ongoing operations.
- **Motion Capture (Mocap) Rigs:** Using multi-camera setups or instrumented gloves requires a calibrated environment or wearable sensors, restricting demonstrations to controlled settings.

These methods generally involve high costs and limit the environments where data can be gathered. An alternative approach gaining traction involves using ordinary single-view human video, which removes the need for specialized instrumentation or controlled environments, enabling passive data collection. However, this approach introduces challenges related to noise and partial observations in the input data.

Retargeting human hand motion to a robot arm, particularly from single-view video, necessitates bridging the "embodiment gap" between a human hand and a robot arm. Existing methods for hand-to-robot retargeting often focus on mapping demonstrated wrist or fingertip trajectories to end-effector targets and realizing them on the robot. These methods typically involve reaching demonstrated poses individually, either through per-frame inverse kinematics (IK) or by interpolating sparse keyframes. Approaches such as RoboWheel and YOTO exemplify this local, pose-by-pose strategy. Other generative works, like FlowHOI, synthesize hand-object interactions before retargeting them.

A common limitation across many existing retargeting methods is the absence of comprehensive planning against the surrounding scene. They often prioritize reaching specific poses or contacts without explicitly reasoning about whole-arm collision avoidance along the entire trajectory. This becomes particularly problematic with noisy single-view inputs, where mapped poses might be infeasible, and the reference trajectory itself is imprecise. DemoBridge positions itself within this landscape by addressing the coupled challenges of inaccurate references, kinematic feasibility, and collision avoidance through an integrated, simulation-validated planning approach. It also contributes to the "real-to-sim-to-real" and imitation learning paradigms by generating physics-validated robot trajectories suitable for simulation rollouts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a toolkit, DemoBridge, that can convert a single-view RGB stereo recording of a human hand demonstration into an executable, physics-validated robot-arm trajectory. This objective is driven by the motivation to facilitate the collection of robot manipulation data more efficiently and broadly, specifically by leveraging readily available single-view human videos.

The authors identify two fundamental and interconnected difficulties that impede effective retargeting from single-view human demonstrations:

1. **Kinematic Challenges (Embodiment Gap):** A robot arm, with its articulated body and substantial collision volume, differs significantly from a human hand. Directly mapping a human hand's trajectory to a robot end-effector often results in inverse kinematics (IK) solutions that are not collision-free, even for single target poses. This problem is compounded when retargeting an entire trajectory, as collision-free feasibility must be maintained at every waypoint. Existing methods that reach demonstrated poses one at a time often fail to account for whole-arm collision along the path.

2. **Perceptual Inaccuracies:** Single-view video inherently introduces noise, jitter, and occlusion into hand keypoint and object pose tracking. This means the demonstrated reference trajectory is often inaccurate, particularly at crucial moments such as contact points. The noise in the reference directly impacts the feasibility of robot execution.

The core motivation is to address these difficulties jointly rather than in isolation. The authors argue that feasibility, contact, and the fidelity to the demonstrated reference are interdependent factors that together determine an executable motion. Current systems, which typically perform per-frame IK or interpolate sparse keyframes, are local in nature. They reproduce individual poses but do not reason about whole-arm collision avoidance over the entire path or the inherent noise in the reference.

DemoBridge aims to overcome these limitations by:
- **Optimizing the entire joint trajectory at once:** This allows for joint reasoning over alternative grasp poses, whole-arm and grasped-object collision avoidance, and fidelity to the demonstrated path.
- **Integrating a physics simulator in the loop:** Each planned phase is validated in the simulator, and failures trigger a re-plan. This ensures the emitted action sequence is dynamically stable and physically executable, rather than merely geometrically feasible. It also means demonstrations that cannot be reproduced exactly are re-planned, not discarded.
- **Enabling passive and in-place data collection:** By requiring only a single camera, the toolkit facilitates gathering manipulation data in diverse, uncontrolled environments, feeding methods that learn from human video.

The specific contributions of DemoBridge towards these objectives are:
- A unified retargeting planner that integrates grasp selection, whole-arm and grasped-object collision, and tracking of the demonstrated end-effector path within a single collision-aware trajectory optimizer.
- A simulation-in-the-loop, annotation-free retargeting process that validates phases in a physics simulator (Isaac Sim) and automatically infers grasp and release timings from hand-object relations.
- A modular, configuration-driven toolkit architecture that allows for easy swapping of perception backends, robots, and pipeline stages without requiring code modifications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DemoBridge is structured as a modular toolkit designed to retarget human hand demonstrations to a robot arm within the Isaac Sim physics simulation environment. The system's methodology can be broken down into its architectural layers, perception backend choices, and the core retargeting solver.

#### 3.1. System Overview and Architecture

The system operates with four decoupled, independently testable layers and a shared data store:
- **UI/CLI (User Interface / Command Line Interface):** A client-side interface for triggering pipeline stages, viewing results, and handling minimal human-in-the-loop steps (e.g., click-to-segment masking for object segmentation using SAM).
- **Pipeline:** This orchestration layer manages the sequential steps of the retargeting process: scene reconstruction, demonstration recording, object tracking, hand tracking, and retargeting. Each step functions as a stage, interacting with a shared database and services through a uniform interface. This decoupling supports debugging and swapping of models.
- **Services:** Heavy computational models run as long-lived, dedicated workers (e.g., motion planner) that load weights once and serve requests via sockets. A manager activates only necessary workers, and a uniform client enables stages to call models without explicit knowledge of their execution environment. The motion planner, for instance, is compiled once per scene and reused across demonstrations.
- **Data:** A database organizes scenes, demonstrations, and their interrelationships, while large data files are stored on disk. This structure maintains explicit relationships between scenes and their associated demonstrations.
- **Sensing:** This layer provides raw appearance and geometry inputs (RGB/stereo streams) and converts them into metric depth using swappable stereo modules (e.g., TRI-STEREO or FOUNDATIONSTEREO).

#### 3.2. Design Choices for Perception

Each perception stage in DemoBridge is designed with a backend interface, allowing models to be interchanged via configuration without code alterations.
- **Scene Reconstruction:** Generates watertight per-object meshes from a reference frame and object masks, utilizing backends such as RECGEN or SAM3D.
- **Hand Tracking:** Detects 2D hand landmarks (e.g., with MEDIAPIPE HANDS or HAMER) and lifts them to a metric-scale 3D wrist/palm reference using depth information.
- **Object Tracking:** Recovers per-frame 6D object poses using backends like ANY6D or FOUNDATIONPOSE.

#### 3.3. Retargeting Solver

The core of DemoBridge is its retargeting solver, which operates in three sequential parts: event extraction, a simulation-in-the-loop coordinator, and a collision-aware motion planner.

##### 3.3.1. Event Extraction

This component identifies reliable contact events (grasp and release) from noisy, single-view tracking data.
- **Noise Filtering:** Raw tracks undergo noise reduction, including occlusion gap interpolation, median filtering for spike removal, and Savitzky–Golay smoothing for hand keypoints and wrist poses.
- **3D Lifting and Denoising:** Single-view keypoints are lifted to 3D using depth maps. To counteract errors caused by object foreground-occlusion, a MANO hand model is fitted to keypoints, providing clean, anatomically consistent 3D joint positions.
- **Contact Inference:** Grasp and release events are inferred by detecting kinematic co-motion: an object is deemed "held" if it moves consistently with the gripper over a specified time window while the gripper is in motion. This process outputs a sequence of "hold" and "idle" states, transitioning between "rest," "grasp," "transport," and "release" events. Each grasp event records the human's hand pose, used as a prior for grasp selection.

##### 3.3.2. Simulation-in-the-Loop Coordinator

The coordinator manages the task sequence and integrates the physics simulator (Isaac Sim) for validation.
- **State Machine:** It is driven by a state machine encompassing "rest," "grasp," "transport," and "release" phases.
- **Planner Query and Validation:** For each phase, the coordinator queries the motion planner, providing the current state, phase-specific reference trajectories, scene collision information, and the current attachment state (which object is held). The planner returns a motion, which is then executed in Isaac Sim.
- **Outcome Evaluation and Backtracking:** The simulator returns a "settled state" (achieved configuration, object poses, held objects). A phase is committed only if the simulator confirms successful execution (e.g., gripper holds object, carried object follows motion, neighbors remain stable). If validation fails, the coordinator backtracks to the previous state and retries, potentially with alternative grasp candidates (antipodal samples ranked by demonstrated hand pose). This closed-loop validation ensures dynamic consistency and re-plans instead of discarding failed demonstrations.

##### 3.3.3. Motion Planner

This is the algorithmic core, addressing both global routing and local trajectory refinement.

- **Trajectory Optimization:** The planner minimizes a nonlinear least-squares cost function over a joint trajectory Q, built upon PyRoKi and enhanced with an augmented-Lagrangian wrapper for constraints. The objective function J(Q; m) includes:
 - `L_track`: End-effector tracking of the demonstrated reference path.
 - `L_attach`: Rigid attachment of a held object to the gripper.
 - `L_smooth`: Penalization of velocity and acceleration for smooth motion.
 - `L_coll`: Penalization of self-collision and collision with active scene geometry.
 - `L_lim`: Enforcement of joint limits.
 A vector of binary masks `m` specializes the objective for different phases and attachment states. Collision primitives are managed efficiently, with world-frame and gripper-frame versions for graspable objects, allowing mask switching instead of problem rebuilding. A clearance buffer `δ_s(t)` prevents collision, tapering for the grasp object during approach to allow contact.

- **Global Routing:** A local descent of the trajectory optimizer cannot escape local minima or change homotopy classes. The global stage provides an initial seed in a collision-free homotopy class through three steps:
 1. **Seat IK:** Solves inverse kinematics for the gripper to reach the demonstrated terminal pose (`x_T`) while enforcing hard no-penetration constraints, finding a deep, collision-free seated target configuration (`q_⋆`).
 2. **Seed Generation:** If a reference path is available, a collision-aware sweep projects it as a homotopy prior. Otherwise, RRT-Connect generates a path from the current configuration (`q_0`) to `q_⋆`. This stage addresses global feasibility.
 3. **Refinement:** The globally routed seed (`Q_0`) is then passed to the trajectory optimizer for local refinement (`Q_⋆`), which smooths the trajectory, enforces dynamic limits, and tracks the reference. This complementary approach leverages global routing for feasibility and local optimization for smoothness, dynamics, and fidelity.

- **Single-Stage Ablation:** For comparative analysis, a variant that omits the global routing stage and runs a single local descent of the trajectory optimizer from the current state is considered.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DemoBridge was conducted in two parts: a controlled synthetic benchmark for the motion planner and real-demonstration tasks for the full pipeline.

#### 4.1. Synthetic Planner Benchmark

The synthetic benchmark involved procedurally generated cluttered tabletop scenes for a Franka arm, with two versions: "base" (reaching a target grasp) and "attach" (carrying and placing a grasped object along a round-trip path). Scenes were designed to be feasible by construction but challenging, containing obstacles outside the robot's collision-free witness path.

**Metrics:**
- **Endpoint error:** Euclidean distance between final gripper pose and target grasp pose (success if < 5mm).
- **Collision:** Number of scenes (out of 50) with any object penetration > 2mm, audited with 32 interpolation substeps.
- **Normalized Dynamic Time Warping (nDTW) similarity:** Between executed and reference end-effector paths (0-1], 1 indicating exact match).

**Evaluation Settings and Baselines:**
- **Goal-only:** Planner given only the terminal pose (tests goal-reaching). Compared against PyRoKi trajectory optimization (trajopt) and DemoBridge's single-stage and multi-stage planners.
- **With-reference:** Planner given the full demonstrated end-effector path (tests path following). Compared against trajopt, segmented trajopt (chaining coarse waypoints), and DemoBridge's planners.

**Results (Table I Summary):**

1. **Collision Avoidance:** The multi-stage planner demonstrated strong collision avoidance, colliding in at most one out of 50 scenes across all conditions (base/attach, goal-only/with-reference). In contrast, the single-stage ablation (without global planning) collided in 29-30 out of 50 base scenes, and trajopt collided in up to 7 base scenes. This indicates the multi-stage planner's effectiveness in maintaining collision-free motion, even with carried objects.

2. **Goal-Only Performance:** In the goal-only setting, the multi-stage planner achieved superior endpoint accuracy (average 3.2-3.3mm error) compared to trajopt (9.1-10.1mm) and the single-stage planner (6.9-16.8mm), while incurring no collisions. This suggests that the additional mechanisms for retargeting do not compromise standard goal-reaching capabilities.

3. **With-Reference Path Following:** When a reference path was provided, only the multi-stage planner combined high path fidelity, accurate reach, and collision-freedom.
 - Trajopt largely ignored the reference (nDTW 0.08-0.11), prioritizing the goal.
 - Segmented trajopt improved path fidelity (nDTW 0.56-0.70) but still showed higher endpoint errors and collisions on the base scene.
 - The single-stage planner tracked the path more closely (nDTW 0.75-0.93) but suffered significantly higher endpoint errors (18.9mm on base) and collision rates (29/50 on base) due to clutter.
 - The multi-stage planner maintained high path fidelity (nDTW 0.78-0.86), low endpoint error (3.0-4.6mm), and minimal collisions (0-1/50). This shows it is the only method that balances all three criteria.

4. **Importance of Global Planning:** The comparison between the multi-stage planner and its single-stage ablation highlights that the global planning stage is critical for robustness in cluttered environments. It effectively seeds the local optimizer in a collision-free homotopy class, enabling it to navigate clutter without sacrificing accuracy or path fidelity.

#### 4.2. Real Demonstrations

The full DemoBridge pipeline was evaluated on three tabletop tasks with varying precision requirements, each with ten human demonstrations: "Mug-on-plate" (forgiving placement), "Cup-stack" (precision-critical seating), and "Pour-milk" (trajectory-following for object orientation).

**Metrics:** Success rates were measured cumulatively through four stages:
- **Reach:** Gripper approaches object region.
- **Grasp:** Gripper successfully picks up the object.
- **Transport:** Carried object follows demonstrated motion without environmental collision.
- **Place:** Task completion (object placed).

**Baselines:** Re-implementations of YOTO† and RoboWheel† were used, with manually annotated grasp/release frames as they lack reliable timing extraction. DemoBridge operates annotation-free, inferring timing from hand-object relations.

**Results (Table II Summary):**

1. **Baseline Limitations:** Both YOTO† and RoboWheel† showed limited success beyond the "Reach" stage. They rarely achieved successful grasping and failed to complete "Transport" or "Place" for most demonstrations.
 - **Grasp Timing vs. Reference Accuracy:** Even with provided grasp timing, baselines struggled. The authors attribute this to the inaccuracies of single-view references (a few millimeters error), which are sufficient to miss grasps when the object is close to the gripper's opening width. Baselines directly track this imprecise reference, lacking the ability to re-seat the grasp.
 - **Collision in Pose-by-Pose Retargeting:** YOTO†, interpolating between keyframes without scene collision checks, often resulted in collisions during transport. RoboWheel†, greedily tracking the dense path, frequently drove the arm into infeasible configurations, leading to IK failures or re-plans before placement.

2. **DemoBridge's Performance:** DemoBridge significantly outperformed the baselines, carrying a majority of demonstrations to completion across all tasks.
 - It achieved high success rates in "Grasp" (8/10 to 9/10), "Transport" (8/10 to 9/10), and "Place" (6/10 to 7/10).
 - This improved performance is attributed to its whole-trajectory optimization coupled with continuous collision awareness, allowing it to adapt to noisy references and environmental constraints.

3. **Failure Analysis:** Failures primarily occurred when the feasible set of robot configurations was narrow, especially when a demonstrated grasp pose pushed the arm near its reach limits or required specific, constrained gripper orientations.
 - "Mug-on-plate" experienced more failures at the "Reach" stage (8/10 success) compared to "Cup-stack" (9/10 success), indicating that some demonstrated mug orientations led to harder-to-reach configurations.
 - "Cup-stack," despite its precision-critical "Place" stage, maintained high grasp and transport success, suggesting its grasp configurations were generally well-conditioned.
 - Once a grasp was successfully achieved, the "Transport" stage rarely failed.

4. **Perception Influence:** While the success rates primarily reflect retargeting quality, the authors acknowledge limitations in object-pose tracking (e.g., flipped object orientation in "Pour-milk" shown in Fig. 3C). However, for the evaluated demonstrations, tracking accuracy generally remained within task tolerance, indicating that tracking errors were not the primary cause of the observed retargeting failures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DemoBridge represents a step towards addressing a core challenge in robot learning: the reliable transcription of human manipulation demonstrations from ordinary single-view video into robot-executable actions. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

1. **Democratization of Data Collection:** By removing the need for specialized multi-camera rigs, instrumented gloves, or teleoperation, DemoBridge enables passive and in-place collection of robot manipulation data. This could significantly lower the cost and barrier to entry for acquiring large-scale datasets, fostering broader research and application of demonstration-based learning.

2. **Advancement in Retargeting Robustness:** The toolkit directly confronts the dual challenges of the embodiment gap and noisy single-view perception. Its unified, collision-aware trajectory planner, coupled with simulation-in-the-loop validation, enhances the robustness of retargeting. This allows the system to generate dynamically stable and physically executable robot trajectories that are faithful to the demonstrated manipulation, even when the initial human reference is imprecise or leads to kinematically constrained robot poses.

3. **Support for Learning from Human Video:** DemoBridge's capability to convert human video into robot-executable trajectories directly benefits methodologies like "real-to-sim-to-real" and imitation learning that aim to leverage human demonstrations. The output trajectories double as ready-to-use simulation rollouts, which can serve as ground truth or initial seeds for policy learning within simulation environments.

4. **Modularity and Extensibility:** The modular, configuration-driven architecture allows researchers and practitioners to easily swap out perception backends, integrate different robot models, or introduce new pipeline stages without extensive code modifications. This flexibility ensures that DemoBridge can readily adopt future advancements in perception models (e.g., improved hand or object tracking) and adapt to new robot embodiments, contributing to its longevity and utility within the research community.

5. **Handling of Complex Interactions:** The ability to infer grasp timing automatically and reason jointly over alternative grasp poses, whole-arm, and grasped-object collision, and fidelity to the demonstrated path, positions DemoBridge as a tool for more complex manipulation tasks. Its re-planning mechanism on simulation failure prevents discarding valuable human demonstrations, promoting higher success rates.

Despite its current limitations, such as challenges with object tracking during occlusion and the lack of bimanual support, DemoBridge lays a foundation for more robust and accessible robot learning from human demonstrations. Its open-source availability further contributes to research by providing a validated toolkit for direct experimentation and further development.
