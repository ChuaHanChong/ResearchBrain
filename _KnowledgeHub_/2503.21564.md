# Cooking Task Planning using LLM and Verified by Graph Network

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Osaka developed a framework that combines a multimodal Large Language Model (LLM) with a Functional Object-Oriented Network (FOON) to generate executable cooking task plans for robots from online videos. This system enhances plan reliability by using FOON for logical validation and iterative re-planning, achieving full task-sequence graphs for 4 out of 5 recipes and successfully inferring omitted actions for real-robot execution.

## Problem

- Traditional robot task planning methods (e.g., PDDL, FOON) lack scalability and flexibility for diverse, unstandardized human demonstrations in videos.
- Large Language Models (LLMs) can interpret video content but are prone to 'hallucination,' generating factually incorrect or logically inconsistent plans for complex, long-horizon tasks.
- Online cooking videos are highly unstandardized, often containing incomplete information or varying scene states, making it difficult to generate complete and executable robot plans.

## Method

- A hybrid framework integrates a multimodal LLM with a ==Functional Object-Oriented Network (FOON)== to interpret cooking videos and generate task plans.
- The LLM infers target object states and action sequences from video key frames and subtitles, employing Chain of Thought and few-shot prompting.
- FOON serves as a logical validator, checking action feasibility against predefined rules and the robot's environment state, providing structured feedback for iterative LLM re-planning until a consistent and executable task graph is formed.

## Results

- The LLM successfully filtered unnecessary video segments with a **94%** success rate and produced valid JSON outputs for graph conversion with **98%** success.
- The proposed LLM + FOON method generated full task-sequence graphs for 4 out of 5 recipes, significantly outperforming an LLM-only baseline (1 out of 5 recipes).
- A dual-arm robot successfully executed a gyudon recipe, with the system correctly inferring an omitted 'Cut' action and generating a complete, executable plan through three rounds of re-planning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Iterative logical validation and re-planning using FOON effectively mitigates the hallucination problem in LLM-generated task plans, significantly enhancing plan reliability.
> - The use of 'environment object nodes' and 'target object nodes' within FOON allows the system to bridge the gap between observed video states and the robot's specific workspace configuration.
> - This approach enables robots to autonomously interpret unstandardized human cooking demonstrations, inferring complex object state changes and even omitted actions for comprehensive task planning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the challenging problem of enabling robots to perform complex manipulation tasks, specifically cooking, by leveraging the vast amount of unstructured information available in online cooking videos. The broader research landscape for robot task planning from diverse data sources can be categorized into several approaches:

Traditionally, robot task planning has relied on formal methods such as Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) or task graphs like Functional Object-Oriented Networks (FOON). These methods are capable of ensuring logical consistency in generated plans. However, they typically suffer from limitations in scalability and flexibility, particularly when dealing with the high variability and ambiguity inherent in human-generated video demonstrations. Creating detailed PDDL domains or comprehensive task graphs manually for every new task is resource-intensive and not conducive to learning from diverse, unstandardized data.

More recently, advancements in large language models (LLMs) and multimodal language models (e.g., Vision-Language Models, VLMs) have led to approaches that directly generate task instructions from various inputs, including text, images, and videos. These LLM-based methods offer significant flexibility and can interpret general knowledge from vast datasets. However, a notable drawback of LLMs is their susceptibility to "hallucination," where they produce factually incorrect or logically inconsistent outputs, especially in complex, long-horizon tasks. While some studies have attempted to mitigate this with prompt-based feedback mechanisms, these often do not include rigorous logical verification of the generated plan's executability.

To bridge the gap between the flexibility of LLMs and the logical correctness of traditional methods, hybrid approaches have emerged. These methods combine LLMs with classical planning techniques. While promising, existing hybrid solutions often struggle when LLMs fail to accurately interpret domain specific constraints or generate appropriate planning structures (e.g., PDDL domains), leading to errors that propagate through the planning process.

This work contributes to the hybrid approach by proposing a novel framework that integrates an LLM with FOON, specifically to address the limitations of both LLM-only and existing hybrid methods. It leverages the LLM's capability to interpret complex visual and linguistic information from cooking videos and infer high-level task plans. Crucially, it introduces FOON as a robust mechanism for validating the logical consistency of LLM-generated plans and providing structured feedback for iterative re-planning. This combination aims to create task sequences with manipulation motions that are not only interpretable from unstandardized videos but also logically correct and executable by a robot, thereby improving reliability over prior LLM-based methods that lack explicit logical verification mechanisms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that can autonomously generate cooking task plans for robots directly from online cooking videos, which include subtitles. This aims to leverage the extensive and globally accessible repository of cooking knowledge available on video streaming platforms.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges inherent in translating human cooking demonstrations into robot-executable plans:

1. **Complexity of Cooking Tasks:** Cooking involves a sequence of intricate manipulation actions, often requiring reasoning about object states, tool usage, and temporal dependencies. Robots performing these tasks need detailed, logically consistent plans.
2. **Variability in Video Data:** Online cooking videos are highly unstandardized. They exhibit significant variations in camera angles, specific tools and utensils used, and the spatial arrangement of the kitchen environment. This variability makes it difficult for a robot system to accurately recognize scene states and generalize actions.
3. **Incomplete Information:** Many cooking videos are edited for brevity, often omitting preparatory steps, movements of kitchen utensils, or intermediate ingredient states. A robot system must be able to infer and compensate for these missing actions and state changes to generate a complete and executable plan.
4. **Limitations of Existing Planning Methods:**
 - **Traditional Methods (e.g., PDDL, FOON):** While capable of ensuring logical consistency, these methods require extensive manual domain engineering or task graph construction, which is not scalable for diverse video inputs.
 - **LLM-based Methods:** Large Language Models possess general knowledge and reasoning abilities crucial for interpreting video content and generating robot actions. However, LLMs are prone to "hallucination," producing factually incorrect or logically unsound outputs, especially in complex, long-horizon planning scenarios. Relying solely on LLMs can lead to plans that are not executable or safe for a robot.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives are:
- To enable an LLM to interpret cooking video data (images and subtitles) to infer preparation states of food and generate initial sequences of cooking tasks.
- To integrate a graph-based representation, specifically Functional Object-Oriented Networks (FOON), with the LLM. This integration is intended to validate the logical correctness of the LLM-generated plans and provide structured feedback to correct any inconsistencies.
- To modify FOON's object nodes (into "environment object nodes" and "target object nodes") to account for differences between the observed environment in the video and the robot's local execution environment.
- To develop an iterative error correction and re-planning mechanism, where FOON identifies logical flaws in LLM-generated actions and prompts the LLM for revisions until a fully feasible and logically sound task plan is produced.
- To evaluate the effectiveness of this combined approach in generating executable cooking task plans for a dual-arm robot system, including the ability to infer omitted actions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method integrates a multimodal Large Language Model (LLM) with a Functional Object-Oriented Network (FOON) to generate and validate cooking task plans from subtitled online videos for robotic execution. The framework comprises several key stages:

#### 3.1. Overview of the Proposed Method

The process begins with pre-processing a YouTube cooking video with subtitles. The extracted visual (images) and textual (subtitles) information, along with predefined robot actions and the robot’s workspace configuration, are fed into an LLM. The LLM generates a sequence of cooking task plans. Each plan is then converted into a FOON functional unit and added to a task graph. If the conversion or validation fails, FOON identifies the error and its cause, generating a feedback prompt for the LLM to revise the plan. This iterative loop continues until a complete and logically consistent FOON graph is successfully generated, which then informs the robot's motion planning.

#### 3.2. Functional Object-Oriented Network (FOON)

FOON serves as the core validation mechanism. Its basic unit is a "functional unit," representing a single action. Each functional unit consists of:
- **Object nodes:** Representing ingredients, cooking utensils, or containers.
- **Hand nodes:** Describing the state of the robot's hand (e.g., empty, holding an object).
- **Motion nodes:** Detailing the action performed (e.g., "Pick," "Place," "Cut").
A functional unit specifies the input states of objects and hands *before* an action and their output states *after* the action. By connecting multiple functional units, a task graph can be constructed. The step-by-step verification of object states ensures logical soundness.

**Extensions to FOON:**
- **Environment Object Nodes:** These represent the real-time state of objects within the robot's local execution environment. They include attributes like object placement (e.g., "Cutting board" in "Right storage") and cooking status of ingredients. These nodes are dynamically updated.
- **Target Object Nodes:** These represent the *desired* estimated state of objects as observed in the reference video frames. They also include cooking status attributes and define the target conditions that the robot must achieve at the output of a functional unit.
- **Functional Unit with Variables:** To enhance reusability, actions are represented as "functional units with variables." These are templates where object-dependent elements (e.g., which hand is used, specific object location) are treated as variables, allowing the same action unit to be instantiated for different objects or contexts.

#### 3.3. Video Pre-processing

YouTube videos with subtitles are pre-processed to extract relevant information:
1. **Frame Extraction:** Series of images are extracted from the video.
2. **Subtitle Extraction & Segmentation:** Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is used to extract subtitles. Video scenes are segmented based on these subtitles.
3. **Key Frame Arrangement:** For each segmented scene, representative key frames are arranged into a 3x3 grid, which serves as the visual input for the LLM.

#### 3.4. Graph Generation

The task graph is generated through an iterative process involving LLM inference and FOON validation:
1. **Estimation of Target Object Node:** The LLM is prompted with the arranged key frames, subtitles, robot background information (e.g., possible actions, object node attributes), and specific instructions. It employs a Chain of Thought (CoT) approach and few-shot prompting to infer the desired state of target objects (e.g., "chopped onion") at the end of each cooking scene. The LLM outputs these states in a structured JSON format, which are then converted into target object nodes.
2. **Action Estimation:** A separate LLM agent compares the current state of the "environment object node" with the "target object node" derived from the video. It then infers the sequence of allowed robot actions required to transition from the current environment state to the desired target state. The LLM output includes the action name and corresponding object-dependent variables (e.g., object to pick, hand to use).
3. **Task Validation and Graph Generation:** Before an LLM-predicted motion node is added to the task graph, its feasibility is validated:
 - The LLM's predicted action and its variables (e.g., "Pick | Knife | Right hand | Right storage") are used to instantiate a functional unit from a predefined Motion Library.
 - The input requirements of this instantiated functional unit (e.g., "Right hand must be empty") are compared against the current state of the environment object node.
 - If all input conditions match the environment state, the action is deemed feasible, and the environment object node is updated according to the functional unit's output. The action is then added to the task graph.
 - If there is a mismatch, the task is considered infeasible, triggering the re-planning phase.
4. **Re-planning:** In case of validation failure, the specific cause (e.g., "Right hand is not empty") is identified by comparing mismatched attributes. This error information is then provided back to the LLM as feedback in a corrective prompt. The LLM revises its action plan, and the validation process is repeated iteratively until a feasible plan for all tasks is generated.

#### 3.5. Motion Planning

This work primarily focuses on task plan generation. For motion planning, the robot's reachable workspace is discretized. Initial object placement coordinates and poses are pre-determined to ensure all necessary tasks can be executed without collision or reachability issues. For each validated task, feasible motions (e.g., grasping poses, trajectories) are generated using custom functions that employ techniques such as RRT-Connect and linear trajectory planning, based on the object coordinates and grasping poses.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed method through experiments focusing on graph generation accuracy and real-world robot execution.

#### 4.1. Experiment on Graph Generation

Ten cooking videos with subtitles from YouTube Shorts (each under one minute, featuring actions like cutting, mixing, heating, pouring) were used for evaluation. The success rate was measured across three key steps: video pre-processing, target object node estimation, and task-sequence graph generation.

1. **Video Pre-processing:** OCR was used to extract subtitles. The extracted amount of subtitles exceeded the ground truth by 17% (270 words extracted vs. 230 groundtruth), primarily due to OCR misrecognition leading to redundant instructions. However, these redundancies were found to be filterable by the LLM and did not impede the task planning process.

2. **Target Object Node Estimation:** This step was evaluated on 276 images from the 10 videos, where 134 images depicted scenes unrelated to cooking. ChatGPT-4o was used as the LLM.
 - **Removal of unnecessary segments:** The LLM successfully identified and removed unnecessary scenes from the input videos with a 94% success rate, indicating its ability to filter non-cooking related content and reduce planning complexity.
 - **Valid graphs from LLM output:** The LLM achieved a 98% success rate in producing JSON-formatted outputs that could be successfully converted into a graph representation. A noted failure case involved the LLM creating an object node of type "Dish," which was not among the predefined object types (ingredient, container, tool, machine), suggesting a need for more diverse few-shot prompting examples.
 - **Target object node state estimation:** The accuracy for correctly estimating the target object node states from images was 86%. Failures were attributed to two main factors: (1) cooking actions present in videos that fell outside the predefined set of allowed robot actions, and (2) variations in object naming due to paraphrasing by the LLM.

3. **Generation of Task-sequence Graph:** Five selected cooking recipes (using slightly corrected target object nodes for feasibility) were used to evaluate the complete task planning process for a robot. The proposed method was compared against a baseline approach that used only a few-shot LLM without FOON-based validation.
 - **Baseline (LLM-only):** This approach successfully generated a full task-sequence graph for only 1 out of 5 recipes.
 - **Proposed Method (LLM + FOON validation):** The proposed method successfully generated full task-sequence graphs for 4 out of 5 recipes. This result indicates that the iterative validation and re-planning using FOON significantly enhances the ability to create logically consistent plans for complex, long-horizon tasks.
 - **Failure case for proposed method:** The single failure occurred when the LLM, without explicit instruction, decided to temporarily move chopped ingredients from the cutting board to a bowl. This bowl was later required for seasoning preparation, leading to a conflict that the sequential, per-action validation could not retroactively correct. This highlights a limitation where validation for future tasks cannot influence already committed past actions in the current framework.

#### 4.2. Experiment Using a Real Robot

To demonstrate the method's application in a real-world scenario, the recipe for preparing gyudon (beef bowl) was used, involving "Pick," "Place," "Pour," "Cut," "Mix," and "Cook" motions. The input video omitted the "Cut" action. A dual-arm UR3e robot setup was used with predefined object placements to ensure reachability.
- **Video Processing and Target Node Estimation:** The input video was pre-processed into 13 scenes, from which the LLM correctly identified 8 necessary scenes for the cooking task, effectively filtering out unnecessary introduction or title segments.
- **Task Planning and Execution:** The system successfully generated a full task graph after three rounds of re-planning, iteratively correcting errors. The robot then successfully executed the gyudon recipe. Notably, since chopped onions were not initially present in the environment, the system correctly inferred and generated a "Cut" action (using a knife and cutting board) before proceeding to place the onions into the frying pan, demonstrating its capability to supplement missing actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that significantly advances the capability of robotic systems to learn and execute complex, long-horizon tasks from diverse, unstructured human demonstrations.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Mitigation of LLM Hallucination:** The integration of Functional Object-Oriented Networks (FOON) for logical validation and iterative re-planning effectively addresses a primary challenge of LLM-based planning—the risk of generating factually incorrect or inconsistent plans. This enhances the reliability and trustworthiness of LLM-generated robot actions.
- **Robustness to Video Variability:** The system demonstrates an ability to interpret unstandardized cooking videos, segment relevant scenes, and infer necessary object state changes and even omitted actions. This is crucial for leveraging the vast amount of human demonstration data available online.
- **Logical Consistency and Executability:** By comparing LLM-generated plans against FOON's logical constraints and the robot's environment state, the method ensures that the resulting task plans are not only conceptually sound but also physically executable by a robot. The experimental results, particularly the higher success rate compared to an LLM-only baseline, affirm this improved reliability.
- **Environment-Independent Planning with Feedback:** The use of "environment object nodes" and "target object nodes" allows the system to bridge the gap between observations in a video and the specific configuration of a robot's workspace, generating relevant feedback for re-planning when discrepancies occur.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy:** This framework can enable robots to more autonomously acquire new skills from human demonstrations, reducing the need for extensive manual programming or expert intervention for each new task. This could lead to more versatile and adaptable robotic assistants in various domains.
- **Broader Application Beyond Cooking:** While demonstrated for cooking, the hybrid LLM-FOON approach is generalizable to other complex manipulation tasks in diverse environments (e.g., assembly, laboratory automation, household chores) where logical consistency and robust error handling are critical.
- **Foundation for Future AI-Robotics Integration:** The iterative feedback loop between a powerful generative AI (LLM) and a symbolic knowledge representation (FOON) provides a valuable paradigm for combining the strengths of different AI methodologies. This could serve as a model for developing more intelligent and resilient robotic systems.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Work

The research also identifies key limitations and areas for future development:
- **Sequential Validation Constraint:** The current per-action validation prevents retroactive modification of previous actions in the task graph. This can lead to issues if an action, valid in isolation, creates an unforeseen conflict for a subsequent task. Future work aims to incorporate a more holistic or look-ahead validation mechanism that allows for adjustments to earlier parts of the plan.
- **Real-World Error Recovery:** The current system assumes successful execution of an action if its pre-conditions are met. It does not account for real-world uncertainties such as inaccuracies in object position estimation or grasp failures. Future improvements will focus on developing an online system capable of real-time object information updates and dynamic re-planning to recover from execution failures.
