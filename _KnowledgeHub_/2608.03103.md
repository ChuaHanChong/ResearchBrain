# A Hierarchical Approach to Imitation Learning for Manipulation Tasks Requiring Time Varying Forces

> [!summary] Summary
> DPA-FTG is a hierarchical imitation-learning framework for contact-rich manipulation tasks requiring time-varying force regulation, such as chiseling or scraping. A slow-frequency Task Selection Policy (5 Hz), implemented as a conditional diffusion model, selects among a VQ-VAE-learned vocabulary of latent task primitives, which a fast-frequency GRU-based Motion Generation Policy (60 Hz) unzips into force-modulated motor commands, achieving high task success and lower peak contact forces than a diffusion-policy baseline.

## Problem

- Contact-rich manipulation tasks, like chiseling or scraping, require precise, time-varying force regulation and high-frequency control (e.g., 60 Hz) due to dynamic interactions and deformable materials.
- State-of-the-art Imitation Learning methods, such as Diffusion Policies, have inherent inference latency (5-10 Hz), making them unsuitable for real-time, high-frequency control in dynamic environments.
- Existing action-chunking techniques in DPs and reactive policies often lead to open-loop execution within chunks or focus on positional corrections, lacking explicit dynamic force regulation for tasks needing sustained oscillatory motions.

## Method

- The **DPA-FTG** architecture introduces a hierarchical ==slow-fast== approach, decoupling a high-level Task Selection Policy (5 Hz) from a low-level Motion Generation Policy (60 Hz).
- A ==Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE)== learns a discrete vocabulary of latent "task primitives" by compressing expert trajectories, including joint velocities and force profiles, into a compact codebook.
- The high-level policy, implemented as a ==conditional Diffusion Model==, selects a latent primitive based on visual and physical context, while the low-level policy, a ==GRU==, unzips this primitive into high-frequency motor commands, modulated by real-time force feedback.

## Results

- DPA-FTG achieved **100%** Episode Completion and **100%** Task Success on the compliant sheet separation task in-distribution, demonstrating significantly lower mean peak chisel forces (**27.7 N**) compared to the RDP baseline (**44.7 N**).
- Ablation studies confirmed the crucial contributions of task selection, fast-loop force feedback, VQ quantization, and decoder weight transfer to the overall system's performance, safety, and data efficiency.
- The system demonstrated zero-shot generalization to novel geometries, achieving **80%** Task Success on hexagonal shapes and **60%** on triangular shapes, though limitations were observed at acute corners due to latent primitive mismatch or compounding drift.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling low-frequency strategic intent from high-frequency reactive execution effectively bridges the fundamental frequency mismatch between diffusion policies and contact-rich manipulation requirements.
> - Learning discrete latent primitives using VQ-VAE prevents mode-averaging of distinct expert strategies, ensuring the low-level controller receives valid and interpretable skill tokens.
> - Initializing the low-level GRU motion policy from the VQ-VAE decoder and fine-tuning it as a Denoising Autoencoder for Dynamics, with zero-initialized sensor inputs, enables real-time force-conditioned motion generation with implicit admittance-like behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses challenges in robotic manipulation, specifically focusing on contact-rich material separation and surface preparation tasks in industrial automation. These tasks, such as chiseling, scraping, and prying, require precise, often oscillatory, force modulation and bimanual coordination. They are currently performed manually due to their complexity, which involves deformable materials, unknown surface characteristics, unpredictable bond strengths, and high-frequency dynamic interactions (exceeding 10 Hz, requiring control loops of at least 60 Hz).

Traditional automation techniques, typically relying on rigid position control, are often insufficient for these multi-modal scenarios where optimal strategies involve selecting from various task primitives based on local conditions. Explicit analytical modeling for such tasks is challenging due to unknown friction coefficients, variable adhesive properties, and material deformation. This has led to a shift towards data-driven methods, particularly Imitation Learning (IL), to acquire skills directly from expert demonstrations.

Diffusion Policies (DPs) represent a state-of-the-art approach in IL, capable of capturing the multi-modal distributions of expert behaviors. However, DPs are inherently limited by their iterative denoising process, which introduces inference latencies (typically 5-10 Hz). This speed constraint makes them unsuitable for tasks demanding high-frequency control (e.g., 60 Hz) and real-time reactivity to sudden force transients, such as those caused by instantaneous fracture events. Existing action-chunking techniques in DPs mitigate latency by predicting sequences of actions, but their open-loop execution within a chunk renders the system unresponsive to rapid environmental changes.

Prior work has attempted to bridge this gap. Algorithmic acceleration methods for DPs (e.g., DDIMs, Consistency Models, RTI-DP, Genetic Denoising) aim to speed up the generative core. Architectural approaches (e.g., Latent Weight Diffusion, RA-DP) restructure the control loop. Hierarchical control paradigms, such as Hierarchical Diffusion Policy (HDP) and Hierarchical Robot Transform (HiRT), factorize manipulation into high-level planning and low-level execution. Notably, the Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP) introduced a "slow-fast" architecture using a slow latent diffusion policy and a fast asymmetric tokenizer for closed-loop tactile feedback. However, RDP primarily focuses on positional correction based on tactile deformation, rather than explicitly modeling dynamic force regulation and generating high-frequency oscillatory motions.

This work positions itself within the domain of hierarchical control for imitation learning, specifically building upon the concept of slow-fast architectures. It aims to overcome the identified limitations of existing diffusion policies by decoupling low-frequency planning from high-frequency force regulation, thereby enabling the generation of dynamic, force-conditioned motions at control frequencies suitable for contact-rich interaction. Unlike RDP, which corrects a latent trajectory, DPA-FTG's fast policy *generates* the high-frequency motion parameterized by the slow policy. This distinction targets tasks requiring time-varying forces and sustained oscillatory behaviors, addressing a specific unmet need in the application of diffusion policies to manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robotic system capable of performing complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks that require precise, time-varying force regulation, while leveraging the multi-modal learning capabilities of Diffusion Policies. This is driven by several key motivations and aims to address specific limitations of current state-of-the-art methods:

1. **Bridging the Frequency Mismatch:** The core motivation is to resolve the fundamental conflict between the low inference speed of Diffusion Policies (typically ~5 Hz) and the high control frequency (e.g., 60 Hz) required for stable and safe execution in dynamic contact-rich environments. Tasks like chiseling involve oscillatory motions exceeding 10 Hz and necessitate millisecond-level reaction to force transients to prevent damage or failure.

2. **Overcoming Open-Loop Execution Limitations:** Standard Diffusion Policies often rely on "action chunking," predicting a sequence of actions that are then executed open-loop. This renders the robot unresponsive to sudden, unpredictable events like bond fractures or unexpected stiff contacts within the execution window, leading to unstable force growth and potential damage. The objective is to introduce closed-loop control within this window.

3. **Enabling Dynamic Force Regulation:** Current reactive policies, such as Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP), primarily focus on positional correction. The motivation here is to enable explicit *dynamic force regulation* for tasks where applying specific force profiles (e.g., oscillatory chiseling) to detach components is crucial, rather than merely reaching a Cartesian pose.

4. **Learning Multi-Modal Strategic Behaviors:** Many material separation tasks involve ambiguous states where multiple valid strategies (e.g., varying tool attack angles, switching between pushing and prying) are possible. Diffusion Policies are adept at capturing these multi-modal expert behaviors, and the objective is to retain this capability while integrating high-frequency reactivity.

5. **Achieving Asymmetric Bimanual Coordination:** For tasks like compliant sheet separation, coordination between two arms (e.g., one for chiseling, one for peeling/stabilizing) is essential. The system needs to learn and execute these coordinated behaviors effectively.

6. **Ensuring Safety and Robustness:** The research is motivated by the need for robotic systems to operate safely in environments with unpredictable interactions. This includes maintaining contact stability, preventing tool slip or workpiece damage, and recovering from minor failures without human intervention.

7. **Generalization to Novel Conditions:** The objective is for the learned policy to generalize to variations in object pose, orientation, and previously unseen geometries, expanding its applicability beyond explicitly trained scenarios.

To achieve these objectives, the paper proposes a hierarchical decomposition: decoupling low-frequency "task selection" (strategic intent) from high-frequency "trajectory generation" (reactive execution). This aims to allow the system to select complex contact strategies over a longer horizon while continuously modulating its motion based on real-time force feedback.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Diffusion Policy Augmented by Fast Trajectory Generation (DPA-FTG) approach is designed as a hierarchical "slow-fast" architecture to address the frequency mismatch between high-level planning and low-level control in contact-rich manipulation. It comprises two main components: a high-level Task Selection Policy and a low-level Motion Generation Policy, connected via a learned latent primitive space.

#### 3.1. Overview of Approach

DPA-FTG shifts the concept of "chunking" from the action space to a latent task space. A high-level policy predicts a **Latent Primitive** (z_k), which encodes semantic intent (e.g., "oscillate forward") over a longer horizon. This latent primitive is then passed to a high-frequency **Force-Reactive Motion Decoder** that unzips the intent into motor commands, allowing real-time modulation based on force feedback.

The system's control law is defined as: a_t = π_motion(z_k, o_fast_t), where z_k is generated by π_task(·|o_slow_k). Here, k represents coarse planning steps (approx. 5 Hz), and t represents fine control steps (60 Hz).

#### 3.2. Learning the Latent Primitive Space

To create a compact, discrete vocabulary of skills (task primitives), DPA-FTG utilizes a **Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE)**. This approach compresses high-dimensional expert trajectories (joint velocity commands `a_i` and contact forces `f_i`) into a discrete codebook `C = {e_k}`.

- **Motivation for VQ-VAE:** Unlike continuous VAEs that might average distinct strategies, the discrete bottleneck of VQ-VAE forces the model to commit to specific, distinct task primitives, preventing mode averaging that could lead to physically invalid behaviors.
- **Trajectory Representation:** Trajectories are represented as windows of actions and force profiles (`τ_t = {(a_i, f_i)}`). Including force profiles is critical, as it ensures the learned primitives capture dynamic context, differentiating between kinematically similar motions that have different contact dynamics.
- **Architecture and Quantization:** The VQ-VAE consists of an encoder `E_ϕ`, a discrete codebook `C`, and a decoder `D_ψ`. The encoder maps an input trajectory `τ_t` to a continuous latent vector `z_e`, which is then "snapped" to the nearest vector `e_k` in the codebook to produce `z_q`.
- **Training Objective:** The VQ-VAE is trained with a multi-objective loss: action reconstruction loss, an auxiliary force reconstruction loss (to ensure `z_q` encodes dynamic context), and losses for updating the codebook and committing the encoder output to the codebook manifold.

#### 3.3. High-Level Task Selection Policy

The `π_task` policy selects the appropriate latent primitive `z_k` based on visual and physical state, operating at a lower frequency (5 Hz). It is implemented as a **conditional Diffusion Model (DDPM)**.

- **Generative Nature:** Unlike a classification network, a diffusion model is generative, allowing it to represent the full distribution of valid strategies, which is important for ambiguous states and preventing mode collapse.
- **Training Supervision:** The VQ-VAE codebook vector `e_n_k` corresponding to the expert's demonstration at step `k` is used as the ground-truth target. The diffusion model denoises noisy versions of `e_n_k` back towards `e_n_k`, conditioned on the slow context.
- **Inference Process:** Starting from Gaussian noise, the model iteratively denoises the latent vector over `K` steps to produce a continuous embedding `˜z_k`. This `˜z_k` is then quantized by finding its nearest neighbor in the VQ-VAE codebook `C` to obtain `z_k`. This final quantization ensures that the low-level controller always receives a valid, recognizable skill token.
- **Observation Input (`o_slow_k`):** Consists of RGB images from global and wrist-mounted cameras (processed by a frozen ResNet-18), and a history of force data (`F_t-T_h:t`) processed by a 1D-CNN encoder. These features are concatenated with the robot's current joint configuration and used to condition the U-Net denoising backbone via Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM).

#### 3.4. Low-Level Motion Generation Policy

The `π_motion` policy generates high-frequency (60 Hz) trajectories, executing the semantic skill `z_k` while modulating motion based on real-time contact dynamics. It is implemented as a **Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)**.

- **Policy Initialization and Transfer Learning:** To leverage the pre-trained kinematic knowledge, `π_motion` weights are directly initialized from the VQ-VAE Decoder. The Action Head is retained, while the Force Reconstruction Head is discarded.
- **Input Injection with Zero-Initialization:** The GRU's input layer is expanded to accept instantaneous force `F_t` and proprioception (`q_t, ˙q_t`). Crucially, the weights connecting these new sensor inputs are initialized to zero. This ensures that at the start of fine-tuning, the policy's behavior matches the pre-trained open-loop decoder, with feedback gradually incorporated during training.
- **Closed-Loop Control Law:** The GRU updates its hidden state `h_t` based on the latent `z_k`, instantaneous fast observations `o_fast_t = {F_t, q_t, ˙q_t}`, and previous hidden state `h_t-1`. An MLP then maps `h_t` to the action `a_t`. The recurrent state maintains the phase of periodic motions.
- **Implicit Admittance via Denoising:** To prevent the recurrent network from collapsing into an open-loop driver, `π_motion` is fine-tuned as a Denoising Autoencoder for Dynamics. Gaussian noise and small perturbations are injected into the fast inputs during training while supervising clean expert actions. This learning objective fosters an implicit admittance-like behavior, where the policy adjusts its velocity based on force deviations to maintain contact stability.

#### 3.5. Asynchronous Inference and Deployment

DPA-FTG employs an **asynchronous execution architecture** with two parallel threads to bridge the planning (5 Hz) and control (60 Hz) timescales:

- **Planner Thread (5 Hz):** Runs the iterative denoising process for `π_task` (approx. 200 ms inference time). Once a new target latent `z_k` is computed, it updates a thread-safe shared atomic buffer.
- **Control Thread (60 Hz):** Continuously reads the most recent latent `z_curr` from the buffer and real-time sensor data. It executes a single forward pass of `π_motion` to generate the immediate motor command.

This "Zero-Order Hold" strategy ensures that the fast control loop is never blocked by the diffusion planner's latency, maintaining continuous 60 Hz reactivity by executing the last known valid intent until a new one is available.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of DPA-FTG involved experiments on a bimanual robotic cell performing compliant sheet separation, a task chosen for its requirement of high-frequency, force-controlled motion.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Hardware:** Two KUKA LBR iiwa 7 manipulators. The left arm is equipped with a custom chisel and an ATI Axia M20 Force sensor. The right arm uses a UMI-designed parallel gripper for peeling. A motorized turntable holds the workpiece.
- **Workpiece:** A surrogate, compliant aluminum sheet adhered to a rigid base using a magnetic fixture. This fixture simulates progressive bond fracture with consistent, rapid disengagement and re-engagement of magnets, mimicking the force transients of adhesive bonds.
- **Sensor Suite:** Multi-view visual feedback from two global RealSense D415 cameras, a wrist-mounted RealSense D405 on the gripper arm, and a wrist-mounted RealSense D415 on the chisel arm. The chisel arm's force sensor operates at 1 kHz.
- **Data Collection:** Expert demonstrations (51 total, 17 per training geometry) were captured using a Motion Capture (MoCap) system, allowing human experts to directly manipulate tools. This provided high-fidelity proprioceptive supervision for training. The task involved five phases: Approach, Chiseling (high-frequency oscillation), Gripper Approach, Simultaneous Chiseling and Peeling, and Separation.
- **Generalization Set:** Three training geometries (Octagon, Square, Circle) and two unseen test geometries (Hexagon, Triangle) were used to evaluate zero-shot generalization.

#### 4.2. Evaluation Metrics

- **Episode Completion Rate (%):** Percentage of trials reaching the terminal condition without intervention or safety abort.
- **Peak Chisel Force (∥F∥_max):** Maximum measured force magnitude during chiseling.
- **Execution Quality (0-3):** An ordinal score (3=Excellent, 2=Acceptable, 1=Unsafe, 0=Aborts) based on stable contact, substrate damage, gripper slips, and force spikes.
- **Task Success Rate (%):** Stricter metric, requiring both Episode Completion and Execution Quality ≥ 2.
- **Execution Speed (× human performance):** Ratio of average human completion time to robot completion time.

#### 4.3. Baselines and Ablations

- **DP-Vanilla:** Standard vision-based Diffusion Policy.
- **DP with Force Feedback:** Force-conditioned diffusion policy, but still open-loop during action chunks.
- **Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP):** Slow-fast architecture with a slow latent diffusion policy and a 20 Hz fast reactive policy for tactile feedback correction.
- **Behavioral Cloning (BC):** Non-generative supervised learning baseline at 60 Hz.
- **Ablations:** Variants of DPA-FTG removing Task Selection (fixed skill), Fast-Loop Force feedback, VQ Quantization, and Decoder Weight Transfer.

#### 4.4. Performance Comparison (In-Distribution)

Table 1 compares DPA-FTG against RDP on 15 controlled trials (5 per training geometry).
- **DPA-FTG:** Achieved 100% Episode Completion and 100% Task Success. Mean Execution Quality was 2.73, with a low Mean Peak Chisel Force of 27.7 N. Execution speed was 0.85 × human performance.
- **RDP:** Achieved 100% Episode Completion but only 33.3% Task Success. Mean Execution Quality was 1.33, and Mean Peak Chisel Force was 44.7 N. While RDP had a slightly higher execution speed (0.89 × human), this came at the cost of safety and execution quality, indicating frequent unsafe force spikes.

Table 2 provides an overall summary of in-distribution performance for all methods:
- DPA-FTG showed superior performance across all metrics, with 100% Task Success and the lowest peak forces.
- DP with Force improved Episode Completion over DP-Vanilla but remained limited by open-loop execution, leading to low Task Success (20%) and high peak forces.
- DP-Vanilla performed poorly in this contact-rich setting, with low completion and success rates, and the highest peak forces.
- BC consistently triggered safety stops during preliminary rollouts and was not formally evaluated.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

Table 3 details the ablation results for DPA-FTG on 15 in-distribution trials:
- **`w/o Task Selection` (fixed skill token):** Maintained 100% Episode Completion but significantly reduced Task Success to 53.3% and increased peak forces to 41.2 N. This indicates the importance of context-dependent skill switching.
- **`w/o Fast-Loop Force` (no `F_t` in `π_motion`):** Achieved 100% Episode Completion but lowered Task Success to 73.3% and increased peak forces to 34.5 N. This confirms that high-frequency force feedback is crucial for preventing unstable oscillations.
- **`w/o VQ Quantization` (continuous latent to controller):** Decreased Episode Completion to 93.3% and Task Success to 60%, with higher peak forces (38.7 N). This supports the role of a discrete codebook in avoiding invalid mode-averaging.
- **`w/o Weight Transfer` (train `π_motion` from scratch):** Showed the largest degradation, with 86.7% Episode Completion and 46.7% Task Success, and high peak forces (42.5 N). This demonstrates the benefit of initializing the low-level controller from the VQ-VAE decoder for data efficiency and stability.

#### 4.6. Generalization to Novel Geometries

Table 4 presents zero-shot generalization results on unseen Hexagon and Triangle shapes (5 trials/shape):
- **DPA-FTG:** Generalized to both, but with reduced performance compared to in-distribution. On Hexagon, 100% Episode Completion and 80% Task Success. On Triangle (extrapolative, acute corners), 80% Episode Completion and 60% Task Success. Failures on Triangle included tool slip near acute corners and early aborts.
- **Baselines:** Showed sharper degradation. DP-Vanilla and DP w/ Force performed poorly due to open-loop overshoots at acute corners. RDP maintained higher Episode Completion but low Task Success, often completing unsafely due to force spikes during corner transitions.

#### 4.7. Failure Mode Analysis (Generalization Set)

Out of 10 zero-shot trials, DPA-FTG had 3 failures: 2 unsafe completions and 1 abort. Two distinct failure modes were identified:
- **Latent Mismatch (Unsafe Completions):** Occurred when the high-level planner selected an incorrect skill primitive (e.g., prying instead of cornering) at novel geometric features like sharp corners. This resulted in inappropriate, high-force interactions. Potential causes were attributed to limited demonstration coverage of acute-corner transitions and the geometric sensitivity of the visual encoder.
- **Compounding Drift (Aborts):** Attributed to a loss of tool-edge contact (e.g., lateral slip) causing the system state to move outside the expert demonstration distribution. Lacking an explicit recovery skill, the policy's pose error accumulated, leading to jamming or workspace limit violations and a safety stop.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in applying diffusion policies to robotic manipulation, specifically addressing the challenges of contact-rich tasks requiring dynamic force control. The DPA-FTG architecture offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Real-time Reactive Control with Diffusion Policies:** By proposing a hierarchical "slow-fast" approach, the work successfully integrates the expressive, multi-modal learning capabilities of diffusion policies with the requirement for high-frequency, real-time force regulation. This resolves a fundamental limitation that has hindered the deployment of DPs in dynamic, contact-rich environments.

2. **Enhancing Safety and Robustness in Contact-Rich Tasks:** The explicit decoupling of low-frequency planning from high-frequency force-conditioned execution leads to demonstrably safer operations. The ability of the low-level policy to modulate motion instantaneously based on real-time force feedback significantly reduces peak forces and improves execution quality, which is critical for preventing damage to tools or workpieces in industrial settings.

3. **Expanding the Scope of Robotic Automation:** The methodology is applicable to a range of industrially relevant tasks, such as material separation, surface preparation (e.g., sealant removal, corrosion scraping), and complex disassembly (e.g., EV battery remanufacturing). These tasks are often manual, physically demanding, and hazardous, making robotic automation beneficial for worker safety and efficiency.

4. **Advancing Skill Learning from Demonstration:** The use of a VQ-VAE for learning a discrete vocabulary of latent primitives provides a structured way to encode distinct expert strategies, preventing mode collapse that can occur with continuous latent spaces. This facilitates the learning and selection of complex, context-dependent skills.

5. **Demonstrating Bimanual Coordination for Complex Interaction:** The successful application to a bimanual compliant sheet separation task highlights the potential for this architecture to learn and execute coordinated actions involving asymmetric roles (e.g., chiseling and peeling), which is a key requirement for many advanced manipulation problems.

6. **Addressing Generalization Challenges:** While showing some limitations, the work demonstrates zero-shot generalization to novel geometries, suggesting the potential for deployment in varied and unstructured environments without extensive re-training. Further improvements in this area could significantly broaden practical applicability.

The limitations identified, particularly regarding generalization to acute geometries and the lack of autonomous primitive learning, offer clear directions for future research. Integrating faster sampling techniques (e.g., Consistency Policies) at the high-level could further reduce planning latency, potentially enhancing overall system responsiveness. Overall, this research provides a framework that allows diffusion policies to operate effectively in a domain previously constrained by their computational characteristics, contributing to the development of more capable and robust autonomous manipulation systems.
