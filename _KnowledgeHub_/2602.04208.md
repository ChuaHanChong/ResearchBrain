# SCALE: Self-uncertainty Conditioned Adaptive Looking and Execution for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A method named SCALE enhances the test-time robustness of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by dynamically adjusting visual perception and action decoding based on the model's self-uncertainty. This training-free, single-pass strategy yields consistent performance improvements across diverse robotic manipulation tasks in simulation and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Autoregressive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models struggle with robustness during test-time in diverse, real-world environments.
- Existing Test-Time Scaling (TTS) methods for VLAs demand additional training, often degrade under domain shifts, and require multiple forward passes, hindering real-time robotic control.
- Current TTS approaches primarily refine action decoding, neglecting adaptive visual perception, which is crucial for handling perceptual ambiguity.

## Method

- **SCALE** is proposed as a training-free, single-pass inference strategy for VLA models that jointly modulates visual perception and action decoding.
- It employs a novel self-uncertainty measure, derived from the Kullback-Leibler divergence to high and low uncertainty reference distributions, to quantify both distributional ambiguity and top-1 decisiveness.
- The method adaptively adjusts the vision encoder's attention temperature and action sampling temperature based on this uncertainty, balancing exploration and focused execution.

## Results

- SCALE demonstrated consistent success rate improvements, such as **5.8** percentage points (p.p.) for OpenVLA on LIBERO and **19.5** p.p. in real-world in-distribution tasks, across diverse VLA backbones and robotic manipulation benchmarks.
- The method outperformed greedy decoding, naive fixed-parameter sampling, and training-required Test-Time Scaling methods (e.g., MG-Select by **10.7** p.p. on LIBERO), all while maintaining single-pass inference efficiency.
- Ablation studies showed that both adaptive action decoding and adaptive visual attention contributed positively, with their combination yielding synergistic gains (e.g., **10.6** p.p. on LIBERO-Long for OpenVLA from a baseline of **52.7%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An intrinsic self-uncertainty signal can effectively drive adaptive modulation of both visual perception and action in VLA models, enhancing test-time robustness.
> - Dynamically adjusting visual attention based on uncertainty allows VLA models to adapt how they perceive the environment, particularly under perceptual ambiguity.
> - Jointly adapting both perception and action provides synergistic performance improvements, outperforming individual component contributions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a promising direction for developing general-purpose robotic control systems by mapping multimodal observations and language instructions to robot actions in a closed-loop manner. Autoregressive VLAs, which leverage pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to encode visual observations and sequentially decode action tokens, have emerged as a dominant paradigm. Despite their generalization capabilities, the inherent diversity of real-world environments necessitates enhanced robustness at test-time, moving beyond sole reliance on training-time optimization.

Within this context, Test-Time Scaling (TTS) methods have gained attention for improving VLA robustness by allocating additional computational resources during inference. Existing TTS approaches for VLAs typically involve strategies such as Best-of-N selection, often requiring external verifiers or self-verification mechanisms. However, these methods introduce several limitations that hinder their practical deployment. They necessitate additional training for verifiers, may exhibit degraded performance under domain shifts beyond the verifier's training distribution, and incur multiple forward passes, which conflicts with real-time robotic control requirements. Furthermore, a significant methodological limitation of these approaches is their exclusive intervention at the action decoding stage, leaving visual representations fixed. This fixed perception is often insufficient in situations characterized by perceptual ambiguity, where adapting how the scene is perceived can be as crucial as determining the appropriate action.

This work introduces SCALE (Self-uncertainty Conditioned Adaptive Looking and Execution) as a response to these limitations. It proposes a training-free and single-pass inference strategy that jointly modulates both visual perception and action. By drawing conceptual motivation from uncertainty-driven exploration principles within Active Inference theory, SCALE positions itself as an alternative to existing TTS methods, aiming to provide adaptive execution across varying conditions while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for real-time robotic deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models at test time, enabling them to operate effectively in diverse and novel real-world environments without the practical and methodological drawbacks of current Test-Time Scaling (TTS) methods.

The motivation for this work stems from two main challenges identified in the current VLA landscape:

1. **Limitations of Existing Test-Time Scaling (TTS) Methods:**
 - **Practical Constraints:** Current TTS approaches for VLAs often require substantial overhead. This includes the need for additional training to develop external verifiers or self-verification modules, which adds complexity and computational cost. These verifiers can also degrade in performance when confronted with domain shifts not present in their training data. Critically, these methods typically involve multiple forward passes during inference (e.g., Best-of-N sampling followed by verification), leading to increased latency that makes them impractical for real-time robotic control.
 - **Fixed Perception:** Methodologically, existing TTS methods tend to focus solely on refining action decoding. They typically maintain a fixed visual representation pipeline, meaning the model's perception of the environment remains constant regardless of the situation. This approach is deemed insufficient under conditions of perceptual ambiguity—such as the presence of visually similar distractors—where reconsidering *how* the environment is perceived is argued to be as important as deciding *what* action to execute.

2. **Inspiration from Active Inference Theory:**
 - The research draws conceptual motivation from Active Inference theory, a framework suggesting that intelligent agents adapt both their perception and action mechanisms to reduce uncertainty in their environment. This principle is observed in human cognition and exploration strategies, where individuals adjust their sensory intake and physical actions based on their current state of uncertainty. The authors hypothesize that integrating such an uncertainty-driven, adaptive modulation of perception and action could significantly improve VLA performance in challenging scenarios.

3. **Need for a Comprehensive Self-Uncertainty Measure:**
 - To operationalize uncertainty-driven adaptation, a robust measure of the model's self-uncertainty is required. Existing uncertainty estimation techniques in generative models, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), often focus on overall distributional uncertainty (e.g., entropy or divergence from a uniform distribution). However, for VLAs, where a greedy decoding strategy typically selects a single, top-1 action that can have irreversible consequences in the environment, the model's *decisiveness* or confidence in its primary choice is considered equally vital. Therefore, a new self-uncertainty measure that simultaneously captures both the general spread of the predicted distribution and the model's conviction in its top-1 action is motivated.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives for developing SCALE are:
- To create a simple, training-free, and single-pass inference strategy for VLAs.
- To enable joint, adaptive modulation of both visual perception and action decoding.
- To develop a novel self-uncertainty measure that effectively quantifies both distributional ambiguity and decisiveness in the top-1 prediction.
- To demonstrate that this adaptive strategy can broaden exploration under high uncertainty and sharpen focus under high confidence, leading to improved task success rates across various robotic manipulation benchmarks in both simulated and real-world environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SCALE (Self-uncertainty Conditioned Adaptive Looking and Execution) methodology proposes a single-pass inference strategy that dynamically adjusts both visual perception and action decoding based on the model's internal estimate of self-uncertainty. This approach is conceptually motivated by Active Inference theory, which emphasizes balancing exploration and exploitation.

The method is applied to autoregressive VLA policies $\pi_\theta$, which, at each timestep $t$, predict a sequence of $K$ action tokens $a_t = (a_1^t, \dots, a_K^t)$. These predictions are conditioned on visual representations $v_t = f_\phi(o_t; \gamma)$ obtained from a vision encoder $f_\phi$ (modulated by attention temperature $\gamma$) and a language instruction $I$. Each action token $a_k^t$ is drawn from a categorical distribution $p_k^t = \text{softmax}(\ell_k^t)$, where $\ell_k^t$ is the logit vector.

The core of SCALE involves three main components: a novel self-uncertainty measure, adaptive action decoding, and adaptive visual attention.

#### 3.1. Self-Uncertainty via Distributional Positioning

To quantify self-uncertainty that captures both overall distributional ambiguity and the model's decisiveness in its top-1 prediction, SCALE defines two reference distributions:
- **Low-uncertainty reference ($q_{low}$):** A one-hot distribution concentrated on the model's highest-probability token. This represents a state of complete certainty and commitment to a single choice, with a small $\epsilon$ for numerical stability distributed among other tokens.
- **High-uncertainty reference ($q_{high}$):** A uniform distribution over the entire action vocabulary $V$. This signifies maximum ambiguity and complete distributional uncertainty.

The self-uncertainty score $u_k^t$ for a token position $k$ at timestep $t$ is defined as the difference between the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence of the predicted distribution $p_k^t$ from these two references:
$u_k^t = D_{KL}(p_k^t \parallel q_{low}) - D_{KL}(p_k^t \parallel q_{high})$
This formulation effectively positions the predicted distribution $p_k^t$ on a spectrum between full certainty and full ambiguity. A positive $u_k^t$ indicates the predicted distribution is closer to uniform (high uncertainty), while a negative $u_k^t$ indicates it is closer to one-hot (low uncertainty). This measure is directly computable from output logits, requiring no additional training.

#### 3.2. Uncertainty-Driven Adaptive Inference

SCALE leverages $u_k^t$ to dynamically modulate action decoding and visual attention according to the principle of broadening exploration under uncertainty and sharpening focus under confidence.

#### 3.2.1. Adaptive Action Decoding

For action decoding, SCALE adjusts the sampling temperature $\tau_k^t$ for each action token $a_k^t$ based on its corresponding self-uncertainty $u_k^t$:
$\tau_k^t = T_0 \cdot \sigma(u_k^t)$
Here, $T_0$ is a maximum temperature that defines the exploration range, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function. The sigmoid function transforms $u_k^t$ into a value between 0 and 1, which can be interpreted as the posterior probability of being in an "uncertain" state. A high $u_k^t$ results in $\tau_k^t$ approaching $T_0$, leading to more explorative (diverse) sampling. Conversely, a low $u_k^t$ results in $\tau_k^t$ approaching 0, leading to near-greedy (focused) execution. The action token $a_k^t$ is then sampled from $\text{Cat}(\text{softmax}(\ell_k^t / \tau_k^t))$.

#### 3.2.2. Adaptive Visual Attention

For visual perception, SCALE modulates the attention temperature $\gamma_t$ of the vision encoder $f_\phi$. This modulation targets uni-modal self-attention layers within the vision encoder, chosen over cross-modal attention in the VLA backbone because it influences the initial extraction of visual information. Recognizing that perception benefits from temporal context, the modulation is guided by changes in uncertainty over time rather than instantaneous values.

First, token-level uncertainties $u_k^t$ are aggregated into a step-level uncertainty $u_t = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K u_k^t$.
An Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of past uncertainties, $\bar{u}_t = \alpha \bar{u}_{t-1} + (1-\alpha)u_t$, is maintained to capture the recent history of scene complexity.
To maintain single-pass efficiency, the visual attention at timestep $t$ is modulated by the uncertainty deviation from the *previous* step: $\Delta u_{t-1} = u_{t-1} - \bar{u}_{t-2}$. This relies on the assumption of high temporal correlation between consecutive visual frames.
The attention temperature $\gamma_t$ for the vision encoder is then computed as:
$\gamma_t = \kappa^{\text{tanh}(\Delta u_{t-1})}$
where $\kappa > 1$ bounds $\gamma_t$ to ensure stability. This $\gamma_t$ scales the self-attention mechanism within the vision encoder as $\text{softmax}(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d} \cdot \gamma_t})V$. If uncertainty rises ($\Delta u_{t-1} > 0$), $\gamma_t > 1$, flattening the attention distribution for broader visual exploration. If uncertainty falls ($\Delta u_{t-1} < 0$), $\gamma_t < 1$, sharpening attention for more focused perception.

#### 3.3. Single-Pass Closed-Loop Operation

The entire SCALE procedure operates in a single forward pass per control step. Uncertainty is computed from logits during action decoding at the current step, and this uncertainty (from the previous step for visual attention) then modulates the visual encoder for the subsequent step. This creates a feedback loop where improved perception leads to better uncertainty estimates, which in turn guide more effective actions, and vice versa. This design eliminates the need for additional rollouts, external verifiers, or auxiliary training, making it efficient for real-time robotic deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of SCALE encompassed both simulated and real-world benchmarks, utilizing various autoregressive VLA backbones including OpenVLA, $\pi^0$-FAST, and SpatialVLA. The results consistently demonstrated the efficacy and efficiency of the proposed method.

#### 4.1. Consistent Performance Improvement Across Benchmarks and Backbones

SCALE exhibited consistent improvements over greedy decoding across all evaluated scenarios:
- **Simulation Benchmarks:**
 - **LIBERO:** On the multi-task LIBERO benchmark, SCALE improved OpenVLA's average success rate by 5.8 percentage points (p.p.) and $\pi^0$-FAST's by 1.8 p.p.
 - **SIMPLER-WidowX:** For precision pick-and-place tasks, SCALE achieved significant gains, boosting $\pi^0$-FAST by 14.6 p.p. and SpatialVLA (zero-shot and fine-tuned) by 10.4 p.p. and 3.4 p.p. respectively.
 - **LIBERO-PRO-Long:** On this challenging unseen benchmark designed to test robustness beyond memorization, SCALE improved OpenVLA by 3.5 p.p. and $\pi^0$-FAST by 3.1 p.p.
- **Real-World Experiments:** The gains were more pronounced in real-world "Put A on B" pick-and-place tasks.
 - **In-Distribution (ID):** SCALE improved OpenVLA by 19.5 p.p. and $\pi^0$-FAST by 13.9 p.p.
 - **Out-of-Distribution (OOD):** Under unseen object conditions, SCALE showed improvements of 16.7 p.p. for OpenVLA and 12.5 p.p. for $\pi^0$-FAST.
These results indicate SCALE's generalization capability across different architectures, task complexities, and deployment environments.

#### 4.2. Superiority over Naive Decoding Strategies

Conventional fixed decoding strategies such as temperature sampling, top-k sampling, and top-p sampling often failed to provide robust improvements and, in some cases, even degraded performance compared to greedy decoding. For instance, on LIBERO with $\pi^0$-FAST, all three naive sampling methods resulted in lower success rates than greedy decoding. This suggests that fixed hyperparameters are insufficient for adapting to varying uncertainty levels across diverse states and tasks. In contrast, SCALE's dynamic adjustment of sampling temperature based on predicted uncertainty consistently yielded robust improvements.

#### 4.3. Outperformance of Training-Required Test-Time Scaling (TTS) Methods

A key finding is that SCALE outperformed existing training-required TTS methods while maintaining training-free, single-pass inference efficiency.
- On LIBERO with OpenVLA, SCALE achieved an average success rate of 81.5%, surpassing MG-Select (70.8%) by 10.7 p.p.
- For LIBERO-Long tasks, SCALE (63.3%) also exceeded RoboMonkey (56.5%) by 6.8 p.p. and TACO (60.0%) by 3.3 p.p.
This demonstrates that adaptive modulation based on intrinsic uncertainty can be more effective than external verification or multiple inference passes, especially for complex, long-horizon tasks requiring sustained adaptability.

#### 4.4. Detailed Analyses of SCALE Components

- **Ablation Study:** Both adaptive action decoding and adaptive visual attention contribute positively to performance. When combined, SCALE achieved a 10.6 p.p. improvement (63.3% from a 52.7% baseline on LIBERO-Long), which exceeded the sum of individual gains (5.3 p.p. for decoding, 3.3 p.p. for attention), indicating a synergistic effect between the two components.
- **Self-Uncertainty Measure:** A comparison against alternative uncertainty metrics (p_max, Gini Impurity, Entropy, Self-Certainty) showed that the proposed dual-reference self-uncertainty measure yielded the highest success rate (63.3%), outperforming the next best alternative (Gini Impurity, 57.8%) by 5.5 p.p. This supports the design choice of capturing both distributional spread and top-1 decisiveness.
- **Visual Modulation Design Choices:**
 - Modulating uni-modal attention in the vision encoder was found to be more effective than modulating cross-modal attention in the VLA backbone.
 - An adaptive, continuous scaling strategy for modulation outperformed a fixed, binary switching approach.
 - Using a change-based uncertainty signal (deviation from EMA) proved more effective than using instantaneous uncertainty values, supporting the hypothesis that perception should respond to transitions in scene complexity.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative visualizations demonstrated the operational mechanisms of SCALE:
- **Adaptive Visual Attention:** Under increasing self-uncertainty, SCALE dynamically broadened visual attention, re-directing focus from task-irrelevant distractors (e.g., microwave door) to the target object (e.g., mug), facilitating successful manipulation. In contrast, fixed-attention baselines remained fixated on distractors, leading to task failure.
- **Adaptive Action Decoding:** During high-uncertainty phases (e.g., transporting an object), SCALE's adaptive decoding led to explorative sampling, enabling the robot to find an elevated trajectory to avoid collisions. Greedy decoding, in contrast, followed a direct path that resulted in collision. Uncertainty was observed to decrease once a stable, successful trajectory was established.

#### 4.6. Efficiency and Statistical Reliability

SCALE maintains the efficiency of a single inference pass per control step, avoiding the significant latency increases associated with multiple forward passes in generate-and-verify TTS methods. Its performance was shown to be negligibly close to a computationally expensive Two-step Inference Oracle while being twice as fast. Statistical analysis further confirmed that the reported improvements were stable across random seeds and exceeded the corresponding standard deviations. Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis indicated that SCALE is robust across a broad range of its key parameters.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents SCALE as a methodological advance in the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic control. By introducing a training-free and single-pass inference strategy, SCALE addresses several practical and methodological limitations prevalent in existing Test-Time Scaling (TTS) methods.

The primary significance of this work lies in its demonstration of consistent and robust performance improvements across a variety of VLA architectures and diverse robotic manipulation tasks, encompassing both simulated and real-world environments, as well as in-distribution and out-of-distribution scenarios. This enhanced robustness is achieved without requiring additional training, external verifiers, or multiple forward passes, which distinguishes SCALE from prior TTS approaches that often incur high computational overhead and deployment complexities. The single-pass efficiency of SCALE makes it a more practical solution for real-time robotic control applications where low latency is critical.

Conceptually, SCALE operationalizes principles from Active Inference theory, demonstrating how an intrinsic self-uncertainty signal can effectively guide adaptive behavior in embodied agents. By jointly modulating visual perception and action, the method enables robots to dynamically balance exploration and exploitation. This capability allows the agent to broaden its attention and explore diverse actions when uncertain, and to sharpen its focus and execute near-greedy actions when confident, mimicking aspects of intelligent decision-making observed in biological systems. The novel dual-reference self-uncertainty measure, which captures both distributional ambiguity and top-1 decisiveness, is a key technical contribution enabling this adaptive behavior.

The potential impact of SCALE extends to improving the reliability and generalizability of embodied AI systems. More robust robotic control can contribute to advancements in various sectors, including enhanced automation in manufacturing, more capable assistive technologies in healthcare, and safer human-robot collaboration in unstructured environments. The development of more adaptable and efficient robot learning paradigms, as exemplified by SCALE, is fundamental for deploying intelligent robots in increasingly complex and unpredictable real-world settings.

The authors acknowledge that the broader implications of more capable robotic systems include both benefits and potential societal concerns, such as workforce displacement and safety challenges in human-robot interaction. However, they clarify that their contribution is primarily methodological, focusing on improving the performance and efficiency of existing VLA architectures rather than introducing entirely new capabilities. Therefore, the work is not anticipated to introduce unique ethical concerns beyond those already inherent in the broader field of robot learning, with an emphasis on considering safety protocols and human oversight in practical applications.
