# A Control-Centric Benchmark for Video Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University developed VP^2, a new control-centric benchmark for evaluating action-conditioned video prediction models based on their performance in simulated robotic manipulation tasks. Their findings indicate a frequent misalignment between perceptual video metrics and control success, and suggest complex relationships between model capacity, data quantity, and planning efficacy.

## Problem

- Existing video prediction evaluation metrics, such as SSIM, LPIPS, and FVD, primarily assess human perceptual similarity or pixel accuracy but often do not reliably correlate with performance in downstream robotic control tasks.
- A lack of a standardized, control-centric benchmark prevents direct assessment of action-conditioned video prediction models within a full planning pipeline for robotic manipulation.
- The absence of a standardized setup makes it difficult for researchers to isolate and evaluate the performance of video prediction models independently from the complexities of implementing an entire control system.

## Method

- Introduces **Video Prediction for Visual Planning (VP^2)**, a novel benchmark comprising two multi-task simulated robotic ==manipulation environments== (Tabletop robosuite and RoboDesk) with diverse tasks and visual goal specifications.
- Integrates a pre-configured ==sampling-based planner== (**Visual Foresight** using ==MPPI==) that uses the ==action-conditioned video prediction model== to simulate future frames and execute actions based on a task-specific, ==goal-driven cost function==.
- Provides standardized training datasets for each environment and a simple, flexible interface for evaluating video prediction models without requiring extensive control or reinforcement learning expertise.

## Results

- Conventional perceptual metrics like FVD and LPIPS often poorly correlate with robotic control success; models with better perceptual scores sometimes achieved significantly lower task success (e.g., **35%** vs. **65%** on `robosuite` pushing tasks).
- Evaluated state-of-the-art video prediction models exhibited substantial performance gaps compared to the true simulator baseline on complex VP^2 tasks, despite approaching simulator performance on simpler ones.
- Increasing model capacity or training data volume for video predictors yielded diminishing returns or inconsistent improvements in control, and a simple ensemble method for uncertainty awareness showed mixed but task-specific performance gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Traditional perceptual metrics for video prediction often fail to predict actual success in downstream robotic control tasks, leading to potentially misleading model selection for embodied agents.
> - For successful robotic control, qualities beyond mere visual fidelity are crucial; simply scaling model capacity or data quantity does not guarantee proportional improvements in planning performance beyond certain thresholds.
> - Uncertainty awareness in video prediction models, while computationally intensive, shows potential for improving planning by enabling the detection of unreliable predictions, which is vital for robust robotic agents.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This research is situated within the field of embodied artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on how agents can learn models of world dynamics from video data to facilitate downstream tasks like planning and policy learning. Video, as a task-agnostic and widely available modality, holds promise for self-supervised learning of dynamics models from raw agent observations. The core challenge in this domain is formulating video prediction as a problem of inferring future video frame distributions given initial frames and agent actions. Recent advancements have demonstrated the increasing effectiveness of large deep networks in modeling complex video data across diverse applications, including simulated games, driving, robotic manipulation, and locomotion.

However, a significant gap in the existing research landscape pertains to the evaluation of these video prediction models. Current practices predominantly rely on metrics that assess human perceptual similarity (e.g., SSIM, LPIPS, FVD) or pixel-wise comparisons (e.g., L2, PSNR). While these metrics have been established to correlate with human perception, their utility as indicators of performance for downstream control tasks in embodied agents remains an open question. Prior work has explored evaluation methods such as predicting physical events or training Q-learning agents in game environments. However, a standardized benchmark that directly assesses action-conditioned video prediction models in the context of robotic manipulation through a full planning pipeline, while isolating the video predictor's performance, has largely been absent.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing a control-centric benchmark. It contrasts with general video prediction benchmarks by focusing specifically on robotic control success rather than general prediction quality. It also differentiates from existing model-based and offline reinforcement learning benchmarks by providing a complete, standardized control setup—including environments, task specifications, training datasets, and planning algorithms—to ensure that only the video prediction model itself is the variable being evaluated. The work aims to empirically investigate the correlation between traditional video prediction metrics and actual control performance, and to understand how factors like model scale, data quantity, and uncertainty awareness influence an agent's ability to plan effectively using learned video dynamics.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and introduce a novel benchmark, termed Video Prediction for Visual Planning (VP$^2$), designed to evaluate action-conditioned video prediction models based on their performance in simulated robotic manipulation tasks through sampling-based planning.

The motivation for this objective stems from several considerations:

1. **Unreliability of Existing Metrics for Downstream Tasks:** A central motivation is the empirical observation that conventional video prediction metrics (such as L2, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and FVD), which primarily gauge perceptual quality or pixel accuracy, often demonstrate an unreliable correlation with success in downstream robotic control tasks. The authors present a case study showing that models optimized for perceptual metrics may generate visually plausible but physically inaccurate predictions, leading to planning failures (Figure 1). This highlights a critical misalignment between current evaluation practices and the functional requirements of embodied agents.

2. **Need for a Control-Centric Evaluation Standard:** As video prediction models grow in complexity and scale, a standardized method is required to determine their practical utility for control. The existing absence of a benchmark that directly measures prediction models' impact on an agent's planning abilities impedes progress in developing models specifically tailored for robotic control applications. The VP$^2$ benchmark aims to provide this missing evaluation axis.

3. **Facilitating Model-Based Control Development:** Dynamics models learned from video are fundamental for empowering embodied agents through policy learning or planning. By offering a standardized control setup, the benchmark aims to isolate the effects of the video prediction model itself on manipulation performance. This allows researchers to focus on developing more effective predictive models without needing to implement or tune an entire control system from scratch.

4. **Accessibility and Flexibility:** A key motivation for VP$^2$'s design is to make it accessible to researchers who may not have extensive experience in control or reinforcement learning. The benchmark is designed with a simple interface, requiring only a single forward prediction call from a video prediction model. Concurrently, it aims to be flexible, imposing minimal restrictions on the model's architecture or underlying deep learning framework, thereby encouraging diverse model development.

5. **Exploring Factors Affecting Control Performance:** Beyond simply evaluating models, the benchmark serves as a tool to investigate fundamental questions regarding video prediction for control. This includes analyzing the impact of scaling model size, varying the quantity of training data, and incorporating mechanisms for uncertainty awareness on the ultimate success of robotic manipulation tasks. The goal is to derive insights that can guide the development of future video prediction models better suited for control applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The VP$^2$ benchmark is designed to standardize the evaluation of action-conditioned video prediction models in a robotic control context. It comprises three main components: environments and task definitions, a sampling-based planner, and training datasets.

#### 3.1. Benchmark Design Principles

The benchmark adheres to three primary intentions:
- **Accessibility:** Evaluating models should not require control or reinforcement learning expertise, facilitated by a simple interface—a single forward prediction call.
- **Flexibility:** It places minimal restrictions on the type or architecture of video prediction models.
- **Emphasis on Model-Based Advantages:** It highlights scenarios where a single model can address diverse, dynamically specified goals.

#### 3.2. Environment and Task Definitions

VP$^2$ includes two simulated multi-task robotic manipulation environments:
- **Tabletop robosuite environment:** Utilizes `robosuite` infrastructure and the iGibson renderer for realistic visuals. It features four task categories: pushing a large cube, small cube, cylinder, or sphere. For each category, 25 task instances are defined, involving randomized object textures and specific initial simulator states and goal RGB images (64x64x3 pixels). The action space is 4-dimensional, controlling end-effector position changes and gripper actions.
- **RoboDesk environment:** Based on `RoboDesk`, it defines seven tasks with clear visual success cues: pushing red, green, or blue buttons; opening a slide or drawer; and pushing an upright or flat block off the table. Task instances are defined similarly, with 30 per category. The action space is 5-dimensional, controlling gripper position, wrist angle, and gripper command.

#### 3.3. Sampling-Based Planning (Visual Foresight)

The benchmark incorporates a pre-configured model-predictive control (MPC) method called visual foresight for evaluation.
- **Planning Process:** At each control step, a sampling-based planner (`MPPI` – Model-Predictive Path Integral) generates candidate action sequences. The video prediction model then simulates future video frames for each sequence.
- **Cost Function:** The predicted future frames are scored against a specified goal image using a cost function `C`.
 - For `robosuite` tasks, the cost is the pixel-wise mean squared error (MSE) between predicted frames and the goal image.
 - For `RoboDesk` tasks, the cost is a weighted combination of MSE and logits from a pre-trained, task-specific deep convolutional classifier (which predicts task success).
- **Execution:** The action sequence with the lowest cost is selected, and its first action is executed in the simulator. Replanning occurs at each step to mitigate compounding model errors.
- **Parameters:** The planner uses two context frames and predicts a horizon of `T=10` future frames. Default MPPI hyperparameters (e.g., 200 samples, scaling factor γ=0.05) are provided and tuned for strong performance with a perfect dynamics model.

#### 3.4. Training Datasets

VP$^2$ provides distinct training datasets for each environment:
- **robosuite Tabletop environment:** Contains 50,000 trajectories, each 35 timesteps long, collected using a hand-scripted policy to push a random object in a random direction. Gaussian noise (σ=0.05) is added to actions.
- **RoboDesk environment:** Comprises 35,000 trajectories (5,000 per task category), also 35 timesteps long. These are collected using hand-scripted policies with added Gaussian noise (σ=0.1 and σ=0.2) to actions, encouraging varied success rates.
Both datasets include 256x256 RGB image observations and corresponding actions.

#### 3.5. Empirical Analysis Approach

The paper uses the VP$^2$ benchmark to conduct empirical studies:
- **Baseline Performance:** Five state-of-the-art or competitive video prediction models (FitVid, SVG', MCVD, Struct-VRNN, MaskViT) are evaluated, along with a "simulator as model" baseline to establish an upper bound.
- **Perceptual Metrics vs. Control:** FitVid and SVG' models are trained with varying LPIPS loss weightings (0, 1, 10) to observe how changes in perceptual quality (measured by FVD, LPIPS, SSIM) affect control success across different tasks.
- **Model Capacity:** Variants of FitVid and SVG' with differing parameter counts (from 2.3M to 325M) are tested on `robosuite` tasks to analyze the effect of model size.
- **Data Quantity:** FitVid and SVG' models are trained on progressively larger subsets (1K to 50K trajectories) of the `robosuite` dataset to assess the impact of training data volume.
- **Uncertainty Awareness:** An ensemble disagreement method (using N=4 models with a penalty based on `L1` deviation from the mean prediction) is integrated into the planning cost function for FitVid and SVG' to investigate if uncertainty estimation improves control.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research yielded several findings regarding the evaluation and development of video prediction models for robotic control:

#### 4.1. Mismatch Between Perceptual Metrics and Control Performance

The empirical case study demonstrated that commonly used perceptual and pixel-wise metrics (FVD, LPIPS, SSIM) often do not reliably predict performance on downstream robotic manipulation tasks.
- For instance, on `robosuite` pushing tasks, models trained with a high LPIPS loss weighting (LPIPS=10) often achieved low FVD scores (indicating better perceptual quality) but resulted in significantly reduced control success (e.g., FitVid: FVD 24.3, Success 35% vs. MSE: FVD 30.7, Success 65%).
- Conversely, for tasks like "RoboDesk: upright block off table", FVD showed a better correlation with task success compared to LPIPS and SSIM, but this correlation was not universal across all tasks.
- Models could exhibit almost identical scores on certain perceptual metrics while displaying widely divergent control performances, indicating that these metrics can lead to erroneous model selection for control applications.

#### 4.2. Performance of Existing Models on VP$^2$ Tasks

Evaluation of five prominent video prediction models (FitVid, SVG', MCVD, Struct-VRNN, MaskViT) against the VP$^2$ benchmark revealed performance gaps:
- For simpler tasks (e.g., "push blue button"), the performance of learned models approached that of the true simulator dynamics, indicating effective learning for straightforward manipulations.
- However, for more complex `robosuite` tasks and other `RoboDesk` tasks (e.g., "open drawer," "open slide," "upright block off table"), there remained significant performance discrepancies between learned models and the simulator baseline, suggesting that current video prediction models struggle with complex, long-horizon, or less predictable dynamics crucial for these tasks.

#### 4.3. Effect of Model Capacity

Experiments on varying model sizes for FitVid and SVG' on `robosuite` tasks indicated that increasing model capacity did not consistently lead to improved control performance.
- While some larger SVG' configurations showed marginal gains, there was no strong positive trend.
- A hypothesis proposed is that larger models, such as the full FitVid architecture, may overfit to the action sequences present in the training dataset, limiting their generalization for planning.
- Regarding computational efficiency, the MCVD model, while achieving competitive control performance, exhibited a forward prediction time more than 10 times longer than the full FitVid model, highlighting a challenge in deploying diffusion models for real-time planning.

#### 4.4. Effect of Training Data Quantity

The study on data quantity for FitVid and SVG' on `robosuite` tasks demonstrated that increasing the amount of training data improved performance at lower data regimes (from hundreds to thousands of trajectories).
- However, these gains quickly plateaued after a certain point (e.g., around 15,000 trajectories), suggesting diminishing returns for additional data volume within the tested range for these specific tasks.
- The authors hypothesize this plateau is due to the relatively constrained distribution of actions captured by the scripted data collection policy, which limits the diversity of learned dynamics despite more samples.

#### 4.5. Uncertainty Estimation via Ensembles

Investigating uncertainty awareness through an ensemble disagreement method yielded mixed but promising results:
- Applying a score penalty based on ensemble disagreement during planning resulted in slightly improved performance on specific tasks, such as "upright block off table."
- For other tasks (e.g., "push green button," "open slide"), performance was comparable to or slightly weaker than using a single model.
- These findings suggest that enhancing models with better uncertainty awareness—allowing them to detect out-of-distribution action queries—could be a beneficial avenue for improving downstream planning performance, even though a naive ensemble approach linearly increases computational cost.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The introduction of the Video Prediction for Visual Planning (VP$^2$) benchmark represents a significant contribution to the field of embodied AI and machine learning for robotics. Its primary significance lies in establishing a standardized, control-centric evaluation paradigm for action-conditioned video prediction models, directly addressing a recognized gap in existing assessment methodologies.

**Key areas of significance and potential impact include:**

1. **Re-calibration of Model Evaluation:** The empirical demonstration of a frequent misalignment between traditional perceptual metrics and actual control performance is critical. This finding challenges the conventional reliance on metrics like FVD, LPIPS, and SSIM as sole indicators of model utility for robotics. It advocates for the concurrent use of control-centric benchmarks like VP$^2$ to provide a more holistic and task-relevant understanding of model capabilities. This may lead to a re-evaluation of how video prediction models are designed and optimized.

2. **Accelerated Research in Model-Based Control:** By providing a comprehensive and open-sourced framework—including environments, specific task definitions, training datasets, and a fully implemented planning pipeline—VP$^2$ significantly lowers the barrier to entry for researchers interested in model-based reinforcement learning and video prediction for robotics. This standardization can accelerate the development, comparison, and iteration of new video prediction models specifically tailored for control applications, as researchers can focus solely on the predictive model itself.

3. **Guidance for Model Design and Development:** The empirical studies conducted using VP$^2$ offer insights that can directly inform future model development. The observation that simply scaling model size or data quantity does not guarantee proportional gains in control performance suggests that qualities beyond mere visual fidelity are crucial. The findings regarding uncertainty awareness, in particular, highlight the potential benefit of designing models that can explicitly quantify or detect when they are making unreliable predictions, which is vital for robust robotic planning. This could spur research into uncertainty-aware or physically-grounded video prediction architectures.

4. **Enabling Multi-Task and Goal-Conditioned Planning:** The benchmark's design, which supports multi-task environments and goal specification via images, is particularly impactful. It encourages the development of generalizable video prediction models capable of being leveraged for a wide array of tasks and dynamically specified goals, moving beyond single-task or fixed-goal scenarios common in many RL benchmarks. This is a crucial step towards more adaptable and capable embodied agents.

5. **Foundation for Future Work:** VP$^2$ serves as a foundational testbed not only for video prediction models but also for novel planning algorithms and cost functions within a visual MPC framework. While the current benchmark focuses on simulated, shorter-horizon tasks, it explicitly acknowledges these limitations and provides a clear path for expansion to more challenging scenarios as the field progresses.

In essence, VP$^2$ is poised to drive the development of video prediction models that are not only perceptually sophisticated but also functionally effective in enabling intelligent decision-making and robust control for embodied agents.
