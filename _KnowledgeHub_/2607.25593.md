# When Does Legacy Data Start to Help? Emergent Transfer in Cross-Configuration Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Spirit AI and affiliated universities empirically identifies a three-phase pattern for the utility of legacy demonstration data when a robot undergoes hardware upgrades, demonstrating that transfer benefits are contingent on the upgraded system's competence and proposing a practical data collection strategy.

## Problem

- Reusing extensive, costly teleoperated demonstration data becomes challenging when a robot undergoes hardware upgrades (e.g., sensors, end-effectors), altering visual inputs and low-level actions.
- The common assumption that more cross-configuration data is always beneficial is often not fully explored for direct hardware changes on the same robot platform, potentially leading to negative transfer.
- There is a lack of understanding regarding the specific conditions under which legacy data transitions from ineffective to beneficial for an upgraded robot.

## Method

- Experiments were conducted on a ==26-DoF wheeled humanoid robot== across two hardware generations (Gen-1 and Gen-2) with different cameras and grippers, evaluating transfer on tabletop manipulation tasks.
- A theoretical framework was developed, defining a '==transfer threshold==' based on the target policy's ==latent stage decodability== and ==gradient alignment==, explaining the emergent three-phase transfer pattern.
- A '**phase-aware data collection rule**' was formulated, which estimates task difficulty and a corresponding transfer threshold, guiding when to collect new-hardware data versus when to co-train with legacy data.

## Results

- Co-training exhibited a three-phase pattern: no gain below the transfer threshold (Phase I; **0.0%** for flower insertion at **10.0%** baseline), then substantial gains at intermediate baselines (Phase II; **+63.4 pp** for flower insertion, **23.3%** to **86.7%**).
- Gains diminished (Phase III) as standalone performance approached saturation (e.g., **26.6 percentage points** gain for **71.7%** baseline vs. **8.3 percentage points** gain for **85.0%** baseline in pen insertion).
- The phase-aware data collection rule successfully guided training on a held-out mobile watering task, demonstrating that **1.5 hours** of new data was sufficient to effectively leverage legacy data, potentially reducing new data collection needs by over **80%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Legacy data transfer exhibits an 'inverted-U' relationship with the target policy's standalone success rate, meaning its utility is conditional and not consistently positive.
> - A task-dependent 'transfer threshold' (τ(T)) exists; below this threshold, legacy data provides no benefit due to insufficient internal representation of task stages.
> - Optimal gains from legacy data occur at intermediate levels of target policy competence, diminishing as the policy approaches saturation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of embodied AI, specifically addressing challenges in robot learning and data reuse amidst hardware evolution. Recent advancements in large-scale robot learning and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated the capacity of broad datasets and high-capacity policies to enhance generalization across tasks, scenes, and robot embodiments. Initiatives such as Open X-Embodiment, Octo, OpenVLA, X-VLA, and HPT have shown that training policies on diverse robot platforms can improve performance and adaptation to new observation and action spaces.

A related line of work, exemplified by RoboNet and BridgeData, has established that utilizing data from other robots or domains can reduce the amount of new data required for a target domain. These studies generally support the premise that prior robot data can be beneficial for learning in new or upgraded systems.

However, the current paper identifies a specific, underexplored problem: the practical challenge of reusing extensive, costly teleoperated demonstration data when a robot undergoes hardware upgrades. Such upgrades, involving changes to sensors (e.g., cameras) and end-effectors (e.g., grippers), alter visual inputs and low-level action execution, potentially disrupting the utility of legacy data. While cross-robot learning broadly confirms the usefulness of prior data, it does not explicitly clarify *when* this data becomes beneficial after a direct hardware change on the *same* robot platform.

Traditional transfer learning and domain adaptation literature suggests that source data may offer limited benefit, or even lead to "negative transfer," if the source and target domains are not sufficiently aligned. This research aims to fill this gap by empirically investigating the conditions under which legacy data transitions from being ineffective to highly beneficial for an upgraded robot, specifically focusing on the emergent patterns of transfer in cross-configuration robot learning. It contrasts with the common implicit assumption that "more cross-configuration data is always helpful," by identifying a more nuanced, phase-dependent utility of legacy data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate when legacy demonstration data begins to benefit a robot that has undergone hardware upgrades. This core question is driven by several motivations:

1. **Practical Challenge of Hardware Evolution:** Robots deployed in real-world environments frequently undergo hardware iterations. Sensors are upgraded for better fidelity or wider fields of view, and end-effectors are redesigned for improved dexterity. These changes, while enhancing capabilities, simultaneously create a practical problem: large volumes of expensive teleoperated demonstration data collected on previous hardware configurations ("legacy data") may become difficult to reuse. This represents a significant loss of investment and valuable task knowledge.

2. **Questioning the "Always Helpful" Assumption:** A common intuition in robot learning, supported by broad cross-robot training successes, is that incorporating more data, even from different configurations, is generally beneficial. This paper explicitly challenges this assumption by exploring whether legacy data always provides a positive transfer or if its utility is conditional on certain factors. The authors hypothesize that significant hardware shifts in sensing and actuation could introduce domain gaps that make legacy data less immediately transferable.

3. **Understanding Transfer Dynamics:** Beyond simply determining if legacy data helps, the research aims to characterize *how* its benefit changes as the upgraded robot acquires competence in a new task. This involves investigating if there are distinct phases of transfer, where legacy data's impact varies depending on the target system's baseline performance. This exploration seeks to uncover underlying mechanisms governing the effectiveness of cross-configuration data reuse.

4. **Developing Actionable Guidelines for Data Collection:** A key motivation is to translate empirical observations and theoretical insights into practical guidance for robot developers. The goal is to formulate a data collection strategy that efficiently leverages legacy data, rather than blindly collecting new data or mixing all available data. This involves identifying a "transfer threshold" that can inform decisions on when to continue collecting new-hardware data and when to begin co-training with legacy demonstrations.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to provide a more nuanced understanding of data reuse in evolving robotic systems, offering both empirical characterization and theoretical explanations for the observed phenomena, ultimately leading to more efficient robot learning strategies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed an experimental and theoretical approach to investigate the utility of legacy data in cross-configuration robot learning.

**Robot Platform and Hardware Configurations:**
Experiments were conducted on a wheeled humanoid robot featuring 26 actuated degrees of freedom (DoF), including omni wheels, dual 7-DoF arms, a 6-DoF waist-leg mechanism, and a 2-DoF neck. The study focused on two hardware generations (Gen-1 and Gen-2) that shared the same base, arm kinematics, and overall morphology. The key differences were:
- **Gen-1 (Legacy):** Used three monocular RGB cameras (640x480 resolution, 87°x58° field of view) and an 8 cm position-controlled parallel-jaw gripper.
- **Gen-2 (Upgraded):** Replaced Gen-1 cameras with three fisheye cameras (1920x1536 resolution, 120°x120° field of view) and an 8 cm hybrid force/position-controlled gripper with wrist force-torque sensing.

**Tasks:**
The main study included four fixed-base tabletop manipulation tasks: pen grasp, pen insertion, flower grasp, and flower insertion. Object poses were randomized within a defined workspace. Flower insertion was identified as the most challenging due to the compliant stem and narrow vase. A held-out task, mobile dual-arm watering, was used for validation, involving navigation, dual-arm grasping, and pouring with the full 26-DoF robot.

**Policy Architecture and Training:** All policies were initialized from a pre-trained π0.5 vision-language-action (VLA) model and fine-tuned using behavior cloning. The policy input consisted of multi-view RGB observations, proprioceptive states, and a language instruction. It predicted a 60-step action chunk for closed-loop execution. Both hardware generations utilized the same normalized arm-action representation and gripper opening position output, with configuration-specific low-level controllers handling the differences between position-controlled and hybrid force/position-controlled grippers. The policy did not receive explicit hardware-generation labels.

**Demonstration Data:**
All demonstrations were collected via teleoperation at 30 Hz.
- **Gen-1 Legacy Data:** Fixed datasets for pen and flower insertion were used (e.g., 45.54h for pen insertion, 17.10h for flower insertion).
- **Gen-2 Data:** Multiple batches were collected, including "early" and "quality-refined" datasets. Refined batches involved stricter operator training, per-trajectory quality filtering, and tighter object-pose tolerances.
- **Co-training:** When co-training, Gen-1 and Gen-2 data were typically sampled with equal probability.

**Evaluation Protocol:**
- **Metric:** End-to-end task success rate, where a trial was counted as successful only if the full task was completed within the time limit (30s for grasping, 60s for insertion) without human intervention.
- **Trials:** Each experimental condition was evaluated over *n* = 60 real-robot trials.
- **Co-training Gain (∆SR):** Defined as SR<sub>co-train</sub> - SR<sub>single</sub>, comparing the success rate of a co-trained policy against a policy trained only on new-hardware data.
- **Statistical Analysis:** Two-sided Fisher's exact tests were used to evaluate differences between policies, with *p* < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Wilson 95% confidence intervals were reported.

**Theoretical Framework:**
The authors developed a theoretical account to explain the observed three-phase pattern:
1. **Stage Structure and Decodability:** A trajectory is decomposed into latent stages (e.g., approach, grasp, insert). The "stage decodability" (ρ) of the target policy's internal representation quantifies its ability to correctly identify these stages. Standalone success rate (SR) is used as an observable proxy for ρ.
2. **Transfer Threshold (τ(T)):** Defined as the minimum standalone success rate for task T above which co-training with legacy data yields a positive expected gain.
3. **Gradient Alignment:** The theory posits that legacy data becomes useful when the target policy's representation of task stages is sufficient to align legacy and target gradients (i.e., same-stage gradients point towards a shared objective). Below this threshold, gradients may conflict or cancel, leading to no benefit.
4. **Residual Policy Uncertainty:** The benefit of legacy data is modeled as being proportional to the remaining uncertainty within each stage of the target policy, decreasing as standalone performance approaches saturation.
5. **Inverted-U Gain Law:** These principles combine to predict an inverted-U relationship between co-training gain and standalone success rate (Theorem 2).
6. **Task Complexity:** The transfer threshold τ(T) is hypothesized to correlate with task complexity H(T), defined as task horizon L(T) multiplied by log(1/spatial tolerance ϵ(T)).

**Phase-Aware Data Collection Rule:** Based on the theoretical model, a practical rule was formulated:
1. Estimate task difficulty H(T) and corresponding transfer threshold ˆτ(T).
2. Train a standalone policy on new-hardware data and measure its success rate SR2 (T).
3. If SR2 (T) < ˆτ(T), collect more new-hardware data; otherwise, co-train with legacy data. This rule was validated on the held-out mobile watering task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's empirical results revealed a consistent three-phase pattern in the effectiveness of co-training legacy data with new-hardware data, depending on the standalone success rate of the upgraded robot.

**Discovery 1: Phase I Failure – Co-training Below the Threshold**
At low levels of standalone competence on the upgraded hardware, legacy data provided no measurable benefit. For instance, with only 4.31 hours of early Gen-2 flower-insertion data, the standalone policy achieved a 10.0% success rate. Co-training this policy with 17.10 hours of legacy Gen-1 flower data resulted in no change, with the success rate remaining at 10.0% (∆SR = 0.0%, statistically insignificant). This indicated that below a certain task-dependent transfer threshold (τ(T)), legacy data was ineffective. Importantly, in the same training run, Gen-1 flower insertion performance improved from 50.0% to 78.3% (+28.3%), demonstrating that the lack of benefit for Gen-2 was not due to the legacy data itself but its interaction with the low competence of the Gen-2 policy.

**Discovery 2: Phase II – Large Gains at Intermediate Baselines**
Once the upgraded hardware policy achieved a minimum level of task competence, crossing the transfer threshold, co-training yielded substantial improvements. For example, using quality-refined Gen-2 data, flower insertion reached a standalone success rate of 23.3%. Co-training with legacy Gen-1 data then increased this to 86.7%, representing a gain of 63.4 percentage points (p ≈ 1.8 × 10^-12). Similarly, Gen-2 pen insertion, with a standalone rate of 71.7%, improved to 98.3% after co-training, a gain of 26.6 percentage points (p ≈ 4.0 × 10^-5). This phase demonstrated that legacy data becomes highly beneficial when the target policy has acquired basic task structure but still has considerable room for improvement.

**Discovery 3: Phase III – Diminishing Saturation**
At high standalone baselines, the marginal gains from co-training with legacy data diminished. For Gen-2 pen insertion, an initial standalone success rate of 71.7% saw a gain of 26.6 points. However, with additional Gen-2 data, leading to a higher standalone baseline of 85.0%, the co-training gain reduced to +8.3 points. Near-ceiling performance in Gen-1 grasping tasks also showed minimal, statistically insignificant changes, including some minor negative shifts. This indicated that as the target policy approaches saturation, the information provided by legacy data has less capacity to reduce remaining policy uncertainty.

**Synthesis: The Three-Phase Model**
These findings collectively established an "inverted-U" relationship between co-training gain and standalone success rate: near-zero gain below τ(T), a peak in gains at intermediate competence, and diminishing returns near saturation. The study also observed bidirectionality, where the same co-training run could benefit one configuration (Gen-1) while failing to benefit another (Gen-2), highlighting the task- and configuration-dependent nature of the threshold.

**Theoretical Account and Validation:**
The researchers provided a theoretical framework to explain these empirical observations. Theorem 1, the **Transfer-Threshold Transition**, posits that below τ(T), expected gradient alignment between legacy and target data is non-positive due to undecodable latent task stages. Above τ(T), as stage decodability improves, same-stage gradients align, providing a positive training signal. Theorem 2, the **Inverted-U Gain Law**, describes how the expected co-training gain, above the threshold, is proportional to the remaining within-stage policy uncertainty (modeled as `η(1 - SR)`) and stage decodability, while accounting for configuration-conflict costs.

**Validation on a Held-Out Mobile Dual-Arm Watering Task:**
To validate the phase model and its practical utility, a phase-aware data collection rule was tested on a novel mobile dual-arm watering task. This rule involved estimating task complexity (H(T)) and its corresponding transfer threshold, then collecting new-hardware data until the standalone success rate exceeded this threshold before engaging in co-training.
- **0.5h Gen-2 data:** Standalone performance was low (e.g., 3.3% for "Pick kettle"). Co-training yielded no measurable gain (Phase I).
- **1.5h Gen-2 data:** Standalone performance improved significantly (e.g., 51.7% for "Pick kettle"). Co-training produced large gains (e.g., 40.0 percentage points to 91.7% for "Pick kettle," p=1.5×10^-6) (Phase II).
- **8h Gen-2 data:** Standalone performance was high (e.g., 86.7% for "Pick kettle"). Co-training provided minimal, non-significant gains (e.g., -1.7% for "Pick kettle") (Phase III).
This validation demonstrated that the phase-aware rule could effectively guide data collection, showing that 1.5 hours of new data was sufficient to leverage legacy demonstrations effectively, potentially reducing the need for longer collection times (e.g., 8 hours).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of embodied AI by providing a nuanced understanding of data reuse in evolving robotic systems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Challenging Assumptions in Data Reuse:** The work empirically disproves the general assumption that simply adding more cross-configuration data is always beneficial. By identifying a "three-phase pattern," it demonstrates that the utility of legacy data is conditional on the target system's current competence, thereby offering a more refined perspective on data integration strategies.

2. **Introducing the Transfer Threshold Concept:** The formulation of a "task-dependent transfer threshold" (τ(T)) provides a concrete, measurable criterion for determining *when* legacy data will become genuinely useful. This concept moves beyond qualitative assessments of domain similarity and offers a quantitative basis for decision-making in real-world robot development.

3. **Guiding Efficient Data Collection:** The proposed "phase-aware data collection rule" represents a practical tool for optimizing resource allocation. As demonstrated by the held-out watering task, this rule can prevent unnecessary expenditure on collecting new-hardware data by indicating the optimal point at which to introduce legacy demonstrations. This has direct implications for reducing the time, cost, and effort associated with robot data acquisition during hardware iterations.

4. **Providing a Theoretical Explanation for Emergent Transfer:** The theoretical account, grounded in gradient alignment and residual policy uncertainty, offers insights into the mechanisms underlying the observed three-phase pattern. By connecting emergent transfer to the decodability of latent task stages and the remaining uncertainty in the target policy, the research contributes to a deeper understanding of how knowledge transfer occurs in complex robot learning systems.

5. **Informative for System Design and Iteration:** The findings suggest that hardware iteration should be treated not as a binary data-reuse decision, but as a dynamic process. Developers can use the phase model to assess the state of different tasks on an upgraded robot, allowing for targeted data collection efforts rather than a uniform approach. This enables more intelligent management of robotic system evolution.

6. **Broader Implications for Transfer Learning:** While focused on hardware iteration, the concept of an "emergent transfer" and a task-dependent threshold may have broader relevance for understanding transfer learning in other contexts, such as curriculum learning, multi-task learning, or adapting policies across significantly different robot embodiments.

In summary, this research refines the understanding of data reuse in robotics, providing both empirical evidence for a conditional benefit of legacy data and a theoretical framework to explain it. Its practical implications lie in guiding more efficient and intelligent data collection strategies for robot systems undergoing hardware evolution.
