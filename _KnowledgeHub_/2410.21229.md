# HOVER: Versatile Neural Whole-Body Controller for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> HOVER introduces a neural whole-body controller for humanoid robots that unifies diverse control modes into a single policy, leveraging distillation from large-scale human motion data. The system demonstrates superior performance compared to individually trained specialist controllers and multi-mode reinforcement learning baselines in over 15 control configurations, showcasing robust real-world deployment and seamless mode transitions on a Unitree H1 robot.

## Problem

- Humanoid control relies on specialized policies for specific tasks (e.g., locomotion, manipulation), which limits versatility and transferability across different operational modes.
- Developing new controllers for each task is repetitive and time-consuming, hindering efficient expansion of robot capabilities.
- Existing methods struggle to enable seamless, instantaneous transitions between different control modes during dynamic operations.

## Method

- A multi-mode policy distillation framework is employed to train a unified neural controller (**HOVER**) by mimicking an 'oracle' motion imitator, which is trained on large-scale human motion data.
- A comprehensive and atomic command space is designed, encompassing kinematic position tracking, local joint angle tracking, and root tracking, with a one-hot masking vector to define specific control modes.
- Human motion data from the AMASS dataset is retargeted to humanoid-feasible motions, providing a rich foundation for the oracle policy trained via goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (PPO).

## Results

- HOVER consistently outperformed specialist policies trained for prior work's control modes and other useful modes, showing superior generalization in both simulation (outperforming in at least 7 of 12 metrics) and real-world Unitree H1 experiments (outperforming in 11 of 12 metrics).
- Compared to a multi-mode RL baseline, HOVER achieved consistently lower tracking error across all 32 evaluated metrics and eight distinct control modes, demonstrating the efficacy of the distillation approach.
- Successfully deployed on a 19-DOF Unitree H1 robot, HOVER executed accurate tracking of complex dynamic motions, demonstrated robustness to abrupt command mode switches, and supported teleoperation with randomized masking of command inputs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Full-body kinematic motion imitation offers a common abstraction for acquiring general-purpose motor skills, which can be adapted to various control modes.
> - Policy distillation from an oracle trained on full-body human kinematics provides stronger motor priors, leading to a more competent and generalizable whole-body controller across diverse modes than direct multi-mode RL.
> - A unified and dynamically maskable command space allows a single neural policy to handle arbitrary combinations of control objectives, significantly enhancing versatility without requiring task-specific retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robotics research faces the challenge of developing versatile whole-body controllers capable of adapting to a wide array of tasks, ranging from bipedal locomotion to complex manipulation. Historically, approaches to humanoid control have often involved developing specialized policies tailored to specific tasks and their corresponding command spaces. For instance, some controllers prioritize root velocity tracking for navigation, while others focus on joint angle or end-effector tracking for precise manipulation. This specialization has led to a fragmented landscape where individual controllers are effective within their defined scope but lack transferability and versatility across different operational modes.

This work addresses the limitation of task-specific controllers by proposing a unified framework. Existing methods, such as ExBody, H2O, OmniH2O, and HumanPlus, have demonstrated impressive results in their respective domains but typically operate within a single, pre-defined control mode. This often necessitates repetitive development and retraining when transitioning between different tasks or control requirements. The paper identifies the common underlying objective of stable, human-like motion across these diverse modes, suggesting that a shared foundation of motor skills could enable a more generalist approach.

The concept of a multi-mode or unified controller has been explored in related areas, such as MHC for learning multi-modal humanoid control and MaskedMimic in computer graphics for flexible kinematic tracking through distillation. However, these often have limitations, such as not supporting arbitrary subsets of chosen modes or requiring additional policies to be trained. HOVER seeks to build upon these ideas by creating a single, versatile neural controller that can directly control real humanoids across a comprehensive range of command configurations, aiming to consolidate the strengths of various specialized approaches into one adaptable system. This contribution positions HOVER as an advancement towards more general-purpose and scalable humanoid robot control, bridging the gap between specialized task execution and broad operational adaptability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified neural controller for humanoid whole-body control that can support diverse control modes, encompassing a wide range of tasks from navigation to manipulation. This includes enabling seamless transitions between these different modes, ultimately offering a robust and scalable solution for humanoid control.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several identified limitations of existing humanoid control paradigms:

- **Lack of Versatility and Transferability:** Current approaches typically involve training individual policies (specialist controllers) for specific command spaces, such as root velocity tracking for locomotion or joint angle tracking for manipulation. This specialization limits the transferability of these policies across different tasks. A robot proficient in locomotion on uneven terrain, for example, would struggle to perform precise bimanual manipulation without a different, specialized controller.
- **Repetitive Development and Time Consumption:** The need for task-specific controller interfaces and reward designs makes the development process repetitive and time-consuming. Each new task or scenario often requires significant effort in designing, training, and fine-tuning a new controller.
- **Inability for Seamless Mode Transitions:** The reliance on distinct controllers for different tasks prevents fluid and instantaneous switching between control modes during operation. This restricts a humanoid robot's adaptability in dynamic real-world environments where task requirements may change rapidly.
- **Inefficient Skill Acquisition:** While diverse control modes are applied to a shared hardware platform, existing methods do not effectively leverage the underlying commonalities in motion objectives, such as maintaining balance and performing human-like movements. This leads to inefficient skill acquisition, as general physical knowledge is not adequately shared across different specialist policies.

To address these motivations, the authors introduce the key insight that **full-body kinematic motion imitation can serve as a common abstraction** for various tasks. By leveraging large-scale human motion data, a policy can acquire general-purpose motor skills, which can then be adapted to multiple control modes. This approach is hypothesized to lead to a "generalist policy" that not only supports diverse modes but also potentially outperforms individually trained specialist policies by capitalizing on shared physical knowledge and promoting better generalization. The aim is to improve efficiency and flexibility for future humanoid applications by eliminating the need for retraining policies for each new control mode.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HOVER framework employs a multi-mode policy distillation process to consolidate diverse control modes into a unified policy. This methodology is structured around several key components:

**A. Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (RL) Formulation:**
The problem is formulated as a goal-conditioned RL task. A policy $\pi$ is trained to track real-time human motion. The state at time $t$, $s_t$, comprises the agent's proprioception ($s^p_t$) and a target goal state ($s^g_t$). The goal state provides a unified representation of the target motion, which is detailed further in the command space design. A reward $r_t$ is defined as $R(s^p_t, s^g_t)$ for policy optimization. The action $a_t \in \mathbb{R}^{19}$ represents target joint positions, which are fed to a PD controller to actuate the robot. The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used to maximize cumulative discounted reward.

**B. Command Space Design for Humanoid Control:**
A comprehensive and unified command space is designed based on two criteria:
- **Generality:** It must encompass most existing control configurations and be expressive enough to interface with various real-world control devices (e.g., joysticks, motion capture, VR headsets).
- **Atomicity:** It must consist of independent dimensions to enable arbitrary combinations of control options.

The unified command space is composed of three distinct control modes, applied independently to upper-body and lower-body regions:
- **Kinematic Position Tracking:** Targets 3D positions of key rigid body points on the robot.
- **Local Joint Angle Tracking:** Targets joint angles for each robot motor.
- **Root Tracking:** Targets root velocity, height, and orientation (roll, pitch, yaw angles).
A one-hot masking vector is introduced to selectively activate specific components of this command space for tracking, thereby defining different control modes. The authors state that prior works' control modes can be viewed as subsets of this unified command space.

**C. Motion Retargeting:**
To provide a rich foundation of human-like motion, the framework utilizes large-scale human motion data from the AMASS dataset. A three-step procedure is used to retarget this data to a humanoid-feasible motion dataset ($\hat{Q}$):
1. **Keypoint Computation:** Humanoid keypoint positions are computed using forward kinematics, mapping joint configurations to workspace coordinates.
2. **SMPL Model Fitting:** The SMPL model parameters are optimized to align with the humanoid's computed keypoints.
3. **AMASS Retargeting:** The AMASS dataset is retargeted by matching corresponding keypoints between the fitted SMPL model and the humanoid using gradient descent. This process ensures the generated motions are feasible for the humanoid robot.

**D. Oracle Policy Training from Large-Scale Human Motions:**
An "oracle" motion imitator, $\pi_{oracle}$, is trained using the retargeted human motion dataset.
- **State Space:** The oracle's proprioception, $s^p_{oracle,t}$, includes rigid-body position, orientation, linear and angular velocities, and the previous action. The oracle's goal state, $s^g_{oracle,t}$, contains the reference pose and one-frame differences between the reference and current state for all rigid bodies of the humanoid.
- **Network Architecture:** A three-layer MLP with dimensions.
- **Reward Design:** The reward $r_t$ is a sum of penalty, regularization, and task rewards (detailed in Table II of the paper), designed to encourage accurate motion imitation, maintain physical realism, and prevent undesirable behaviors.
- **Domain Randomizations:** Standard domain randomization techniques are applied to physical parameters of the simulated environment and humanoids to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.

**E. Multi-Mode Versatile Controller via Distillation:**
The HOVER policy ($\pi_{student}$) is distilled from the trained oracle policy ($\pi_{oracle}$).
- **Student Proprioception:** The student's proprioception, $s^p_{student,t}$, includes joint positions, joint velocities, base angular velocity, gravity vector, and a history of past actions, stacked over the last 25 steps to provide temporal context.
- **Command Mask:** The student's task command input, $s^g_{student,t}$, is defined by applying both mode-based ($M_{mode}$) and sparsity-based ($M_{sparsity}$) masks to the target commands. $M_{mode}$ selects specific command modes independently for the upper and lower body (e.g., upper body tracks kinematic positions, lower body tracks joint angles and root). Subsequently, $M_{sparsity}$ allows for further selective tracking within a mode (e.g., tracking only hands within kinematic position tracking). Both masks are binary and generated from a Bernoulli distribution B(0.5), randomized at the beginning of each episode.
- **Policy Distillation Process (DAgger):** The DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) framework is used for distillation. In each episode, the student policy is rolled out in simulation to collect trajectories of its states and goal states. For each timestep, corresponding oracle states are computed, and the oracle policy is queried to obtain reference actions ($\hat{a}_t$). The student policy is then updated by minimizing the L2 loss function: $L = ||\hat{a}_t - a_t||_2^2$, where $a_t$ is the action taken by the student policy. This process iteratively guides the student to mimic the oracle's behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates HOVER through extensive experiments in simulation using the retargeted AMASS dataset ($\hat{Q}$) and on a real-world Unitree H1 humanoid robot. The findings address three primary questions:

**Q1: Can HOVER as a generalist policy outperform policies trained for a specific command configuration?**
- **Simulation Performance vs. Specialists (Prior Work Modes):** As detailed in Table III and Figure 3, HOVER consistently demonstrated superior generalization compared to specialist policies trained specifically for prior work's control modes (ExBody, HumanPlus, H2O, OmniH2O). In each of these command modes, HOVER outperformed the corresponding specialist in at least 7 out of 12 reported metrics (e.g., joint error, global/local body position error, root velocity/rotation error). This indicates that the distillation approach provides a more robust and versatile foundation, even when operating in a single, fixed control mode.
- **Simulation Performance vs. Specialists (Other Useful Modes):** Evaluation against specialists trained for additional specific modes (left-hand, right-hand, two-hands, and head modes) further supported this finding. Table IV shows that HOVER consistently outperformed these specialists in their respective tracked metrics.
- **Real-World Performance vs. Specialists:** Real-world experiments on the Unitree H1 robot, tracking 20 different standing motions, reinforced the simulation results. Table V indicates that HOVER surpassed specialist policies in 11 out of 12 metrics, demonstrating successful real-world tracking of complex motions, including root pitch and full-body kinematic running (Figure 5).

**Q2: Can HOVER outperform other methods of training a multi-mode humanoid controller?**
- **Comparison with Multi-Mode RL Baseline:** The paper compared HOVER with a multi-mode RL baseline that used the same command masking process but was trained from scratch with an RL objective, without distillation from an oracle. As shown in Figure 4, HOVER achieved consistently lower tracking error across all 32 evaluated metrics and eight distinct control modes. This result highlights the importance of policy distillation from an oracle that tracks full-body kinematics, demonstrating its effectiveness in learning a more competent generalist whole-body controller compared to direct multi-mode RL training. The performance improvement is attributed to the oracle providing strong motor priors that benefit generalization across modes.

**Q3: Can HOVER transfer to real-world hardware and execute versatile multi-mode control?**
- **Real-World Deployment and Tracking:** HOVER was successfully deployed on a 19-DOF Unitree H1 humanoid robot. Quantitative evaluations on standing motions (Table V) confirmed its ability to accurately track diverse motions in a physical setting. The robot successfully tracked complex full-body kinematic motions, including dynamic running, and root pitch motion (Figure 5).
- **Seamless Multi-Mode Transitions:** HOVER demonstrated robustness to abrupt command mode switches during locomotion. As illustrated in Figure 6, the robot successfully transitioned between modes such as ExBody to H2O during forward walking, and HumanPlus to OmniH2O during turning and backward walking.
- **Teleoperation with Robustness to Occlusions:** A real-time teleoperation demo using Vision Pro showcased HOVER's ability to handle randomized masking of command inputs (e.g., tracking only head position while ignoring hand movements). This demonstrates the controller's robustness in scenarios with partial or occluded reference motions, crucial for real-world applications.

Overall, the findings indicate that HOVER, through its policy distillation framework and versatile command space design, achieves both strong multi-mode control capabilities and competitive performance against single-mode specialist controllers in both simulation and real-world environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of HOVER represents a notable advancement in humanoid whole-body control, with several potential areas of significance and impact:

- **Unification of Control Paradigms:** HOVER's primary contribution is its ability to consolidate diverse, previously specialized control modes into a single, unified neural policy. This moves away from the fragmented approach of training individual controllers for distinct tasks (e.g., locomotion, manipulation, teleoperation), offering a more integrated and holistic control solution for humanoid robots.
- **Enhanced Versatility and Adaptability:** By supporting over 15 control modes and enabling seamless transitions between them, HOVER significantly enhances the versatility and adaptability of humanoid robots. This allows a robot to dynamically adjust its control strategy based on changing task requirements or environmental conditions without needing to load or switch to a different policy. Such adaptability is crucial for deploying humanoids in complex, unstructured real-world environments.
- **Improved Performance and Generalization:** The research demonstrates that HOVER not only supports multiple modes but also outperforms specialist policies trained individually for specific modes. This superior performance is attributed to the policy leveraging shared physical knowledge and motor priors acquired from large-scale human motion data. This suggests that a generalist approach, built on a robust foundation of human-like motor skills, can lead to more effective and robust control across a spectrum of tasks.
- **Increased Efficiency in Development and Deployment:** By eliminating the need for retraining policies for each new control mode, HOVER can substantially reduce the development time and computational resources required for humanoid robot applications. This efficiency makes it more practical to expand the capabilities of humanoid robots to new and evolving tasks.
- **Robustness in Real-World Applications:** The successful transfer of HOVER to a real humanoid robot (Unitree H1) and its demonstrated robustness during mode switches and teleoperation with potentially incomplete command inputs (e.g., masked body parts) indicate its readiness for practical applications. This includes scenarios where sensors might be occluded or where complex, multi-stage tasks require dynamic adaptation of control objectives.
- **Foundation for Future Autonomous Capabilities:** The framework provides a strong foundation for future research, particularly in developing automated mode-switching modules. Such modules could enable humanoids to autonomously select the most appropriate control mode based on task context, leading to more intelligent and self-sufficient robotic systems. This could also pave the way for more intuitive and flexible interfaces for human-robot interaction, such as advanced teleoperation or programming by demonstration.
- **Scalability for Complex Humanoid Tasks:** As humanoid robots are envisioned for increasingly complex tasks involving loco-manipulation, interaction with dynamic environments, and collaboration with humans, a versatile and unified control architecture like HOVER becomes essential for scalability and practical deployment.
