# Hidden in Plain Sight: Diffusion-Based Unrestricted Robotic Attacks on Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> DURA is a targeted adversarial patch attack on vision-language-action robot policies. Instead of optimizing pixels, it noises a benign seed patch into a latent diffusion model and walks the DDIM trajectory, injecting an attack update each step while a clean anchor latent keeps the patch natural-looking. It works white-box via gradients or black-box via action-output queries, reaching 79.3-100% attack success on OpenVLA and pi0-FAST.

## Problem

- Existing VLA visual attacks produce noise-like or conspicuous patches that look nothing like scene content, so they are easy to spot in real workspaces.
- Most prior VLA attacks assume privileged white-box access to parameters, gradients, or logits, which closed-source deployed robotic systems rarely expose.
- Pixel-space and transfer-based attacks need lengthy per-instance optimization or surrogate models, costing hours of compute and degrading across architectures and action representations.

## Method

- ==Diffusion-guided patch optimization==: encode a benign seed patch, partially noise it to an intermediate timestep, then denoise with ==DDIM== while a clean anchor trajectory mixes in at weight 0.2 to hold naturalness. This is the core of **DURA**.
- White-box direction estimation backpropagates a targeted ==action loss== through the victim policy and ==VAE decoder== at each denoising step, avoiding backprop through the whole DDIM trajectory.
- Black-box direction estimation uses a ==score-function estimator== over K one-step forward-noised latent samples, scoring candidates only by the victim's returned action sequence, with a ==variance-reduction baseline==.

## Results

- On OpenVLA across four LIBERO suites, DURA hits **100%** white-box attack success and **86.0%** black-box with simulated patches, beating the query-based baseline by **43.7** points.
- On pi0-FAST, DURA reaches **100%** success in both settings versus **58.0%** and **31.3%** for the strongest baselines, showing transfer across discrete-token and action-chunk architectures.
- Attacks survive input-transformation defenses at **90-100%** success, and patches covering only **2%** of image area already reach **77%** success, saturating near **5%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Searching along a frozen diffusion model's latent trajectory gives adversarial optimization a natural-image prior for free, trading conspicuous artifacts for semantically plausible patch content.
> - Restricting black-box query sampling to the diffusion prior's own noise distribution makes each query far more informative than generic pixel-space estimation at equal budget.
> - Input-transformation defenses assume attacks look like high-frequency noise; naturally-structured adversarial content passes through them, so ordinary workspace objects become an attack surface.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an advancing paradigm in general-purpose robotic control. These models integrate visual perception, language comprehension, and action prediction into a unified policy, enabling robots to execute diverse manipulation tasks based on image observations, robot states, and natural language instructions. Representative systems such as RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, Octo, π0, and SpatialVLA have demonstrated increasing capabilities in task generalization, embodiment, and action representation, moving VLA models closer to deployment in real-world, open-ended robotic systems.

However, the increasing autonomy and deployment potential of VLA models introduce critical safety considerations. The broader research landscape in adversarial machine learning has shown that deep learning models, including those in computer vision and natural language processing, are susceptible to adversarial attacks. This vulnerability extends to robotic systems, where carefully crafted inputs can lead to disrupted execution, policy deviation, or unintended target behaviors, potentially causing physical harm.

Prior work on adversarial attacks against VLA models has explored both visual and language channels. Visual attacks have included pixel-space perturbations and patch-based methods aimed at disrupting or steering robot actions, such as UMA, UPA, UADA, TMA, FreezeVLA, and transferable attacks like UPA-RFAS, EDPA, VLA-Hijack, and TRAP. Language-based attacks involve adversarial instructions, as explored by Jones et al., and agentic black-box attacks like SABER.

Despite these advancements, existing visual attacks on VLA models often present several limitations that restrict their real-world applicability:
- **Conspicuous Artifacts:** Many methods generate pixel-space perturbations or patches that are visually distinct or appear as meaningless noise, making them easily detectable in physical environments.
- **White-box Access:** A significant portion of these attacks rely on privileged access to the victim model's internal parameters, gradients, or logits. Such access is typically unavailable for closed-source or deployed robotic systems.
- **Time Efficiency:** Crafting adversarial examples often requires extensive per-instance optimization, incurring high computational or query costs, which limits practical deployment.

Concurrently, diffusion models have emerged as powerful generative priors for synthesizing natural images and have been leveraged to create naturalistic adversarial examples in other domains, such as vision-language models (e.g., AdvDiffVLM). However, adapting diffusion priors for VLA models presents unique challenges because target robot actions lack a direct visual semantic representation, and successful attacks require consistent behavior across evolving scene dynamics.

This work, DURA (Diffusion-Based Unrestricted Robotic Attack), positions itself within this landscape by addressing the aforementioned limitations. It aims to develop a method for generating visually natural, black-box deployable, and computationally efficient adversarial patches specifically for VLA models, thereby exposing a more realistic and concerning safety risk for physically deployed robotic systems. By focusing on unrestricted, diffusion-guided patches, DURA extends the concept of natural adversarial examples to the complex domain of robotic control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, visually natural, and deployable adversarial patch attack for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This objective is driven by several key motivations and aims to address specific shortcomings of existing adversarial attack methodologies in the context of robotics:

1. **Expose Underexplored Adversarial Robustness:** VLA models are rapidly moving towards real-world deployment for diverse manipulation tasks. However, their adversarial robustness, particularly against sophisticated visual attacks, remains largely underexplored. The research is motivated by the understanding that exploiting such weaknesses can lead to tangible physical-world harm if robots operate unpredictably or maliciously.

2. **Enhance Visual Naturalness of Adversarial Patches:** Existing visual attacks on VLA models often produce patches that appear as conspicuous artifacts or noise, making them easy to detect and mitigate in real-world environments. A central motivation is to generate adversarial patches that are visually natural and semantically plausible, blending seamlessly into the scene. This "hidden in plain sight" characteristic is crucial for realistic deployment of attacks and for assessing a more practical safety risk.

3. **Achieve Black-box Deployability:** Many powerful adversarial attacks necessitate white-box access, requiring knowledge of model parameters, gradients, or logits. This level of access is rarely available for proprietary or closed-source robotic systems. A key motivation is to enable effective attacks under a black-box threat model, where the attacker can only observe the predicted actions of the victim model. This significantly broadens the scope of real-world applicability for such attacks. The method also aims to support white-box settings when such access is available.

4. **Improve Time Efficiency:** Current adversarial patch generation methods often involve lengthy per-instance optimization, which can be computationally expensive and time-consuming. The research aims to develop a more efficient attack framework that can generate effective adversarial patches without prohibitive query or compute costs, thus increasing its practical utility.

5. **Demonstrate Real-World Effectiveness:** The ultimate motivation is to demonstrate that these advanced, visually natural, and black-box attacks can translate from simulation to the real physical world. This involves evaluating the attack's ability to reliably steer a physical robot arm towards attacker-specified target actions under real imaging conditions (e.g., printing artifacts, lighting variations, camera perspective).

6. **Highlight a Critical Safety Risk for Embodied AI:** By demonstrating that adversarial control can be embedded in visually ordinary workspace content, the research aims to expose a significant and underappreciated safety risk for embodied AI systems. The findings are intended to serve as a call to action for the development of stronger VLA defenses that specifically account for natural-looking, physically placeable adversarial content, moving beyond defenses against conspicuous or noise-like perturbations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DURA (Diffusion-Based Unrestricted Robotic Attack) framework is designed to generate visually natural, localized adversarial patches that steer Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models towards attacker-specified target actions, operating effectively in both white-box and black-box settings.

**Threat Model:**
The study focuses on targeted physical adversarial patch attacks. The attacker is assumed to be able to place a single localized patch within the robot's field of view. Critical constraints include the inability to modify model parameters, training data, robot state, or language instructions during inference. The patch content is "unrestricted" in its pixel values but confined to a limited, physically realizable image region. The attack's objective is to consistently drive the victim VLA policy to an attacker-specified target action whenever the patch is visible. Two access modes are considered:
- **White-box access:** Gradients of the victim policy are available.
- **Black-box access:** Only the predicted action sequence from the victim policy is observable.

**Problem Formulation:**
Let π_θ be the victim VLA policy, which outputs an action sequence **a** given a visual observation **x** and language instruction **ℓ**. The attacker defines a target action sequence **a**^⋆. An adversarial patch **p** is rendered into clean scene frames {**o**_b} using a compositing operator C, resulting in patched inputs **x**~_b(**p**). This operator accounts for varying patch poses and placements across observations.
The patch **p** is optimized using a targeted batch objective:
$$L_{attack}(p) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \sum_{i \in I} w_i L_i(y_{b,i}(p), a^{\star}_i)$$
where *I* indexes attacked action dimensions, *w_i* are weights, and *y_b(p)* is the victim policy's output for the patched input. The form of *y_b(p)* and the per-dimension loss *L_i* vary by access mode:
- **White-box:** *y_b(p)* denotes differentiable action-token outputs (e.g., logits), and *L_i* is the targeted cross-entropy (CE) loss.
- **Black-box:** *y_b(p)* represents the non-differentiable, executable action sequence *Q_θ*, and *L_i* is the squared L2 norm between the predicted and target actions.

**Diffusion-based Unrestricted Robotic Attacks (DURA):**
DURA formulates patch generation as an attack-guided denoising process within the latent space of a frozen pretrained latent diffusion model (LDM), comprising a VAE encoder (E), VAE decoder (D), and a frozen denoiser (ˆϵ_ϕ).

1. **Diffusion-guided Patch Optimization:**
 - **Initialization:** A benign seed patch *p_clean* is encoded into the latent space: *z_clean_0* = E(*p_clean*). This latent is then partially noised to an intermediate timestep *t_start*, yielding *z_adv_t_start*, which serves as the starting point for the adversarial denoising trajectory. This retains some natural structure while allowing for adversarial manipulation.
 - **Clean Anchor Trajectory:** An unperturbed DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) pass is run from *z_clean_0* to precompute a clean anchor trajectory {*z_anc_t*}. This trajectory guides the optimization process to maintain visual naturalness.
 - **Iterative Denoising and Steering:** DURA iterates through denoising steps from *t_start* down to 1. In each step:
 - The current adversarial latent *z_adv_t* is softly mixed with the clean anchor *z_anc_t*: *z_mix_t* = (1 − *α_w*) *z_adv_t* + *α_w* *z_anc_t* (*α_w* is anchor strength).
 - One DDIM denoising step is applied: *u_t-1* = DDIM(*z_mix_t*, *t*; ˆϵ_ϕ).
 - An adversarial update is injected: *z_adv_t-1* = *u_t-1* − *s* ⋅ *g_t*, where *s* is the attack step size and *g_t* is the latent attack direction. The DDIM step aligns with the generative prior, while the adversarial update steers the decoded patch towards the target robot action.

2. **Attack Direction Estimation:** The method for determining *g_t* varies by access mode:
 - **White-box Setting:** *g_WB_t* = ∇_u_t-1 *L_attack*(D(*u_t-1*)). This is computed by backpropagating the targeted VLA loss through the VAE decoder D(*u_t-1*), the patch rendering process, and the victim VLA policy.
 - **Black-box Setting:** Gradients are unavailable. DURA employs a score-function estimator. It samples *K* perturbations (*ϵ_k*) around the current denoised latent *u* = *u_t-1* to generate *K* query latents *z_t,k*. These are decoded, rendered into patches, and used to query the victim policy for losses *L^(k)_attack*. The attack direction *g_BB_t* is estimated as:
 $$g_{BB_t} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} [L_{attack}^{(k)} - b] \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}} \epsilon_k$$
 where *b* is a variance-reduction baseline.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Datasets:** LIBERO (simulation benchmark) and BridgeData V2 (real-world data for physical patch generation).
- **VLA Models:** OpenVLA (7B) and π_0-FAST, covering discrete action tokens and action chunks respectively.
- **Attack Configuration:** Targeted attacks; patches optimized over batches of frames from multiple rollouts to ensure generalization.
- **Baselines:** White-box (UMA, UPA, UADA, TMA, FreezeVLA) and black-box (TMA-NES, UPA-RFAS). TMA-NES adapted from TMA for query-based black-box access.
- **Patch Generation Settings:** "Simulated" patches (OpenVLA-7B trained on LIBERO) and "Physical" patches (OpenVLA-7B trained on BridgeData V2).
- **Real-world Evaluation:** A Franka robot arm, using printed DURA patches, observing behavior with a fixed RGB camera.
- **Implementation Details:** *t_start* = 0.5, 200 DDIM steps, anchor weight *α_w* = 0.2, target-action loss weights for translation/rotation/gripper, *K* = 2048 queries/update for black-box.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Attack Success Rate (ASR):** Percentage of rollouts where the attacked policy fails to complete the task. Higher ASR indicates stronger task disruption.
 - **Attack Precision (AP):** Quantifies consistency between executed behavior and attacker-specified target action (e.g., freezing or moving forward). Higher AP indicates more consistent targeted steering.
 - **Visual Naturalness:** Assessed qualitatively and quantitatively using metrics like NIQE, CLIP-Natural, DISTS, SSIM, BSE (Boundary Seam Energy), and Patch TV (Total Variation).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted both in simulation and the real physical world demonstrated DURA's effectiveness across various settings and against different VLA models, while also addressing limitations of previous attack methods regarding naturalness, deployability, and efficiency.

**Quantitative Results (Attack Success Rate - ASR):**

- **OpenVLA-7B on LIBERO:**
 - **White-box Access:** DURA consistently achieved 100% ASR across all four LIBERO task suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long) for both simulated and physical patch generation settings. This performance matched the strongest label-supervised baseline (UADA) and surpassed other target-only attacks like TMA and FreezeVLA, despite DURA being a target-only method.
 - **Black-box Access:** DURA achieved an average ASR of 86.0% with simulated patches and 79.3% with physical patches. This significantly outperformed the black-box baseline TMA-NES, improving ASR by 43.7 and 40.0 percentage points respectively. The baseline clean policy and clean patch exhibited much lower ASRs (23.5% and 39.5%), confirming that DURA's failures were due to adversarial manipulation.
- **π_0-FAST on LIBERO:**
 - DURA achieved 100% ASR across all four LIBERO task suites in both white-box and black-box settings. This represented a substantial improvement over the strongest baselines, which reached only 58.0% ASR in white-box (TMA) and 31.3% / 26.0% ASR in black-box (TMA-NES / UPA-RFAS). These results indicate DURA's transferability and effectiveness across VLA models with different action representations.

**Quantitative Results (Attack Precision - AP):**

- DURA achieved high Attack Precision (AP) in targeted attacks. Under white-box access, DURA attained an average AP of 82.0% (ranging 61-99% across suites), and under black-box access, it achieved 67.3% (ranging 48-84%). In contrast, the clean policy only yielded an AP of 25.0%. This indicates that DURA's induced failures stemmed from consistent steering towards the intended target action, rather than arbitrary task degradation.

**Real-Robot Results:**

- Experiments on a physical Franka robot arm in a "place bread slice into basket" task demonstrated DURA's real-world efficacy. A printed adversarial patch, when placed in the camera's view, caused the robot to deviate from its intended path and execute attacker-specified actions, such as freezing or moving forward erroneously. Removing the patch allowed the robot to resume normal operation, and reinserting it reinstated the adversarial behavior. This indicated that the attack was controllable and repeatable on physical hardware.

**Visual Naturalness:**

- Qualitative comparisons (Figure 4) showed DURA patches to be visually more coherent and semantically recognizable compared to the noise-like or irregular patterns generated by pixel-space baselines (UADA, UPA, TMA).
- Quantitative metrics (NIQE, CLIP-Natural, DISTS, SSIM, BSE, Patch TV) further supported these observations (Figure 5). DURA patches exhibited better local naturalness and fewer visible artifacts, remaining closer to the "no-patch" clean scene reference across perceptual similarity and local artifact measurements. Pixel-space attacks typically introduced high-frequency textures and noticeable boundary seams, which DURA's diffusion prior avoided.

**Query Budget (Black-box):**

- DURA demonstrated effective attacks with moderate query budgets. For example, it achieved an ASR of 88% with K=512 queries per update, and further improved to 100% with K=2048 queries. In contrast, TMA-NES maintained a low ASR (40-48%) even with increased query budgets, suggesting that DURA utilized the query budget more efficiently by optimizing within a diffusion-guided latent space.

**Time Efficiency:**

- DURA exhibited a more favorable time-ASR trade-off compared to pixel-space optimization baselines (Figure 7). In the white-box setting, DURA achieved high ASR within minutes, whereas baselines like TMA, UPA, and UADA required hours for comparable success rates. In the black-box setting, DURA achieved higher ASRs with longer optimization times, consistently outperforming TMA-NES, which remained less effective even with extended runtimes.

**Impact of Victim Model:**

- The consistent high performance of DURA on both OpenVLA (discrete action tokens) and π_0-FAST (action chunks) indicated that the attack method generalizes effectively across different VLA models and action architectural formulations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Hidden in Plain Sight: Diffusion-Based Unrestricted Robotic Attacks on Vision-Language-Action Models" carries significant implications for the safety, security, and development of embodied AI systems.

**Exposure of a Critical Safety Risk:**
The most immediate and impactful finding is the demonstration that Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are vulnerable to visually natural, localized, and deployable adversarial attacks. Unlike previous attacks that often rely on conspicuous noise or pixel-level perturbations, DURA shows that adversarial control can be subtly embedded within ordinary, natural-looking workspace content. This reveals a critical safety risk: the physical workspace itself can become an attack surface, where an innocuous-looking object (a printed patch) can covertly hijack robot behavior. This shifts the perception of adversarial threats from theoretical vulnerabilities to practical, real-world hazards for physically deployed robots.

**Advancement in Adversarial Machine Learning for Robotics:**
DURA introduces a novel and effective methodology for generating unrestricted adversarial patches. By leveraging the strong generative priors of diffusion models and optimizing along latent trajectories, the method achieves a balance of attack effectiveness, visual naturalness, and computational efficiency that surpasses existing pixel-space and query-based techniques. This work pushes the boundaries of adversarial attacks in robotics by:
- **Enhancing Stealth:** Generating patches that are difficult for human observers to detect, increasing the practicality of attacks in real-world scenarios.
- **Enabling Black-box Attacks:** Providing a robust framework for scenarios where internal model access is unavailable, which is common for commercial or closed-source robotic systems.
- **Demonstrating Real-world Transferability:** Validating the attack's effectiveness on a physical robot arm using printed patches, bridging the gap between simulated vulnerabilities and tangible physical-world risks.

**Implications for VLA Model Development and Deployment:**
The findings underscore an urgent need for VLA model developers and researchers to prioritize adversarial robustness. Current defense strategies that primarily target conspicuous artifacts or minor pixel perturbations may be insufficient against attacks like DURA. The paper implicitly calls for new research directions in:
- **Robustness Evaluation:** Developing benchmarks and methodologies to evaluate VLA models against naturalistic, physically realizable adversarial content.
- **Novel Defense Mechanisms:** Engineering robust defenses that can detect or mitigate visually subtle and context-aware adversarial manipulations. This might involve perceptual anomaly detection, robust perception pipelines, or adversarial training incorporating diffusion-generated examples.
- **Safety Protocols:** Re-evaluating existing safety protocols for robot deployment, especially in open-world environments where malicious patches could be introduced.

**Broader Impact on Embodied AI Security:**
This research contributes to a broader understanding of security vulnerabilities in embodied AI. As generalist robotic agents become more autonomous and interact with complex, unstructured environments, the ability of an attacker to subtly influence their behavior poses a significant threat to their reliability and trustworthiness. The work provides a strong motivation for a holistic approach to robot security, encompassing not just software vulnerabilities but also the interaction of AI with the physical world through sensory inputs. Ultimately, DURA highlights the critical importance of designing AI systems that are not only capable but also robust and trustworthy in the face of sophisticated and stealthy adversarial manipulations.
