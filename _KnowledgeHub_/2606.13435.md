# GIVE: Grounding Human Gestures in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Nanyang Technological University developed GIVE, a method to integrate human gestures into pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models without architectural modifications. This approach, which uses dual visual-semantic guidance, achieved an 80.0% success rate across manipulation stages in real-world human-robot interaction tasks, demonstrating improved robustness and generalization.

## Problem

- Contemporary VLA models are predominantly text-driven, overlooking crucial non-verbal cues like gestures, which convey a significant portion of human intent (estimated at 65-70%).
- Relying solely on text for inferring human intent often leads to misinterpretations and execution failures when language instructions are ambiguous or underspecified.
- Existing gesture integration methods either lack comprehensive semantic understanding (vision-based) or precise spatial anchoring (text-based reasoning), hindering robust intent interpretation.

## Method

- **GIVE** (Gesture Intent via Visual-Semantic Enhancement) introduces a dual-path ==visual-semantic== guidance mechanism to model human intent and integrate it into pre-trained VLA models without architectural modifications.
- The Visual Gesture Prompting pathway augments raw visual observations by overlaying hand skeletons and index-finger pointing rays directly onto the 2D image plane for precise spatial grounding of manipulation targets.
- The Semantic Intent Parsing pathway utilizes a VLM to translate visual gesture cues into high-level, object-agnostic textual descriptions for task semantics and temporal state transitions, which are then appended to original text instructions.

## Results

- GIVE achieved an **86.7%** Identify Success Rate and an **80.0%** success rate across all subsequent stages (Grasp, React, Handover) in real-world human-robot interaction tasks, significantly outperforming the baseline's **46.7%** Identify SR and **6.7%** Grasp SR.
- An ablation study demonstrated that the visual overlay strategy for incorporating gesture cues (skeleton and fingertip rays) achieved the highest Identify SR of **70.0%**, superior to token-based injection methods.
- The system showed robustness across unseen spatial layouts and generalized to new human participants, maintaining consistent identification success rates due to its explicit hand pose estimation and visual guidance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human communication is inherently multimodal, and explicitly incorporating non-verbal cues like gestures is crucial for robust human-robot interaction beyond purely text-driven systems.
> - Precise spatial grounding of objects is effectively achieved through direct visual prompts (like overlaid skeletons and pointing rays), while higher-level task semantics and temporal intent understanding benefit from textual descriptions generated by VLMs.
> - Architecturally efficient integration of new modalities into pre-trained VLA models can be achieved by augmenting existing input channels (visual overlays, text concatenation) without modifying the core network, preserving learned representations and reducing computational overhead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Enabling robots to interact seamlessly with humans and comprehend their intentions represents a fundamental objective within embodied artificial intelligence. Human communication is inherently multimodal, frequently complementing verbal language with non-verbal cues such as gaze, posture, and gestures to convey intent. Among these, gestures are considered particularly important, offering a direct and physically grounded channel for expressing intentions, such as pointing to specify an object or an open palm for a handover request.

Despite the recognized significance of non-verbal communication, contemporary Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which have emerged as a paradigm in robotic manipulation, primarily operate as text-driven systems. This approach overlooks the multimodal nature of human interaction, where a substantial portion of meaning (estimated at 65% to 70%) is conveyed through non-verbal channels. Cognitive linguistics further suggests a tight coupling between speech and gestures, reflecting an underlying unified intention. Consequently, VLA models that rely solely on text for inferring human intent often struggle with accurately grounding intentions and performing reliable manipulation, especially when language instructions are ambiguous, underspecified, or when dynamic interaction intents need to be inferred from subtle cues. This limitation can lead to intent misinterpretations and execution failures in real-world human-robot interaction (HRI) scenarios.

Existing gesture-based approaches to intent grounding generally fall into two categories: vision-based prompting and text-based reasoning. Vision-based methods utilize visual information like hand keypoints, pointing rays, or segmentation masks for spatial grounding but frequently lack a comprehensive semantic understanding of task phases. Conversely, text-based reasoning approaches leverage large language models (LLMs) or vision-language models (VLMs) for higher-level intention reasoning but often lack the precision required for spatially anchoring gestures to physical objects or dynamic robot behaviors. The current work positions itself to bridge these gaps by developing a method that integrates both visual and semantic cues to enhance gesture understanding in VLA models, thereby providing both precise spatial grounding and high-level intent interpretation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models with human gesture understanding to improve the reliability and effectiveness of human-robot interaction (HRI). The authors aim to enable VLA policies to better associate human gestures with robotic manipulation behaviors and adapt to dynamic interaction intents.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations regarding the limitations of existing VLA models in HRI contexts:
1. **Multimodal Nature of Human Communication:** Human interaction is inherently multimodal, where non-verbal cues like gestures complement verbal instructions. Current VLA models predominantly treat robotic manipulation as a purely text-driven task, neglecting these crucial non-verbal signals.
2. **Ambiguity in Text-Only Instructions:** When language instructions are ambiguous or underspecified, relying solely on text for intent grounding often leads to misinterpretations and unreliable manipulation actions by robots. Gestures, such as pointing, can provide immediate and precise clarification of target objects and interaction states.
3. **Limitations of Existing Gesture Integration Methods:** Prior approaches for incorporating gestures often either lack semantic understanding (vision-based methods) or precise spatial anchoring (text-based reasoning methods), hindering comprehensive intent interpretation.
4. **Architectural Constraints of Pre-trained VLA Models:** Integrating new modalities like gestures into powerful pre-trained VLA models can be challenging, often requiring architectural modifications or extensive retraining that may disrupt their learned representations and increase computational overhead.

To address these challenges, the authors propose GIVE (Gesture Intent via Visual-Semantic Enhancement), which aims to inject human gesture information into pre-trained VLA models without necessitating architectural modifications. The core insight is that visual cues provide precise object grounding, while semantic cues capture high-level intent transitions driven by gesture changes. By jointly leveraging these complementary modalities, GIVE seeks to provide robust and adaptable guidance for robotic actions.

Specifically, the contributions highlighted by the authors are:
1. **A Simple and Effective Method:** Introducing GIVE as a method to integrate human gesture cues into pre-trained VLA models without modifying their core architecture, thereby enabling effective human intent grounding.
2. **Dual-Path Gesture Guidance Strategy:** Developing a strategy that transforms human gestures into complementary semantic and visual guidance, providing structured multimodal conditioning for robust action generation.
3. **Extensive Real-World Validation:** Demonstrating consistent performance improvements over baselines in real-world HRI experiments using a dual-arm robot, while also showing robustness and generalization across unseen spatial layouts and human participants.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research addresses the problem of enhancing robotic manipulation policies in human-robot interaction (HRI) by incorporating human gestures. The proposed method, GIVE, introduces a dual-path visual-semantic guidance mechanism to model human intent, integrating it into pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models without architectural modifications.

**Problem Formulation:** The system operates in a sequential grasp-then-handover HRI task with multiple stages (target indication, object grasping, object delivery). At each timestep, the robot receives multi-view visual observations (global and wrist cameras) and a language instruction. A control policy (π_θ) is tasked with outputting a continuous 6-DoF end-effector pose and a 1-D gripper command. The central challenge is to identify human intent from interactive gestures, including object specification and appropriate handover responses.

**GIVE Overview:** GIVE operates through two main pathways:
1. **Visual Gesture Prompting:** Augments raw visual observations with explicit gesture information (V).
2. **Semantic Intent Parsing:** Generates high-level textual descriptions of human gestures and task instructions (S).
These augmented visual inputs (V) and semantic textual inputs (S) are then combined and fed into a pre-trained VLA model for action generation.

#### 3.1. Visual Gesture Prompting

This pathway aims to provide precise spatial grounding of manipulation targets, particularly when language instructions are ambiguous or multiple candidate objects are present.
- **Gesture Pointing Ray:** Given a third-person global observation, a hand pose estimator (Pavlakos et al., 2024) extracts 2D joint keypoints to represent gesture intent (e.g., pointing, receiving). To resolve pointing ambiguity, especially when hands are distant or objects are clustered, an index-finger pointing ray is constructed. This ray extends the local finger direction into the workspace based on the base, middle, and fingertip keypoints of the index finger, using an extension factor (K) to provide a clearer geometric cue for the target object.
- **Visual Overlay:** Unlike methods that convert hand poses into abstract embeddings or tokens, GIVE projects the hand skeleton and the pointing ray directly onto the 2D image plane and renders them as overlaid visual prompts. This augmented observation (V) preserves the original visual structure while explicitly encoding the spatial relationship between the gesture and candidate objects. This design avoids modifying the pre-trained token space of the robot foundation model and additional cross-modal alignment networks, which the authors suggest can disrupt pre-trained representations.

#### 3.2. Semantic Intent Parsing

While visual prompting aids spatial grounding, VLA models require textual instructions for higher-level task semantics and intent understanding, especially for temporal state transitions (e.g., waiting for a handover).
- **VLM Integration:** A pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically Gemini 2.0 Flash API (Gemini Team et al., 2023), is employed to translate visual gesture cues into high-level semantic guidance and execution instructions.
- **Stable-State Triggering:** To prevent high-frequency VLM inference from bottlenecking the continuous control loop, the VLM is invoked only when the estimated human hand gesture remains stable for 1.0 second. This query occurs once at the beginning of each task phase, and the generated semantic result is cached and reused throughout that phase.
- **Structured Semantic Output:** The VLM outputs a structured semantic tuple S = ⟨T_ges, T_task⟩, where T_ges describes the human gesture (e.g., "pointing at target object") and T_task provides robot execution instructions (e.g., "Pick up... and wait").
- **Object-Agnostic Design:** The textual descriptions are intentionally object-agnostic, using generic terms like "target object." This design choice mitigates potential VLM hallucination or misclassification of specific object categories and addresses spatial ambiguity when multiple visually identical objects are present. By abstracting object identity from the semantic pathway, precise object grounding is delegated entirely to the explicit visual prompts.

#### 3.3. Action Generation

The robot's continuous control policy (π_θ) integrates these intent priors with a pre-trained VLA backbone, specifically the π_0.5 model.
- **Direct Latent Concatenation:** To avoid architectural modifications, the semantic tuple (S) is appended to the original text instruction, and the geometric visual prompt (V) is overlaid onto the global observation.
- **Multimodal Representation:** These augmented inputs are then processed by the VLM backbone to produce a composite multimodal representation (Z).
- **Flow Matching Objective:** Conditioned on Z, the policy generates actions using a Flow Matching objective, which regresses the vector field of the action distribution to decode cross-modal features into final action trajectories.

#### 3.4. Experimental Details

- **Task:** A two-phase sequential HRI workflow: (i) Phase 1 (Pointing & Picking): Human points to a target object, robot identifies and grasps it. (ii) Phase 2 (Response & Handover): Robot monitors human hand, waits for an "open palm" gesture, then plans and executes a handover.
- **Hardware:** Galaxea R1-Lite wheeled robot with two 6-DoF arms. Dual-perspective observations from a body-mounted camera (global) and a wrist-mounted camera (egocentric).
- **Environment:** Three target objects (peach, mango, carrot) tested across three distinct spatial locations to evaluate spatial robustness.
- **Training:** Full-parameter fine-tuning of the pre-trained π_0.5 model on a dataset of 270 real-world human-robot demonstration episodes. AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10^-8, global batch size of 64, for approximately 26 hours on 4 NVIDIA A800 GPUs.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Four conditional success rates (SR): Identify SR (correctly moving toward target), Grasp SR (stable grasp), React SR (initiating movement after gesture switch), Handover SR (accurate delivery/release).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate the contribution of GIVE's visual and semantic pathways, its ability to resolve spatial ambiguity, and its robustness and generalization capabilities across unseen spatial layouts and participants.

#### 4.1. Performance and Ablation (Addressing Q1)

- **Overall Performance:** The pure end-to-end baseline (π_0.5) demonstrated limited performance, achieving only 46.7% Identify SR and 6.7% Grasp SR, indicating unreliable target localization and difficulty in handling dynamic interactions. GIVE, by integrating visual geometric cues and semantic instructions, achieved an 86.7% Identify SR and maintained an 80.0% success rate across all subsequent stages (Grasp, React, and Handover), demonstrating more robust performance in dynamic HRI.
- **Gesture Keypoint Integration Strategies (Fig. 4):** An ablation study on how gesture cues are incorporated revealed that the visual overlay strategy (skeleton and fingertip rays) achieved the highest overall performance, with 70.0% Identify SR. Token-based variants, such as VLM-based token injection or injection into the action head, showed lower performance in downstream manipulation (Grasp SR of 33.3% and 6.7% respectively), suggesting that direct visual overlay better preserves pre-trained representations while extra tokenization may disrupt feature alignment.
- **Contribution of Dual Pathways (Table 2):**
 - Adding only visual geometric prompting (VGP) with keypoints improved Identify SR to 56.7% but showed limited gains in later stages.
 - Explicitly introducing fingertip-directed rays alongside keypoints (VGP with keypoints + ray) further boosted Identify SR to 70.0% and Grasp SR to 60.0%, highlighting its importance for early-stage grounding. However, it was insufficient for handling temporal state transitions (23.3% React SR).
 - The full GIVE model, combining VGP and Semantic Intent Parsing (SIP), achieved complementary improvements, leading to consistently high success rates across all stages (86.7% Identify SR, 80.0% Grasp, React, and Handover SR). This indicates that the semantic pathway is crucial for intent understanding and temporal state transitions.

#### 4.2. Rationale for Visual-Semantic Guidance (Addressing Q2)

To validate the necessity of explicit visual prompting for spatial grounding, a pre-trained VLM (Gemini) was used to parse scene descriptions from raw RGB images versus images with visual overlay.
- **VLM Parsing Results (Table 3):** Using raw RGB images, the VLM achieved 75.0% accuracy in Gesture Recognition and 80.0% in Execution Suggestion. However, its Target Grounding accuracy was only 40.0%. When explicit geometric cues were rendered onto the 2D observation space, Target Grounding accuracy significantly improved to 90.0%.
- **Conclusion:** These findings support the dual-path design, demonstrating that VLMs are effective for high-level task phase reasoning but less reliable for precise spatial anchoring of objects, which is effectively handled by explicit visual prompts. This informed the object-agnostic design of the semantic pathway, delegating precise spatial grounding to the visual pathway.

#### 4.3. Robustness and Generalization (Addressing Q3 & Q4)

- **Unseen Spatial Layouts (Table 4):** The baseline model showed significant performance degradation and high variance across different object locations and interactive objects. In contrast, GIVE maintained higher and more stable success rates across various challenging spatial setups (Locations A, B, and C with different objects), indicating enhanced robustness to spatial distribution shifts.
- **Unseen Human Participants (Table 5):** The baseline's identification capability deteriorated considerably when interacting with two previously unseen participants, suggesting sensitivity to variations in physical scale and appearance. GIVE achieved consistent identification success rates across the seen participant (8/10) and both unseen participants (8/10 for Pt. A, 6/10 for Pt. B), highlighting its generalization capabilities. This is attributed to the hand pose estimator providing an explicit hand skeleton for visual guidance, effectively decoupling intent reasoning from human visual appearance variations.

#### 4.4. Limitations

Despite its effectiveness, the authors noted limitations:
- The reliance on the accuracy of the hand pose estimator, which may degrade under challenging conditions like severe motion blur or occlusions.
- The use of a fixed extension scalar (K) for the pointing ray, which is not adaptive to scene depth and can lead to suboptimal or ambiguous pointing in environments with large depth variations or when the human hand is exceptionally far from the workspace.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents GIVE, a method that addresses a notable gap in current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by integrating human non-verbal cues, specifically gestures, into robotic manipulation policies. The work offers several points of significance and potential impact:

1. **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** By explicitly incorporating gesture understanding, GIVE enables robots to interpret human intentions more accurately and reliably, particularly when verbal instructions are ambiguous or underspecified. This leads to more natural, intuitive, and effective HRI, moving beyond purely text-driven interactions.
2. **Improved Intent Grounding and Task Success:** The dual-path approach, leveraging both visual and semantic guidance, allows for robust grounding of human intent, leading to a substantial increase in target object recognition accuracy and overall task success rates in complex manipulation scenarios like grasp-then-handover tasks.
3. **Architectural Efficiency:** A key aspect of GIVE is its ability to enhance pre-trained VLA models without requiring architectural modifications or additional learnable modules within the policy network. This design simplifies integration, reduces computational overhead, and preserves the foundational capabilities of existing VLA models, making the approach practical for real-world deployment.
4. **Robustness and Generalization:** The demonstrated robustness to unseen spatial layouts and generalization to diverse human participants (with varying physical characteristics) indicates that GIVE contributes to more adaptable and widely applicable robotic systems. This is particularly relevant for collaborative and assistive robotics where interactions with different users and dynamic environments are common.
5. **Complementary Multimodal Integration:** The insight that visual cues excel at precise object grounding while semantic cues are crucial for high-level intent transitions provides a principled framework for combining different modalities. This explicit decoupling and complementary integration could influence future designs of multimodal AI systems, particularly in embodied intelligence.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** While acknowledging limitations such as reliance on hand pose estimator accuracy and fixed ray extension, this work opens avenues for future research. Areas such as developing more robust gesture perception under occlusion and implementing depth-adaptive ray mechanisms could further enhance the system's capabilities.

Overall, GIVE represents a step towards more capable and context-aware robots that can better understand and respond to the nuances of human communication, thereby facilitating safer, more efficient, and more natural human-robot collaboration in various domains.
