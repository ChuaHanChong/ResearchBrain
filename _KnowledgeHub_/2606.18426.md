# VEGA: Learning Navigation VLAs from In-the-Wild Egocentric Video with Geometric Trajectory Supervision

> [!summary] Summary
> VEGA is a method for training navigation Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models using unlabeled egocentric videos with geometric trajectory supervision. It produces policies capable of robust obstacle avoidance and multimodal goal reaching by leveraging reconstructed scene geometry for rich training data.

## Problem

- General mobile robot navigation lacks large-scale, diverse datasets providing explicit goal-conditioned, obstacle-aware trajectories in cluttered, real-world environments.
- Abundant internet-scale egocentric videos are not directly usable for robot policy learning due to the absence of robot actions, explicit navigation goals, and obstacle-aware trajectories.
- Current navigation VLAs excel at semantic understanding but often lack the precise geometric reasoning needed for safe close-range obstacle avoidance, while classical planners lack open-vocabulary semantic capabilities.

## Method

- **VEGA** reconstructs dense 3D geometry, extracts obstacles and free space, and generates diverse multimodal goals (text, image-region, waypoint) from unlabeled egocentric video frames.
- A ==Model Predictive Path Integral== (MPPI) planner generates obstacle-aware, goal-conditioned waypoint trajectories for each scene using a Euclidean Signed Distance Field (ESDF) for safety guidance.
- A multimodal goal-conditioned VLA (based on {pi}₀.₅) is trained using a flow-matching action expert, leveraging a frozen vision-language backbone and learning continuous, multimodal action distributions from the generated trajectories.

## Results

- On the VEGA-Bench evaluation, VEGA reduced collisions by **33.0%** (**4.52%**) and improved obstacle clearance by **17.9%** (**2.77**) compared to state-of-the-art baselines.
- In real-world robot deployment, VEGA achieved at least a **150.0%** improvement in success rate, a **66.7%** reduction in collisions, and a **60.0%** improvement in obstacle clearance over VLA baselines.
- VEGA demonstrated strong multimodal goal reaching capabilities, achieving **1.0** success for text and image goals, and **0.8** for waypoint goals on VEGA-Bench, producing distinct trajectories for different goal specifications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Geometric reasoning and planning (e.g., ESDF, MPPI) can be used offline during training to effectively distill obstacle-aware behavior into a purely vision-based policy for real-time deployment.
> - Abundant, unlabeled egocentric videos, despite their inherent lack of robot actions or explicit goals, can be transformed into a rich source of geometric and semantic supervision for VLA navigation.
> - Employing multimodal goal anchoring and a flow-matching objective helps policies learn robust goal grounding and diverse action distributions, mitigating goal-conditioned mode collapse.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a research direction aimed at developing general-purpose robot control by enabling robots to interpret visual observations and language instructions into actions. This field leverages semantic knowledge derived from large-scale vision-language pretraining. Significant progress in VLA models has been observed in robotic manipulation, primarily due to the availability of large, diverse datasets tailored for various manipulation tasks and robot embodiments.

In contrast, mobile robot navigation faces a data scarcity challenge. Existing datasets for ground mobile robot navigation, while valuable for specific tasks like social navigation, visual goal reaching, or cross-embodiment learning, often lack the scale and granularity required for training general-purpose VLAs. Autonomous driving datasets provide large-scale navigation data, but they focus on structured driving environments and do not typically capture the close-range obstacle avoidance, object-centric goals, and cluttered indoor/outdoor scenarios that general mobile robots encounter. The limited supervision in these datasets often provides only a single implicit or explicit destination for a given trajectory, omitting the multitude of other semantically meaningful and physically reachable goals within the same scene. This sparsity also limits the coverage of dense, close-range clutter, where safe navigation requires adapting trajectories based on the specific goal, local obstacles, and available free space. Consequently, policies trained on such data may exhibit weak goal grounding, poor close-range obstacle avoidance, or unsafe trajectories in complex environments, potentially learning goal-agnostic shortcuts or overfitting to dominant traversal patterns.

Classical navigation systems, relying on explicit geometric reasoning through maps, distance fields, and trajectory optimization, can provide robust, geometry-aware behavior. However, these systems often require online mapping and do not inherently possess the open-vocabulary semantic reasoning capabilities that VLAs offer. There is therefore a need for methods that can integrate the semantic understanding of VLAs with the geometric safety and collision avoidance capabilities of classical planning approaches. Collecting sufficiently diverse and dense teleoperated robot data to learn this combined behavior directly is not considered scalable, as the data requirements for environments, goals, obstacle layouts, and feasible paths grow substantially. This research aims to address these challenges by leveraging widely available egocentric videos as a scalable source of navigation-relevant visual observations, transforming them into usable supervision for goal-conditioned mobile robot navigation.

Related work includes other VLA models for navigation like NaVILA and OmniVLA, which rely on existing navigation datasets and often employ single-target action supervision. Methods exploring learning navigation from egocentric videos, such as those using visual odometry to extract trajectory distributions or LeLaN which generates trajectory labels from a pre-trained navigation policy, have also been investigated. However, these prior approaches either do not explicitly account for multiple goal-conditioned, obstacle-aware trajectories within a single scene or are limited by the obstacle avoidance capabilities of their underlying pre-trained policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method, named VEGA (Vision-language navigation from Egocentric Video with Geometric Action supervision), for training navigation Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models using unlabeled egocentric navigation videos.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in the field of mobile robot navigation:
1. **Data Scarcity for General-Purpose VLAs:** Unlike manipulation tasks, general mobile robot navigation lacks large-scale, diverse datasets that provide explicit goal-conditioned, obstacle-aware trajectories in cluttered, real-world environments. Existing datasets are often sparse in terms of goal variety per scene and coverage of complex obstacle configurations.
2. **Limitations of Egocentric Video Data:** While internet-scale egocentric videos offer a scalable source of real-world visual observations, including cluttered scenes and natural human motion, they are not directly usable for robot policy learning. These videos lack robot actions, explicit navigation goals in a robot's coordinate frame, and obstacle-aware trajectories.
3. **Bridging Semantic Understanding and Geometric Safety:** Current navigation VLAs excel at semantic understanding (e.g., from language instructions) but often lack the precise geometric reasoning required for safe close-range obstacle avoidance in complex environments. Conversely, classical geometry-based planners ensure safety but lack open-vocabulary semantic capabilities. There is a need to combine these strengths without requiring computationally expensive online geometric processing at deployment.
4. **Addressing Deficiencies in Prior VLA Navigation:** Previous VLA navigation models typically rely on datasets with sparse goal-conditioned supervision, meaning a single observed trajectory for a scene. This can lead to policies that struggle with diverse goal conditions, exhibit "mode collapse" (producing similar paths for different goals), or have limited capacity for fine-grained obstacle avoidance in dense clutter. Furthermore, single-target action supervision might not fully capture the multimodal nature of feasible action distributions (e.g., multiple paths around an obstacle).
5. **Scalability of Supervision:** Traditional methods of acquiring high-quality navigation data, such as teleoperated robot demonstrations or manual action labeling, are costly and do not scale efficiently across numerous environments, goal types, and obstacle configurations. The researchers aim to automate the generation of rich supervision from readily available, unlabeled video data.

In summary, VEGA aims to transform abundant, action-free egocentric videos into a rich source of geometric and semantic supervision, enabling the training of VLA navigation policies that are both goal-conditioned and robustly obstacle-aware, thereby addressing the data gap and combining the benefits of semantic reasoning and geometric safety.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VEGA's methodology involves a multi-stage process to generate obstacle-aware, goal-conditioned trajectory distributions from unlabeled egocentric videos, which are then used to supervise a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model. The core idea is to leverage reconstructed scene geometry solely during the training phase to distill safe navigation behavior into a vision-based policy that requires only RGB observations and goal conditioning at inference time.

#### 3.1. Trajectory Distribution Generation from Unlabeled Egocentric Videos

The process begins with curating unlabeled egocentric videos from various online sources (e.g., walking tours, bike rides). Frames are uniformly sampled from these videos, with each frame treated as a distinct local navigation scene.

##### 3.1.1. Geometry Recovery and Visibility-Aware ESDF Construction

For each sampled RGB frame ($I_t$), VEGA reconstructs a dense metric 3D point map ($P_t$) in the camera frame using a monocular geometry foundation model, MoGe-2.
1. **Ground Plane Alignment:** A local ground plane is fitted to the reconstructed points using RANSAC, and the point map is rotated to align this plane with the robot's (xy)-plane.
2. **Obstacle Extraction:** Obstacle points ($P_{obs}$) are identified by height filtering within a defined local bird's-eye-view (BEV) domain. These points are then rasterized to create a BEV occupancy grid ($M_{occ}$).
3. **Visibility Mask:** Raytracing through the organized point map constructs a visibility mask ($M_{vis}$), distinguishing known free space ($X_{free}$), occupied space ($X_{occ}$), and unobserved space ($X_{unk}$).
4. **Euclidean Signed Distance Field (ESDF):** An ESDF is computed over the local frame. Positive ESDF values indicate distance to the nearest observed obstacle (known free space), while negative values denote distance to the nearest known-free cell (occupied or unobserved regions). This ESDF guides obstacle-aware trajectory generation by penalizing negative values.

##### 3.1.2. Multimodal Goal Anchoring

To provide diverse goal conditioning, VEGA extracts multimodal goals from each RGB frame:
1. **Object Detection:** Florence-2, an object detection model, is used to identify objects and their bounding boxes.
2. **Waypoint Goal Generation:** For each detected object, the corresponding region in the MoGe-2 point map is queried and projected onto the robot-centric ground plane, yielding a 2D waypoint goal ($g_{wp}$). This provides aligned text, image-region, and spatial waypoint goals ($g_{text}, g_{box}, g_{wp}$).
3. **Auxiliary Waypoints:** Additional waypoint goals are sampled from the observed free-space subset ($X_{free}$) to provide supervision for general traversable locations.

##### 3.1.3. MPPI-Based Trajectory Generation

For each identified waypoint goal ($g_{wp}$), a Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planner generates a target waypoint trajectory ($y^{*}_{t:t+H,j}$).
1. **Trajectory Rollouts:** Candidate trajectories are rolled out using non-holonomic robot dynamics (unicycle model).
2. **Cost Function:** Rollouts are scored using a cost function that considers:
 - Goal-reaching error (terminal, closest, running).
 - Collision cost (heavy penalty for entering occupied, unknown, or out-of-bounds ESDF regions).
 - Clearance cost (encouraging a minimum safe distance from obstacles, accounting for robot radius).
 - Control effort and smoothness costs.
3. **Reference Trajectory Selection:** The lowest-cost, collision-free rollout is selected as the reference trajectory. If a goal is not reachable within the local map, the best collision-free partial trajectory making progress toward the goal is used. MPPI parameters include a 5-second horizon, 8192 rollouts per iteration, and specific cost weights.

##### 3.1.4. Generated Training Tuples

Each sampled frame generates multiple goal-conditioned training tuples: $(I_t, g_j, m_j, \hat{y}_{t:t+H,j})$, where $I_t$ is the RGB observation, $g_j$ is the $j$-th goal, $m_j$ is the goal modality (text, box, or waypoint), and $\hat{y}_{t:t+H,j}$ is the MPPI-generated waypoint trajectory for that goal.

#### 3.2. Multimodal Goal-Conditioned VLA Architecture and Training

VEGA adapts the $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA architecture, which incorporates a pretrained vision-language backbone (SigLIP vision encoder, Gemma language backbone) with a flow-matching action expert.
1. **Frozen Backbone:** The pretrained vision-language backbone (SigLIP and Gemma) is kept frozen, leveraging its pre-trained semantic knowledge.
2. **Waypoint Goal Encoder:** A trainable, lightweight MLP is introduced to map 2D robot-frame waypoint goals ($g_{wp}$) into the same token space as the pretrained VLM backbone, allowing it to condition the model through a shared prefix-token interface alongside text and image-region goals.
3. **Flow-Matching Action Expert:** The action expert is trained to generate local 2D waypoint chunks ($\hat{y}_{t:t+H}$) in the robot-centric frame. It is conditioned on the frozen VLM context.
4. **Flow Matching Objective:** Training uses the standard flow-matching objective, learning a vector field to transform a simple prior noise distribution ($y_0$) into the target data distribution ($y_1$), represented by the MPPI-generated trajectories. This approach allows the policy to model continuous, multimodal action distributions, such as multiple feasible paths around an obstacle.
5. **Trainable Components:** Only the waypoint encoder and the flow-matching action expert are optimized during VEGA training.

#### 3.3. Deployment Methodology

For real-world deployment, VEGA uses a ROS 2-based architecture on a Ghost Robotics Vision 60 quadruped. This architecture separates policy inference from control:
1. **Model Runner:** Asynchronously predicts short-horizon local waypoint sequences from current RGB observations, odometry, and goal conditioning.
2. **Path Manager:** Maintains the latest predicted waypoint sequence, transforming waypoints from the local planning frame to the odometry frame, and prunes already reached or outdated waypoints.
3. **Low-Level Planner:** Tracks the current waypoint provided by the path manager and converts it into velocity commands for the robot.
This modular design ensures consistent evaluation across different visuomotor policies and handles variable inference latency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates VEGA using a newly introduced benchmark, VEGA-Bench, and through real-world robot deployment. Comparisons are made against state-of-the-art navigation VLAs, including OmniVLA, NaVILA, and $\pi_{0.5}$.

#### 4.1. VEGA-Bench Evaluation

VEGA-Bench is an evaluation suite comprising 250,000 scenes and approximately 5 million multimodal (text, image, waypoint) navigation goals, paired with reconstructed scene geometry. It enables the assessment of goal progress, collision avoidance, and obstacle clearance. Evaluation was performed on a held-out test set not used for training VEGA.

The results, as summarized in Table 2, indicate the following:
- **Trajectory Quality:**
 - VEGA achieved a goal progress score of 0.31, which is comparable to OmniVLA (0.35) and higher than $\pi_{0.5}$ (0.29) and NaVILA (0.26).
 - VEGA demonstrated a collision percentage of 4.52%, representing a 33.0% reduction compared to the strongest baseline (OmniVLA at 6.75%).
 - VEGA achieved an obstacle clearance of 2.77, an improvement of 17.9% over the strongest baseline (OmniVLA at 2.35).
- **Goal Reaching by Modality:**
 - VEGA achieved a 1.0 success rate for text goals, 1.0 for image goals, and 0.8 for waypoint goals. This performance suggests strong goal grounding across different modalities.
 - In comparison, OmniVLA achieved 0.8 (text), 0.2 (image), and 0.6 (waypoint). $\pi_{0.5}$ and NaVILA only reported for text goals (0.8 and 0.6, respectively), with "N/A" for image and waypoint, implying these methods might not fully support or perform well on these modalities in the benchmark.
 - The paper notes that VEGA produced distinct trajectories for different goal specifications within the same scene, unlike some baselines which often generated similar paths, indicating that VEGA mitigates goal-conditioned mode collapse.

#### 4.2. Real-World Robot Evaluation

VEGA was deployed on a Ghost Robotics Vision 60 quadruped in three cluttered real-world environments featuring both static and dynamic obstacles. The evaluation involved 5 trials per scenario.

The results, presented in Table 1 and qualitatively depicted in Figure 3, show:
- **Scenario 1 (Static indoor, goal "person wearing orange jacket", chair obstacle):**
 - VEGA achieved a 1.0 success rate and 0.2 collisions, with 0.93 obstacle clearance.
 - Baselines (OmniVLA, $\pi_{0.5}$, NaVILA) had lower success rates (0.4, 0.0, 0.2) and higher collision rates (1.0 for all). $\pi_{0.5}$ and NaVILA failed to achieve any clearance, while OmniVLA's clearance was not applicable due to universal collisions.
- **Scenario 2 (Nearby furniture, dynamic person obstacle, goal "blue chair"):**
 - VEGA achieved a 0.6 success rate and 0.2 collisions, with 0.56 obstacle clearance.
 - Baselines exhibited lower success (0.0 for all), higher collisions (0.6 to 1.0), and generally lower clearance.
- **Scenario 3 (Partially observable goal "scooter", dynamic approaching person):**
 - VEGA achieved a 1.0 success rate and 0.0 collisions, with 0.94 obstacle clearance.
 - Baselines had lower success (0.0 for all) and higher collisions (0.4 to 1.0), with lower clearances or not applicable.

Across all real-world trials, VEGA achieved at least a 150.0% improvement in success rate, at least a 66.7% reduction in collisions, and at least a 60.0% improvement in obstacle clearance compared to the tested VLA baselines. The qualitative examples illustrate that VEGA consistently reached goals while avoiding obstacles, whereas baselines frequently collided, stopped prematurely, or failed to remain goal-directed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a scalable and effective methodology for training obstacle-aware navigation Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The significance and potential impact of VEGA are multifaceted:

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity:** VEGA provides a method to overcome the current data bottleneck in mobile robot navigation. By transforming readily available, unlabeled egocentric videos into structured, goal-conditioned, and obstacle-aware supervision, it bypasses the high costs and scalability limitations associated with manual action labeling or teleoperated robot data collection. This enables the leveraging of internet-scale video resources for robot learning.

2. **Integration of Semantic and Geometric Intelligence:** The approach successfully combines the open-vocabulary semantic reasoning capabilities of VLAs with the geometric safety and collision avoidance typically found in classical planning systems. This integration is crucial for robots operating in complex, cluttered, and dynamic real-world environments where both understanding high-level goals and navigating safely are essential.

3. **Improved Navigation Performance:** The empirical results on both the synthetic VEGA-Bench and real-world robot deployments demonstrate that VEGA-trained policies achieve superior performance in terms of goal progress, collision avoidance, and obstacle clearance compared to existing VLA baselines. The notable improvements in real-world success rates and collision reduction highlight the practical utility of this approach for robust robot navigation.

4. **Geometry-Supervised Training for Vision-Based Policies:** A key aspect of VEGA is its strategy of using geometry exclusively during training to distill obstacle-aware planning directly into a vision-based policy. This eliminates the need for online mapping, distance field computation, or trajectory optimization during deployment, thereby reducing sensor and computational requirements for the deployed robot.

5. **Mitigation of Goal-Conditioned Mode Collapse:** The methodology, particularly through the generation of multiple distinct goal-conditioned trajectories for each scene and the use of flow-matching for action prediction, helps in learning robust goal grounding. This prevents the VLA from collapsing to a single dominant trajectory, ensuring the policy generates varied and appropriate paths for different multimodal goal specifications within the same environment.

6. **Introduction of a New Benchmark:** The creation of VEGA-Bench, with its large scale (250k scenes, 5M multimodal targets) and detailed geometric evaluation metrics, provides a valuable resource for the research community. It establishes a standardized platform for evaluating goal progress, collision rates, and obstacle clearance of navigation VLAs, facilitating further advancements in the field.

The limitations, such as the memoryless nature of the policy and the lack of explicit dynamic obstacle motion modeling, also open avenues for future research, suggesting directions for enhancing robustness and predictive capabilities in highly dynamic settings. Overall, VEGA contributes to making general-purpose robot navigation more achievable by providing a scalable and effective method for training intelligent, safe, and versatile robot policies.
