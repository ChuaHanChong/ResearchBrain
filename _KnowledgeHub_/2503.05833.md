# Refined Policy Distillation: From VLA Generalists to RL Experts

> [!summary] Summary
> Refined Policy Distillation (RPD) introduces an RL-based method to train compact, task-specific robot policies by leveraging action guidance from large generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This approach enhances RL training efficiency and robustness, often enabling the distilled policies to surpass the VLA teacher's performance on specific manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models show broad generalization but often have suboptimal performance, requiring costly fine-tuning for new environments due to reliance on limited human demonstration data.
- Traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) suffers from sample inefficiency, instability, and sensitivity to hyperparameters, making real-world deployment challenging, especially in sparse reward settings.
- Existing policy distillation (PD) methods primarily focus on transferring knowledge between RL policies, lacking a framework to leverage the broad, generalist knowledge embedded in VLA models.

## Method

- **Refined Policy Distillation** (RPD) integrates a ==Behavioral Cloning (BC)== objective into the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, using actions from a VLA teacher to guide the RL student policy's exploration.
- The RPD objective combines the standard PPO clipped surrogate loss with a Mean Squared Error (MSE) term that minimizes the difference between the student policy's action mean and the VLA teacher's sampled actions.
- A generic Python interface and HTTP API server were developed to seamlessly integrate various VLA models into the RL training loop, allowing for efficient querying of VLA actions given observations.

## Results

- RPD-MSE consistently outperformed vanilla PPO and other distillation baselines across diverse manipulation tasks, achieving faster learning, higher success rates, and increased training robustness.
- In sparse reward settings, RPD drastically improved learning success on multiple tasks where vanilla PPO often failed to learn entirely, demonstrating its effectiveness in challenging exploration scenarios.
- RPD policies showed improved generalization and robustness to visual domain shifts, such as altered camera perspectives, outperforming both the degraded VLA teachers and vanilla PPO in these tests.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Even VLA teachers with suboptimal or zero direct success rates can provide valuable directional cues to an RL agent, significantly improving its sample efficiency and convergence speed, particularly in sparse reward environments.
> - The combination of an on-policy RL objective with VLA-guided behavioral cloning enables the student policy to not only distill the teacher's knowledge but also to actively refine and often surpass its performance.
> - The RPD-MSE loss variant effectively balances exploration and imitation, proving more robust and leading to higher-performing policies compared to other behavioral cloning-like losses or vanilla PPO.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has experienced a notable shift from developing task-specific Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies to utilizing large generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models. VLAs are trained through supervised imitation learning on extensive datasets and demonstrate considerable generalization capabilities across various real-world manipulation tasks with a single model. This paradigm offers advantages in terms of ease of training and broad applicability.

However, VLAs present several limitations. Their performance is inherently constrained by the quantity and diversity of human expert demonstrations used for training, which are costly and time-consuming to acquire in robotics. This data limitation often leads to challenges in generalizing across different environmental setups. Consequently, adapting VLAs to new environments frequently necessitates fine-tuning with additional domain-specific data, reintroducing human effort for data collection. Recent efforts, such as those by Xu et al., have explored using RL-generated data for VLA fine-tuning to expedite adaptation.

In contrast, traditional RL offers potential for greater scalability. RL agents learn by maximizing rewards through autonomous interaction with their environment, generating their own data. This process can be extensively parallelized in simulation, leading to more compact policies with fewer parameters, which translates to faster inference and increased efficiency. Nevertheless, RL also faces challenges, including sensitivity to hyperparameter configurations, sample inefficiency requiring extensive exploration (often precluding real-robot execution), and the persistent "sim-to-real" gap, necessitating further fine-tuning on physical systems.

Policy Distillation (PD) is an established technique in RL that aims to transfer knowledge from a teacher policy to a student policy to accelerate training and reduce policy complexity. Previous work in PD has primarily focused on distilling from one RL policy to another, often involving methods like KL divergence or MSE loss between Q-functions or policy distributions. Some approaches frame PD as a supervised policy transfer problem, allowing distillation across different RL algorithms. However, these methods typically distill from RL teacher policies, not large generalist VLAs. Proximal Policy Distillation (PPD) is a recent example that distills a PPO teacher into a PPO student by modifying the PPO loss with a KL divergence term. While conceptually transferable to stochastic VLA teachers, sampling action distributions from large VLAs for KL divergence can be computationally intensive.

Behavioral Cloning (BC) is another related paradigm, falling under offline imitation learning, where a policy learns to mimic trajectories from a fixed dataset. BC has also been combined with RL to guide agent exploration with pre-recorded data. The present work positions itself within this landscape by seeking to leverage the general knowledge embedded in VLAs to address the sample inefficiency and slow convergence of RL, while simultaneously refining VLA performance beyond mere imitation. Unlike prior PD methods, this research specifically targets distilling from generalist VLA models, which are distinct from traditional RL teachers, aiming to produce compact, high-performing expert policies that can surpass the VLA's initial capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Refined Policy Distillation (RPD), a novel RL-based policy refinement method designed to bridge the performance gap between generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models and specialized expert policies. While VLAs exhibit notable generalization capabilities, their success rates often do not match those of expert policies, and they commonly require fine-tuning when environmental setups change.

The core motivations driving this work are multifaceted:

1. **Leveraging VLA Generalization for RL Efficiency:** VLAs, despite their limitations, encapsulate broad task knowledge acquired from large-scale datasets. The authors are motivated to utilize this general understanding to inform and guide the training process of Reinforcement Learning algorithms. This guidance is intended to make RL exploration more efficient, leading to increased sample efficiency and faster convergence, particularly in scenarios where the VLA's direct success rate might be low or even zero. The hypothesis is that even a VLA with suboptimal performance can provide valuable directional cues to an RL agent.
2. **Addressing RL Sample Inefficiency and Instability:** Traditional RL algorithms, especially on-policy methods like PPO, are known for their sample inefficiency and sensitivity to hyperparameters. They often require extensive environmental interactions, making real-world deployment challenging. By integrating VLA guidance, the authors aim to stabilize the RL training process and significantly reduce the number of samples required for the agent to learn high-performing policies.
3. **Refining Policy Performance Beyond VLA Capabilities:** A key motivation is not merely to distill the VLA's performance but to *refine* it. The goal is for the RL student policy, through autonomous interaction and reward maximization, to surpass the VLA teacher's performance on specific tasks, thereby becoming a true "expert" policy. This goes beyond the typical scope of behavioral cloning, which often limits the student's performance to that of the teacher.
4. **Creating Compact and Efficient Expert Policies:** VLAs are often large-scale models with numerous parameters, leading to higher inference latency and computational resource requirements. The authors aim to distill the relevant task knowledge into more compact, task-specific RL policies, which can offer faster inference and improved efficiency for deployment.
5. **Improving VLA Robustness to Environmental Changes:** VLAs are known to be sensitive to minor environmental alterations, such as changes in camera perspective, which can severely degrade their performance and necessitate costly re-training or fine-tuning. A motivation is to demonstrate that RPD can enable the distilled RL policies to generalize to such variations, even when the underlying VLA teacher struggles, thus enhancing the practical applicability of VLA-derived knowledge.
6. **Gaining Insights into VLA Performance in Simulation:** Since much of RL research occurs in simulation, the authors are motivated to evaluate VLAs, specifically fine-tuned versions of Octo and OpenVLA, within a simulated environment (ManiSkill3). This provides insights into their performance in such settings, which is a crucial step for applying RL techniques to VLA refinement and bridging the real-to-sim gap in a research context.

In summary, the objectives center on developing a method that effectively combines the generalist knowledge of VLAs with the optimization power of RL to produce highly performant, compact, and robust expert robot policies, while also addressing challenges in RL training efficiency and VLA adaptability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Refined Policy Distillation (RPD) method developed in this paper combines Reinforcement Learning (RL) with a Behavioral Cloning (BC) objective to distill and refine task-specific expert policies from large generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The core idea is to guide the student RL policy during exploration using actions generated by a VLA teacher.

**A. Refined Policy Distillation Objective:**
RPD modifies the objective function of the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. The overall RPD objective, `L_RPD(θ)`, is defined as:
`L_RPD(θ) = L_RL(θ) - L_MSE(θ)`

1. **`L_RL(θ)` (RL Objective):** This component is the standard PPO clipped surrogate objective function, which aims to maximize the expected sum of discounted rewards by optimizing the policy parameters `θ`. PPO is an on-policy algorithm that updates policies based on data collected from the current policy, using importance sampling to prevent large deviations from the previous policy.
2. **`L_MSE(θ)` (Behavioral Cloning Objective):** This component is the expectation of the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the action mean `μ(π_θ(a_t | s_t))` predicted by the PPO policy and the expectation `E[a_VLA_t]` of the actions `a_VLA_t` sampled from the VLA teacher policy `π_VLA(a_t | s_t)`. The MSE term is minimized (hence the negative sign in `L_RPD`) to pull the action mean of the RL policy closer to the predictions of the VLA. The authors note that sampling a single VLA action per step for estimation yields good performance, balancing accuracy with computational cost.

The paper also investigates two alternative loss variants for the BC component:
- **RPD-L1:** Uses an L1 loss instead of MSE.
- **RPD-BC:** Employs a naive maximum likelihood loss as typically used in BC, which includes a variance component.

The overall training objective is to find optimal weights `θ*` that maximize `L_RPD(θ)`.

**B. VLA Integration:**
To integrate various VLAs into the RL training loop, a generic policy interface was defined. This interface accepts a batch of visual observations and language instructions, then returns a relative task space motion command, including the gripper state. To manage dependencies and facilitate communication, separate Python environments were created for different VLAs, and a generic VLA interface was exposed via an HTTP API server. During training, the RL agent communicates with this server to send observation batches. For co-located VLA inference and RL training, shared memory is used to bypass serialization bottlenecks. A specific challenge noted for OpenVLA was its lack of native batch processing support and CPU-intensive preprocessing, requiring manual optimization and parallelization across multiple GPUs.

**C. Tasks, Dataset, and VLA Fine-Tuning:**
The evaluation of RPD was conducted using eight distinct manipulation tasks from the ManiSkill3 simulation environment. Tasks were selected based on the availability of demonstrations within episode length limits and the presence of relative task space actions (7-dimensional: `Δx, Δω, gripper`). The ManiSkill3 "human camera" perspective, which includes visual cues (e.g., green spheres for goal locations) and offers a side view akin to Open X-Embodiment dataset setups, was used for both VLA and RL agent observations.

Initial evaluation of published Octo and OpenVLA checkpoints on ManiSkill3 tasks showed zero success rates, highlighting a real-to-sim gap. To enable realistic assessment of RPD, the authors fine-tuned Octo and OpenVLA on RL-generated expert demonstrations from ManiSkill3. These demonstrations were rendered with the human camera at 256x256 resolution. The maximum episode length was increased to 300 steps for fine-tuning. Two tasks, PullCubeTool and PokeCube, were intentionally excluded from the VLA fine-tuning dataset to test cross-task generalization. The authors note that for these two tasks, VLAs often achieved sparse reward success without actually using the tool, which was accounted for in the dense reward evaluation.

**D. Baselines and Experimental Setup:**
RPD variants were compared against two baselines:
1. **Vanilla PPO:** Implemented using CleanRL, included in ManiSkill3.
2. **Proximal Policy Distillation (PPD):** An in-house implementation extending CleanRL's PPO. For PPD, KL divergence was computed by sampling ten actions from the VLA and fitting a multivariate Gaussian distribution.

All RPD variants, PPO, and PPD used the same policy architecture and constant hyperparameters, largely adopted from the ManiSkill3 PPO baseline, with adjustments only for batch size to fit VLAs on the same GPU. Experiments were conducted for both dense and sparse reward settings. Cross-task and cross-setup generalization were tested on the held-out tasks (PullCubeTool, PokeCube) and on a variant of the PushCube task with a changed camera perspective.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Refined Policy Distillation (RPD) involved comparisons against vanilla PPO and PPD baselines, across multiple tasks, reward settings, and generalization scenarios, using Octo and OpenVLA as teacher models.

**A. RPD Variants and Baselines Comparison (PickCube-v1):**
On the PickCube-v1 task, RPD-MSE, the main RPD variant, demonstrated superior performance. It consistently outperformed RPD-L1, RPD-BC, vanilla PPO, and PPD. RPD-L1 closely followed RPD-MSE. The distilled policy with RPD-MSE quickly surpassed the success rates of both the Octo and OpenVLA teachers, indicating successful policy refinement. RPD-MSE using OpenVLA performed slightly below RPD-MSE with Octo, which the authors attribute to inherent performance differences in the foundation models.

Vanilla PPO eventually converged to similar success rates as RPD-MSE and RPD-L1 (around 80%) but exhibited substantially slower learning and greater fluctuations during training. RPD-BC, which includes a variance component in its loss, largely plateaued after slightly exceeding the Octo baseline, suggesting it acts more as a distillation method without refinement, potentially due to overfitting the teacher's variance. The PPD baseline failed to lift the policy above the Octo baseline, with authors speculating this might be due to the VLA's bimodal action distributions, which could make KL divergence estimation problematic for approximated normal distributions, or PPD's sensitivity to hyperparameters.

**B. RPD Performance on Diverse Tasks (Dense Rewards):**
Across six different manipulation tasks (PickCube-v1, LiftPegUpright-v1, PullCube-v1, PushCube-v1, RollBall-v1, StackCube-v1) included in the VLA fine-tuning dataset, RPD-MSE with Octo consistently learned faster than the vanilla PPO baseline. In many instances, RPD converged to higher success rates and rewards. For tasks like RollBall and StackCube, where vanilla PPO failed to solve the tasks with standard hyperparameters, RPD achieved at least partial success using the same parameters. The reduced standard deviation in RPD training curves also indicated increased robustness compared to vanilla PPO. These results demonstrate that RPD improves PPO's training speed and final performance, effectively refining the distilled policy even with dense rewards.

**C. Sparse Rewards and Sample Efficiency:**
The study also evaluated RPD in sparse reward settings, where non-zero rewards are typically provided only at the end of an episode upon successful task completion. RPD on sparse rewards consistently outperformed vanilla PPO across all six tested tasks. In most of these tasks, the PPO baseline completely failed to learn, while RPD achieved successful task completion. This finding supports the hypothesis that RPD's teacher guidance is particularly beneficial for exploration in sparse reward environments, enabling the student to successfully complete episodes and obtain crucial reward signals, thereby drastically improving sample efficiency.

**D. Generalization to New Environments:**

1. **Hold-Out Tasks (PullCubeTool-v1, PokeCube-v1):** These tasks were excluded from VLA fine-tuning to test generalization. For dense rewards, RPD failed to learn if the VLA could not provide meaningful actions (e.g., when tool use was strictly required by the dense reward, which the VLA ignored). In such cases, vanilla PPO performed better. However, in the sparse reward setting, where the VLA's behavior of simply pushing/pulling the cube (without using the tool) was sufficient for "success," RPD successfully learned the reinterpreted tasks and outperformed PPO. This suggests that RPD can benefit from VLA actions that provide helpful bias, even if they don't perfectly solve the task as intended by dense rewards.

2. **Changed Camera Perspective (PushCube-v1):** VLAs exhibited significant performance degradation when the camera angle for the PushCube task was slightly altered (Octo from 67% to 0% success, OpenVLA from 27% to 4.5%). Vanilla PPO, learning its visual encoder from scratch, performed similarly on this variation as on the original task, as expected. Notably, RPD-MSE with both Octo and OpenVLA managed to outperform PPO in this scenario. This indicates that RPD is robust to visual changes and can leverage even degraded VLA actions to guide exploration effectively, despite a considerable drop in the VLA's direct success rate. The authors suggest that even "bad" VLA actions can still offer valuable directional cues for the student policy.

**E. VLA Performance in Simulation:**
As a secondary contribution, the fine-tuned versions of Octo and OpenVLA demonstrated measurable success rates on ManiSkill3 tasks (e.g., PullCube-v1 with 92% for OpenVLA, PushCube-v1 with 67% for Octo), indicating that VLAs can be successfully adapted and utilized in simulated environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Refined Policy Distillation (RPD) represents a significant development in the field of robot learning, particularly at the intersection of generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and Reinforcement Learning (RL).

**Novelty and Advancement:**
RPD is presented as the first approach of its kind to distill task knowledge from large generalist VLAs into refined expert policies using on-policy RL. This distinguishes it from previous policy distillation methods that typically focused on RL-to-RL policy transfer. By combining the broad knowledge of VLAs with the iterative optimization capabilities of RL, RPD offers a unique mechanism to leverage existing large models while overcoming their inherent limitations.

**Practical Benefits for Robot Control:**
1. **Enhanced Performance and Efficiency:** RPD enables the creation of compact, fast, and high-performing task-specific expert policies. These policies not only distill knowledge from VLAs but actively refine it, consistently surpassing the VLA teacher's performance. This results in more capable and reliable robot controllers for specific tasks.
2. **Increased Sample Efficiency and Stability:** The method significantly improves the sample efficiency and stability of RL training. By guiding RL exploration with VLA actions, RPD accelerates convergence, particularly in challenging sparse reward settings where traditional RL often struggles. This reduction in required interaction steps is crucial for making RL more feasible for real-world robotic applications, where data collection is expensive and time-consuming.
3. **Robustness to Environmental Variations:** RPD demonstrates resilience to minor environmental changes, such as altered camera perspectives. This is a critical advantage, as VLAs are typically highly sensitive to such shifts, often requiring extensive re-calibration or fine-tuning. RPD's ability to benefit from even degraded VLA actions provides a pathway for deploying VLA-derived skills in diverse and slightly varying environments without prohibitive re-training costs.
4. **Improved Usability and Accessibility of VLAs:** By providing a mechanism to extract and refine task-specific expertise from generalist VLAs, RPD enhances their practical usability and accessibility. It allows researchers and practitioners to leverage the generalized knowledge of large foundation models more effectively for specific robotic tasks, even if the base VLA performance is suboptimal for that exact setup.

**Impact on Future Research Directions:**
1. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The successful demonstration of RPD in simulation, including the fine-tuning of state-of-the-art VLAs in simulated environments, provides a promising foundation for future research. Combining RPD with sim-to-real transfer methods could facilitate the deployment of these refined expert policies onto physical robots, expanding the applicability of VLA-based skills to real-world hardware.
2. **Autonomous Data Generation for VLA Improvement:** The distilled expert policies generated by RPD could be utilized to collect new, high-quality training data. Following approaches like that proposed by Xu et al., this RL-generated data could then be used to further fine-tune and improve the generalist VLA models themselves, creating a self-improving loop that reduces reliance on human demonstrations for VLA development.
3. **Advancements in Generalist Robot Learning:** As new and more capable VLAs continue to emerge, the performance of RPD is expected to improve further, leading to even more robust and capable distilled expert policies. This research contributes to the broader goal of developing generalist robot intelligence by providing a robust framework for converting broad VLA knowledge into refined, task-specific mastery.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge that RPD's effectiveness is constrained by the quality of the VLA teacher's actions. If the VLA provides entirely unhelpful or misleading actions due to a significant domain shift or an entirely unseen task, RPD may hinder RL agent performance. Additionally, for tasks that are inherently simple or when PPO is already highly tuned and sample-efficient, RPD may offer only marginal improvements. The high data requirements for online RL, even with RPD's sample efficiency gains, still pose challenges for direct deployment on physical platforms, necessitating sim-to-real methods.

In conclusion, RPD represents a valuable contribution that enhances the practical utility of generalist VLAs by transforming their broad knowledge into specialized, high-performance, and robust RL policies.
