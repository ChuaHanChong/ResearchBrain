# SUGAR: A Scalable Human-Video-Driven Generalizable Humanoid Loco-Manipulation Learning Framework

> [!summary] Summary
> SUGAR is a framework that enables humanoid robots to learn generalizable loco-manipulation skills directly from diverse human videos. It leverages a three-stage pipeline to transform noisy kinematic data into robust, autonomous policies capable of zero-shot real-world transfer, achieving high success rates across six complex tasks and demonstrating autonomous failure recovery.

## Problem

- Existing methods for humanoid loco-manipulation (RL from scratch, reference-motion tracking, teleoperation) face scalability issues, high data collection costs, and limited generalization to novel scenarios.
- Utilizing abundant human video data for robot learning is challenging due to inherent imperfections (occlusion, contact artifacts, retargeting errors) in extracted kinematic priors, making them physically implausible for direct use.
- Prior video-based methods are limited to locomotion or rigid reference replay, lacking object interaction capabilities or robust generalization and sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich tasks.

## Method

- An automated pipeline extracts coarse kinematic interaction priors (human-object motions and VLM-labeled contact labels) from unannotated human videos.
- A privileged ==reinforcement learning== 'refiner' policy transforms these imperfect kinematic priors into physically feasible, high-fidelity skill demonstrations using a unified mimic-style reward and a Progressive State Pool for stable learning.
- A hierarchical autonomous policy, consisting of a Behavior Cloning/RL-trained Command Tracker and a ==Diffusion Policy==-based Command Generator, distills these refined demonstrations for robust real-world execution, trained on a rollout dataset to bridge kinematic planning and dynamic execution.

## Results

- SUGAR demonstrated superior performance over reference-tracking baselines (Resmimic, HDMI) across six diverse loco-manipulation tasks on a Unitree G1 humanoid, showing higher success rates and lower object position errors.
- The framework exhibited positive data scaling, with success rates improving from **32.7%** to **76.0%** for tasks like Kick Box when increasing training data from 20 to 100 trajectories, indicating efficient leverage of larger datasets.
- Zero-shot real-world transfer was achieved on a Unitree G1 robot, showcasing robust closed-loop execution, autonomous failure recovery from disruptions, stability under external disturbances, and generalization to objects with varying properties.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A physics-based 'refiner' policy is crucial for transforming imperfect kinematic priors from human videos into physically feasible and dynamically consistent expert demonstrations, essential for stable policy learning.
> - The Progressive State Pool effectively stabilizes training by providing diverse, physically consistent initialization states, overcoming issues caused by initial kinematic reconstruction errors and improving skill acquisition.
> - A hierarchical policy structure, with a low-level command tracker and a high-level diffusion-based command generator trained on closed-loop rollouts, enables robust long-horizon task completion and autonomous failure recovery in real-world settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of generalizable whole-body loco-manipulation in complex, unstructured environments remains a significant challenge in robotics. Current approaches to teaching humanoids such skills generally fall into three categories, each with inherent limitations that hinder scalability and generalization:

1. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) from Scratch:** This paradigm has demonstrated notable successes in specific, well-defined tasks (e.g., soccer, tennis, door opening). However, it typically requires extensive, task-specific reward engineering and meticulous environment design, making it costly to extend to a diverse range of behaviors. The engineering effort increases substantially with task complexity and diversity.
2. **Reference-Motion Tracking:** Methods in this category achieve high-fidelity robot behaviors by having policies mimic pre-recorded motion trajectories. While effective for replicating known movements, this approach binds the policy rigidly to the demonstrations. This limits generalization to novel object geometries, configurations, or environmental variations, and often struggles with the sim-to-real transfer gap, particularly in contact-rich interactions.
3. **Teleoperation-Based Imitation Learning:** This involves collecting robot demonstrations through human teleoperation and subsequently training autonomous policies. While this can yield high-quality, embodiment-consistent data, it is a labor-intensive process requiring specialized hardware and considerable human effort, posing a bottleneck for scaling up data collection to encompass a wide array of tasks.

A promising alternative for acquiring diverse robot skills is the use of human videos, given their abundance and low collection cost. However, extracting usable data from human videos for robot learning presents its own set of challenges:
- **Data Imperfection:** Kinematic data derived from human videos is inherently imperfect, suffering from issues such as occlusion, contact artifacts, and retargeting errors. This renders the data physically implausible or unsuitable for direct policy learning.
- **Limited Scope in Prior Work:** Existing video-based methods have either focused exclusively on object-free locomotion, lacking object interaction capabilities, or have used video data for rigid reference replay, which does not generalize to novel scenarios or reliably transfer to real-world hardware. Some concurrent works in humanoid interaction from human videos rely on kinematic motion synthesis with manual anchor points, rather than large-scale, multi-trajectory human-object interaction (HOI) data.

SUGAR positions itself by addressing these limitations. It aims to bridge the gap between scalable, diverse, and inherently imperfect human video data and the robust, generalizable, and reference-free loco-manipulation capabilities required for real-world humanoid robots. The framework proposes a method to convert these noisy kinematic priors into physically feasible training signals and subsequently into deployable autonomous policies, thereby offering a scalable pathway that minimizes task-specific engineering and reliance on teleoperation. This work aims to enable humanoids to coordinate locomotion, balance, and contact-rich object manipulation in unstructured environments, a capability that existing approaches have struggled to achieve broadly.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data-driven framework that enables humanoid robots to learn generalizable whole-body loco-manipulation skills directly from diverse human videos, without relying on task-specific reward engineering or explicit reference-motion conditioning during inference.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges observed in the current state of humanoid robotics:

1. **Scalability Bottlenecks of Existing Methods:** The established paradigms for teaching humanoids—task-specific reinforcement learning, reference-motion tracking, and teleoperation-based imitation learning—all face significant scalability issues. These methods incur high data collection and engineering costs that escalate rapidly with increasing task diversity, thereby impeding progress toward building general-purpose humanoid assistants. The authors seek a more efficient and scalable data source.
2. **Untapped Potential of Human Videos:** Human videos offer an abundant, low-cost source of diverse human behaviors, including complex loco-manipulation interactions. This vast repository of data represents a potential solution to the scalability bottleneck. However, this potential remains largely unrealized because the kinematic data extracted from these videos is inherently imperfect. It contains physical inaccuracies due to occlusion, contact artifacts, and retargeting errors, making it unsuitable for direct application in physics-based robot simulation and policy learning.
3. **Limitations of Current Video-Based Approaches:** Prior attempts to utilize human videos for humanoid learning have either focused on locomotion without object interaction or involved rigidly replaying recorded human-object interaction trajectories. Such methods fail to generalize to novel object configurations or reliably transfer to physical robot hardware due to the sim-to-real gap and the lack of physical plausibility in the extracted data. There is a clear need for a framework that can transform noisy kinematic priors into physically grounded and dynamically consistent skills.
4. **Need for Autonomous and Reference-Free Policies:** To function effectively as general-purpose assistants, humanoid robots must be able to perform tasks autonomously, adapting to new situations without being rigidly bound to specific reference motions. Current reference-tracking methods, while capable of high-fidelity behavior, lack this essential generalization capability. The motivation is to develop policies that can infer and execute the underlying task logic rather than simply replaying movements.
5. **Robustness in Real-World Deployment:** Any learned skill must transfer robustly from simulation to the real world, handling noisy perception, dynamic discrepancies, external perturbations, and enabling autonomous recovery from failures. The current state often sees policies that work well in simulation but struggle with the complexities of real-world physics and uncertainties.

In response to these motivations, SUGAR aims to achieve the following specific objectives:
- To develop a fully automated pipeline for extracting coarse kinematic interaction priors (human-object motion trajectories and contact labels) from unannotated human videos.
- To design a privileged physics-based refiner that can transform these imperfect kinematic priors into physically feasible, high-fidelity skill demonstrations using a unified mimic-style reward and a progressive state pool.
- To create a systematic hierarchical policy training pipeline that distills these refined skill demonstrations into a deployable, reference-free autonomous policy, composed of a command generator and a command tracker.
- To validate the framework's ability to outperform reference-tracking baselines, generalize to unseen object configurations, scale with increasing video data, and achieve zero-shot real-world transfer with robust closed-loop execution and failure recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SUGAR is a three-stage data-driven framework designed to convert diverse human videos into deployable humanoid loco-manipulation skills. The approach progresses from extracting imperfect kinematic priors to refining them into physically feasible skills, and finally to distilling these skills into an autonomous, hierarchical policy.

**Stage 1: Scalable Kinematic Interaction Priors from Human Videos**
This initial stage focuses on automatically extracting kinematic interaction data from raw human videos, eliminating the need for manual annotation.

1. **Human-Object Motion Reconstruction:**
 - **Human Motion:** Human motion sequences ($\hat{p}_R_{1:T}$) are extracted using SAMBody, a tool for 4D human body mesh recovery. These sequences are then refined for spatial accuracy by aligning with depth observations and optimizing through Iterative Closest Point (ICP).
 - **Object Motion:** For objects, a mesh is first generated using SAMObj, and its physical scale is determined by alignment with captured object point clouds. The object's 6D pose trajectories ($\hat{p}_O_{1:T}$) are subsequently estimated using FoundationPose.
2. **Automated Interaction Synthesis:**
 - **Contact Labels:** To assign contact labels ($\hat{l}_t$) without manual effort, a Vision-Language Model (VLM) is queried. The VLM is prompted based on task-specific body parts (e.g., hands for carrying) and the object, using a unified template to infer direct physical contact. In cases of severe occlusion where visual cues are ambiguous (e.g., kicking a box), contact is inferred if the object's velocity exceeds a predefined threshold.
 - **Data Compilation:** Temporal filtering is applied to smooth the reconstructed trajectories. The output of this stage is the kinematic interaction priors dataset $P = \{\hat{\tau}_i\}_{i=1}^N$, where each $\hat{\tau}$ comprises sequences of reconstructed human-object motions and VLM-labeled contact labels. The "hat" notation emphasizes the kinematic nature and potential physical inaccuracies of this data.

**Stage 2: Refining Kinematic Interaction Priors into Physically Feasible Skills**
This stage addresses the physical implausibility of the kinematic priors by training a privileged reinforcement learning (RL) policy, termed "refiner" ($\pi_r$), to transform the noisy dataset $P$ into physically feasible and high-fidelity expert trajectories.

1. **Refiner Policy:** The refiner is an RL policy trained to translate the coarse kinematic interaction priors into physically consistent robot poses, object poses, and actual contact states ($p_R_t, p_O_t, l_t$), while preserving the original task intent. The output is a refined skill dataset $R = \{\tau_i\}_{i=1}^N$, where each $\tau$ also includes recorded expert states $c_t = [q_{cmd}_t, v_{cmd}_t, \omega_{cmd}_t, l_t]$ (joint positions, root linear and angular velocities, and contact labels) for subsequent policy training.
2. **Unified Reward Design:** To minimize task-specific engineering, a unified mimic-style reward function $r = r_{track} + r_{int} + r_{reg}$ is employed:
 - **Tracking Reward ($r_{track}$):** Encourages the robot and object to follow the reference trajectories from dataset $P$. The robot tracks root-relative poses to make the policy robust to global reconstruction errors.
 - **Interaction Reward ($r_{int}$):** Enforces physical consistency by using the VLM-derived contact labels ($\hat{l}_t$) to prevent force-inconsistent motions and penalize spatial decoupling between robot links and the object during contact.
 - **Regularization Term ($r_{reg}$):** Promotes sim-to-real transfer by penalizing excessive torque and non-smooth behaviors.
3. **Progressive State Pool (B) for Initialization:** To overcome issues arising from kinematic reconstruction errors (e.g., penetrations, misalignments) that create physically infeasible starting points, the Progressive State Pool is introduced. This pool incrementally populates with successful intermediate states encountered by the refiner, providing diverse, physically consistent initialization states that stabilize learning and prevent overfitting.
4. **Interaction Robustness Enhancement:** Extensive domain randomization and perturbations are applied during training. This includes varying physical properties (mass, friction) and applying random impulses to both the robot and the object. This forces the refiner to learn robust physical rules rather than simulator shortcuts, leading to expert data that is stable under interference.

**Stage 3: Policy Learning from Refined Demonstrations**
The final stage focuses on training an autonomous policy for real-world execution, leveraging the physically consistent data from dataset $R$. This policy is hierarchical, consisting of a Command Generator and a Command Tracker.

1. **Command Tracker ($\pi_t$):** This low-level policy is designed to follow a given movement intent command ($c_t$) by predicting joint targets ($a_t$), which are then converted to joint torques via a PD controller.
 - **Distillation from Refiner:** The tracker's capabilities are scaled through a combination of Behavior Cloning (BC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). BC is first used to rapidly mimic the refiner's motion patterns, providing a structured initialization, followed by RL optimization using the same rewards as the refiner.
 - **Evolutionary Initialization:** Similar to the refiner, the tracker uses Reference State Initialization (RSI) initially, transitioning to Progressive State Pool Initialization (PSPI) as training progresses to broaden state coverage and prevent overfitting. The tracker's actor uses deployable observations, while its critic and the refiner (actor/critic) use privileged observations for asymmetric actor-critic training.
2. **Task-Guided Command Generator ($\pi_g$):** With the low-level tracker frozen, the high-level command generator is trained as a conditional sequence generation problem. It is implemented as a state-based Diffusion Policy (specifically, a Diffusion Transformer, DiT).
 - **Function:** It predicts a sequence of future commands ($c_{t:t+7}$) to guide the tracker toward task completion, conditioned on object observations, previous commands, and an optional task goal.
 - **Rollout Dataset (D):** To bridge the gap between kinematic planning and dynamic execution, the generator is trained on a rollout dataset $D$. This dataset is collected by driving the *frozen tracker* ($\pi_t$) with the expert states $c_t$ from $R$ as reference commands and recording the actual object states ($\tilde{o}_O_t$) achieved by the tracker during these closed-loop rollouts. This allows the generator to learn to account for real-world execution errors and proactively correct for drift, enabling reliable long-horizon task completion. During inference, the generator predicts a chunk of 8 steps, but only the first 4 are executed before re-planning, balancing reactivity and smoothness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of SUGAR was evaluated across six diverse whole-body loco-manipulation tasks—Carry Box, Push Box, Kick Box, Pick Bottle, Stand Bottle, and Sit Chair—using a Unitree G1 humanoid robot in both simulation and real-world deployment. The evaluation focused on performance, generalization, data scaling, component contributions, and sim-to-real transfer capabilities, using metrics such as Success Rate (SR) and Final Object Position Error (Err).

**Comparison with Baselines:**
- SUGAR demonstrated superior performance compared to reference-tracking baselines, Resmimic and HDMI, across all six tasks in simulation (Table 1).
- The performance gap was particularly pronounced in high-precision tasks like Carry Box, Pick Bottle, and Stand Bottle, where baselines often failed to achieve any success.
- SUGAR achieved significantly higher success rates and lower object position errors on both training and test datasets. The baselines struggled to learn effective skills from the noisy human video data and exhibited limited generalization to unseen configurations. In contrast, SUGAR consistently extracted reusable skills.

**Performance Scaling with Data Size:**
- Experiments with varying amounts of training data (20, 50, and 100 trajectories per task) revealed a positive scaling trend (Table 2, Fig. 4).
- Success rates consistently improved as the volume of training data increased, both on training and test datasets. For instance, the average success rate for test tasks increased from 32.7% (20 trajectories) to 76.0% (100 trajectories) for Kick Box.
- This suggests that SUGAR's architecture can inherently leverage larger datasets to capture more robust and generalizable behaviors without requiring additional task-specific rewards or manual robustness engineering.

**Component Analysis (Ablation Studies):**
Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of key components:
- **Without Refiner (w/o refiner):** Removing the physics-based refiner led to substantial performance degradation across all tasks (e.g., Carry Box success rate dropped from 84.5% to 66.8% on training and 69.6% to 62.0% on test data). This highlights the critical role of the refiner in transforming coarse, noisy kinematic priors into physically valid and dynamically consistent demonstrations, which are essential for stable and effective learning.
- **Without Progressive State Pool (w/o PSPI - SSI/RSI):** Replacing the Progressive State Pool (PSPI) with either Start State Initialization (SSI) or Reference State Initialization (RSI) resulted in noticeable performance degradation (Table 1). PSPI, by providing diverse and physically consistent initialization states, proved crucial for stable training and effective skill acquisition across various interaction stages, mitigating issues from kinematic reconstruction errors.
- **Without Interaction Rewards (w/o IR):** Omitting the interaction reward term led to significant failures in contact-rich tasks such as Carry Box and Pick Bottle (Table 1, Fig. 5b). This indicates that kinematic tracking alone is insufficient to enforce the necessary physical constraints for stable object manipulation.
- **Without Interaction Robustness Enhancement (w/o IRE):** Removing the interaction robustness enhancement, which includes domain randomization and perturbations, resulted in severe overfitting to idealized physics and reduced robustness (Table 1, Fig. 5c). The enhancement forced the model to learn physical compensation, substantially improving its ability to handle external disturbances and varying physical properties.

**Real-World Evaluation:**
- SUGAR demonstrated zero-shot transfer capabilities, with the purely simulation-trained model successfully deployed on a real Unitree G1 humanoid using MoCap for perception (Table 3).
- The robot exhibited robust closed-loop execution, performing tasks continuously over extended horizons despite noisy perception and dynamic discrepancies inherent in the real world.
- A key observation was the policy's **autonomous failure recovery**. When disruptions occurred (e.g., object displacement or partial task failure), the robot was able to self-correct and resume the task rather than terminating, indicating an ability to handle out-of-distribution states (Fig. 6).
- The policy also maintained stability and task execution under **external disturbances** (e.g., human interference), as shown in Fig. 7.
- Furthermore, the learned policy generalized zero-shot to **objects with different shapes, sizes, and appearances** without finetuning (Fig. 8), suggesting that the framework captures transferable interaction strategies rather than overfitting to specific object instances.

**Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- The current data-processing pipeline extracts relatively coarse priors, which limits the framework to learning coarse-grained interaction skills. Acquiring fine-grained skills remains an open challenge.
- Data utilization efficiency is noted as relatively low. Future work could explore data augmentation or generative models to learn effectively from more limited human video data.
- The state-based policy hinders deployment convenience, as visual and language inputs are not directly processed. Developing policies that can integrate such inputs is a future research direction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SUGAR represents a significant step toward developing generalizable and autonomous humanoid loco-manipulation capabilities. The framework's core contributions and validated performance suggest several areas of potential impact:

1. **Scalable Skill Acquisition:** By providing a fully automated pipeline for extracting and refining interaction priors from abundant, low-cost human videos, SUGAR offers a scalable alternative to traditional, resource-intensive methods like task-specific RL and teleoperation. This could accelerate the development of diverse skill repertoires for humanoid robots.
2. **Enhanced Generalizability:** The framework enables the learning of policies that generalize to unseen object configurations and varying environments, moving beyond the limitations of rigid reference-motion replay. This is crucial for humanoids operating in dynamic and unstructured real-world settings, where encountering novel situations is common.
3. **Robust Real-World Deployment:** The demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, coupled with robust closed-loop execution, autonomous failure recovery, and stability under external perturbations, addresses a major challenge in robotics. This increases the practicality and reliability of deploying humanoid robots in real-world applications.
4. **Reduced Engineering Effort:** The unified mimic-style reward design and automated data processing pipeline minimize the need for heavy task-specific reward engineering, allowing researchers and developers to focus more on broader task definitions rather than intricate reward functions.
5. **Bridging Data and Physics Gaps:** SUGAR successfully bridges the gap between imperfect, kinematically derived data from human videos and the requirements of physically plausible robot dynamics. The progressive refinement process transforms noisy observations into usable training signals, making previously unusable data sources viable for robot learning.
6. **Advancement Towards General-Purpose Humanoids:** By enabling humanoids to learn complex, whole-body loco-manipulation skills that integrate locomotion, balance, and contact-rich manipulation, SUGAR contributes to the long-term goal of developing truly general-purpose humanoid assistants capable of interacting effectively with their environment.
7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the need for fine-grained skill acquisition, improved data efficiency, and the integration of visual and language inputs, clearly outline avenues for future research. SUGAR provides a robust foundation upon which these advanced capabilities can be built, potentially leading to more sophisticated and intuitive humanoid robot interactions.

In summary, SUGAR offers a practical and scalable solution for endowing humanoids with versatile loco-manipulation skills, thereby advancing the field closer to realizing autonomous, general-purpose humanoid robotics in real-world scenarios.
