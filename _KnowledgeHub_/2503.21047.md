# World Model Agents with Change-Based Intrinsic Motivation

> [!summary] Summary
> This study from the University of Groningen adapted the Change-Based Exploration Transfer (CBET) framework for the model-based DreamerV3 agent, evaluating its performance against the model-free IMPALA across Minigrid and Crafter environments. The research found that CBET provided a modest performance improvement for DreamerV3 in Crafter but was detrimental in Minigrid, highlighting the context-dependent effectiveness of intrinsic motivation strategies.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning agents frequently struggle in environments with sparse rewards, where infrequent feedback hinders efficient learning and exploration.
- Existing intrinsic motivation and transfer learning strategies require evaluation with modern, sample-efficient algorithms, especially model-based ones.
- The Change-Based Exploration Transfer (CBET) framework had primarily been evaluated with model-free algorithms, leaving its compatibility with world model agents unexplored.

## Method

- Adapted the ==Change-Based Exploration Transfer (CBET)== framework for compatibility with world model agents like **DreamerV3**, using two distinct instances of the agent's ==world model== and ==policy network==.
- Conducted comparative empirical evaluations of **DreamerV3** and **IMPALA**, both with and without CBET, in sparse reward environments (Minigrid and Crafter).
- Evaluated agent performance under ==tabula rasa== conditions (combined ==intrinsic and extrinsic rewards==) and ==transfer learning== conditions (==intrinsic pre-training==, ==extrinsic fine-tuning==).

## Results

- In the Crafter environment, CBET resulted in a modest improvement in mean extrinsic returns for DreamerV3, which consistently outperformed IMPALA in both tabula rasa and transfer learning settings.
- In the Minigrid environment, CBET negatively impacted DreamerV3's performance, leading to reduced mean extrinsic returns and increased variance, with IMPALA achieving higher extrinsic returns during tabula rasa training.
- The adaptation of CBET for DreamerV3 doubled VRAM requirements, and DreamerV3's performance in Crafter was found to be sensitive to the planning ratio, suggesting higher planning ratios might diminish the impact of intrinsic rewards.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The effectiveness of the CBET intrinsic motivation framework is highly context-dependent, yielding benefits for DreamerV3 in complex environments like Crafter but detrimental effects in simpler environments like Minigrid.
> - Even state-of-the-art model-based algorithms such as DreamerV3 can converge to suboptimal policies in specific environments (e.g., Minigrid), where model-free methods like IMPALA may achieve better performance.
> - Adapting transfer learning methods for complex model-based agents by employing multiple world models, as done with CBET for DreamerV3, can significantly increase computational resource requirements, specifically doubling VRAM.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) frequently encounters challenges in environments characterized by sparse rewards, where agents receive infrequent feedback, hindering efficient learning and exploration. This issue necessitates strategies that can either generate internal learning signals or leverage previously acquired knowledge to accelerate the learning process.

Two primary approaches have been developed to address sparse reward problems:
1. **Intrinsic Motivation:** This involves generating rewards internally within the agent, independent of explicit environmental feedback. Examples include:
 - **Count-based methods:** Reward agents for visiting novel or infrequently seen states.
 - **View-based methods:** Encourage exploration of unexplored environmental regions based on an internal map.
 - **Curiosity-driven methods:** Reward agents for making surprising predictions about future states or rewards.
2. **Transfer Learning:** This paradigm involves reusing learned skills or knowledge from one task or environment to accelerate learning in a new, often related, task. This minimizes the need for extensive exploration from scratch.

Change-Based Exploration Transfer (CBET) is a method that integrates both intrinsic motivation and transfer learning. It was designed to address sparse feedback by leveraging a pre-trained model to identify "interesting" interactions, which then guides a task-specific model to optimize extrinsic rewards. Prior evaluations of CBET have primarily focused on model-free RL algorithms, specifically IMPALA.

Recent advancements in RL have introduced more sophisticated algorithms. DreamerV3, a state-of-the-art model-based RL algorithm, has demonstrated high performance in complex sparse reward environments, such as achieving the first diamond from scratch in Minecraft. Its ability to simulate environment dynamics through a world model allows for higher sample efficiency compared to model-free approaches. However, the compatibility and effectiveness of CBET with world model agents like DreamerV3, which operate fundamentally differently from model-free algorithms, remained largely unexplored. This research aims to bridge this gap by adapting CBET for world model architectures and empirically evaluating its impact on DreamerV3 in comparison to IMPALA in various sparse reward settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to adapt the Change-Based Exploration Transfer (CBET) framework to accommodate world model agents, specifically DreamerV3, for use in transfer learning scenarios. This adaptation aimed to enable a comprehensive evaluation of CBET's effectiveness beyond its previous application in model-free algorithms.

Building upon this, the study pursued several key empirical objectives:
1. To compare the performance of DreamerV3 agents, both with and without the CBET mechanism, in sparse reward environments.
2. To compare the performance of IMPALA agents, both with and without the CBET mechanism, in the same sparse reward environments.
3. To conduct these comparisons in two distinct experimental settings:
 - **Tabula Rasa:** Where agents are trained from scratch using a combination of intrinsic and extrinsic rewards.
 - **Transfer Learning:** Where agents are pre-trained in an exploration environment using intrinsic rewards, followed by fine-tuning in a task environment using extrinsic rewards.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several considerations within the broader RL landscape:
- **Addressing Sparse Rewards:** The persistent challenge of sparse rewards in RL environments necessitates robust exploration strategies. Intrinsic motivation and transfer learning are recognized as promising solutions, and CBET combines these.
- **Evaluating CBET with Modern Algorithms:** CBET had primarily been evaluated with the IMPALA algorithm. Given the emergence of more advanced and sample-efficient algorithms like DreamerV3, it was important to investigate if CBET's benefits extended to these different architectural paradigms.
- **Understanding Algorithm-Environment Interactions:** Assessing how different algorithms (model-free vs. model-based) and exploration strategies (with/without CBET) perform across diverse sparse reward environments (Crafter vs. Minigrid) can provide insights into their respective strengths and limitations. The potential for intrinsic motivation strategies to have varied impacts based on the specific environment and agent architecture was a key investigative point.
- **Advancing World Model Capabilities:** By adapting CBET for world models, the researchers aimed to explore methods for enhancing the exploration capabilities of powerful model-based agents like DreamerV3, potentially improving their ability to solve complex, real-world tasks.

Implicitly, the researchers sought to determine if integrating an intrinsic motivation and transfer learning mechanism like CBET could generally improve the performance of modern, high-performing RL agents, or if its impact is contingent upon specific environmental characteristics or agent architectures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a comparative experimental approach, evaluating two distinct reinforcement learning algorithms—IMPALA and DreamerV3—both with and without the integration of the Change-Based Exploration Transfer (CBET) framework. The evaluation was conducted across two sparse reward environments, Minigrid and Crafter, under both tabula rasa and transfer learning conditions.

#### 3.1. Algorithms

- **IMPALA (Importance Weighted Actor-Learner Architectures):** This is a distributed, model-free RL algorithm. It utilizes a centralized learner that processes experiences from multiple parallel actors. IMPALA addresses off-policy learning challenges using V-trace, allowing it to achieve high throughput. While effective in complex environments, it typically requires substantial environment interactions for robust policy learning.
- **DreamerV3:** A state-of-the-art model-based RL algorithm. Its core component is a world model, specifically a Recurrent State-Space Model (RSSM), trained to represent the latent state of the environment as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). This world model enables DreamerV3 to simulate future trajectories and predict rewards, allowing it to learn policies with significantly higher sample efficiency than model-free approaches by reducing direct interaction with the actual environment.

#### 3.2. CBET Framework and Adaptation

- **Original CBET:** The original CBET framework introduced a policy transfer mechanism for model-free agents. It uses an intrinsic reward, $r_i(s)$, defined as $1 / (n(s) + n(c))$, where $n(s)$ is the visitation count of state $s$, and $n(c)$ is a count of environmental changes from the previous state. Pseudocounts and state hashing were used to estimate novelty and handle complex states. The intrinsic counts were randomly reset with probability $p \le 1 - \gamma_i$ to prevent vanishing rewards. For policy transfer, the task policy $\pi_{TASK}(s, a)$ was a softmax combination of an intrinsically pre-trained policy network $f_i$ (fixed) and an extrinsically fine-tuned policy network $f_e$.
- **Intrinsic Reward Mechanism (Adopted):** The intrinsic reward formulation from the original CBET, including pseudocounts, state hashing, and random count resets, was adopted for this study.
- **CBET Adaptation for DreamerV3:** The original CBET policy transfer (Equation 2 in the paper) was designed for model-free agents and was not directly applicable to DreamerV3 due to the tight coupling between DreamerV3's policy network and its world model. The authors proposed an adaptation for world model agents (Equation 3): $\pi_{TASK}(x, a) = \sigma(f_i(w_i(x), a) + f_e(w_e(x), a))$. This involves using two distinct instances of DreamerV3, each with its own world model ($w_i$, $w_e$) and policy network ($f_i$, $f_e$). The task policy combines the outputs of these two policy networks, which now take latent states generated by their respective world models from the observation $x$. This modification operates at a high level, intended to be applicable across diverse model-based architectures.

#### 3.3. Environments

Two sparse reward environments were selected:
- **Minigrid Worlds:** Procedurally generated, grid-based environments requiring agents to navigate, collect keys, and unlock doors. Rewards are sparse, only provided upon goal completion. The agent has limited visibility (7x7 grid in front).
 - **Task Environment:** 'Unlock' (locate key, unlock door in a two-room layout).
 - **Exploration Environment:** 'Doorkey' (similar to 'Unlock' but requires reaching an additional green square).
- **Crafter:** A procedurally generated grid world focusing on resource gathering, item crafting, and achieving specific goals. Rewards are sparse, granted for completing 22 achievements of varying difficulty. It features partial observability and episodes end only upon agent death. Crafter has a standardized budget of one million environment frames for performance comparison. Transfer learning pre-training was done on a fixed seed, with assessment on randomized seeds.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Tabula Rasa Experiments:**
 - Computational budget: 3 million steps in Minigrid, 1 million steps in Crafter.
 - Runs: 5 training runs for each algorithm-environment combination (20 total experiments).
 - Total reward: Computed as $r_t(s) = r_e(s) + \alpha \cdot r_i(s)$, where $\alpha$ is the intrinsic strength coefficient, tuned via grid search (0.001-0.005 range).
- **Transfer Learning Experiments:**
 - Pre-training: 1 million steps in the exploration environment (using intrinsic rewards only).
 - Fine-tuning: 1 million steps in the task environment (using extrinsic rewards only).
 - Runs: Single run for each algorithm and environment combination due to computational constraints. Only CBET variants were used.
- **Hyperparameters:** Minimal tuning, primarily using default settings from original CBET and DreamerV3 papers. IMPALA's actors were reduced to 8. DreamerV3 used a planning ratio of 64 and an XL configuration (200M parameters).
- **Computational Resources:** Experiments were conducted on a high-performance computing cluster using Nvidia V100 GPUs and Intel Xeon Gold 6150 CPUs.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Cumulative extrinsic reward (return). Evaluation episodes (8 per run) were scheduled every 10,000 steps. Results were presented using a rolling average of 200,000 steps with standard error.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded differentiated outcomes for DreamerV3 and IMPALA agents, both with and without the CBET mechanism, across the Minigrid and Crafter environments.

#### 4.1. Tabula Rasa Experiments

- **Crafter Environment:**
 - CBET implementation resulted in a modest improvement in mean extrinsic returns for DreamerV3 compared to the standard DreamerV3 algorithm.
 - DreamerV3, both with and without CBET, demonstrated superior performance over IMPALA in terms of extrinsic returns.
- **Minigrid Environment:**
 - CBET negatively impacted DreamerV3's performance, leading to reduced mean extrinsic returns and a substantial increase in result variance.
 - DreamerV3 converged to a suboptimal policy in Minigrid, with IMPALA achieving higher extrinsic returns, indicating IMPALA's better performance in this specific environment.

#### 4.2. Transfer Learning Experiments (CBET Variants Only)

- **Minigrid Environment:**
 - DreamerV3 initially obtained near-zero returns for the first 200,000 steps of fine-tuning.
 - IMPALA demonstrated better initial performance, outperforming DreamerV3 during this early phase.
 - DreamerV3 subsequently recovered from its initial performance deficit and eventually surpassed IMPALA in terms of extrinsic returns later in the fine-tuning process.
- **Crafter Environment:**
 - DreamerV3 consistently outperformed IMPALA throughout the transfer learning experiment, maintaining higher extrinsic returns.

#### 4.3. Additional Observations

- **Impact of CBET:** The effectiveness of CBET with DreamerV3 was not uniform; it showed a positive influence in Crafter but a detrimental effect in Minigrid.
- **Algorithm Performance by Environment:** DreamerV3 outperformed IMPALA in Crafter, aligning with its reputation for state-of-the-art performance in complex environments. However, IMPALA surpassed DreamerV3 in Minigrid during tabula rasa training, and during the initial phase of transfer learning, suggesting environment-specific challenges for DreamerV3.
- **DreamerV3's Suboptimal Policy in Minigrid:** The finding that DreamerV3 converged to a suboptimal policy in Minigrid contrasted with its typical high performance, highlighting that even advanced algorithms can have specific environmental limitations.
- **Pre-training Effect on DreamerV3 in Minigrid:** The near-zero initial returns in Minigrid's transfer learning scenario suggested that pre-training DreamerV3 with intrinsic CBET rewards in the 'Doorkey' exploration environment did not immediately yield a policy that effectively maximized extrinsic rewards in the 'Unlock' task environment.
- **Computational Constraints:** The adaptation of CBET for DreamerV3 doubled the VRAM requirements due to the need for two separate world models and policy networks, posing a significant computational burden.
- **Crafter Performance Discrepancy:** Both standard and CBET-augmented DreamerV3 models underperformed compared to reported results in the original DreamerV3 paper for Crafter. This was attributed to a lower planning ratio used in the experiments due to hardware limitations.
- **Planning Ratio Impact:** A grid search indicated that the performance gap between DreamerV3 with and without CBET in Crafter appeared to decrease as the planning ratio increased, suggesting that higher planning might make intrinsic rewards less critical.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of intrinsic motivation and transfer learning in modern reinforcement learning by empirically evaluating the Change-Based Exploration Transfer (CBET) framework with a state-of-the-art model-based agent, DreamerV3.

The key significance of the findings lies in demonstrating that the impact of intrinsic motivation and transfer learning techniques, specifically CBET, is not universally beneficial across different algorithms and environments.
- **Context-Dependent Effectiveness of CBET:** The study reveals that CBET can enhance DreamerV3's performance in complex environments like Crafter, but can be detrimental in simpler environments like Minigrid. This variability suggests that the behaviors promoted by CBET may not always align with specific task objectives, particularly when combined with different model architectures. This finding is crucial for researchers and practitioners, indicating that the choice of exploration strategy must be carefully considered in relation to the agent's architecture and the characteristics of the target environment.
- **DreamerV3's Environmental Specificity:** The observation that DreamerV3, despite its general state-of-the-art performance, can converge to suboptimal policies in certain environments (e.g., Minigrid) where a model-free algorithm like IMPALA excels, highlights the importance of evaluating even advanced algorithms across a diverse set of environments. This adds to a narrow set of documented scenarios where DreamerV3's advantages do not hold. It implies that while model-based approaches offer sample efficiency, their robustness or suitability for all types of environments may still have limitations.
- **Computational Challenges of Model-Based Transfer:** The adaptation of CBET for DreamerV3 by employing two distinct world models significantly increased computational resource requirements (doubling VRAM). This identifies a practical barrier to implementing such transfer learning methods with complex model-based agents, underscoring the need for more resource-efficient transfer learning techniques, such as fractional transfer methods, in future work.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper identifies several promising avenues for future research:
 - **Intrinsic Reward Scheduling:** Developing dynamic schedulers for the intrinsic strength coefficient ($\alpha$) could optimize the exploration-exploitation balance over time, akin to learning rate schedulers, thereby reducing extensive hyperparameter tuning.
 - **Deeper Interaction Analysis:** Further investigation into the intricate interactions between intrinsic rewards and transfer learning methods in more complex environments could yield deeper insights.
 - **Algorithmic Optimizations for DreamerV3:** Reducing the computational demands of DreamerV3 and exploring modifications to enable asynchronous training with multiple actors could enhance its scalability and applicability.

Overall, this research provides empirical evidence that the effectiveness of exploration strategies is highly dependent on the interplay between the algorithm, the environment, and the specific implementation of intrinsic motivation. It contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how to optimize performance in sparse reward RL environments, moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach and informing the development of more robust and versatile RL models.
