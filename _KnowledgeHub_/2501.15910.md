# The Sample Complexity of Online Reinforcement Learning: A Multi-model Perspective

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems and Inria Paris introduce a multi-model framework for online reinforcement learning in continuous state-action spaces with nonlinear dynamics and non-episodic settings. The framework provides frequentist policy-regret guarantees across finite, general, and parametrized model classes, while ensuring closed-loop stability.

## Problem

- Achieving sublinear policy regret in online, non-episodic reinforcement learning for continuous state-action spaces with general nonlinear dynamics.
- Addressing the exploration-exploitation dilemma in systems where dynamics are unknown and state resets are not possible, leading to time-correlated information.
- Providing robust, nonasymptotic frequentist performance guarantees for algorithms over a finite time horizon, moving beyond asymptotic or Bayesian guarantees.

## Method

- A suite of algorithms employs ==Hedge-type updates==, sampling models from a distribution based on their one-step prediction error.
- Certainty-equivalent control is applied using the selected model's policy, augmented with an explicit Gaussian excitation signal to ensure sufficient information for model discrimination.
- Model identification is decoupled from control policy synthesis, enabling pre-computation of feedback policies offline and reducing online computational burden.

## Results

- Achieved policy regret bounded by `O(d_u ln(N)/∆)` for finite model sets, `O(sqrt(N))` for general bounded L-Lipschitz functions, and `O(sqrt(d_u N p))` for parametrically defined models, matching known theoretical lower bounds up to logarithmic factors.
- Demonstrated rapid convergence of the selected model to the true (or best approximating) system dynamics, with the probability of misidentification decaying at `1/(k-M)^2` for iteration `k`.
- Ensured uniform boundedness of the second moment of state trajectories `E[|x_k|^2]` over time, providing guarantees for closed-loop stability and benign transient behaviors even under exploration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A multi-model perspective, integrating elements from Hedge-type updates and posterior sampling, effectively addresses the exploration-exploitation dilemma in continuous nonlinear systems.
> - The explicit addition of excitation during online learning is crucial for satisfying persistence of excitation conditions and enabling rapid, provable model identification.
> - By leveraging Lyapunov stability analysis and concentration inequalities, sublinear policy regret and uniform boundedness of state trajectories can be guaranteed for broad classes of nonlinear systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of online reinforcement learning (RL), specifically addressing challenges in continuous state and action spaces with nonlinear dynamical systems in a non-episodic setting. The work positions itself at the intersection of online decision-making, reinforcement learning, and adaptive control.

Traditional reinforcement learning often operates in episodic settings or with discrete state-action spaces. Extensions to continuous, non-episodic scenarios, particularly with nonlinear dynamics, introduce complexities such as continuous learning without state resets and time-correlated information, which complicate the direct application of standard statistical tools.

Existing paradigms in online RL include:
- **Posterior Sampling Reinforcement Learning (PSRL):** Algorithms like Thompson sampling (Osband et al., 2013) have shown promise, initially for tabular Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) in episodic settings. Subsequent work extended these to non-episodic settings and continuous spaces, often relying on Bayesian guarantees or strong mixing assumptions. This paper distinguishes itself by providing frequentist policy-regret guarantees in a non-episodic context for continuous state-action systems, introducing explicit excitation, and operating under persistence of excitation assumptions, which can be weaker than the mixing assumptions found in some prior work.
- **Optimism in the Face of Uncertainty (OFU):** This principle, widely used in multi-armed bandits, has been applied to RL. OFU methods typically maintain confidence sets for models and select policies that are "optimistic." However, these approaches can be computationally intensive due to the iterative computation of confidence sets and optimistic policies, and their complexity measures (e.g., eluder dimension) can be challenging to bound beyond linear MDPs. The presented work offers an alternative by decoupling model identification from certainty-equivalent control, simplifying policy evaluation and using established complexity measures like packing numbers.
- **Adaptive Control:** Historically, adaptive control has focused on asymptotic stability and boundedness in deterministic or slowly time-varying systems. This paper expands beyond asymptotic guarantees to characterize nonasymptotic performance in stochastic settings. It also improves upon regret rates seen in switching control methods for multi-model systems.
- **Online Optimization:** Other approaches involve direct online optimization over policy classes (e.g., disturbance-action policies). These often assume prior knowledge of system dynamics, removing the exploration-exploitation dilemma central to RL, and are more akin to online/stochastic optimization.
- **Decision-Estimation Coefficient:** This theoretical framework provides abstract characterizations of statistical complexity and regret lower bounds for interactive decision-making. While general, it does not directly address specific challenges in online non-episodic RL with continuous nonlinear dynamics, such as dependent states and stability, and its applicability to nonlinear dynamics can be complex.

A critical gap identified in the literature is the achievement of sublinear policy regret in online, non-episodic settings with a broad range of nonlinear dynamics. Previous works often impose stronger structural assumptions (e.g., contraction, linear features, or specific parametrizations) or yield less favorable regret rates. This paper aims to address this gap by providing a suite of algorithms with provable frequentist regret bounds for various model classes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to analyze the sample complexity of online reinforcement learning in challenging scenarios involving nonlinear dynamical systems with continuous state and action spaces, operating in a general non-episodic setting.

The key motivations driving this work include:

- **Addressing the Exploration-Exploitation Dilemma:** In online reinforcement learning, an agent must simultaneously learn the unknown dynamics of a system (exploration) and optimize its performance (exploitation). These objectives are often in conflict, particularly when the system dynamics are unknown *a priori*.
- **Handling Non-Episodic Settings:** The paper focuses on scenarios where the decision-maker learns continuously without the ability to reset the system state. This introduces complexities as observed information is correlated over time, necessitating robust analytical tools.
- **Extending to Nonlinear Dynamics and Continuous Spaces:** The current research frontier in RL is moving from linear to nonlinear dynamics and from discrete to continuous state-action spaces. The authors aim to provide algorithms and theoretical guarantees that are applicable to these more general and realistic settings.
- **Providing Strong Theoretical Guarantees:** A key motivation is to deliver nonasymptotic, frequentist policy-regret guarantees. Such guarantees are crucial for understanding the performance of learning algorithms over a finite time horizon and for demonstrating their robustness in various environments, in contrast to Bayesian guarantees which hold in expectation over a prior distribution of environments.
- **Developing Practical and Interpretable Algorithms:** The authors aim for algorithms that are simple to implement, can incorporate prior knowledge (e.g., a set of candidate models), and exhibit stable transient behaviors.
- **Generalizing Existing Results:** The work seeks to recover and extend known sample-complexity results for simpler settings (e.g., linear time-invariant dynamical systems) to more general nonlinear contexts, while improving on regret rates obtained by other approaches like switching control.
- **Decoupling Model Identification and Control:** A specific motivation is to simplify the overall problem by separating the tasks of identifying the best model from the class of candidates and applying a certainty-equivalent control policy based on that model. This separation can enable the use of efficient, potentially offline, policy evaluation techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on a suite of online reinforcement learning algorithms built upon principles from Hedge-type updates and posterior sampling reinforcement learning, adapted for continuous state-action spaces and nonlinear dynamics. The approach is analyzed across three distinct settings for the dynamical system model class:

**Core Algorithmic Principles:**
- **Model Selection via Softmax over Prediction Error:** The algorithms maintain a measure of one-step prediction error (`s_i_k`) for each candidate model `f_i`. This error is defined as a sum of squared differences between observed next states and model predictions, normalized by `1 + |(x_j, u_j)|^2 / b^2`. This term can be interpreted as the negative log-likelihood under Gaussian process noise. The decision-maker samples a model (or parameter `θ`) from a distribution proportional to `exp(-ηs_i_k)`, where `η` is a learning rate. This is analogous to Hedge (multiplicative weights) updates in online learning.
- **Certainty-Equivalent Control with Excitation:** Once a model `i_k` (or parameter `θ_k`) is selected, the corresponding "certainty-equivalent" feedback policy `μ_i_k(x_k)` is applied. To ensure sufficient information gathering and rapid convergence of the posterior, an explicit excitation signal (`n_uk` sampled from a Gaussian distribution) is added to the control action: `u_k = μ_i_k(x_k) + n_uk`. This excitation is critical for satisfying persistence of excitation conditions.
- **Intermittent Model Updates:** The model (or parameter) selection is not performed at every step but every `M` steps. In between these updates, the selected model remains fixed. This ensures that the system is sufficiently excited by the chosen policy to allow for meaningful model discrimination.
- **Separation Principle:** The algorithms explicitly decouple optimal model identification from certainty-equivalent control. This means that the feedback policies (`μ_i`) for candidate models can be pre-computed offline (e.g., via dynamic programming, model predictive control, or proximal policy optimization with a simulator), simplifying the online computational burden.

**Analytical Framework:**
The analysis relies on several mathematical tools and assumptions:
- **Lyapunov Stability Analysis:** A key technique involves using a "cost-to-go" function `V(x)` as a Lyapunov function to analyze the stability and performance of the system, particularly its expected cumulative loss (regret).
- **Bellman-type Inequalities:** Assumptions about `V(x)` and the stage cost `l(x,u)` relate the current value to expected future values and costs, providing bounds essential for regret analysis. These are strengthened in the infinite/parametric settings to ensure stability under small model/policy perturbations.
- **Smoothness Assumptions:** Lipschitz continuity for dynamics, policies, stage costs, and cost-to-go functions are assumed to enable quantitative analysis of system behavior and bounds on errors.
- **Persistence of Excitation:** This is a crucial assumption, adapted for each setting, guaranteeing that the applied control actions generate enough information to distinguish between different candidate models. It implies that the expected squared difference between candidate models (or between a candidate and the true model) is sufficiently large when driven by the exploration noise.
- **Concentration of Measure Arguments:** These are used to bound the probability of misidentifying the true model, showing that this probability decays rapidly.
- **Packing Numbers:** For general function classes (Setting S2), packing numbers are employed as a complexity measure. The analysis involves constructing an `ϵ`-cover of the continuous model space, effectively reducing the problem to a finite-model setting in an online fashion.

**Three Settings for Dynamical Systems:**

1. **Setting S1: Finite Set of Nonlinear Candidate Models (`F = {f_1..., f_m}`):**
 - The algorithm directly samples from the discrete set of `m` models.
 - Regret analysis focuses on the interplay between `N`, `m`, and a model separation constant `∆`.

2. **Setting S2: General Class of Dynamical Systems (Bounded Set in a Normed Vector Space `F`):**
 - This generalizes S1 to infinite (possibly non-parametric) model classes (e.g., all bounded L-Lipschitz functions).
 - The algorithm dynamically constructs an `ϵ`-packing (`F_ϵ_k`) of the relevant part of `F` around the currently best-fitting model (`f*`). The size of this packing, `m(ϵ)`, is bounded by the packing number of `F`.
 - The analysis then proceeds similarly to S1 but incorporates the `ϵ` discretization width and `m(ϵ)`.

3. **Setting S3: Parametrized Models (`F = {f_θ | θ ∈ Ω}` with `Ω ⊂ R^p`):**
 - This setting is particularly relevant for modern machine learning architectures (e.g., neural networks, transformers).
 - The algorithm samples parameters `θ_k` from an approximate posterior distribution, which, for linear-in-parameter models (e.g., `f_θ(x,u) = φ(x,u)^T θ`), becomes a (truncated) Gaussian distribution, allowing for efficient updates via recursive least squares.
 - The regret analysis directly incorporates the parameter dimension `p`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several key findings regarding the sample complexity and performance of online reinforcement learning algorithms in challenging, non-episodic settings with continuous state-action spaces and nonlinear dynamics.

**Policy Regret Guarantees:**
The primary contribution is a suite of algorithms that provide nonasymptotic, frequentist policy-regret guarantees, structured across three different model class settings:

- **Setting S1 (Finite Set of Models):** For a finite set of `m` nonlinear candidate models, the proposed algorithm achieves a policy regret bounded by `O(d_u ln(N)/∆ + d_u ln(m)/∆)`, where `N` is the time horizon, `d_u` is the input dimension, and `∆` characterizes the separation between models. This logarithmic dependence on the number of models (`m`) and the time horizon (`N`) aligns with established results in online learning for discrete choices.
- **Setting S2 (Bounded Set in a Normed Vector Space):** For a general class of dynamical systems represented as a bounded set in a normed vector space, the policy regret is bounded by `O(Nϵ^2 + d_u ln(N)/ϵ^2 + d_u ln(m(ϵ))/ϵ^2)`. Here, `ϵ` is a user-specified discretization width, and `m(ϵ)` is the packing number, which quantifies the complexity of the function class. The authors note that for a class of bounded L-Lipschitz functions, this translates to `o(N)` regret (no-regret learning), with specific examples (e.g., `d_x = d_u = 1`) yielding `O(sqrt(N))` regret.
- **Setting S3 (Parametrized Models):** When the dynamics are parametrized by a compact, real-valued set of `p` parameters (e.g., neural networks), the algorithm achieves a policy regret of `O(sqrt(d_u N p))`. This result notably recovers earlier sample-complexity bounds derived for linear time-invariant dynamical systems (e.g., Linear Quadratic Regulators), where `p` would correspond to the number of system parameters (e.g., `d_x^2 + d_x d_u`).

**Model Identification and Stability:**
- **Rapid Model Convergence:** The analysis demonstrates that the probability of misidentifying the true system dynamics (or the best approximating model) decays at a rate of `1/(k-M)^2` for iteration `k`. This rapid convergence implies that the sequence of chosen models will converge to the optimal model almost surely in finite time.
- **Bounded State Trajectories:** A significant finding for control-theoretic applications is the guarantee that, provided the stage cost satisfies a lower bound (e.g., `l(x, u) ≥ L_l |x|^2/2`), the algorithms ensure boundedness of the second moment of state trajectories (`E[|x_k|^2]`) uniformly over time. This indicates benign transient behaviors and closed-loop stability, which are critical in physical systems.

**Optimality and Generalization:**
- **Near-Optimal Regret Rates:** The derived regret bounds are stated to be optimal up to logarithmic factors, matching known theoretical lower bounds for online regression problems that are embedded within this framework (e.g., `Ω(d_u ln(m))` for S1 and `Ω(sqrt(N d_u p))` for S3).
- **Generalizability:** The framework applies to a broad range of nonlinear systems and does not rely on strong structural assumptions like contraction or linear representations of nonlinear features, which were prerequisites in some prior works achieving sublinear regret.

**Empirical Validation (Numerical Examples in Appendix B):**
- **Linear Time-Invariant System:** Simulations on a 20-dimensional linear system demonstrated rapid convergence to near-optimal steady-state performance (within approximately 25 steps) for both finite and parametrized model settings. The algorithms scaled favorably, with performance being consistent even when the number of candidate models increased from 10 to 10,000.
- **Nonlinear Pendulum-on-a-Cart System:** The algorithm successfully performed a swing-up and balancing task for a nonlinear system, even when the true dynamics were not contained within the set of 500 candidate models. This showcased robustness to model mismatch and the ability to achieve complex control objectives in practice.
- **Computational Efficiency:** For nonlinear systems, the computational bottleneck was identified in the policy evaluation step (e.g., solving optimal control problems via MPC), which is independent of the number of candidate models, rather than the posterior sampling process itself. This suggests scalability with respect to model complexity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the fields of reinforcement learning and control, with potential impacts on both theoretical understanding and practical applications:

- **Advancing Online Reinforcement Learning Theory:** The work provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for online reinforcement learning in complex, non-episodic settings with continuous state-action spaces and nonlinear dynamics. By establishing frequentist, nonasymptotic policy-regret guarantees, it offers a deeper understanding of the learning performance over finite horizons, which is crucial for real-world deployments.
- **Broader Applicability:** The suite of algorithms and their analyses accommodate diverse classes of dynamical systems—from finite sets of candidate models to infinite, bounded function classes, and parametrically defined models (including neural networks). This generality expands the scope of provably efficient RL beyond the more restrictive assumptions (e.g., linearity, strong structural properties like contraction) often made in prior work.
- **Bridging Disparate Fields:** The research successfully integrates insights and tools from online learning (Hedge updates), control theory (Lyapunov functions, dissipativity, persistence of excitation), and statistical learning theory (packing numbers). This interdisciplinary approach enriches each field and opens new avenues for cross-pollination of ideas and techniques.
- **Practical Algorithm Design:** The proposed algorithms are conceptually simple, can naturally incorporate prior domain knowledge (via candidate model sets), and separate model identification from control policy synthesis. This decoupling allows for the use of powerful, potentially offline, control design techniques (e.g., model predictive control, dynamic programming, PPO with a simulator), making the online learning process more tractable and efficient in practice.
- **Improved Performance Guarantees:** The derived regret bounds are optimal up to logarithmic factors and demonstrate significant improvements over some existing methods, such as adaptive switching control, particularly in the finite model setting. The recovery of `O(sqrt(N))` type regret bounds for nonlinear systems (or `O(sqrt(N p))` for parametric models) extends state-of-the-art results from linear quadratic regulators to more complex scenarios.
- **Guaranteed Stability and Boundedness:** The explicit demonstration of closed-loop stability and boundedness of state trajectories, especially under non-optimal policies during exploration, addresses a critical concern in adaptive control and the safe deployment of learning-based control systems.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper identifies several promising areas for future work, including the development of computationally tractable algorithms for general infinite model classes (S2), designing more sophisticated exploration strategies beyond additive Gaussian noise, and extending the framework to handle partial observability and non-additive noise, further solidifying the multi-model perspective on online decision-making.

In summary, this work represents a substantial theoretical and algorithmic advancement in online reinforcement learning, providing a robust framework with strong guarantees for controlling complex, unknown nonlinear systems in continuous, non-episodic environments.
