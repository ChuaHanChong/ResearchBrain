# ARMOR: Robust Reinforcement Learning-based Control for UAVs under Physical Attacks

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of British Columbia and UC Berkeley developed ARMOR, an adaptive robust reinforcement learning controller for UAVs that maintains safe operation and completes missions despite adversarial sensor manipulation. It achieves this by learning robust latent state representations through a two-stage training framework, resulting in an 88% mission success rate under various physical attacks and superior zero-shot generalization to unseen attack types.

## Problem

- Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) rely on onboard sensors for control, but these sensors are vulnerable to physical attacks (e.g., GPS spoofing, gyroscope tampering).
- Corrupted sensor data leads to inaccurate state estimates, causing unsafe control actions, trajectory deviations, and potential UAV crashes.
- Existing safe RL methods are ineffective when state perception itself is manipulated, and adversarial training incurs high costs and poor generalization to novel attack patterns.

## Method

- **ARMOR** proposes a model-free ==reinforcement learning== controller that explicitly designs robust latent state representations to withstand physical attacks on UAV sensors.
- It employs a two-stage offline training framework: a 'teacher encoder' leverages privileged attack information in simulation to learn robust latent states, and a 'student encoder' then approximates these states using only historical sensor data for real-world deployment.
- The same RL control policy is reused across both stages, accepting the robust latent representation as input, which enhances robustness and training efficiency.

## Results

- ARMOR achieved an average mission success rate of **88%** and 0 crashes across five types of physical attacks (GPS, gyroscope, accelerometer, magnetometer, optical flow), consistently outperforming the HRP baseline.
- The framework demonstrated significantly faster training convergence (approximately **2x** faster than RARL) under adversarial conditions while achieving comparable or slightly superior performance.
- ARMOR exhibited strong zero-shot generalization to unseen single and multi-sensor attack types, achieving up to **70%** success rates and reducing state drift by up to 10 times compared to RARL, which often failed (**0%** success).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the learning of robust latent state representations from the control policy enables greater resilience against physical sensor attacks.
> - Leveraging privileged information during an initial teacher training phase allows for efficient learning of robust control policies that can be transferred to a student model for practical deployment.
> - Utilizing historical sensor data within the student encoder allows it to implicitly infer and mitigate the effects of adversarial disturbances without direct knowledge of the attack.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) are integral to various applications, from logistics to surveillance, relying on onboard sensors for perception, navigation, and control. The integrity of these sensor measurements is critical for safe and reliable mission execution. However, these sensors are susceptible to physical attacks, such as GPS spoofing, gyroscope tampering with acoustic noise, and optical sensor spoofing with laser beams. Such attacks corrupt the UAV's state estimates, potentially leading to unsafe control actions, trajectory deviations, and mission failure or crashes.

Model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as an adaptive control approach for UAVs in complex environments. Nevertheless, RL-based controllers, being sensor-dependent, are also vulnerable to physical attacks. Existing safe RL methods, including shielding and control barrier functions (CBFs), primarily focus on ensuring safety under normal operating conditions by constraining actions or defining safe sets. These methods are generally ineffective against physical attacks that manipulate the UAV's perception of its state, causing it to execute unsafe actions while believing they are within safe limits. For instance, GPS spoofing can induce gradual deviations that, while appearing minor, accumulate to significant trajectory errors.

Another category of robust RL techniques, such as adversarial training, aims to learn policies resilient to disturbances by co-training a protagonist policy with an adversary that injects perturbations. While these methods improve robustness, they face limitations under physical attacks. These limitations include high training costs due to iterative adversarial scenario generation, a lack of generalizability to specific or novel attack patterns, and limited zero-shot effectiveness against previously unseen attacks. Robust Markov Decision Process (MDP) methods also develop conservative policies but are constrained by predefined uncertainty sets, potentially leading to overly conservative policies that compromise performance.

Prior work in representation learning for robotics has explored methods like contrastive learning and variational autoencoders (VAEs) to enhance control under normal conditions or to infer proprioceptive control using privileged information. However, these approaches have not primarily focused on developing robust latent state representations specifically optimized for control under adversarial perturbations or sensor manipulation.

ARMOR addresses this gap by proposing a model-free RL controller that explicitly designs robust latent state representations to withstand physical attacks. It aims to overcome the limitations of existing safe RL and adversarial training methods regarding attack resilience, training efficiency, and generalization to unseen attacks. By decoupling the learning process and leveraging privileged information during an initial training phase, ARMOR seeks to provide a more efficient and generalizable solution for robust UAV control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an attack-resilient, model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) controller, named ARMOR (Adaptive Robust Manipulation-Optimized State Representations), to enable robust and safe operation of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) even when subjected to adversarial sensor manipulation.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations regarding UAV operation and the limitations of current control strategies:

1. **Critical Reliance on Sensors and their Vulnerability**: UAVs are extensively deployed in diverse applications, from agriculture to emergency services, and their autonomous functions are entirely dependent on accurate data from onboard sensors (e.g., GPS for position, gyroscopes for orientation, accelerometers for velocity, magnetometers for heading, optical flow sensors for motion). However, these sensors are susceptible to physical attacks (e.g., GPS spoofing, acoustic noise injection into gyroscopes, electromagnetic interference for magnetometers, laser beam attacks on optical flow sensors). These attacks corrupt the physical state estimates of the UAV.

2. **Consequences of Corrupted State Estimates**: When sensor data is compromised, the UAV’s perception of its own state becomes inaccurate. This can lead to the generation of unsafe control actions, causing the UAV to deviate from its planned trajectory, become unstable, or even crash. The problem is exacerbated because the UAV's controller may interpret these corrupted states as valid, leading to actions that appear safe within its internal model but are, in reality, highly dangerous.

3. **Ineffectiveness of Existing Safe RL Methods against Physical Attacks**: Traditional safe RL techniques, such as shielding and control barrier functions (CBFs), are designed to enforce safety constraints under normal operational uncertainties or by preventing explicitly defined unsafe actions. They are not intrinsically designed to handle scenarios where the *perception* of the state itself is maliciously altered. Under physical attacks, these methods can fail because the controller might mistakenly perceive itself to be within safe boundaries, even as its true physical state deviates significantly.

4. **Limitations of Adversarial Training**: Adversarial training is a common approach to enhance robustness in RL, where a policy learns to perform tasks while an adversary attempts to disrupt it. However, for physical attacks, this approach exhibits several drawbacks:
 - **High Training Cost**: It typically requires iterative generation of adversarial scenarios, leading to substantial computational overhead and prolonged training times.
 - **Limited Generalizability**: Policies trained adversarially often learn to defend against specific attack patterns encountered during training, struggling to generalize to novel or previously unseen attack types.
 - **Lack of Zero-Shot Effectiveness**: They frequently lack the ability to effectively counter attacks that were not explicitly included in the training dataset from the outset.

Given these challenges, the research aims to achieve the following specific objectives:

- **Develop a Robust Latent State Representation**: Instead of directly using raw sensor observations, ARMOR aims to learn a lower-dimensional, robust latent representation of the UAV's physical state. This representation is designed to be resilient to physical attacks, allowing the control policy to operate effectively despite sensor corruption.
- **Establish a Two-Stage Offline Training Framework**:
 - **Stage 1 (Teacher Encoder)**: Train an RL policy using a "teacher encoder" that has access to privileged information about attacks (e.g., target sensor, magnitude of bias, attack duration). This stage leverages this ideal, attack-aware information to learn highly robust control policies and latent state representations efficiently.
 - **Stage 2 (Student Encoder)**: Develop a "student encoder" that, crucially, relies *only* on historical sensor data, without access to the privileged information. This student encoder is trained via supervised learning to approximate the robust latent states generated by the teacher encoder, thereby enabling real-world deployment.
- **Improve Generalization and Reduce Training Cost**: By decoupling the learning process and leveraging privileged information, ARMOR aims to improve the policy’s generalization to unseen attack scenarios and types, while simultaneously reducing the high training costs associated with iterative adversarial training methods.

Ultimately, the motivation is to enhance the safety and reliability of UAV operations in contested environments where physical sensor attacks are a realistic threat, providing a more practical and effective solution than current methodologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ARMOR employs a two-stage offline training framework designed to develop an attack-resilient, model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) controller for UAVs. The central idea is to learn a robust latent representation of the UAV’s physical state that can withstand adversarial sensor manipulation.

**UAV Control Design and Threat Model:**
The UAV control architecture comprises motion generation and tracking control, with trajectories defined in an inertial frame. Physical attacks introduce biases (b_t) in sensor measurements, causing the control policy π(·) to generate actions (a_t) that deviate the true state (o_t) from the reference trajectory (g(t)). A mission is considered unsafe if the position deviation (∆p_t) exceeds a safety threshold (ϵ). The threat model encompasses various physical attacks targeting GPS, gyroscopes, accelerometers, magnetometers, and optical flow sensors, which inject noise or malicious signals, leading to corrupted state estimates and potentially severe consequences.

**Stage I – Training with Privileged Information (Teacher Encoder):**
In the first stage, ARMOR assumes a fully observable simulation environment where a "teacher encoder" has access to both the UAV’s standard onboard sensor readings and "privileged information."

- **State Definition**: The full state s_t is defined as a tuple ⟨o_t, x_t⟩.
 - `o_t` represents the observable physical state of the UAV, including position, angular orientation, heading, acceleration, and linear/angular velocities.
 - `x_t` contains privileged information, such as the specific sensor under attack, the corresponding corrupted physical states, the magnitude of the injected bias, and the duration of the attack. This information is available only during this training phase.
- **Teacher Encoder Architecture**: The teacher encoder is implemented as a multi-head Variational Autoencoder (VAE). It maps the input state s_t = ⟨o_t, x_t⟩ to three outputs:
 - `¯l_t = (μ_t, σ_t)`: A robust latent representation of the UAV's state, characterized by its mean (μ_t) and variance (σ_t). The variance provides an uncertainty estimate.
 - `ŷ_t`: A predicted attack type, which is an auxiliary classification output. This encourages the latent space to capture attack-specific patterns.
 - `ŝ_t`: A reconstruction of the input s_t, used to evaluate the quality of the latent representations through reconstruction loss. The decoder for this reconstruction is discarded after training.
- **Loss Function**: The teacher encoder is trained by minimizing a combined loss function (`L_teacher`) that includes:
 - Reconstruction loss (`L_recon`): Measures the fidelity of the input reconstruction.
 - Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (`L_KL`): Regularizes the latent space, encouraging it to follow a prior distribution.
 - Auxiliary attack classification loss (`L_aux`): Penalizes incorrect attack type predictions.
- **RL Policy Training**: A control policy π(a_t|¯l_t) is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Crucially, the policy receives the latent representation `¯l_t` from the teacher encoder as its input, rather than raw sensor data. This design encourages the policy to rely entirely on the robust, attack-aware latent representation, thereby improving its resilience to sensor manipulation.
- **Reward Function**: The reward function is designed to incentivize task completion, safety, and stability. It rewards minimizing the distance to a target waypoint and penalizes deviations from the planned trajectory, excessive tilt (θ_t), and abrupt control actions (a_t).

**Stage II – Transfer Learning Adaptation (Student Encoder):**
The second stage addresses the practical constraint that privileged information (`x_t`) is unavailable in real-world deployment. A "student encoder" is introduced to approximate the teacher’s robust latent representation using only readily available onboard sensor data.

- **Student Encoder Architecture**: The student encoder is implemented as a Temporal Variational Autoencoder (TVAE) built with a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network. LSTMs are used to effectively model sequential dependencies in time-series sensor data.
- **Input**: The student encoder takes a sequence of historical UAV physical states in a sliding window (`H:= {o_{t-N}..., o_{t-1}}`) as input. This historical context allows the encoder to implicitly capture the impact of adversarial disturbances over time.
- **Outputs**: Similar to the teacher encoder, the student encoder outputs:
 - `l_t`: A latent representation (mean and variance) that approximates the teacher's `¯l_t`.
 - Attack-type prediction.
 - Reconstruction of the input sequence (during training).
- **Training**: The student encoder is trained via supervised learning, using the teacher encoder’s outputs as target values. The loss function (`L_student`) for the student encoder comprises three main terms:
 - `∥l_t(H_t) - ¯l_t(o_t, x_t)∥²`: Minimizes the L2 distance between the student's latent representation and the teacher's latent representation.
 - `∥a_t(l_t) - a_t(¯l_t)∥²`: Aligns the control actions produced by the RL policy when fed with the student's latent representation (`l_t`) versus the teacher's (`¯l_t`). This term is crucial for ensuring that the *same* RL policy can be effectively reused during deployment.
 - `L_attack`: Penalizes errors in the student's attack-type classification.
- **Deployment**: After training the student encoder, the RL control policy developed in Stage I remains unchanged. For online deployment, this policy takes `l_t` (the latent representation generated by the student encoder) as its input, enabling attack-resilient control without privileged information.

**Key Innovations**: ARMOR differentiates itself from previous work, such as Chen et al., by:
1. Ensuring the control policy relies *entirely* on the robust latent representation generated by the teacher encoder, rather than mixing it with raw observations, which enhances robustness.
2. Reusing a *single* control policy across both training and deployment stages, simplifying the training pipeline and improving sample efficiency compared to training separate policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ARMOR was conducted in a 3D quadcopter simulation environment using `gym-pybullet` and the PyBullet physics engine, simulating realistic UAV dynamics. The UAV's state included position, velocity, orientation, and body angular rates, while actions were defined by individual rotor thrusts. The objective was to reach a randomly sampled goal position in 3D space.

**Physical Attack Scenarios:**
ARMOR was tested against five types of physical attacks, simulated using RAVAGE, targeting GPS, gyroscope, accelerometer, magnetometer, and optical flow sensors. These attacks varied in bias type (drift, oscillatory, random), bias range, and duration.

**Comparison Baselines:**
The performance of ARMOR was compared against:
- **Baseline-RL**: A PPO policy directly processing high-dimensional physical state information without any encoder.
- **RARL (Robust Adversarial Reinforcement Learning)**: An adversarial training approach where a protagonist control policy learns against an adversary injecting disturbances.
- **HRP (Hybrid Recovery Policy)**: Combines a neural network policy with a stabilizing PID controller, with the learned policy operating within safe regions and deferring to the PID controller outside these regions.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
Performance was assessed using:
1. **Mission Success Rate**: Percentage of episodes where the UAV reached the goal within a 5m error margin.
2. **Crash Rate**: Percentage of episodes where the UAV’s state exceeded safety bounds, leading to termination.
3. **State Drift**: Mean absolute deviation in physical states from ideal states during attacks.

**A. ARMOR Training Performance**
- **Nominal Conditions (No Attack)**: Under no-attack scenarios, Baseline-RL, the Teacher Encoder policy (using privileged info), and the Student Encoder policy (using historical sensor data) all achieved comparable final episodic rewards (~2200 after 500,000 timesteps). This suggests that the encoder-based latent representations do not hinder optimal control learning in benign conditions.
- **Adversarial Conditions (Physical Attacks)**: In the presence of attacks, both the Teacher Encoder and Student Encoder policies demonstrated significantly faster convergence (approximately 2x faster) compared to RARL. RARL required nearly 1,200,000 timesteps to reach a reward of 2100, while ARMOR's policies achieved similar rewards in under 600,000 timesteps. This indicates that ARMOR’s two-stage training effectively accelerates learning and transfers robustness from the privileged learning phase to the online deployment phase without requiring iterative adversarial scenario generation.

**B. Effectiveness of ARMOR under Physical Attacks**
- **Qualitative Example (GPS Spoofing)**: Under a GPS spoofing attack, the Baseline-RL policy caused the UAV to deviate significantly from its intended path, resulting in a crash. In contrast, ARMOR enabled the UAV to maintain stable flight with minimal deviation, successfully completing the mission.
- **Quantitative Performance (Table III)**: ARMOR demonstrated robust resilience across all five evaluated physical attack types, achieving an average mission success rate of 88% and incurring 0 crashes. Even in cases where missions failed, ARMOR consistently prevented crashes and maintained minimal state drift.
 - **Comparison with HRP**: ARMOR consistently outperformed HRP across all attack types, achieving higher success rates, lower crash rates, and significantly reduced state drift. For example, under GPS attacks, ARMOR had an 87% success rate and 0 crashes with 0.1m state drift, compared to HRP's 40% success rate, 50% crashes, and 6.2m state drift. Similar trends were observed for gyroscope, accelerometer, magnetometer, and optical flow attacks.
 - **Comparison with RARL**: ARMOR’s effectiveness was comparable to, and often slightly superior to, RARL. On average, ARMOR achieved an 88% mission success rate compared to RARL’s 83% and exhibited lower state drift across all attack types. For example, under gyroscope attacks, ARMOR achieved an 83% success rate with 2.3 ± 1.6 deg state drift, while RARL achieved 78% success with 4 ± 2 deg state drift.

**C. Zero-Shot Performance**
The research evaluated ARMOR's ability to generalize to attack types not encountered during training, comparing it against RARL.

- **Trained on GPS, Tested on Gyroscope/Multi-Sensor Attacks (Table IV)**: When policies were trained exclusively on GPS manipulations (drift biases) and tested on unseen gyroscope attacks (oscillatory biases) or multi-sensor (gyroscope + accelerometer) attacks:
 - RARL exhibited poor performance, achieving 0% success, high crash rates (60-75%), and significant state drift.
 - ARMOR achieved a 60% success rate under gyroscope attacks and 50% under multi-sensor attacks, while maintaining crash rates below 10% and reducing state drift by approximately 4 times compared to RARL.
- **Trained on Gyroscope, Tested on GPS/Multi-Sensor Attacks (Table V)**: When policies were trained exclusively on gyroscope manipulations (oscillatory biases) and tested on unseen GPS attacks (slow drift biases) or multi-sensor (GPS + accelerometer) attacks:
 - RARL again generalized poorly, achieving only a 5% success rate, high crash rates (70-80%), and substantial state drift.
 - ARMOR demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization, achieving a 70% success rate under GPS attacks and 55% under multi-sensor attacks, while maintaining low crash rates (5-8%) and reducing state drift by over 10 times compared to RARL.

These results indicate that ARMOR significantly outperforms RARL in terms of zero-shot generalization, demonstrating its capability to handle unseen single-sensor and multi-sensor attack types effectively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ARMOR framework presents a significant advancement in the field of robust control for Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) under adversarial conditions, with several implications for the broader domain of autonomous systems.

**Enhanced Safety and Reliability of UAVs:** The primary significance of ARMOR lies in its ability to enable UAVs to maintain safe and stable flight trajectories despite being subjected to sophisticated physical sensor attacks. By learning and leveraging robust latent state representations, ARMOR allows the control policy to operate effectively even when raw sensor data is corrupted. This directly addresses a critical vulnerability in autonomous UAV operations, which is essential for their deployment in safety-critical applications such as logistics, surveillance, and emergency response where malicious interference is a realistic threat. The demonstrated prevention of crashes and reduction of state drift under various attacks contribute to a more reliable and dependable UAV system.

**Improved Training Efficiency:** ARMOR’s two-stage training framework offers a practical advantage over traditional adversarial training methods. By decoupling the robust representation learning from iterative adversarial scenario generation, ARMOR significantly reduces the computational cost and training time required to develop attack-resilient controllers. This efficiency makes the development and deployment of robust RL policies more scalable and accessible, potentially lowering the barrier for integrating such capabilities into real-world autonomous systems.

**Superior Generalization Capabilities:** A notable contribution of ARMOR is its strong zero-shot generalization to unseen attack types, including both single and multi-sensor attacks. Traditional robust RL methods often struggle with this, as their effectiveness is typically limited to the specific attack patterns encountered during training. ARMOR’s ability to generalize to novel attacks is crucial for real-world scenarios where anticipating and training for every conceivable attack is impractical. This generalization capability enhances the practical applicability of robust RL in dynamic and uncertain adversarial environments.

**Foundation for Future Autonomous Systems:** The methodology proposed by ARMOR—particularly the concept of learning robust latent state representations via a teacher-student framework that leverages privileged information—has potential applicability beyond UAVs. The principles could be extended to other robotic systems or safety-critical autonomous platforms that rely on sensor feedback and face similar vulnerabilities to physical manipulation. This work provides a foundation for developing more resilient autonomous agents across various domains.

**Directions for Future Research:** The paper identifies that while ARMOR demonstrates promising zero-shot robustness to single unseen attack types, generalization to complex multi-sensor attacks (where compounding effects of perturbations are more severe) remains an area for further investigation. This highlights a crucial direction for future research, along with the integration of theoretical safety guarantees and constraint satisfaction under adversarial conditions, which could further solidify the trustworthiness of such systems.

In summary, ARMOR contributes to the development of more resilient and reliable autonomous systems by providing an efficient and generalizable approach to mitigate the risks posed by physical sensor attacks, thereby expanding the potential and trustworthiness of UAV technology.
