# CAST: Alternating State-Value Targets and Expanded Policy Gradients for Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> CAST is a model-based RL method built on TD-MPC2 that swaps the action-value critic for an ensemble state-value critic. The critic is trained on a hybrid target mixing one real planner-generated transition with one imagined policy transition, giving a fixed point between the planner's and policy's values, and the policy is improved by backpropagating through imagined model rollouts. It leads average return at 1M steps and transfers to a Unitree Go2 handstand.

## Problem

- Planning-based methods train the critic on the learned policy's value, though the planner-augmented behavior policy that actually collects replay data performs substantially better.
- Prior fixes only push the policy toward the planner via KL alignment or behavior cloning, leaving the critic anchored to the weaker policy rather than the stronger planner.
- Learning an action-value critic for the planner is impractical, since every temporal-difference target would need a fresh planner query instead of reusing stored replay transitions.

## Method

- ==Alternating state-value target==: the critic regresses on one real replay transition under the planner followed by one imagined transition under the current policy, contracting to a characterized alternating fixed point.
- ==State-value ensemble critic== replaces the ==action-value critic==; a one-step action-value is reconstructed from learned reward and dynamics heads, and the planner uses the terminal state value directly.
- ==Expanded policy gradient==: the policy is unrolled k steps through the differentiable learned dynamics and reward before bootstrapping with the frozen target state-value critic.

## Results

- Average return over 14 DeepMind Control and HumanoidBench tasks reaches **764** at 1M steps, beating BOOM's **721** and BMPC's **644**; **839** versus **849** at 2M steps.
- Largest gains on H1hand-run, **570** versus **326** for BOOM at 1M and **867** versus **682** at 2M, with the best pooled normalized area-under-curve profile across suites.
- Ablations: purely off-policy bootstrapping is worst on both Dog-run and Humanoid-run, purely on-policy lags, and horizons of two or three add nothing over one.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Train the critic toward the stronger data-collecting behavior, but regularize it by interleaving the learned policy so bootstrapping does not cause destabilizing policy jumps.
> - A state-value critic sidesteps needing the behavior policy's action distribution, since only visited states are required and action-values are recomposed from the model.
> - Longer imagined rollouts for value targets or policy gradients did not help here, suggesting compounding model error offsets the extra horizon.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

CAST belongs to model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL), a class of reinforcement-learning methods that learn an approximate environment model and use it for prediction, planning, or policy optimization. MBRL is particularly relevant to robotics because it can reduce the number of real-world interactions required to learn a behavior. However, learned models are imperfect, and long-horizon planning can become unreliable when model errors accumulate.

The paper focuses on planning-based MBRL, in which a learned policy proposes actions or action distributions while an online planner refines them before execution. The planner used in CAST is Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control. At each control step, MPPI evaluates candidate action sequences using the learned dynamics and reward models, then executes the first action of the selected sequence.

A central issue in this research area is the mismatch between the policy that generates data and the policy that is optimized by gradient-based learning. In the paper’s notation, the planner-guided behavior is denoted by \(\beta\), while the learned parametric policy is denoted by \(\pi_\theta\). Replay-buffer transitions are generated primarily by \(\beta\), because the planner selects the actions executed in the environment. In contrast, conventional actor-critic updates often train a critic to estimate the value of \(\pi_\theta\). This creates a distributional and objective mismatch.

Several recent methods address this problem differently. BOOM aligns the learned policy with the planner’s action distribution through a likelihood-free or forward-KL-based objective. BMPC uses a state-value critic and behavior cloning to move the learned policy toward planner-generated actions. TD-MPC and TD-MPC2 jointly learn latent dynamics, rewards, a policy, and an action-value critic, with the critic used both for policy improvement and for terminal evaluation during planning.

CAST takes a different position. Instead of primarily forcing \(\pi_\theta\) to imitate \(\beta\), it seeks to make the critic reflect the stronger planner-guided behavior while retaining the learned policy as a stabilizing influence. It also replaces the action-value critic with a state-value critic. This places the work at the intersection of online planning, model-based policy gradients, state-value estimation, and methods for managing planner-policy distribution mismatch.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper has three main objectives.

First, it aims to improve sample efficiency by using information from the planner-guided behavior when learning the value function. The authors argue that the planner generally selects better actions than the learned policy, particularly during early training. If the critic estimates only the value of \(\pi_\theta\), policy improvement may rely on a weaker target than the behavior that actually generated the replay data.

Second, the authors seek to avoid the instability that can result from training directly on the value of the planner policy. A critic trained purely from planner-generated transitions may produce value estimates substantially higher than those associated with the current learned policy. Using such estimates for policy updates could cause large and unstable changes to \(\pi_\theta\). CAST addresses this by combining a real transition generated by \(\beta\) with an imagined transition generated under \(\pi_\theta\).

Third, the paper investigates whether a state-value function can provide a more practical alternative to an action-value function in a planner-guided setting. Estimating \(Q^\beta(z,a)\) would require accounting for the planner’s action distribution at each state. Since planner actions are produced through computationally expensive optimization, constructing such targets could require repeated planner queries for individual training examples. A state-value function \(V(z)\) depends only on visited states and can therefore be trained from replay data without rerunning the planner for every target.

The authors also aim to retain a differentiable policy-improvement mechanism. Since a state-value critic has no action input, the policy cannot be optimized directly through \(Q(z,\pi_\theta(z))\). CAST therefore reconstructs an action-value estimate using the learned reward and dynamics models and propagates policy gradients through imagined model trajectories.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Base Architecture

CAST is implemented by modifying TD-MPC2. Observations are encoded into latent states using an encoder \(h_\xi\). A latent dynamics model \(d_\xi(z,a)\) predicts the next latent state, and a reward model \(R_\xi(z,a)\) predicts the immediate reward. The components are multilayer perceptrons using LayerNorm and Mish activations. Latent states are projected onto products of simplices using SimNorm, which constrains their scale and encourages a structured representation.

The original TD-MPC2 action-value ensemble is replaced with an ensemble of five state-value functions \(V_\psi^i(z)\). A target value ensemble is maintained using Polyak averaging. As in TD-MPC2, rewards and values are represented as distributions over symlog-spaced bins and trained using soft cross-entropy targets rather than direct scalar regression.

Whenever an action-value estimate is needed, CAST composes the learned reward, dynamics, and target value models:

\[
\hat Q(z,a)=R_\xi(z,a)+\gamma \bar V_\psi(d_\xi(z,a)).
\]

This is not an independently learned action-value function. It is a one-step estimate constructed from the state-value critic and the learned model.

#### 3.2. Planner Modification

MPPI samples candidate action sequences and rolls them forward through the latent dynamics model. In conventional TD-MPC2, the terminal value is an action-value estimate evaluated at a sampled terminal action. CAST instead uses the terminal state-value directly:

\[
\hat G=\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^h R_\xi(z_{t+h},a_{t+h})+
\gamma^H\bar V_\psi(z_{t+H}).
\]

This eliminates the need to sample a terminal policy action for planner scoring. The learned policy remains important because it supplies a prior for a fraction of candidate trajectories, while MPPI refines the distribution and executes the first action of the selected sequence.

#### 3.3. Alternating State-Value Target

The defining feature of CAST is its critic target. A replay transition generated by the behavior policy \(\beta\) provides \(r_t\) and \(s_{t+1}\). The next state is encoded as \(z_{t+1}\), an action is sampled from the current policy \(\pi_\theta\), and the learned model predicts the next latent state. The target is

\[
y_t=r_t+\gamma\left[
R_\xi(z_{t+1},\tilde a_{t+1})
+\gamma\bar V_\psi(\tilde z_{t+2})
\right].
\]

Thus, the target consists of one real transition under \(\beta\), followed by one imagined transition under \(\pi_\theta\). Repeated application corresponds to the policy sequence

\[
\beta \rightarrow \pi_\theta \rightarrow \beta \rightarrow \pi_\theta \rightarrow \cdots.
\]

The paper formally characterizes this procedure using the composition of Bellman operators \(T^\beta T^{\pi_\theta}\). Under fixed policies, deterministic dynamics, and an exactly representable value function, this operator is a \(\gamma^2\)-contraction and therefore has a unique fixed point. The resulting value is the discounted return of alternating behavior and policy actions, rather than exactly \(V^\beta\) or \(V^{\pi_\theta}\).

#### 3.4. Expanded Policy Gradient

The policy is optimized by differentiating through the learned reward and dynamics models. For a one-step horizon, the policy objective uses

\[
J(z;\theta)=R_\xi(z,\pi_\theta(z))+
\gamma\bar V_\psi(d_\xi(z,\pi_\theta(z))).
\]

More generally, CAST can unroll the policy for \(H_\pi\) imagined steps, accumulate predicted rewards, and bootstrap from the terminal state-value. Gradients are propagated through the entire imagined trajectory. The primary experiments use \(H_\pi=1\), while longer horizons are evaluated in ablations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Benchmark Performance

CAST was evaluated on 14 high-dimensional continuous-control tasks: seven from the DeepMind Control Suite and seven from HumanoidBench. The methods were run with three random seeds and compared with SAC, DreamerV3, TD-MPC2, BMPC, and BOOM.

The learning curves indicate that CAST generally reaches strong performance earlier than the comparison methods. The improvement is particularly visible in several HumanoidBench tasks, including H1hand-run, H1hand-pole, and Dog-run. The area-under-the-learning-curve analysis supports this interpretation. CAST has the strongest overall normalized AUC profile across the 14 tasks, with performance close to or above the leading methods over much of the training interval.

At one million environment interactions, CAST obtains an average return of 764 across the 14 tasks, compared with 721 for BOOM, 644 for BMPC, and 340 for TD-MPC2. CAST is especially competitive on the HumanoidBench tasks at this intermediate training point. It records the highest reported values on H1hand-stand, H1hand-walk, H1hand-run, H1hand-sit, and H1hand-pole, although some confidence intervals are wide.

At two million interactions, the performance differences narrow as the planning-based methods approach convergence. BOOM has the highest average return at 849, while CAST achieves 839 and BMPC 691. CAST remains competitive with BOOM and exceeds BMPC in average final performance. Its performance is not uniformly best: BOOM is stronger on several HumanoidBench tasks, while CAST is stronger on some DeepMind Control tasks such as Dog-stand and Dog-trot.

#### 4.2. Ablation Results

The value-target ablation compares the hybrid target with a purely off-policy target based on replay transitions and a purely on-policy model-based target. On Dog-run and Humanoid-run, the purely off-policy alternative performs worst and exhibits the instability anticipated by the authors. The purely on-policy alternative is more competitive but generally trails the hybrid formulation. These results support the interpretation that CAST benefits from combining planner-generated data with policy-generated imagined transitions.

The authors also vary the number of real behavior-policy transitions before the imagined policy step. The standard configuration uses one real transition, \(H_V=1\). Using two or three real transitions remains competitive on Dog-run but performs worse on Humanoid-run. This suggests that the balance between planner anchoring and policy regularization is task-dependent, with the one-to-one configuration providing a practical default.

Finally, extending the policy-gradient horizon from one to two or three steps produces similar learning curves on the two tested tasks. The one-step configuration has a small empirical advantage and narrower confidence intervals. The results therefore do not show a clear benefit from longer gradient propagation under the tested settings.

#### 4.3. Real-Robot Demonstration

The paper reports a sim-to-real deployment on a Unitree Go2 quadruped. The task is a dynamic handstand in which the robot lifts its rear legs and balances on its front legs. Training is performed entirely in simulation with dynamics randomization and a curriculum that increases randomization as the simulated success rate improves. The learned policy is then deployed directly on the physical robot without online planning during execution. The reported behavior transfers successfully and produces stable handstand motions. This experiment is presented as a deployment demonstration rather than a controlled comparison against alternative algorithms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CAST’s main significance lies in reframing the planner-policy mismatch as a value-learning problem rather than solely as a policy-alignment problem. By training a state-value critic toward an alternating process involving both the planner and the learned policy, it uses planner-generated experience without requiring the policy to imitate every planner action.

The state-value formulation also has computational implications. It avoids repeated planner queries when forming critic targets and permits planner terminal evaluation using only a state value. This could be useful in robotic systems where planner inference is expensive and must be separated from high-frequency control.

The expanded model-based policy gradient provides a complementary mechanism: the planner improves actions during data collection, while differentiable imagined rollouts improve the parametric policy. This separation may support transfer, because the policy can be deployed without the computational cost of online planning, as illustrated by the Go2 experiment.

The evidence nevertheless has limitations. The theoretical result assumes fixed policies, exact model representation, and deterministic dynamics, whereas practical training uses changing policies, approximate learned models, and stochastic action distributions. The benchmark study uses only three seeds, and the real-world evaluation covers one robot and one behavior without a comparative hardware study. In addition, CAST’s final performance advantage is primarily an intermediate-training effect; after two million interactions, BOOM slightly exceeds it in average return. Further work would be needed to test robustness to substantial model error, different planning budgets, real-world adaptation, and tasks where planner computation is more constrained.
