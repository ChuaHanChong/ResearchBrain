# Subspace Geometry Governs Catastrophic Forgetting in Low-Rank Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research provides a geometric theoretical framework explaining catastrophic forgetting in Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for continual learning. It establishes a quantitative link between forgetting and the minimum principal angle of task gradient subspaces, revealing an approximate rank-invariance property in diverse task settings and reconciling prior conflicting observations about LoRA rank effects.

## Problem

- Catastrophic forgetting hinders the ability of large pre-trained models (LPMs) to adapt to new tasks sequentially without losing proficiency in previously learned ones.
- The theoretical mechanisms governing catastrophic forgetting within the Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) framework, particularly the role of adapter rank, are not fully understood.
- Existing gradient subspace methods for mitigating forgetting lack a precise, predictive functional form directly linking subspace angles to the extent of forgetting.

## Method

(b) The geometric forgetting law: the separation term (1 − cos^2(θmin)) increases with principal angle, and empirically correlates with observed forgetting.*

- A ==geometric theoretical framework== is developed, including a ==Geometric Forgetting Law== ($F = \alpha(1 - \cos^2 \theta_{\min}) + \beta$), that quantitatively models forgetting based on the ==minimum principal angle== between task gradient subspaces.
- A unified ==rank-angle interaction theory== is formulated to explain how the ==LoRA adapter rank== influences forgetting, contingent on the geometric relationship (principal angle) between task gradient subspaces.
- The theory is empirically validated across synthetic tasks, ==Split-CIFAR100 (ViT-LoRA)==, and ==Sequential GLUE (RoBERTa-LoRA)==, measuring forgetting, principal angles, and effective rank.

## Results

- The geometric forgetting law accurately predicted forgetting on synthetic tasks with a correlation of **r=0.994** and an **R²=0.987**, alongside a low Coefficient of Variation (CV) of **0.84%** across ranks, indicating rank-invariance.
- Experiments on Split-CIFAR100 and Sequential GLUE showed approximate rank-invariance with CVs of **18.5%** and **9.9%** respectively, and a layer-wise analysis on CIFAR-100 supported a positive correlation between geometric separation and forgetting (**r=0.525**).
- Explicit orthogonalization methods like O-LoRA offered no statistically significant advantage over vanilla LoRA when natural task orthogonality was already high, aligning with the theoretical prediction that their benefit is minimal in such cases.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Catastrophic forgetting in LoRA is primarily governed by the geometric relationship (minimum principal angle) between task gradient subspaces, with greater angular separation correlating with increased forgetting.
> - LoRA exhibits approximate rank-invariance in forgetting when task gradient subspaces are sufficiently diverse (high principal angles), implying that adapter rank has a negligible impact in such regimes.
> - The effect of adapter rank on forgetting is regime-dependent: it is pronounced for similar tasks (low principal angles) but becomes negligible for diverse tasks (high principal angles).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of large pre-trained models (LPMs) in real-world scenarios frequently necessitates their adaptation to new tasks without losing proficiency in previously learned ones, a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has emerged as a parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) method, enabling adaptation by constraining updates to low-rank subspaces. While LoRA offers a practical solution to reduce the computational and memory costs of adapting LPMs, its theoretical behavior, particularly concerning catastrophic forgetting in continual learning settings, has remained incompletely understood.

This work addresses this gap by providing a geometric theoretical framework to characterize catastrophic forgetting in LoRA-based continual learning. It builds upon prior research in several areas:
- **Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT):** LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) is a prominent PEFT method, and understanding its mechanisms, including its capacity for memorization (Steele, 2026), is an active area of research. This paper contributes to the theoretical grounding of LoRA, specifically concerning rank choices in continual learning.
- **Continual Learning and Forgetting:** Catastrophic forgetting (McCloskey & Cohen, 1989) is a central challenge in neural networks. Existing strategies include regularization-based, replay-based, and architecture-based methods. More recent work has investigated forgetting in the context of instruction tuning (Luo et al., 2023) and PEFT methods (Biderman et al., 2024). This study offers a novel perspective by linking forgetting in LoRA to the geometry of gradient subspaces.
- **Gradient Subspace Methods:** The concept that gradient descent operates within low-dimensional subspaces (Gur-Ari et al., 2018) has motivated continual learning methods like Orthogonal Gradient Descent (OGD) (Farajtabar et al., 2020) and Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) (Saha et al., 2021). Extensions such as O-LoRA (Wang et al., 2023) and InfLoRA (Liang & Li, 2024) apply these ideas to LoRA. While these methods leverage gradient subspace geometry to mitigate forgetting, they typically do not provide a predictive, functional form linking subspace angles directly to the extent of forgetting. This paper’s contribution lies in proposing and validating such a quantitative relationship, as well as characterizing the interaction between adapter rank and subspace angles.
- **LoRA and Forgetting Interaction:** Prior empirical studies, such as Biderman et al. (2024), observed that higher LoRA rank could lead to increased forgetting. This work provides a theoretical context for such findings, demonstrating that the influence of rank is regime-dependent, specifically when task subspaces exhibit significant overlap.

In summary, this research positions itself at the intersection of PEFT, continual learning, and gradient subspace analysis, aiming to develop a predictive geometric theory that explains and reconciles observed behaviors of LoRA concerning catastrophic forgetting.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation of this research is to develop a deeper theoretical understanding of how Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) behaves in continual learning settings, specifically concerning the phenomenon of catastrophic forgetting. While LoRA offers a practical solution for adapting large pre-trained models efficiently, the underlying mechanisms governing forgetting within this framework are not well understood.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Develop a Geometric Forgetting Law:** The primary objective is to propose and empirically validate a quantitative relationship between forgetting and the geometric properties of task gradient subspaces. The hypothesis is that forgetting (F) can be described by the functional form F = α(1 − cos²θ_min) + β, where θ_min represents the minimum principal angle between consecutive task gradient subspaces. This aims to move beyond qualitative reasoning by providing a predictive equation for forgetting.

2. **Investigate Approximate Rank-Invariance:** A specific objective is to determine if and when forgetting becomes independent of the adapter rank in LoRA. The research aims to observe and quantify an "approximate rank-invariance" property, suggesting that at high subspace angles (i.e., when tasks are sufficiently distinct), the adapter rank may have a negligible impact on forgetting. This has practical implications for selecting appropriate adapter sizes.

3. **Formulate a Unified Rank-Angle Interaction Theory:** The study aims to reconcile seemingly contradictory findings in the literature, particularly the observation by Biderman et al. (2024) that higher LoRA rank can increase forgetting. The objective is to develop a unified theory that explains how adapter rank influences forgetting depending on the geometric relationship (angle) between task gradient subspaces, identifying specific regimes where rank effects are pronounced versus where rank-invariance holds.

4. **Analyze the Effectiveness of Orthogonal Methods:** Another objective is to understand when explicit orthogonalization methods, such as O-LoRA, are most beneficial for mitigating forgetting. The research seeks to demonstrate that such methods provide minimal additional benefit when natural task orthogonality is already high, thereby explaining their variable effectiveness observed in different contexts.

The overarching motivation for these objectives is to provide principled guidance for practitioners implementing continual learning with parameter-efficient fine-tuning. By offering a quantitative, geometric framework, the authors aim to enable more informed decisions regarding adapter rank selection and the application of orthogonalization strategies, ultimately contributing to more robust and efficient continual learning systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a combined theoretical and empirical approach to investigate the geometric foundations of catastrophic forgetting in LoRA-based continual learning.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup

The study considers a continual learning scenario where a pre-trained model $W_0$ is adapted to a sequence of $T$ tasks. For each task $t$, a low-rank update $\Delta W_t = B_t A_t$ is learned, where $A_t \in R^{r \times d}$ and $B_t \in R^{d \times r}$ for a chosen rank $r$.

Central to the theoretical framework are two definitions:
- **Gradient Subspace ($G_t$):** Defined as the span of gradients of the loss function $L_t(\theta)$ over the parameter manifold $\Theta$ explored during training ($G_t = \text{span}\{\nabla L_t(\theta) : \theta \in \Theta\}$).
- **Principal Angles ($\theta_k$):** These measure the geometric relationship between two subspaces $G_i$ and $G_j$. The minimum principal angle, $\theta_{\min}$, captures the maximum alignment between the subspaces. The study uses a separation term $(1 - \cos^2 \theta_{\min})$, which is zero for aligned subspaces ($\theta_{\min}=0$) and maximal for orthogonal subspaces ($\theta_{\min}=\pi/2$).

#### 3.2. Theoretical Framework

The core of the methodology is the proposed geometric forgetting bound, presented as **Theorem 1 (Geometric Forgetting Bound (Empirically Parameterized))**. This theorem postulates that forgetting on task $i$ after training on task $t$ ($F_{i,t}$) is bounded by:
$F_{i,t} \le \alpha \cdot (1 - \cos^2(\theta_{\min}(i, t))) + \beta$

The derivation relies on four assumptions:
- (A1) Gradient subspaces $G_t$ are stable during training.
- (A2) Task interference is primarily local (between consecutive tasks).
- (A3) The loss landscape is locally L-smooth.
- (A4) (Empirical) The effective rank of updates saturates at high angles.

The proof sketch involves a Taylor expansion of the loss change, where the first-order term's dependence on the cosine of the angle between the gradient and the update is highlighted. The residual forgetting is then linked to the second-order curvature effects, leading to the $(1 - \cos^2 \theta_{\min})$ dependence. The authors explicitly state that while the angular dependence is rigorous under (A1)-(A3), assumption (A4) is empirical, and the specific functional form F = α(1 − cos²θ_min) + β is an empirically validated parameterization rather than a purely deductive result. The positive sign of $\alpha$ indicates that in the experimental regime, task diversity (high angles) correlates with increased forgetting.

Based on Theorem 1 and the empirical observation (A4), the paper introduces **Corollary 2 (Rank Invariance at High Angles)**. This corollary suggests that if the effective rank of updates saturates (empirically observed to be near 1), then forgetting becomes independent of the nominal LoRA rank $r$ when task subspaces are sufficiently orthogonal.

Finally, **Proposition 3 (Extended Rank-Angle Theory)** extends the framework to explain when rank *does* matter. It proposes that the effective rank $r_{eff}(\theta, r)$ is a function of both the nominal rank $r$ and the principal angle $\theta$, specifically $r_{eff}(\theta, r) = \min(r, \frac{c}{1-\cos(\theta)})$. This formulation predicts that rank affects forgetting when tasks are similar (low $\theta$, $r_{eff} \rightarrow r$), but forgetting becomes rank-invariant when tasks are orthogonal (high $\theta$, $r_{eff} \rightarrow c$).

#### 3.3. Experimental Validation

The theoretical predictions are validated across three experimental setups:

1. **Synthetic Tasks:** Controlled experiments are conducted by generating gradient matrices with explicitly specified principal angles. This allows for direct testing of the proposed geometric forgetting law under idealized conditions.
2. **Split-CIFAR100:** The study uses a visual transformer (ViT-Base) with LoRA adapters (ranks 4, 8, 16) on CIFAR-100 split into 10 sequential tasks. Adam optimizer, learning rate $10^{-4}$, and 5 epochs per task are used across 5 random seeds.
3. **Sequential GLUE:** A RoBERTa-base model with LoRA adapters (ranks 4, 8, 16) is evaluated on 5 sequential GLUE tasks (SST-2, MRPC, RTE, QNLI, QQP). AdamW optimizer, learning rate $2 \times 10^{-5}$, and 3 epochs per task are used across 3 seeds.

**Metrics:**
- **Forgetting:** Measured as the accuracy drop on previous tasks after learning new ones.
- **Principal Angles:** Computed via Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of accumulated gradient matrices.
- **Effective Rank:** An entropy-based measure of the dimensionality of the gradient subspace.

Additional analyses include layer-wise correlation studies on CIFAR-100 to address confounding factors and a comparison between vanilla LoRA and O-LoRA to assess the benefits of explicit orthogonalization methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across synthetic and real-world benchmarks, validating its geometric theory of forgetting in LoRA.

#### 4.1. Synthetic Validation

On synthetic tasks with controlled principal angles, the proposed geometric forgetting law ($F = \alpha(1 - \cos^2 \theta_{\min}) + \beta$) demonstrated a strong predictive capability. The interference term $(1 - \cos^2 \theta_{\min})$ showed a high correlation of $r = 0.994$ with measured forgetting. The linear fit achieved an $R^2$ of 0.987, with estimated parameters $\alpha = 1.93$ and $\beta \approx -0.07$. Importantly, these experiments also confirmed rank-invariance, with a Coefficient of Variation (CV) of only 0.84% across adapter ranks 1 to 32, indicating nearly identical forgetting regardless of rank.

#### 4.2. Split-CIFAR100 Results

Experiments on Split-CIFAR100 using ViT-LoRA adapters (ranks 4, 8, 16) demonstrated approximate rank-invariance, with a CV of 18.5% across the tested ranks. This value is higher than in synthetic settings but still indicates that rank has a moderate effect on forgetting in this regime. Task-specific adapters, by design, achieved 0.0% forgetting and 98.4% final accuracy. EWC-LoRA, a regularization-based method, reduced forgetting by 34% compared to vanilla LoRA, achieving 9.0% forgetting.

An initial pairwise correlation analysis between the interference term $(1 - \cos^2 \theta)$ and forgetting on CIFAR-100 yielded a negative correlation of $r = -0.507$. However, a layer-wise analysis revealed that this aggregate negative correlation was due to confounding factors, primarily that tasks with similar pretrained representations (low angles) also tended to be easier to transfer, reducing forgetting despite higher geometric interference. When analyzed layer-wise, 6 out of 7 LoRA layers exhibited a positive interference-forgetting correlation, with an aggregate layer-wise correlation of $r = 0.525$, supporting the local validity of the geometric theory.

#### 4.3. Sequential GLUE Results

On Sequential GLUE tasks using RoBERTa-LoRA, approximate rank-invariance was also observed, with a CV of 9.9% across ranks 4, 8, and 16. This lower CV compared to CIFAR-100 suggests that NLP tasks with more diverse domains may naturally exhibit higher subspace orthogonality. Similar to CIFAR, task-specific adapters achieved 0.0% forgetting.

#### 4.4. Layer-wise Analysis

The layer-wise analysis on Split-CIFAR100 clarified the impact of confounding factors. While the overall angle-forgetting correlation appeared negative, isolating layer-specific interactions revealed that 6 out of 7 LoRA layers showed the predicted positive correlation (r=0.525 aggregate) between the geometric separation term and forgetting. This indicates that the theory holds locally, but task difficulty and representation similarity can mask this relationship when considering aggregate metrics on real datasets.

#### 4.5. Orthogonal Method Comparison

A comparison between vanilla LoRA and O-LoRA (rank 8) on Split-CIFAR100 showed no statistically significant difference in forgetting (p = 0.73, Cohen’s d = -0.22). Both methods achieved similar mean subspace angles (approximately 1.05 radians or 60 degrees). This finding supports the theory’s prediction that explicit orthogonalization methods provide minimal benefit when the natural orthogonality between task subspaces is already high. Attempts to evaluate InfLoRA were constrained by out-of-memory errors due to its computational intensity for full null-space projection.

#### 4.6. Reconciliation with Prior Work on Rank Effects

The research successfully reconciled its observation of rank-invariance with prior findings (e.g., Biderman et al., 2024) that higher LoRA rank can increase forgetting. The proposed rank-angle interaction theory posits that the effective rank depends on the principal angle. Specifically:
- When tasks are similar (low principal angles, $\theta \approx 0$), the effective rank approaches the nominal rank, meaning rank affects forgetting. This regime aligns with instruction tuning tasks, which often share structural similarities.
- When tasks are diverse (high principal angles, $\theta \approx \pi/2$), the effective rank saturates to a constant, making forgetting approximately rank-invariant. This regime aligns with the diverse task sequences in the conducted experiments.

This unified view explains that both sets of findings are valid but apply to different regimes characterized by the geometric relationship between task subspaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a novel geometric theory for understanding catastrophic forgetting in Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), offering a quantitative framework that contributes to the theoretical foundations of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) in continual learning.

The key significance of this work lies in:

1. **Quantitative Forgetting Prediction:** The proposed geometric forgetting law, $F = \alpha(1 - \cos^2 \theta_{\min}) + \beta$, provides a predictive functional form that links forgetting directly to the minimum principal angle between task gradient subspaces. This moves beyond qualitative understanding, enabling a more precise characterization and potential prediction of forgetting in LoRA-based continual learning systems. The empirical validation across synthetic tasks (r = 0.994) and local validation on real benchmarks strengthens its utility.

2. **Discovery of Approximate Rank-Invariance:** The finding that forgetting can be approximately independent of the nominal adapter rank (especially at high subspace angles) challenges the intuition that higher rank always leads to more forgetting. This observation, with CV values as low as 0.84% in synthetic settings and 9.9-18.5% on real benchmarks, has direct implications for LoRA adapter sizing.

3. **Unified Rank-Angle Interaction Theory:** The reconciliation of seemingly contradictory findings in the literature (i.e., when rank matters versus when it doesn't) through a unified rank-angle interaction theory is a significant contribution. By demonstrating that rank effects are prominent for similar tasks (low angles) while rank-invariance holds for diverse tasks (high angles), the work provides a comprehensive understanding of LoRA's behavior.

The potential impact of these findings is primarily practical, offering several principled guidelines for practitioners:

- **Adapter Rank Selection:** The research suggests that reducing adapter rank may not be an effective strategy to prevent forgetting when tasks are naturally diverse and exhibit high subspace orthogonality. Instead, practitioners might prioritize using a rank sufficient for task performance, without undue concern for increased forgetting in such regimes. This could lead to more efficient resource allocation, as smaller adapters may suffice without sacrificing continual learning performance.
- **Diagnostic Tool:** Monitoring the principal angles between accumulated gradient matrices can serve as a diagnostic tool to anticipate the extent of forgetting, allowing for proactive interventions or architectural adjustments.
- **Targeted Use of Orthogonalization Methods:** The study clarifies that explicit orthogonalization methods, such as O-LoRA, are most beneficial when natural task orthogonality is low (i.e., for very similar tasks where subspaces overlap significantly). For task sequences with inherently diverse gradients, the computational overhead of these methods may not be justified, as vanilla LoRA can achieve comparable forgetting levels.
- **Task-Specific Adapters:** The consistent observation of zero forgetting with task-specific adapters reinforces their utility when maximum knowledge retention is a critical requirement, despite their higher parameter cost.

While the study uses base-sized models (ViT-Base, RoBERTa-base), the theoretical framework is general and could guide future research on larger models. Limitations, such as confounding factors (task difficulty correlating with subspace angle), computational constraints for advanced orthogonal methods, and statistical uncertainty from limited sample sizes in real-world experiments, are acknowledged. Future work could address these by theoretically deriving phase transition angles and scaling evaluation.

In a broader societal context, improved continual learning capabilities, facilitated by this research, could enable AI models to adapt more efficiently and sustainably to evolving data streams and new requirements. This can lead to more robust and adaptable AI systems in various applications, while also potentially reducing the computational and energy footprint associated with frequent model retraining. The authors also note dual-use concerns that accompany enhanced AI capabilities, recommending careful consideration of downstream applications.
