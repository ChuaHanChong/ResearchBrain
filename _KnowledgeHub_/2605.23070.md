# Flow Mismatching: Unsupervised Anomaly Detection via Velocity Discrepancies in Flow Matching Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Flow Mismatching is an unsupervised industrial anomaly detector. It trains a flow matching model on normal images only, then draws straight paths from Gaussian noise to a test image and scores, per pixel, how far the learned velocity departs from the velocity pointing at the image. It uses only forward passes and reaches 98.7 image AUROC and 67.5 pixel AP on MVTec-AD.

## Problem

- Reconstruction-based detectors built on GANs, autoencoders or diffusion models can faithfully reconstruct plausible defects, which shrinks the residual that is supposed to flag anomalies.
- Feature-based methods depend on memory banks, patch retrieval, or separate pretrained extractors such as DINO or ViT, adding components beyond a single generative model.
- Score-based and inverse-problem flow methods such as D-Flow need costly gradients or optimization at test time, with D-Flow running at about 0.07 images per second.

## Method

- ==Velocity-discrepancy score==: along straight paths from Gaussian noise to the test image, it takes the per-pixel squared gap between the normal-trained flow's predicted velocity and the velocity pointing straight at the image.
- ==Minimum path aggregation== over K random noise seeds acts as consensus voting. Only pixels that stay anomalous across every path keep high scores, which suppresses single-path false positives exponentially.
- ==Time weighting w(t)=t²== over T interior timesteps, backed by a theorem splitting the mismatch into a bounded ==denoising term== plus a ==Fisher divergence== between test-path and normal-path scores.

## Results

- On MVTec-AD a **45M**-parameter U-Net reaches **98.7** image AUROC and **67.5** pixel AP, versus **98.5** and **56.1** for MambaAD. On VisA it reaches **98.0** image AUROC versus **94.8** for AMDM.
- Against flow matching baselines on the same backbone, MVTec-AD pixel AUPRO reaches **91.9**, versus **72.7** for ReconFlow, **70.2** for WT-Flow and **62.3** for D-Flow.
- Minimum aggregation beats averaging, **98.7** versus **94.4** image AUROC on MVTec-AD. Accuracy rises steadily with compute, and the smallest setting, K=T=2, still reaches **95.9** image AUROC at **86.4** FPS.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A generative model can flag anomalies by checking whether its learned dynamics agree with a path toward the input, instead of reconstructing the input and comparing the two.
> - Keeping only the lowest score across random probes suppresses spurious responses far better than averaging them, a cheap fix for any detector whose score depends on a random seed.
> - Pixel-space flows excel at small texture defects but only outline large semantic anomalies, so logical or object-level defects likely need latent-space or semantic representations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Unsupervised anomaly detection (AD) is a critical task, particularly in industrial visual inspection where manufacturing defects are rare, diverse, and costly to overlook. The typical scenario involves training models solely on normal, anomaly-free data and then identifying deviations at inference time.

Existing unsupervised AD methods generally fall into three categories:
- **Reconstruction-based methods**: These approaches, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), autoencoders, and diffusion models, learn to reconstruct normal images. Anomalies are then detected based on reconstruction errors. A limitation of these methods is their potential to reconstruct plausible defects, which can weaken the anomaly signal. Recent examples include Dinomaly, MambaAD, and ViTAD, which leverage powerful visual backbones like Dino, Mamba, and ViT. Diffusion models have also been adapted for AD, such as DiffusionAD, TransFusion, and HDM, often focusing on efficient reconstruction or defect synthesis.
- **Feature-based methods**: These methods typically compare features extracted from test images with prototypes of normal features, often requiring memory banks or specialized feature extractors (e.g., patch retrieval mechanisms).
- **Score-based methods**: These approaches aim to estimate likelihoods or score functions of the data distribution, with anomalies identified as low-likelihood regions. A drawback can be the computational expense of inference-time gradient computations.

Flow Matching (FM) models represent a more recent development in generative modeling, learning a continuous-time velocity field that transports samples from a simple prior (e.g., Gaussian noise) to the data distribution. This paradigm has also been explored for anomaly detection:
- Some FM-based methods modify the flow direction or training objective, such as WT-Flow (using time-reversed flow for density estimation) or Reflect (transporting anomalous latent codes towards the normal distribution).
- Other approaches use FM as a correction mechanism or involve expensive test-time inverse problems, like D-Flow, which searches for the closest generated sample.
- Simpler velocity-based signals have also been explored for AD in other domains, such as tabular data (TCCM) or high-energy physics (It's not a FAD).

This current research, Flow Mismatching, positions itself by diverging from the prevalent reconstruction-based paradigm. Instead of explicitly reconstructing images, it leverages the inherent generative dynamics learned by a flow matching model. The core distinction lies in directly comparing the learned "normal" velocity field with the geometric velocity towards a test image. This approach aims to provide a direct test-time anomaly signal without requiring explicit reconstruction, external feature memories, or computationally intensive back-propagation during inference. The work also provides a theoretical foundation for this velocity mismatch signal.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations observed in existing unsupervised anomaly detection methods, particularly in industrial settings. Reconstruction-based methods, while widely used, can sometimes generate plausible defects, thereby diluting the anomaly signal and leading to false negatives. Feature-based methods often necessitate external memory banks or sophisticated feature extractors, adding complexity. Score-based methods, though theoretically sound, may incur high computational costs due to gradient computations during inference. The need for a more direct, efficient, and robust anomaly detection signal at test time, one that avoids these complexities, drove the development of Flow Mismatching.

The key insight underlying this work is that anomalies inherently manifest as a disagreement between two velocity fields:
1. **The learned normal flow velocity**: When a flow matching model is trained exclusively on normal, anomaly-free images, its learned velocity field (denoted as `v_θ(x, t)`) encapsulates the generative dynamics and manifold of normal data. It represents the "expected" trajectory for a normal sample.
2. **The geometric path velocity to a test image**: When analyzing a test image `y` (which may contain anomalies), an affine path `x_t = (1-t)x_0 + ty` is constructed from a Gaussian noise source `x_0` to `y`. The geometric velocity along this path, `g(x_0, y, t) = y - x_0`, represents the direct vector field required to reach `y`, including any anomalous content present in it.

If the test image `y` is normal, the learned flow `v_θ(x_t, t)` is expected to align closely with the geometric path velocity `g(x_0, y, t)` as `x_t` moves toward `y`. However, if `y` contains anomalies, the learned normal flow will attempt to guide `x_t` towards the normal data manifold, while the geometric path will directly point to `y` (including its anomalous regions). This divergence is hypothesized to be a strong indicator of anomalous content.

Based on this insight, the specific objectives of the research were:
- **Method Development**: To propose "Flow Mismatching," a novel unsupervised anomaly detection method that quantifies and aggregates this velocity discrepancy.
- **Signal Formulation**: To design a purely test-time detector that computes per-pixel velocity discrepancies without relying on test-time optimization, feature memories, or additional calibration.
- **Aggregation Strategy**: To develop robust aggregation strategies over multiple time steps and different random initialization paths to yield stable pixel-wise anomaly heatmaps and image-level anomaly scores.
- **Theoretical Elucidation**: To provide a theoretical framework explaining why the velocity mismatch signal effectively separates anomalies, particularly by relating it to denoising error and Fisher divergence.
- **Empirical Validation**: To demonstrate that Flow Mismatching achieves competitive or superior performance compared to current state-of-the-art reconstruction-based and existing flow matching-based anomaly detection methods on established industrial benchmarks like MVTec-AD and VisA.
- **Efficiency and Flexibility**: To introduce and validate a tunable test-time computational mechanism that allows for a flexible trade-off between detection performance and inference speed.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Flow Mismatching operates by comparing the velocity field learned by a flow matching model with the direct geometric velocity towards a test image. The process is structured as follows:

**Problem Setup:**
A conditional flow matching model, denoted `v_θ(x, t)`, is initially trained exclusively on a dataset of normal (anomaly-free) images. This model learns a deterministic velocity field that describes the generative dynamics from a standard Gaussian prior `N(0, I)` to the distribution of normal data. At test time, for a given image `y` (which may be anomalous), the objective is to generate a pixel-wise anomaly heatmap `H` and an image-level anomaly score `S`.

**Single Path Velocity Discrepancy:**
1. **Path Definition**: For a given test image `y` and a randomly sampled Gaussian noise vector `x_0 ∼ N(0, I)`, an affine interpolation path `x_t = (1 - t)x_0 + ty` is constructed. This path linearly interpolates between `x_0` and `y` over time `t ∈`.
2. **Geometric Velocity**: The geometric velocity along this path is defined as `g(x_0, y, t) = y - x_0`, which is simply the constant derivative `dx_t/dt`. This velocity vector directly points from `x_0` to `y`.
3. **Velocity Mismatch**: At any intermediate point `x_t` along this path, two velocities are compared:
 - `v_θ(x_t, t)`: The velocity predicted by the trained flow matching model, representing the normal generative dynamics at state `x_t` and time `t`.
 - `g(x_0, y, t)`: The geometric velocity towards the target image `y`.
 If `y` contains anomalies, `g` attempts to reproduce these anomalies, while `v_θ` attempts to follow normal patterns. This leads to a local disagreement.
4. **Discrepancy Measurement**: The per-pixel squared discrepancy is calculated as `Z_t(i) = ||v_θ(x_t, t)(i) - g(x_0, y, t)(i)||^2_2`, where `(i)` denotes the value at pixel `i` and the `ℓ2` norm is taken over the channel dimension (e.g., RGB channels). This value serves as a raw indicator of anomaly at pixel `i` at time `t` for a given `x_0`.

**Aggregation of Paths:**
To enhance robustness and reduce variability introduced by individual stochastic `x_0` samples, the method aggregates discrepancies over multiple paths:
1. **Multiple Seeds**: `K` independent Gaussian seeds, `{x_0^1, ..., x_0^K}`, are sampled.
2. **Per-Path Discrepancies**: For each seed `k`, a path `x_t^k` and its corresponding per-pixel discrepancy `Z_t^(k)(i)` are computed.
3. **Path Aggregator**: These `K` per-path discrepancies for each pixel `i` at time `t` are aggregated using a function `A`.
 - **Minimum Aggregator (`δ_min_t(i) = min_k Z_t^(k)(i)`)**: This is the default choice, acting as a consensus mechanism. It aims to reduce false positives by ensuring high anomaly scores only for pixels consistently showing large discrepancies across multiple paths.
 - **Average Aggregator (`δ_avg_t(i) = 1/K Σ_k Z_t^(k)(i)`)**: This targets the population mean of the discrepancy.
 - **α-percentile Aggregator**: Offers an interpolation between minimum and average.

**Time Discretization and Weighting:**
The anomaly signal varies over time `t`. To obtain a comprehensive multi-scale score, discrepancies are aggregated over time:
1. **Time Discretization**: The continuous time interval `(0, 1)` is discretized into `T` evenly spaced interior time steps (excluding `t=0` and `t=1` for numerical stability, as explained in the theory section).
2. **Weighted Aggregation**: The final pixel-level anomaly heatmap `H(i)` is computed as a weighted sum of `δ_t(i)` over all discrete time steps: `H(i) = (1/|T|) Σ_{t∈T} w(t)δ_t(i)`. The weight function `w(t) = t^2` is applied. This weighting is theoretically justified by inverse-variance considerations in the endpoint estimation error, balancing early-time semantic evidence with sharper late-time localization.
3. **Image-Level Score**: The overall image-level anomaly score `S` is derived from the heatmap `H` by taking the maximum pixel value combined with the average of the top 1% pixel values: `S = max_i H(i) + (1/|Ω_topn|) Σ_{i∈Ω_topn} H(i)`.

**Adjustable Test-Time Compute:**
Flow Mismatching provides explicit control over the computational budget at inference time. The number of paths `K` and time steps `T` can be adjusted. Increasing `K` and `T` generally improves the statistical reliability and smoothness of the anomaly maps, enhancing detection performance and robustness. Since these computations involve parallelizable forward passes of the neural network, this offers a flexible trade-off between throughput and accuracy.

**Theoretical Foundation:**
A significant part of the methodology is its theoretical grounding, which explains the efficacy of the velocity mismatch signal:
1. **Link to Oracle Velocity**: The learned flow `v_θ(x, t)` is an approximation of the oracle marginal velocity `v_p(t, x) = E_p[y - x_0 | x_t = x]`, which represents the expected velocity field under the normal data distribution.
2. **Deterministic Identity (Proposition 4.1)**: This proposition establishes a direct link between the `y`-residual (difference between target `y` and its conditional mean `μ_p(x_t)`) and the velocity residual `v_p(t, x_t) - (y - x_0)`. Specifically, `||y - μ_p(x_t)||^2 = (1 - t)^2 ||v_p(t, x_t) - (y - x_0)||^2`.
3. **Tweedie's Lemma (Lemma 4.2)**: This lemma relates the difference in posterior means `μ_q(x) - μ_p(x)` (for anomalous `q` vs. normal `p`) to the difference in score functions `s_q^t(x) - s_p^t(x)` of their respective marginal path distributions.
4. **Population Mismatch Decomposition (Theorem 4.3)**: This central theorem decomposes the weighted population-level mismatch into two components:
 - A **weighted denoising error** term: `t^2 / (1 - t)^2 D_q(t)`, where `D_q(t)` is the mean squared error for estimating `y` from `x_t` under anomaly distribution `q`. This term represents irreducible uncertainty and is shown to be bounded.
 - A **Fisher divergence** term: `(1 - t)^2 J(q_t || p_t)`, which quantifies the discrepancy between the marginal distribution of paths conditioned on an anomalous image (`q_t`) and those conditioned on a normal image (`p_t`). This Fisher divergence term is identified as the primary signal for anomaly separation.
5. **Time Weighting (`w(t)=t^2`) Justification (Proposition 4.4)**: The `t^2` weighting factor arises naturally from inverse-variance weighting of endpoint estimation errors in `y`-space, effectively balancing the bias-variance trade-off across different time steps. It ensures the weighted denoising term remains bounded as `t → 1`.
6. **Path Aggregation Guarantees (Corollary A.5, Proposition A.2)**: For finite `K` paths, minimum aggregation is shown to exponentially suppress false positives. A "quantile bridge" lower bound further connects the quantiles of the learned-field mismatch to the oracle score-gap quantiles, providing robustness for a given test image.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Flow Mismatching was evaluated through extensive experiments on two industrial visual anomaly detection benchmarks: MVTec-AD and VisA. The images were uniformly resized to 128x128 pixels. Performance was measured using standard metrics, including AUROC, AP, and F1-max for both image-level detection and pixel-level localization, with AUPRO additionally reported for localization. The default inference settings utilized `K=10` paths and `T=10` time steps, aggregating paths with the minimum operator.

**Comparison with Reconstruction-based Methods:**
Table 1 demonstrates that Flow Mismatching achieves competitive or superior performance compared to a range of established reconstruction-based anomaly detection methods. On MVTec-AD, it outperformed several contemporary approaches such as RD4AD, CFLOW-AD, UniAD, SimpleNet, DeSTSeg, HVQ-Trans, DiAD, ViTAD, and AMDM, particularly in pixel-level localization metrics (AUROC, AP, F1-max, AUPRO). For instance, Flow Mismatching yielded 97.7% pixel-level AUROC, 67.5% AP, and 91.9% AUPRO on MVTec-AD, which were among the highest reported values. On the VisA dataset, it also showed strong performance across all metrics, generally surpassing or matching the leading reconstruction-based methods. This outcome suggests that direct velocity disagreement can provide a more discriminative anomaly signal than reconstruction errors alone.

**Comparison with Flow Matching-based Methods:**
A controlled comparison with other flow matching-based anomaly detection methods (Reflect, TCCM, WT-Flow, D-Flow, ReconFlow) was conducted, ensuring all methods used the same U-Net backbone architecture and identical training/inference protocols. As detailed in Table 2, Flow Mismatching consistently achieved superior results across all metrics on MVTec-AD. For example, it significantly surpassed these baselines with an image-level AUROC of 98.7% and a pixel-level AUROC of 97.7%. This indicates that the proposed velocity mismatching strategy is more effective for anomaly detection than alternative uses of flow matching models, such as modified training objectives or expensive test-time inverse problems.

**Ablation Studies:**
1. **Test-Time Compute Trade-offs (Table 6, Figure 2)**: An ablation on the number of paths (`K`) and time steps (`T`) revealed a direct relationship between computational budget and performance. Increasing either `K` or `T` monotonically improved both image-level and pixel-level detection and localization metrics. This demonstrated the method's flexibility, allowing users to tune compute for high-throughput (low K, T) or high-accuracy (high K, T) applications. Even a minimal setting (K=T=2) yielded results competitive with some state-of-the-art methods.
2. **Path Aggregator (Table 7)**: Comparing different path aggregation strategies, the minimum aggregator (default setting) consistently produced the best results. This outcome aligns with the theoretical justification that minimum aggregation robustly suppresses spurious path-specific responses and reduces false positives by requiring consistent anomaly signals across multiple stochastic paths. Lower percentiles (e.g., 10th, 20th percentiles) also performed well, outperforming simple averaging.
3. **Conditional Label vs. Pooled Categories (Table 8)**: While the default setting used a single model with conditional category labels, training an unconditional model on pooled categories still yielded strong results, particularly for pixel-level localization. This indicates that Flow Mismatching can adapt to scenarios where explicit category conditioning is not available or desired.
4. **Training Epochs (Table 9, Figure 8)**: An analysis of checkpoints across training epochs demonstrated that performance steadily improved and then plateaued around 2000 epochs. This suggests that the model successfully learned the normal data distribution and its generative dynamics without exhibiting overfitting that would degrade anomaly detection capabilities.

**Qualitative Results:**
Visualizations (Figures 13, 14) demonstrated the method's strong localization capability, accurately highlighting even small or subtle defects within images. The generated heatmaps intuitively corresponded to anomalous regions.

**Failure Case Analysis:**
The primary observed failure mode occurred with large object-level anomalies (Figure 9). In such cases, Flow Mismatching sometimes localized only the boundaries of the anomalous region rather than the entire anomalous object. This suggests a potential bias of the pixel-space evaluation towards local texture and boundary differences, possibly indicating a limitation in capturing high-level semantic inconsistencies with the current architecture.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Flow Mismatching as a notable contribution to the field of unsupervised anomaly detection, particularly for industrial visual inspection. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

- **Novel Paradigm**: Flow Mismatching introduces a novel anomaly detection paradigm that deliberately moves away from reconstruction-based approaches, which often struggle with plausible anomaly reconstruction. By directly leveraging the discrepancy between a learned normal velocity field and the geometric path to a test image, it offers a distinct and effective signal for anomaly identification.
- **Strong Theoretical Foundation**: The work provides a rigorous theoretical decomposition of the velocity mismatch signal. By demonstrating its relation to a denoising error and, crucially, a Fisher divergence term between anomalous and normal path marginal distributions, the paper offers a principled explanation for why this signal effectively separates anomalies. This theoretical clarity also guides practical design choices, such as the `t^2` time-weighting and the use of minimum aggregation over multiple paths.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance**: Empirically, Flow Mismatching achieves competitive or superior performance compared to leading reconstruction-based methods and outperforms other flow matching-based anomaly detection techniques on standard industrial benchmarks (MVTec-AD and VisA). This validates its practical utility and establishes it as a robust solution for detecting subtle defects.
- **Computational Efficiency and Flexibility**: The method features an explicit and tunable test-time computational budget (number of paths `K` and time steps `T`). This allows practitioners to flexibly adjust the trade-off between inference speed and detection accuracy. Since increasing `K` and `T` primarily involves parallelizable forward passes, it can be adapted to various deployment scenarios, from high-throughput screening to high-accuracy critical inspection, without requiring complex test-time optimization.
- **Interpretability and Localization**: The pixel-wise anomaly heatmaps generated by Flow Mismatching directly visualize regions where the learned normal flow disagrees with the geometric path. This provides an intuitive and fine-grained localization of anomalies, which is crucial for practical industrial applications.
- **Advancement of Flow Matching for AD**: By demonstrating a highly effective novel application of flow matching models for anomaly detection, this research contributes to the broader understanding and utility of continuous-time generative models in discriminative tasks. It highlights the untapped potential of direct velocity field diagnostics.
- **Future Research Directions**: The identified failure cases regarding large object-level anomalies, and the limitations related to computational cost for very high-resolution images, suggest clear avenues for future work. These include integrating stronger semantic representations, exploring more advanced neural network architectures, or developing latent-space flow matching formulations to improve performance on complex anomalies and enhance scalability.
