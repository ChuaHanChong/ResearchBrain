# Hierarchical Vision Language Action Model Using Success and Failure Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> The VINE paper introduces a hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that explicitly learns from both successful and failed robot demonstrations to enhance robustness and generalization. It enables a high-level planner to anticipate and avoid failures through a feasibility-guided tree search, achieving up to 17.4% relative improvement in success rate over VLM-as-planner baselines in unseen simulation tasks.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models primarily train on successful demonstrations, discarding valuable information contained in failed attempts.
- This reliance on success-only data leads to VLA models lacking robustness and struggling with generalization when faced with novel task configurations or subtle environmental changes.
- End-to-end reactive policies often attempt direct, collision-prone actions, failing to discern feasible multi-step plans in complex, long-horizon tasks.

## Method

- A hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model (**VINE**) is developed, consisting of a System 2 high-level planner for reasoning and a System 1 low-level action executor for control.
- System 2 incorporates a failure-aware value estimator, trained on both successful and failed demonstrations, to guide a feasibility-guided tree search for optimal subgoal sequences.
- System 1 executes the selected subgoals using a ==flow-matching== model for actions and a learned termination rule, trained solely on successful demonstrations to maintain robust low-level control.

## Results

- VINE-Full significantly outperformed unified VLA models like \pi_0 in simulation, achieving up to 0.422 success rate in unseen plug insertion tasks where \pi_0 reached 0.267.
- The model demonstrated superior performance over VLM-as-planner baselines, showing a **17.4%** relative improvement in unseen drawer packing tasks over GPT-4o with failure examples.
- Real-world experiments on a 6-DoF robotic arm confirmed VINE's efficacy, achieving **65%** success rate for unseen sponge packing compared to \pi_0's **55%**, demonstrating enhanced robustness to domain shifts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly leveraging "negative experience" (failed demonstrations) as a structured learning signal for high-level planning significantly enhances robot robustness and generalization.
> - A hierarchical architecture effectively decouples failure-aware reasoning from low-level control, allowing for targeted training that improves planning without destabilizing core execution skills.
> - Integrating a value function informed by failure data into a tree search mechanism enables robots to proactively anticipate and avoid brittle execution paths, improving reliability in novel scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

VINE operates at the intersection of several rapidly advancing fields: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL), and the utilization of large language models (LLMs) for robotic control and planning.

**Advancements in Vision-Language-Action Models:**
The paper builds upon the recent success of large-scale VLA models like RT-1, RT-2, RT-X, OpenVLA, and $\pi_0$, which have demonstrated impressive capabilities in unifying perception, language, and action through end-to-end learning from vast datasets. These models leverage web-scale vision-language pretraining and large-scale robot demonstration data to learn generalizable robotic policies. VINE acknowledges the power of these models, particularly $\pi_0$, as its backbone for low-level control (System 1). However, it critically observes that prior VLAs typically train on *successful* demonstrations while discarding numerous *failed* attempts. This discarding is a significant missed opportunity, as failures contain crucial information about where and how policies can be fragile.

**Hierarchical Systems and LLMs for Robotics:**
A parallel line of research explores hierarchical control by coupling large language models with low-level controllers. Early examples like Code as Policies and ProgPrompt used LLMs for symbolic planning or code generation, while ReAct-style prompting interleaved planning and action selection. More recent work, such as Hi Robot, HAMSTER, and MolmoAct, trains both levels of the hierarchy to decompose complex instructions into atomic commands or predict 2D/3D paths. VINE adopts this dual-system idea, distinguishing between high-level reasoning (System 2) and low-level control (System 1). Its novelty here lies in integrating *failure-aware reasoning* within the high-level planner, which most existing hierarchical systems largely omit.

**Leveraging Negative Experience and Tree Search:**
The core innovation of VINE is its explicit integration of negative experience. While some prior work in imitation learning (IL) has touched upon mixed-quality data, VINE formalizes it within an HRL framework, treating failures as a structured learning signal rather than noise. This connects to reinforcement learning's natural ability to handle failure data via reward signals. The paper further employs tree search, a foundational planning technique in robotics and recently in LLMs (Tree-of-Thoughts, MCTS), to explore action sequences. Unlike standard MCTS that might rely on random rollouts or success-only value estimates, VINE's tree search is guided by a *failure-aware feasibility estimator* that predicts success probabilities using both successes and failures. This positions VINE as a critical advancement in making robotic policies not just competent, but robust and aware of their own limitations.

In essence, VINE pushes the boundaries of VLA models by addressing their inherent fragility. It synthesizes insights from large-scale VLA pretraining, hierarchical control, and advanced planning techniques, with the unique contribution of explicitly learning from robot failures to achieve more reliable and generalizable execution, particularly in novel and challenging scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the VINE project is to enhance the robustness and reliability of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, especially when confronted with novel or challenging manipulation tasks. The authors identify a significant limitation in existing VLA models: their training largely relies on successful teleoperated demonstrations, systematically discarding failed attempts. The core motivations behind VINE stem from addressing this oversight:

1. **Exploiting Underutilized Data:** Failed demonstrations, which naturally occur during data collection, are currently treated as noise and discarded. The authors hypothesize that these failures contain invaluable information about *where* and *how* robotic policies can be brittle, information that could be leveraged to improve robustness. The objective is to convert this "negative experience" into a structured learning signal.
2. **Learning Failure-Aware Reasoning:** The motivation is to imbue VLA models with an explicit mechanism for anticipating and avoiding failure. Instead of merely learning successful trajectories, the model should learn what *not* to do and which paths are likely to fail, thereby sharpening decision boundaries and guiding it towards more robust behaviors.
3. **Improving Robustness and Generalization:** Standard VLA models often struggle with generalization when faced with novel task configurations or subtle environmental changes, as they lack an explicit mechanism to reason about the feasibility of alternative plans. VINE aims to overcome this by enabling the agent to score candidate plans based on their predicted success probabilities, proactively pruning brittle branches before execution.
4. **Decoupling Reasoning and Control:** To effectively integrate failure signals, the authors are motivated to separate high-level reasoning (planning) from low-level control (action execution). This hierarchical design, inspired by Kahneman's System 1 and System 2, allows the failure supervision to primarily influence the planner's value modules offline, without destabilizing the core low-level control skills. This separation aims for a more principled way to inject learning signals without tuning complex penalties in imitation learning.
5. **Offline Learning from Mixed-Quality Data:** The goal is to achieve these improvements entirely from existing, offline teleoperation datasets, minimizing the need for expensive online interaction or finely-tuned reward functions typically associated with reinforcement learning.

In summary, VINE is motivated by the desire to make VLA models more intelligent and reliable by teaching them not just "what works," but crucially, "what fails," leading to more robust execution in complex, real-world manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VINE introduces a hierarchical Vision-Language-Action framework formulated under Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL), dividing the overall system into two interacting components: System 2 for high-level reasoning and planning, and System 1 for low-level action execution.

**A. Overall Architecture and HRL Formulation:**
The model is built upon a multimodal backbone (specifically, $\pi_0$ merged with PaliGemma) that encodes vision, language, and action information. Both System 1 and System 2 add LoRA adapters to this shared backbone, sharing perception-language representations while keeping their specific functionalities decoupled. The HRL framework interprets System 2 as a meta-controller that selects high-level *options* (subgoals/edges), and System 1 as the low-level controller executing the *intra-option policy*. The interaction induces a Semi-Markov Decision Process (SMDP) over abstract states (nodes).

**B. Data Assumption and Abstraction:**
VINE's key assumption is the availability of mixed-quality offline datasets, containing both successful and failed teleoperated trajectories.
- **Nodes as Abstract States:** Raw robot/world states are abstracted into "nodes" ($n = \phi(s)$). Each node is instantiated as a **2D scene graph**, encoding objects and their spatial/semantic relations. This allows System 2 to reason about changes in the world state. The scene graph is constructed using an object detector (Grounding DINO) and a VLM (Gemini-2.5-Flash) for filtering and relation prediction.
- **Edges as Subgoals/Transitions:** Transitions between nodes are treated as subgoals, or "edges" ($e_k$), executed over a variable number of primitive steps. Keyframes are detected from consistent gripper-state events. An edge implies an intended transition from $n_k$ to $n_{k+1}$.
- **Dataset Construction:** The mixed-quality trajectories are processed into two datasets:
 - $D_{sys2}$: For System 2, containing tuples $(n_k, e_k, n_{k+1}, z_{0:k-1}, \ell, s_0, r_k)$, where $r_k$ is a sparse terminal reward (1 for success, 0 otherwise) and $z$ is the history context.
 - $D_{sys1}$: For System 1, containing continuous action data $(e_k, n_{k+1}, s_t, a_t, \ell)$, conditioned on the intended edge and next node.

**C. System 2: Reasoning and Planning**
System 2 is responsible for performing a **feasibility-guided tree search** to select an optimal sequence of subgoals. This involves:

1. **Option (Edge) Generation:** Using a Language Modeling (LM) head ($ \hat{P}_\theta $), System 2 autoregressively samples candidate next scene graphs ($n_{k+1}$) and then candidate edges ($e_k$) describing the transition. Token-level beam search generates multiple diverse candidates, which are then filtered for linguistic and scene-graph consistency. The LM head is trained with a standard LM loss on $D_{sys2}$.
2. **Node Evaluation (Failure-Aware Value Estimator):** A scalar value head, $V_\theta(n)$, estimates the success probability of reaching the goal set (G) before the failure set (F). This is formalized by Proposition 1, stating that the value function equals the reach-avoid success probability. Training uses a **first-exit bootstrapping target** and an **asymmetric expectile loss** (similar to IQL) with $\tau_e = 0.7$. This conservative loss discourages overestimation and helps learn a reliable value function from offline mixed-quality data.
3. **Tree Search Algorithm:** A batched MCTS-style planner is used:
 - It maintains statistics (N, W, Q) for nodes.
 - At each iteration, it selects promising frontier nodes by their Q-value.
 - It proposes *k* candidate edges (subgoals) using beam search.
 - Each candidate edge is scored based on a mix of its Q-value and the likelihood from the LM head ($S(n_p, e_i) = \alpha Q(n_p) + (1-\alpha) \hat{P}(e_i|n_p)$).
 - The resulting child nodes are evaluated using the learned state-value $V(n_c)$.
 - Values are backed up along the path.
 - After M iterations, the plan from the highest-valued leaf is selected ($ \tau_{sel} $).

**D. System 1: Action Modeling and Execution**
System 1 executes the chosen subgoal sequence from System 2. It implements both the intra-option policy and termination rule, learned jointly by a single network, and is trained only on successful demonstrations from $D_{sys1}$.

1. **Intra-Option Policy (Action Expert):** This is parameterized by a flow-matching model. It predicts the flow ($\dot{A}_\theta$) of low-level action chunks ($A_t$) given the current state ($s_t$), instruction ($\ell$), selected edge ($e_k$), and predicted next node ($n_{k+1}$). It's trained to match an oracle velocity using an L2 loss.
2. **Termination Rule (Done Expert):** A separate head predicts the end-of-edge probability ($p_{done,\theta}$). It is trained with a focal loss.
3. **Inference:** Given an active pair ($e_k, n_{k+1}$) from System 2's plan, System 1 iteratively integrates the learned flow to produce action chunks. The system advances to the next symbolic step when $p_{done,\theta}$ exceeds a threshold.

This methodology effectively creates a "thinking" System 2 that reasons about feasibility and avoids failures, and a "doing" System 1 that executes robust low-level actions, all learned from mixed-quality offline demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments presented in the VINE paper demonstrate significant improvements in robustness and success rates across various manipulation tasks, both in simulation and the real world, primarily attributed to its failure-aware reasoning capabilities.

**A. Simulation Results:**
Evaluations were conducted on two challenging manipulation tasks: **plug insertion** and **drawer packing**, with both "seen" and "unseen" configurations to test generalization.

- **Superiority over Unified VLA Models:** VINE-Full (the complete model) consistently and significantly outperformed existing unified VLA models like OpenVLA-OFT, GR00T N1.5, and $\pi_0$. For instance, in unseen plug insertion, $\pi_0$'s success rate (SR) was 0.267, while VINE-Full achieved 0.422. Similarly, in unseen drawer packing, $\pi_0$ had an SR of 0.575, compared to VINE-Full's 0.675. This highlights that end-to-end reactive policies struggle with generalization and discerning feasible paths in long-horizon, multi-modal tasks.
- **Outperforming VLM-as-Planner Baselines:** Even when state-of-the-art VLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Flash) were used as high-level planners (System 2) for our System 1, VINE-Full showed stronger performance, especially in unseen settings. For unseen drawer packing, VINE-Full achieved 0.675 SR, a 17.4% relative improvement over GPT-4o with failure examples (0.575). While adding failure examples to VLM prompts did improve their performance, VINE's direct training on demonstration data provided a more grounded and robust planning signal.
- **Critical Role of Failure Data and Tree Search:**
 - **VINE-Chain (no branching, no failure data):** This variant performed poorly, especially on unseen tasks (0.244 unseen SR for plug insertion).
 - **VINE-Tree (tree search, no failure data, confidence scoring):** Introducing tree search improved unseen performance, particularly in multi-modal tasks like drawer packing (unseen SR jumped from 0.450 to 0.525). This shows the value of exploring alternatives.
 - **VINE-Full (tree search + failure-conditioned value):** The most significant gains came from training the value function with failure data. VINE-Full achieved the highest unseen success rates (0.422 in plug insertion, 0.675 in drawer packing). This decisively proves that a value function informed by failures provides a superior and more reliable planning signal, crucial for robustness in novel scenarios.
- **Qualitative Results (Figures 4, 5):** VINE demonstrated successful multi-step planning (e.g., correctly inserting chargers, pushing an obstacle before placing an object) and effective execution. Comparison with baselines showed that VINE's failure-aware planning allowed it to find feasible solutions where reactive models failed by attempting direct, collision-prone actions.

**B. Test-Time Scalability:**
By varying the planner's expansion width (K) in the MCTS-style algorithm, the study showed that allocating more inference-time reasoning (higher K) can improve robustness without retraining. Unseen success rates in plug insertion increased from 28.9% (K=1) to 44.4% (K=4), with a near-linear increase in latency. This indicates a favorable accuracy-latency trade-off and highlights the potential for adaptive computation at deployment.

**C. Bootstrapping Pretrained Models:**
VINE was successfully applied to bootstrap a pretrained $\pi_0$ policy in a "Simpler environment." By collecting additional rollouts and retraining with VINE's failure-aware procedure, the model showed modest but consistent gains in average success rate (from 0.389 to 0.406), with notable improvements on specific tasks like "carrot on plate" (0.236 to 0.375). This demonstrates VINE's ability to improve robustness and transfer to weaker objects while largely preserving strengths on others.

**D. Real-World Deployment:**
Experiments on a 6-DoF robotic arm for **sponge and towel packing** tasks confirmed VINE's real-world efficacy.
- For "seen" objects, VINE achieved 75% SR (sponge) and 55% SR (towel), outperforming $\pi_0$ (60% and 45% respectively).
- For "unseen" objects (domain shift via color), the performance gap widened: VINE achieved 65% SR (sponge) and 55% SR (towel), while $\pi_0$ dropped to 55% and 30%.
This validates that VINE's ability to choose the most feasible strategy, even for deformable objects and in the presence of distractors (e.g., pushing a clock aside), directly translates to enhanced robustness on physical robots (Figure 9).

Overall, the findings conclusively demonstrate that explicitly leveraging failure data and integrating it into a hierarchical, tree-based planning framework significantly boosts the success rate and robustness of VLA models, especially in complex, long-horizon tasks and under distribution shifts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The VINE paper makes a substantial contribution to the field of robotic learning, with significant implications and potential impacts:

**A. Significance:**

1. **Paradigm Shift in Data Utilization:** VINE champions the use of "negative experience" (failure demonstrations) as a critical, structured learning signal, moving beyond the traditional approach of discarding such data. This is a fundamental shift that recognizes failures as invaluable sources of information about policy fragility and infeasibility.
2. **Enhanced Robustness through Failure-Aware Planning:** The core significance lies in demonstrating that by explicitly learning what leads to failure, robots can anticipate risky outcomes and select more robust execution paths. This is particularly crucial for real-world deployment, where unforeseen circumstances and minor perturbations are common. VINE effectively converts broad VLA competence into reliable execution.
3. **Principled Hierarchical Learning:** The formulation as Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) provides a clear and principled way to separate high-level reasoning from low-level control. This modularity allows for targeted training, where failure signals primarily influence the planner without interfering with the core motor skills.
4. **Improved Generalization under Distribution Shift:** VINE's superior performance in "unseen" scenarios across simulation and real-world tasks highlights its ability to generalize better than reactive, end-to-end VLAs or even VLM-as-planner approaches that lack deep grounding in task dynamics. This is a major step towards truly general-purpose robotic agents.
5. **Test-Time Adaptivity:** The demonstration of test-time scalability, where increased planning computation (larger K in tree search) directly translates to higher success rates, offers a practical mechanism for deploying robots that can adapt their reasoning effort to task complexity and uncertainty.

**B. Potential Impact:**

1. **More Reliable Robotic Systems:** The immediate impact is the development of robotic systems that are inherently more robust and less prone to failure in complex manipulation tasks. This is critical for applications in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and domestic robotics, where reliability is paramount.
2. **Efficient Data Collection and Utilization:** By making better use of mixed-quality datasets, VINE could reduce the cost and effort of data collection. Teleoperation inevitably generates failures; instead of discarding them, they become valuable assets for training.
3. **Foundation for Human-Robot Collaboration:** A failure-aware robot could potentially explain *why* a particular plan is infeasible, facilitating more transparent and collaborative interactions with human operators. This opens doors for robots to learn from human feedback not just on what to do, but also what to avoid.
4. **Advancements in AI Planning:** VINE's approach to feasibility-guided tree search, incorporating learned success probabilities from negative examples, offers a powerful extension to traditional planning algorithms. This could inspire new directions in AI planning, particularly for domains with complex, stochastic dynamics.
5. **Benchmarking Robustness:** The introduction of new environments and evaluation metrics focused on failure-aware reasoning provides a valuable benchmark for future research aimed at improving robot robustness.

**C. Limitations and Future Directions:**

The authors candidly discuss several limitations:

1. **Lack of Real-time Within-Chunk Feedback:** Planning occurs at subgoal granularity, with System 1 executing long action chunks without real-time feedback. This can lead to accumulated errors and divergence in dynamic scenes.
2. **System 1 Not Trained on Failures:** The low-level controller (System 1) is trained only on successful executions, making it susceptible to overfitting nominal trajectories and limiting its ability to self-correct from perturbations.
3. **Limited State Abstraction:** The 2D scene graph omits critical 3D metric geometry, contact cues, and short-horizon dynamics, which are crucial for contact-rich manipulation. This can miscalibrate feasibility estimates and increase sensitivity to environmental variations.

To mitigate some limitations, the authors propose a **plug-in module for replanning** that monitors uncertainty and triggers a plan switch to an alternative branch in the search tree. This light-weight approach shows promising results in simulation by boosting success rates without retraining.

Future work could focus on:
- Integrating real-time, within-chunk feedback into the planning loop.
- Training System 1 with failure-conditioned signals or more robust control policies.
- Developing richer state abstractions that capture 3D geometry and contact dynamics.
- Automating uncertainty calibration for replanning.

In conclusion, VINE represents a crucial step towards building truly robust and intelligent robotic agents. By leveraging the often-overlooked information from failures, it provides a principled framework for VLA models to not only understand how to succeed but also how to avoid failure, thereby making them significantly more reliable for real-world deployment.
