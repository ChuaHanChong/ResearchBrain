# Tree-Guided Diffusion Planner

> [!summary] Summary
> The Tree-guided Diffusion Planner (TDP) introduces a zero-shot test-time planning framework that enables pretrained diffusion models to effectively solve complex real-world control problems with non-convex or non-differentiable objectives. It consistently improved performance by up to 20% on tasks like KUKA robot arm manipulation and Maze2d Gold-picking, demonstrating robust generalization without task-specific training.

## Problem

- Existing diffusion planners struggle with real-world planning tasks involving non-convex guide functions or non-differentiable constraints, often leading to convergence to local optima.
- Standard gradient guidance for diffusion models exhibits limited exploration, preferring in-distribution trajectories and struggling to find novel compositional solutions for complex objectives.
- Many supervised planning approaches lack zero-shot generalization and test-time flexibility, requiring task-specific training or expert demonstrations for new tasks.

## Method

- Proposes the Tree-guided Diffusion Planner (TDP), a zero-shot framework that frames test-time planning as a tree search problem, balancing exploration and exploitation through a bi-level sampling process.
- Employs parent branching, using fixed-potential Particle Guidance for control states to generate diverse trajectories and standard gradient guidance for observation states to steer towards task-relevant regions.
- Utilizes sub-tree expansion to refine parent trajectories, generating child trajectories from partially noised versions of parents, conditioned on preceding segments, to perform efficient local search with gradient signals.

## Results

- TDP consistently outperformed baselines (Diffuserγ, MCSS, AdaptDiffuser) across challenging tasks, achieving a score of **47.6** ± **4.1** on Maze2d-Large, significantly higher than MCSS (**25.0** ± **3.8**) and Diffuserγ (**9.3** ± **2.6**).
- In KUKA Robot Arm Manipulation, TDP demonstrated an average improvement of 10-**20%** over baselines and consistently found globally optimal placements in non-concave tasks like PNWP (e.g., **66.81** ± **0.17** vs. AdaptDiffuser's **39.72** ± **0.08**).
- On AntMaze Multi-goal Exploration, TDP showed approximately **11%** improvements in found goals and sequence match scores compared to MCSS (e.g., **66.1** ± **1.9** vs. **61.3** ± **2.0** found goals) while also reducing timesteps required per goal.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing state features into "observation" and "control" based on their interaction with the guide function allows for scalable and domain-agnostic state classification at planning time.
> - A bi-level sampling approach, combining broad exploration via particle guidance in parent branching and targeted exploitation via gradient-guided refinement in sub-tree expansion, effectively navigates complex reward landscapes.
> - Initializing gradient guidance from on-subspace points, as ensured by parent branching, is crucial for converging to feasible global optima and addressing the "initialization problem" of standard gradient guidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Diffusion models have emerged as a data-driven framework for planning, demonstrating capabilities in generating temporally extended trajectories by learning from offline demonstrations. This approach offers advantages over single-step model-free reinforcement learning (RL) methods for long-horizon planning. Pretrained diffusion planners can be adapted for test-time control through guidance functions, which provide numerical scores based on user requirements such as state conditions or physical constraints. Early guidance algorithms, like classifier guidance, have been integrated to generate conditional trajectory samples given test-time reward signals. These guidance mechanisms often involve an exploration-exploitation trade-off, balancing adherence to the pretrained model's distribution for feasibility against optimizing an external guide score, which may necessitate exploring trajectories outside the training distribution.

Prior research in diffusion planners has largely focused on enhancing supervised planning capabilities through sequential, hierarchical, or fine-tuning approaches. These methods typically aim to improve the modeling of system dynamics from static offline RL benchmarks, often requiring task-specific training, value estimators, or expert demonstrations. While successful in certain offline benchmarks, these approaches often limit test-time flexibility and zero-shot generalization, as collecting optimal trajectories for each new task can be impractical.

A related direction involves enhancing zero-shot planning capabilities, where planners adapt to unseen tasks using only a pretrained model and dense reward signals. However, test-time planning in these contexts has often been evaluated on relatively simple optimization tasks, frequently involving convex optimization problems where a unique global optimum maximizes a smooth guide function. Examples include minimizing distance to a single goal or matching configurations. The main bottleneck in these scenarios has often been the quality of trajectory samples from pretrained models, leading prior work to prioritize sample quality improvement over a deeper investigation into guidance algorithms themselves.

Training-free guidance methods, drawing from classical planners like A* and model predictive control, leverage structural priors and domain knowledge. However, their flexibility for complex, out-of-distribution generalization is often limited to smooth, differentiable guide functions. Tree-based decision-making structures, such as Monte Carlo Tree Search, have been employed to represent hierarchical sequential decisions and explore full-horizon trajectories. Nevertheless, these methods can face scalability challenges in high-dimensional or continuous control tasks, especially with external guidance, and may struggle with long-horizon dependencies if their aggregation depth is limited.

This work addresses the limitations of existing diffusion planners and guidance strategies by focusing on real-world planning tasks that involve high-level abstractions, often leading to non-convex guide functions or non-differentiable constraints. The proposed method, Tree-guided Diffusion Planner (TDP), introduces a zero-shot test-time planning framework designed to overcome the challenge of balancing exploration and exploitation under complex, non-standard test-time objectives, without requiring additional task-specific training or expert supervision. It differentiates itself by providing a flexible planning algorithm that can handle a broad class of guide functions, moving beyond the reliance on smooth, convex optimization landscapes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and flexible planning framework that enables pretrained diffusion models to effectively solve real-world test-time control problems, particularly those characterized by complex objectives. The authors identify several core challenges that motivate their work:

1. **Incompatibility with Non-Convex Guide Functions and Non-Differentiable Constraints:** Existing test-time guidance methods for diffusion planners typically assume smooth, convex optimization landscapes. However, many real-world planning tasks involve high-level abstractions that introduce non-convex guide functions or non-differentiable constraints. For example, maze navigation might require bypassing an intermediate goal, which imposes a non-differentiable rule. Similarly, multi-reward block stacking necessitates reconciling multiple configuration-dependent reward signals to determine an optimal arrangement, leading to a non-convex reward landscape. Standard gradient-based guidance algorithms exhibit reduced effectiveness in such scenarios.

2. **Limitations of Standard Gradient Guidance:** Naïve gradient guidance, a common approach for test-time planning with diffusion models, often performs optimally under convex and differentiable reward landscapes. However, it struggles with non-convex objectives due to a tendency to converge to local optima. This limitation is exacerbated by the "in-distribution trajectory preference" of pretrained diffusion models, which favor generating trajectories aligned with previously seen data, rather than exploring novel compositional solutions required for complex objectives.

3. **Lack of Zero-Shot Generalization and Test-Time Flexibility:** Recent supervised planning approaches often require task-specific training, such as learning value estimators or fine-tuning with expert demonstrations. This reliance on task-specific data collection or model adaptation limits their flexibility for zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks, especially when collecting optimal trajectories for each new task is impractical or infeasible. The goal is to adapt pretrained models without any additional training or expert supervision at test time.

4. **Balancing Exploration and Exploitation:** Guided planning inherently involves a trade-off between exploiting the pretrained model's knowledge for feasible trajectories and exploring novel regions of the trajectory space to maximize an external guide score. Conventional gradient-based guidance mechanisms struggle to effectively manage this balance, often requiring careful, task-dependent tuning of guidance strength (e.g., `α`), which introduces significant overhead.

To address these motivations, the authors propose the Tree-guided Diffusion Planner (TDP) with the following key objectives:
- To enable zero-shot test-time planning using only pretrained diffusion models and reward signals.
- To effectively balance exploration of diverse trajectory regions and exploitation of gradient information across an expanded solution space.
- To flexibly accommodate complex test-time specifications, including non-convex guide functions, non-differentiable constraints, and multi-reward structures.
- To generate both dynamically feasible and task-specific fit trajectories, even when encountering out-of-distribution scenarios.
- To improve upon the performance of existing state-of-the-art zero-shot planning methods across a range of challenging tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Tree-guided Diffusion Planner (TDP) is presented as a zero-shot test-time planning framework that leverages a pretrained diffusion planner to generate adaptive trajectories. It frames test-time planning as a tree search problem, balancing exploration and exploitation through a bi-level sampling process. The core modules of TDP include state decomposition, parent branching, and sub-tree expansion.

#### 3.1. State Decomposition

TDP autonomously categorizes features within the state vector based on their interaction with the guide function. This process distinguishes between:
- **Observation states:** Features that receive non-zero gradients from the user-defined guide function (`∂J/∂s_i ≠ 0`). These states are directly influenced by the task objective.
- **Control states:** Features that receive zero gradients from the guide function (`∂J/∂s_i = 0`). These states govern the underlying system dynamics but are not directly steered by the immediate task objective.
This gradient-based criterion facilitates scalable and domain-agnostic decomposition at planning time, without requiring prior knowledge of state categories. For example, in a KUKA robot arm task, block positions would be identified as observation states (as they affect a distance-based guide function), while robot joint angles would be classified as control states.

#### 3.2. Parent Branching

The first phase of TDP's bi-level planning involves generating diverse "parent trajectories" to encourage broad exploration of the control state space. This is achieved through a modified denoising process that combines fixed-potential Particle Guidance (PG) for control states with gradient guidance for observation states.
- **Particle Guidance (PG) for Control States:** Unlike conventional gradient methods that pull samples towards high-reward regions, PG introduces repulsive forces. It is implemented using the gradient of a Radial Basis Function (RBF) computed from pairwise distances between control trajectories within a batch. This mechanism promotes diversity among generated samples, leading to a wide coverage of dynamically feasible trajectories, largely independent of task objectives.
- **Gradient Guidance for Observation States:** Simultaneously, observation states are steered towards task-relevant regions defined by the guide function using standard gradient guidance (`∇J`).
The overall guidance in a single denoising step is formulated as an integrated term, where the update for the control components of the predicted mean (`μ_i-1_control`) is influenced by PG, and the update for observation components (`μ_i-1_obs`) is influenced by gradient guidance. This integrated guidance term is a sum of the gradient guidance part and the particle guidance part, conditioning the same pretrained reverse denoising process. This procedure allows for broad exploration in the control state space, which in turn exposes the planner to a richer variety of observation state configurations and stronger gradient signals from the guide function `J`.

#### 3.3. Sub-Tree Expansion

The second phase refines the parent trajectories using task gradient signals through a fast denoising process with a reduced number of steps (`N_f << N`). For each parent trajectory:
- A random "branch site" (`b`) is selected from its intermediate states.
- A "child trajectory" is generated by denoising from a partially noised version of the parent trajectory, conditioned on the preceding segment up to the branch site.
This process enables fine-grained local search around the parent branch, improving alignment with the guide signal. Sub-tree expansion serves two main purposes:
- **Enhance Dynamic Feasibility:** It refines potentially perturbed control states from parent branching, ensuring that child trajectories remain dynamically feasible within the learned distribution.
- **Efficient Local Search:** Parent trajectories act as diverse initial points for local search. The child trajectories leverage gradient guidance to find better local optima around these diverse starting points.

#### 3.4. Necessity of Bi-Level Sampling

The bi-level sampling framework is designed to address the "initialization problem" in gradient guidance. Standard gradient guidance, when initialized from Gaussian noise, can steer samples away from the learned data manifold, leading to off-subspace local optima that are unrealistic or infeasible (as demonstrated in Proposition 1-a). In contrast, TDP's parent branching, especially with unconditional PG, ensures that initial trajectory samples remain close to the learned data subspace. When sub-tree expansion, guided by task objectives, is initialized from these on-subspace points, it can leverage both the underlying data structure and gradient information to converge to global optima (as demonstrated in Proposition 1-b). This structured search over trajectories helps in handling multi-reward structures and avoiding local optima effectively.

#### 3.5. Tree Construction and Action Execution

TDP constructs a trajectory tree where the root is the initial state, parent trajectories form branches, and child trajectories form leaf nodes. Each leaf node represents a complete trajectory with an associated guide score. The path with the highest score is selected as the final solution. TDP supports both open-loop planning (executing the entire planned trajectory) and closed-loop planning (executing the first action and then replanning).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TDP involved three diverse zero-shot planning tasks featuring non-convex and non-differentiable objectives: Maze2d Gold-picking, KUKA Robot Arm Manipulation, and AntMaze Multi-goal Exploration. TDP was compared against several baselines and ablations, including Diffuser, Diffuserγ (TAT), AdaptDiffuser, Monte-Carlo Sampling with Selection (MCSS), Stochastic Sampling (MCSS+SS), and ablations of TDP (TDP (w/o child) and TDP (w/o PG)).

#### 4.1. Maze2d Gold-picking

This task required the agent to pick up a hidden gold objective before reaching a final goal, presenting a non-differentiable constraint and a black-box intermediate goal inference problem.
- **Performance:** TDP consistently outperformed Diffuserγ and MCSS across both single- and multi-task settings. For instance, on Maze2d-Large, TDP achieved a score of 47.6 ± 4.1, significantly higher than MCSS (25.0 ± 3.8) and Diffuserγ (9.3 ± 2.6). Similar trends were observed in multi-task settings.
- **Ablation Study:** TDP (w/o child) achieved an approximately 7% improvement over MCSS overall, indicating the benefit of diverse parent trajectories. TDP (w/o PG) showed a substantial performance drop (e.g., 39.1 ± 4.2 vs. 47.6 ± 4.1 on Maze2d-Large), highlighting the critical role of particle guidance in the parent branching phase for effective exploration and discovery of hidden objectives. The bi-level sampling allowed the planner to better localize the gold position and gather stronger gradient signals.

#### 4.2. KUKA Robot Arm Manipulation

This category included conditional stacking (PNP (stack)), pick-and-place (PNP (place)), and a custom task, pick-and-where-to-place (PNWP), which featured a non-concave guide function with local and global optima.
- **PNP Tasks:** TDP demonstrated an average improvement of 10% over MCSS and 20% over TAT. For PNP (place), TDP achieved 36.94 ± 0.13, outperforming MCSS (31.37 ± 0.10) and AdaptDiffuser (36.17 ± 0.11), showcasing generalization to diverse task configurations without fine-tuning. TDP (w/o PG) also outperformed MCSS on PNP (place), underscoring the contribution of sub-trajectory refinement.
- **PNWP Task:** This task specifically tested exploration capacity to avoid local optima. Mono-level guided sampling methods (AdaptDiffuser, MCSS(+SS), TAT, TDP (w/o child)) tended to converge to local optima. In contrast, TDP and TDP (w/o PG) consistently identified globally optimal placements. TDP achieved 66.81 ± 0.17, significantly higher than AdaptDiffuser (39.72 ± 0.08) and MCSS (35.69 ± 0.07).
- **Diversity Analysis:** TDP, particularly with unconditional PG in parent branching, generated diverse trajectories, crucial for finding global optima in PNWP. This bi-level strategy supported compositional solutions.

#### 4.3. AntMaze Multi-goal Exploration

This challenging task involved a high-dimensional observation space and required visiting multiple goals in a priority-aware sequence, making it a complex non-convex problem.
- **Performance:** TDP exhibited approximately 11% improvements over MCSS in both the number of found goals and the sequence match score, while also reducing the timesteps required per goal. For instance, TDP achieved 66.1 ± 1.9 found goals and a sequence match score of 33.8 ± 1.4, compared to MCSS's 61.3 ± 2.0 and 30.2 ± 1.3, respectively.
- **Ablation Study:** Removing particle guidance (TDP (w/o PG)) degraded all three metrics, confirming its necessity for goal discovery and sequence alignment. Removing child branching (TDP (w/o child)) maintained performance on found goals and sequence match but increased timesteps per goal, suggesting less efficient exploration without the refinement step.

#### 4.4. Standard Maze Benchmarks (Appendix J)

Supplementary results indicated that TDP also surpassed sequential approaches like MCTD and Diffusion-Forcing on standard offline maze benchmarks (e.g., pointmaze, antmaze navigate-v0), achieving optimal or near-optimal performance. This was attributed to TDP's multi-step exploration framework which helps avoid locally greedy decisions.

In summary, the results demonstrate that TDP consistently outperforms existing baselines across tasks with diverse, complex, and unseen test-time objectives. Its bi-level sampling process effectively addresses the challenges of non-convexity, non-differentiable constraints, and the exploration-exploitation trade-off in zero-shot planning scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Tree-guided Diffusion Planner (TDP) represents a notable advancement in the field of decision-making with diffusion models, particularly for test-time guided control problems. Its significance stems from addressing several fundamental limitations of existing planning approaches:

1. **Enhanced Adaptability to Complex Objectives:** TDP provides a flexible framework for handling real-world planning tasks that often involve non-convex guide functions, non-differentiable constraints, and multi-reward structures. This is a critical improvement over conventional gradient-based guidance methods, which typically assume smooth and convex optimization landscapes and often fail in such complex scenarios. The ability to reason over higher-level objectives without requiring specific model redesign or retraining expands the applicability of diffusion planners.

2. **Robust Zero-Shot Generalization:** A core contribution of TDP is its zero-shot test-time planning capability. It leverages pretrained diffusion models and reward signals without necessitating expert demonstrations, task-specific training, or value estimators. This significantly reduces the practical overhead associated with deploying planning systems in new or varied environments, where collecting optimal trajectories or retraining models for each task is often infeasible. This capability is particularly impactful for scenarios demanding out-of-distribution generalization.

3. **Effective Exploration-Exploitation Balance:** The bi-level trajectory sampling process, combining diverse parent trajectories generated via particle guidance (exploration) and refined sub-trajectories via gradient-guided denoising (exploitation), offers a structured mechanism to navigate complex reward landscapes. This approach mitigates the common problem of diffusion planners getting stuck in local optima, allowing for the discovery of globally high-reward solutions that simpler gradient methods might miss.

4. **Improved Performance on Challenging Benchmarks:** TDP consistently demonstrated superior performance over state-of-the-art baselines across a suite of challenging tasks, including Maze2d Gold-picking with non-differentiable constraints, KUKA Robot Arm Manipulation with non-concave objectives (PNWP), and AntMaze Multi-goal Exploration with priority-aware sequencing. These empirical results validate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed framework.

The potential impact of TDP is broad, particularly in areas requiring autonomous decision-making and control in complex, dynamic environments:
- **Robotics:** TDP could enable robots to perform more sophisticated manipulation and navigation tasks, especially in unstructured environments where precise task specifications are difficult to program explicitly or where new goals frequently arise.
- **Autonomous Systems:** Its zero-shot capabilities make it suitable for various autonomous agents, allowing them to adapt to unforeseen conditions or novel objectives without extensive retraining.
- **General AI Planning:** By providing a robust method for handling complex, non-differentiable objectives, TDP contributes to the broader field of AI planning, opening avenues for diffusion models to tackle problems traditionally addressed by symbolic or search-based methods.

While TDP introduces additional computational cost due to expanded search in trajectory space and pairwise distance calculations, the foundational work on balancing exploration and exploitation with pretrained generative models for adaptive control represents a significant step forward. Future research may focus on optimizing these computational aspects while preserving the planning performance.
