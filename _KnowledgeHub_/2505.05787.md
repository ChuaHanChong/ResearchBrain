# Demystifying Diffusion Policies: Action Memorization and Simple Lookup Table Alternatives

> [!summary] Summary
> An investigation into Diffusion Policies for robot manipulation reveals that their strong performance in data-sparse environments stems from memorizing training actions rather than generalizing. The work introduces an Action Lookup Table (ALT) policy, which explicitly implements this memorization, achieving comparable task success with approximately 300 times faster inference and 100 times less memory than Diffusion Policies.

## Problem

- The mechanisms behind Diffusion Policies' strong performance in robot manipulation, despite large model capacity and sparse datasets, were not fully understood.
- Diffusion Policies exhibit an "overfitting paradox" where low training loss and high test loss correlate with good test-time performance, defying traditional generalization principles.
- Their iterative denoising process leads to slow inference times, limiting real-time application in robotics.

## Method

- Conducted empirical analysis on Diffusion Policies, including generalization behavior and early stopping experiments, to investigate ==action memorization==.
- Designed the Action Lookup Table (ALT) policy, an efficient alternative that explicitly implements action memorization using contrastive learning for latent space embedding.
- Evaluated ALT against Diffusion Policies and a KD-Tree baseline on real-robot cup grasping tasks under various in-distribution and out-of-distribution conditions.

## Results

- Diffusion Policies were found to consistently reproduce specific training action sequences, even when presented with various out-of-distribution visual inputs, indicating action memorization.
- The ALT policy achieved **100%** task success on in-distribution real-robot cup grasping, comparable to Diffusion Policies, while outperforming a KD-Tree baseline.
- ALT demonstrated approximately **300x** faster inference time (**0.009s** vs. **2.65s**) and **100x** smaller memory footprint (45.5 MB vs. **5.3 GB**) compared to Diffusion Policies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diffusion Policies in data-sparse robot manipulation regimes primarily rely on "manifold overfitting" and memorization of training action sequences.
> - This memorization, traditionally considered a form of overfitting, is a necessary and effective strategy for achieving strong performance in these specific robotic imitation learning contexts.
> - An explicit action lookup table approach, powered by contrastive learning, can effectively replicate the performance of complex Diffusion Policies at significantly reduced computational cost.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental aspect of imitation learning for robot manipulation, specifically concerning the performance characteristics of Diffusion Policies. In this domain, policies are trained to map visual inputs to action sequences using a limited number of expert demonstrations. Diffusion Policies, first introduced by Chi et al., have established themselves as a robust approach, demonstrating high dexterity in complex, high-dimensional robot manipulation tasks. They are notable for their ability to model multi-modal action distributions, scale to high-dimensional outputs, and generate long-horizon action sequences, often outperforming existing methods on various benchmarks.

However, the underlying mechanisms contributing to the strong performance of Diffusion Policies, particularly when trained on relatively small datasets (e.g., 50–200 demonstrations) while maintaining large parameter counts (over 100 million, comparable to models trained on billions of images), have remained unclear. A notable observation is that Diffusion Policies often exhibit low training loss but high test loss, a classical indicator of overfitting in machine learning, yet this overfitting appears to be a prerequisite for strong test-time performance in robot manipulation. This apparent contradiction forms the central mystery that this paper seeks to explain.

The broader research landscape includes extensive work on diffusion models for generative tasks such as image, video, and audio synthesis. In this context, memorization in diffusion models has been previously studied, with observations indicating that smaller datasets can lead to memorization and that reconstructive memorization can occur even in models trained on vast datasets. This prior work suggests that diffusion models might learn attraction basins for training samples or converge to known training samples regardless of initialization. Methods to mitigate memorization in image generation typically involve modifying loss functions, gradients, or conditioning approaches.

This paper extends the discussion of memorization to the robotics domain, proposing that, contrary to general machine learning principles where memorization is often undesirable, it may be a beneficial strategy for Diffusion Policies in robot manipulation. The authors posit that the combination of a rich input space (images), a relatively limited output space (robot actions), and the inherent lack of task-level supervision in imitation learning, coupled with high model capacity and sparse data, renders overfitting via memorization an effective explanation for Diffusion Policies' observed performance in in-distribution settings. By explicitly investigating this phenomenon, the work positions itself to refine the understanding of powerful generative models in control applications and inform the design of more efficient and robust robotic policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

This research is driven by two primary objectives, each motivated by observed characteristics and limitations of current Diffusion Policies in robot manipulation:

#### 2.1. Objective 1: Demystify the Performance of Diffusion Policies through Action Memorization

- **Motivation**: Diffusion Policies demonstrate high dexterity and robustness in robot manipulation tasks, even when trained on a small number of demonstrations. However, the exact reason for this strong performance is not well understood. The authors highlight several perplexing observations:
 - **Data-Capacity Mismatch**: Diffusion Policies, with over 100 million parameters, are comparable in size to large image generation models trained on billions of images. Yet, for robot manipulation, they are typically trained on a sparse dataset of 50-200 demonstrations. This significant disparity between model capacity and data volume raises questions about how these models generalize.
 - **Overfitting Paradox**: During training, Diffusion Policies are often trained until the training loss is low, but the test loss remains high. In conventional machine learning, this is a classic signal of overfitting, which typically leads to poor generalization and test-time performance. However, for Diffusion Policies in robotics, this "overfitting" is empirically observed to be necessary for strong test-time performance. This challenges traditional machine learning wisdom regarding generalization.
 - **Absence of Action Generalization**: The authors hypothesize that Diffusion Policies, in the sparse data regime characteristic of robot manipulation, may not be learning generalized action patterns. Instead, they might be implicitly memorizing the demonstrated action sequences.

 The motivation for this objective is to resolve these inconsistencies by providing a specific, empirically supported explanation: Diffusion Policies essentially memorize an action lookup table. This implies that, at runtime, the policy identifies the closest training image in a latent space and recalls the corresponding training action sequence, enabling reactivity without explicit action generalization. The authors propose that this mechanism is effective precisely because the data density is insufficient for the model to learn broader action generalizations.

#### 2.2. Objective 2: Develop a Simpler and Faster Alternative Policy based on Action Memorization

- **Motivation**: While Diffusion Policies exhibit strong performance, they suffer from a significant practical drawback: slow inference time. The iterative denoising process inherent to diffusion models leads to substantial computational overhead. This slow inference translates into delayed robot execution, often requiring pauses as the model recomputes the next action sequence. This latency can be problematic for real-time control and closed-loop operation, particularly for resource-constrained robots.

 Recognizing the potential for performance gains if the hypothesized memorization mechanism is indeed the core driver of Diffusion Policy success, the authors are motivated to design a more computationally efficient alternative. The objective is to create a policy that explicitly replicates the action memorization behavior, achieving comparable performance to Diffusion Policies but with drastically reduced inference time and memory footprint. This would provide a lightweight and practical solution for robot manipulation tasks that require rapid, responsive control.

A secondary motivation integrated into Objective 2 is the development of an explicit **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection mechanism**. By designing a policy that relies on similarity in a learned latent space, it becomes possible to set a threshold. If a runtime observation is too far from any training examples in this space, it can be flagged as OOD, providing a simple yet effective runtime monitor for potential policy failures and enhancing safety during robot operation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves a two-pronged approach: an empirical analysis of Diffusion Policies to validate the action memorization hypothesis, followed by the design and evaluation of a novel, lightweight policy explicitly implementing this mechanism.

#### 3.1. Diffusion Policy Analysis

The analysis began with a conceptual illustration of different generalization regimes for generative models. Using a simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)-based diffusion denoising model trained on 2D points arranged on a 1D star-shaped manifold, the authors demonstrated four regimes:
- **Low-capacity, small dataset**: The model failed to adequately fit the data, producing erratic inferences.
- **Low-capacity, large dataset**: The model oversimplified the data manifold, learning only an approximation.
- **High-capacity, small dataset (Diffusion Policy regime)**: The model tended to memorize individual training samples, accurately fitting the limited points but failing to generalize the broader underlying manifold. Inference results largely overlaid the training data, resembling a lookup table.
- **High-capacity, large dataset (Image Diffusion regime)**: The model effectively fit both the data and the true underlying distribution, demonstrating strong generalization.

To empirically test the action memorization hypothesis for Diffusion Policies, a series of robot cup grasping experiments were designed:
- **Policy Training**: A Diffusion Policy was trained for cup grasping using the standard codebase. Thirty demonstrations of cup locations, evenly spaced across the workspace, were provided, with a central square region held out. Each position received a single demonstration to avoid confounding effects of multi-modal action generation. The policy was conditioned on inputs from a fixed third-person camera and a wrist-mounted camera.
- **Generalization Behavior Analysis**: The learned policy was validated on the 30 in-distribution (InD) cases. Subsequently, a variety of interpolation and extrapolation inputs were systematically introduced:
 1. **InD-Interpolate**: Cups placed at test positions precisely between original training positions.
 2. **OOD-Interpolate**: Cups moved from an InD position through an OOD region (the blue tape square) to another InD position.
 3. **OOD-Extrapolate**: Cups gradually moved from an InD position to an OOD location outside the fixed camera’s field of view.
 4. **OOD-Distractors**: OOD visual distractors of varying difficulty (e.g., tape, hammer, altered lighting, and highly irrelevant images like cats and dogs) were introduced while keeping the cup in its training position.
- **Metric**: A custom "similarity score" (`S`) was introduced to quantify how closely an inference action sequence matched sequences from the training set. `S = 1 - s(τ(r),τ(1)) / s(τ(1),τ(2))`, where `s` denotes the average Euclidean distance between matched points on the current action sequence and its closest training sequence, and `s(τ(1),τ(2))` is the distance between the second-closest and closest training sequences. This metric normalizes such that 1.0 indicates perfect re-execution of a training trajectory, and 0.5 indicates interpolation halfway between the two closest trajectories. The score measures recall, not action quality.
- **Early Stopping Experiment**: To further support the necessity of overfitting, an experiment was conducted where a Diffusion Policy was trained with one-third of the dataset reserved for validation. Validation loss was used for early stopping, while training action Mean Squared Error (MSE) was monitored.

#### 3.2. Action Lookup Table (ALT) Policy

Building on the insights from the Diffusion Policy analysis, the authors designed the Action Lookup Table (ALT) policy as a lightweight alternative, conceptualized as a hashing function that explicitly implements action memorization.
- **Architecture**:
 - **Fusion Encoder**: This component integrates three input modalities: a first-person view from the robot arm's end-effector, a third-person view, and the end-effector pose (`p_i`). It maps these inputs into a low-dimensional feature representation (LFR), denoted `z_i`.
 - `f_img`: A pre-trained ResNet-18 backbone, leveraging its ability to extract general-purpose visual features from large-scale datasets like ImageNet, was used as the image encoder.
 - `f_pose`: A separate encoder for the end-effector pose.
 - `f_fusion`: A network combining the features from `f_img` and `f_pose`.
- **Training Phase (Contrastive Learning)**:
 - The fusion encoder was trained using a contrastive learning framework, specifically the NT-Xent loss.
 - For each input data sample (`d_i` = (hand image, third-person image, pose)), two augmented views (`v_i^(1)` and `v_i^(2)`) were generated using distinct augmentation pipelines (`A1` and `A2`).
 - These augmented views were fed into the fusion encoder to produce L2-normalized embeddings (`z_i^(1)` and `z_i^(2)`), forming a positive pair. The NT-Xent loss encourages the embeddings of positive pairs to be close in the latent space while pushing embeddings of negative pairs (from other samples in the batch) apart. The temperature parameter `τ` (set to 0.4) controls the sensitivity of the loss.
- **Inference Phase**:
 - **Latent Space Creation**: After training the fusion encoder, all frames from the raw demonstration dataset were encoded into embeddings. These embeddings, along with their corresponding trajectory ID and local frame index, were stored in a database (referred to as the ALT latent space).
 - **Real-time Inference**:
 1. A new runtime observation (current views and end-effector pose) is encoded into an embedding using the *same trained fusion encoder*.
 2. A search is performed within the ALT latent space to find the most similar stored embedding based on cosine similarity.
 3. **OOD Detection**: If the maximum cosine similarity falls below a predefined threshold `γ`, the input is classified as out-of-distribution, and a predefined safe fallback behavior is triggered.
 4. **Action Retrieval**: If the input is not OOD, the system retrieves the matched trajectory ID and local frame index from the database, enabling the execution of the associated memorized action sequence in real time.
- **Evaluation of ALT**: The ALT policy was evaluated on real-robot cup grasping tasks under both InD and various OOD conditions (similar to those used for Diffusion Policy analysis, including distractors). Its performance was compared against a KD-Tree-based nearest neighbor retrieval method and the Diffusion Policy, focusing on trajectory recall, task success rates, Model Inference Time (MIT), and memory footprint. Different configurations of ALT (with/without pose information, varying `γ`) were also tested.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings that support the hypothesis of action memorization in Diffusion Policies and demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed ALT policy.

#### 4.1. Diffusion Policy Analysis Findings

- **Conceptual Support for Memorization**: The analysis of generative models on a 2D manifold (Fig. 2) provided theoretical intuition. Specifically, a high-capacity model trained on a small dataset (analogous to Diffusion Policies in robotics) was observed to memorize individual training samples, with inference outputs closely aligning with training data points, effectively functioning as a lookup table. This behavior was described as "manifold overfitting."
- **Empirical Evidence of Action Memorization**:
 - **In-Distribution (InD) Behavior**: When presented with InD inputs (cup placed at exact training positions), the Diffusion Policy consistently produced action sequences that almost perfectly overlapped with the corresponding training sequences (Fig. 4 left, Fig. 11). The custom similarity score was consistently close to 1.0 for the nearest training trajectory, with a substantial gap to the second nearest, indicating clear memory-based retrieval rather than interpolation or generalization.
 - **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Behavior**: Across various OOD scenarios (InD-Interpolate, OOD-Interpolate, OOD-Extrapolate, and OOD-Distractors, including wildly OOD images like cats and dogs), the Diffusion Policy did not exhibit generalized action generation. Instead, it nearly always reproduced one of the training action sequences (Fig. 4 top/bottom right, Fig. 12, 13, 14). This suggests a default behavior where the model reverts to a memorized trajectory, even if that trajectory is ineffective for the current OOD input.
- **Early Stopping Experiment**: The experiment demonstrated that while validation loss might reach a minimum early in training (suggesting optimal generalization), the training action MSE remained high at that point (Fig. 7). This implies that extensive training, leading to what would conventionally be considered overfitting, is necessary for Diffusion Policies to produce accurate actions, even for in-distribution inputs. This finding supports the notion that overfitting through memorization is a key component of their performance.

#### 4.2. ALT Policy Findings

The ALT policy was designed to explicitly leverage action memorization and was evaluated against KD-Tree and Diffusion Policy for real-robot cup grasping.
- **Comparable Performance in InD**:
 - All methods (KD-Tree, Diffusion Policy, ALT) achieved a 100% "Recall IDs" rate for in-distribution cases, meaning they successfully retrieved the correct underlying training trajectory. This indicated that the memorization mechanism itself was effective across these approaches.
 - In real-robot rollouts for InD conditions (ID-1), Diffusion Policy and ALT (specifically, "Ours w/o p, γ = 0.75") both achieved 100% task success, whereas the KD-Tree method achieved 63.3%. This demonstrated that ALT could match the task completion performance of Diffusion Policies.
- **OOD Performance and Detection**:
 - The KD-Tree method generally performed well in OOD scenarios (correct matches in most of OOD1-OOD7), though it failed in OOD3 and OOD5.
 - The Diffusion Policy performed well in many OOD scenarios (OOD1-OOD3, OOD6-OOD7) but failed in more severe distribution shifts (OOD4 and OOD5).
 - The ALT policy demonstrated robust OOD detection. Depending on the OOD threshold (`γ`) and inclusion of pose information, ALT correctly identified OOD inputs and either provided accurate matches or flagged the input as OOD. For example, "Ours w/o p, γ = 0.75" successfully handled OOD1-OOD6 scenarios, only flagging OOD for OOD7 (cat image) and OOD8 (dog image). This configuration showed a good balance between recall and OOD awareness.
- **Superior Efficiency**:
 - **Model Inference Time (MIT)**: The ALT policy demonstrated a significantly faster inference time (~0.009s) compared to the Diffusion Policy (~2.65s) and the KD-Tree (~0.09s). This represents an approximate 300-fold speedup over Diffusion Policy.
 - **Memory Footprint**: ALT required a substantially reduced memory footprint (45.5 MB) compared to Diffusion Policy (5.3 GB), making it approximately 100 times smaller.
 - This drastic reduction in computational cost and memory usage, while maintaining comparable task performance, positions ALT as a more practical solution for resource-constrained robotics.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that Diffusion Policies in robot manipulation primarily rely on memorizing training actions due to overfitting in data-sparse regimes. The ALT policy, by explicitly implementing this memorization via contrastive learning and a lookup table, achieves comparable performance to Diffusion Policies with significantly higher efficiency and the added benefit of explicit OOD detection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries several significant implications and potential impacts across the fields of machine learning, robotics, and policy learning:

#### 5.1. Fundamental Understanding of Diffusion Policies in Robotics

The primary significance lies in providing a specific and empirically supported explanation for the performance of Diffusion Policies in robot manipulation. By hypothesizing and validating that these policies essentially function as implicit action lookup tables through severe overfitting and memorization, the work "demystifies" a previously unexplained phenomenon. This insight challenges the conventional machine learning wisdom that overfitting is inherently detrimental, suggesting that in specific contexts—such as imitation learning with sparse data, a rich input space (images), and a comparatively limited output space (robot actions)—memorization can be an effective, even beneficial, strategy. This reframes the understanding of how powerful generative models operate in practical control tasks, potentially influencing future theoretical analyses and model designs.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Efficiency and Practicality for Robot Manipulation

The development of the Action Lookup Table (ALT) policy represents a significant practical advancement. By demonstrating that a much simpler architecture can replicate the effective behaviors of Diffusion Policies while being orders of magnitude more efficient, the work provides a pathway for deploying capable policies on resource-constrained robotic platforms.
- **Real-time Performance**: The approximately 300-fold reduction in inference time (from ~2.65s for Diffusion Policy to ~0.009s for ALT) is critical for closed-loop, real-time robot control, eliminating pauses and enabling smoother, more responsive operation.
- **Resource Constraints**: The substantial decrease in memory footprint (ALT requiring only ~0.85% of Diffusion Policy's memory) addresses a key limitation for embedded systems and smaller robots, expanding the range of hardware capable of executing such advanced manipulation policies.
- **Accessibility**: A simpler, more efficient architecture could lower the computational barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners, making sophisticated robot manipulation techniques more accessible.

#### 5.3. Improved Safety and Robustness through Explicit OOD Detection

The inclusion of an explicit Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection mechanism in the ALT policy is a crucial contribution to robot safety and reliability. Unlike implicit policies that might produce unpredictable or erroneous actions when confronted with novel, unseen situations, ALT can proactively flag inputs that deviate significantly from its training distribution. This capability allows for:
- **Graceful Fallbacks**: When an OOD situation is detected, the robot system can trigger predefined safe fallback behaviors, preventing potentially damaging or undesirable actions.
- **Increased Trust**: Knowing when a policy is operating outside its comfort zone can increase operator trust in autonomous systems, a vital factor for real-world deployment.
- **Diagnostic Tool**: OOD flags provide valuable diagnostic information for understanding policy limitations and guiding future data collection efforts.

#### 5.4. Implications for Data Collection and Policy Design

The findings underscore the importance of data distribution and density in imitation learning. For scenarios where acquiring large, densely covering datasets is challenging—a common reality in robotics—this research suggests that designing policies to effectively memorize and retrieve actions from sparse demonstrations may be a more pragmatic approach than striving for complex generalization. This could guide future efforts in data collection strategies, focusing on critical demonstration points rather than attempting to cover all possible states. Moreover, it encourages the development of policy architectures that explicitly facilitate or enhance this memorization-based retrieval.

The work acknowledges limitations, particularly concerning the scalability of ALT to much larger datasets and its sensitivity to hyperparameters. Future research exploring these aspects will be important for extending the impact of these findings. Overall, this paper provides a significant step forward in understanding and practically implementing visuomotor policies for robot manipulation, offering both fundamental insights and a highly efficient alternative.
