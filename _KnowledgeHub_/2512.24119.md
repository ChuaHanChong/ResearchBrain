# GeoBench: Rethinking Multimodal Geometric Problem-Solving via Hierarchical Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed GeoBench, a hierarchical benchmark with formally verified synthetic data, to diagnostically evaluate multimodal large language models (MLLMs) on geometric reasoning. The framework revealed that MLLMs struggle significantly with self-reflective reasoning, while capacities for sub-goal decomposition and irrelevant premise filtering are strong determinants of problem-solving success.

## Problem

- Existing geometric problem-solving benchmarks suffer from potential data contamination, leading to overestimation of MLLM capabilities due to memorization.
- Current evaluations focus solely on final answers, neglecting the logical steps and reasoning processes crucial for rigorous geometric problem-solving.
- Benchmarks lack diagnostic granularity, making it difficult to pinpoint specific points of failure (e.g., visual perception, planning, theorem application) in MLLM reasoning.

## Method

- GeoBench introduces a ==hierarchical evaluation framework==, inspired by the van Hiele model, to assess geometric reasoning across four levels: Visual Perception, Goal-Oriented Planning, Rigorous Theorem Application, and Self-Reflective Backtracking.
- The benchmark utilizes formally verified synthetic geometric problems generated by ==TrustGeoGen==, ensuring correctness, eliminating data contamination risks, and providing detailed reasoning graphs.
- Six distinct multiple-choice tasks are operationalized within the framework (e.g., Numerical Perception, ==Sub-Goal Decomposition==, ==Faulty Branch Localization==) to enable fine-grained diagnostic assessment of MLLMs.

## Results

- All MLLMs, including top proprietary models like OpenAI-o1, showed profound limitations in the Faulty Branch Localization task, with highest accuracy at **27.89%**, only slightly above random guessing.
- Reasoning MLLMs (e.g., OpenAI-o3, Gemini-2.5-pro) consistently outperformed general MLLMs, with OpenAI-o3 achieving **91.00%** accuracy on Sub-Goal Decomposition.
- The identified correlations between GeoBench tasks and overall problem-solving generalized robustly to out-of-distribution datasets, confirming the benchmark's ability to measure genuine geometric reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MLLMs' performance consistently declines with increasing task complexity, with self-reflective backtracking presenting the most significant challenge.
> - Sub-Goal Decomposition and Irrelevant Premise Filtering are identified as critical reasoning bottlenecks, exhibiting the strongest correlations with overall geometric problem-solving success.
> - Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting does not universally improve performance and can even degrade results in tasks like faulty branch localization, challenging its presumed efficacy in all complex reasoning scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical challenge in the rapidly evolving field of multimodal large language models (MLLMs): their actual ability to perform complex, rigorous geometric reasoning. While MLLMs have achieved remarkable success across various vision-language tasks, including some geometric benchmarks, their underlying reasoning capabilities, especially in mathematically precise domains, remain inadequately understood and evaluated.

**Current Landscape & Limitations:**
The existing landscape of geometric problem-solving (GPS) benchmarks, such as GeoQA, Geometry3K, and MathVista, primarily suffers from several key limitations:
1. **Data Contamination Risk:** Many benchmarks source problems directly from public textbooks, leading to potential data contamination where MLLMs might "memorize" answers or patterns rather than genuinely reason.
2. **Overemphasis on Final Answers:** Most evaluations focus solely on the correctness of the final answer, neglecting the logical steps, theorem application, and reasoning processes crucial to geometric rigor. This black-box approach hinders diagnostic capabilities.
3. **Insufficient Diagnostic Granularity:** When models fail, existing benchmarks often do not provide insights into *why* they failed (e.g., poor visual perception, incorrect planning, flawed theorem application, or inability to correct errors). This lack of granularity stalls targeted improvements.

**GeoBench's Contribution to the Landscape:**
GeoBench positions itself as a transformative step in this landscape by introducing a **hierarchical evaluation framework** and utilizing **formally verified synthetic data**.
- **Addressing Data Contamination:** By employing `TrustGeoGen` (Fu et al., 2025) to generate synthetic problems with formally verified solutions and reasoning graphs, GeoBench largely mitigates the risk of data contamination from publicly available sources. This ensures that models are tested on novel problems requiring genuine reasoning.
- **Moving Beyond Final Answers:** GeoBench explicitly dissects geometric reasoning into four hierarchical levels (Visual Perception, Goal-Oriented Planning, Rigorous Theorem Application, and Self-Reflective Backtracking), operationalized through six distinct tasks. This allows for a granular, diagnostic assessment of MLLMs' capabilities at each stage of problem-solving, rather than just the end result. This aligns with the broader AI community's growing demand for explainable AI and transparency in reasoning.
- **Theoretical Grounding:** The framework is inspired by the van Hiele model of geometric thinking, a well-established cognitive model of how humans develop geometric understanding. This theoretical foundation lends psychological validity to GeoBench's hierarchical structure.
- **Complementary to Existing Work:** While other benchmarks like GeomRel and GeoSense have begun to explore specific subskills (structural diagram comprehension, theorem application), GeoBench offers a more comprehensive and systematically linked evaluation of these subskills within a unified framework. It provides a more holistic view of "structured reasoning capacities," including self-reflective reasoning and theorem orchestration, which previous MLLM works often overemphasize on final answer correctness.

By providing a robust, diagnostic benchmark, GeoBench aims to guide the development of MLLMs that exhibit genuine, interpretable, and verifiable geometric reasoning, rather than merely pattern matching or memorization. It pushes the research frontier towards building more cognitively aligned and trustworthy AI systems for complex mathematical domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind GeoBench stems from the acknowledged shortcomings of current evaluation methodologies for geometric problem-solving (GPS) in vision-language models (VLMs) and multimodal large language models (MLLMs). Despite apparent successes of MLLMs on some existing benchmarks, the authors argue that these evaluations often fail to rigorously assess the true depth of geometric reasoning.

The key motivations are:
- **Mitigate Test Data Contamination:** Existing benchmarks heavily rely on problems from public textbooks, creating a significant risk that MLLMs might "memorize" solutions or exploit patterns rather than perform genuine reasoning, leading to an overestimation of their capabilities.
- **Shift Focus from Final Answers to Reasoning Processes:** Current evaluations predominantly focus on the correctness of the final answer. This narrow focus overlooks the logical steps, theorem applications, and proof generation processes that constitute rigorous geometric reasoning. A diagnostic approach is needed to understand *how* models arrive at answers, not just *if* they do.
- **Enhance Diagnostic Granularity:** When MLLMs fail a geometric problem, existing benchmarks provide little insight into the specific point of failure. It remains unclear whether the failure originates from poor visual perception, ineffective planning, incorrect theorem selection, or an inability to correct logical errors. A fine-grained diagnostic framework is crucial for identifying bottlenecks and guiding model improvements.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the GeoBench project are:
1. **Develop a Hierarchical Evaluation Framework:** To create GeoBench, a novel benchmark structured according to the van Hiele model of geometric thinking, to systematically evaluate geometric reasoning capabilities across four progressive levels: Visual Perception, Goal-Oriented Planning, Rigorous Theorem Application, and Self-Reflective Backtracking.
2. **Generate Formally Verified Tasks:** To construct a dataset of six specific tasks (Numerical Perception, Structural Perception, Irrelevant Premise Filtering, Sub-Goal Decomposition, Theorem Selection, and Faulty Branch Localization) using formally verified geometric problems generated by TrustGeoGen, ensuring correctness and eliminating data contamination.
3. **Systematically Assess MLLM Capabilities:** To thoroughly evaluate the performance of state-of-the-art general and reasoning MLLMs across these six hierarchical tasks, providing a comprehensive diagnostic profile of their strengths and weaknesses in geometric reasoning.
4. **Identify Key Reasoning Bottlenecks:** To determine which specific geometric reasoning abilities (corresponding to the hierarchical tasks) are most critical for overall problem-solving proficiency, thereby providing actionable insights for future model development.
5. **Investigate Prompting Strategies:** To examine the efficacy of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting in geometric reasoning tasks, particularly in scenarios involving misleading information, and challenge conventional assumptions about its universal benefits.
6. **Ensure Generalizability and Robustness:** To validate that the insights gained from GeoBench generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) geometric test sets and that the benchmark results are robust to minor perturbations, confirming its intrinsic value beyond specific dataset biases.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of GeoBench is meticulously designed to create a hierarchical, diagnostic benchmark for geometric problem-solving, grounded in formally verified data. The approach can be broken down into two main phases: input data preparation and hierarchical evaluation framework construction.

#### 3.1. Input Data Preparation (Leveraging TrustGeoGen)

The foundational step involves generating a robust and verifiable dataset of geometric problems. The authors utilize `TrustGeoGen` (Fu et al., 2025), a scalable, rule-driven formal engine, to produce:
- **Geometric Diagrams:** Visual representations of problems.
- **Problem Statements:** Natural language descriptions of the geometric tasks.
- **Reasoning Graphs:** Formal, complete, and verifiable step-by-step solutions for each problem.

`TrustGeoGen` operates by:
1. **Base Scene Generation:** Starting with random base scenes and premises (e.g., points, lines, initial numerical data).
2. **Iterative Construction Augmentation:** Expanding complex geometries by adding new premises, ensuring topological consistency through a compiler. Premises include both geometric relationships ($p_r^i$) and numerical parameters ($p_n^j$).
3. **Rule-Based Inference:** Applying a predefined set of geometric theorems (rules, $R$) to infer new states ($S$) from premises ($P$), forming a complete reasoning graph $G = (P, S, R, \rightarrow)$. This graph formally defines how states are derived from antecedent states and rules.
4. **Goal-Oriented Reasoning Path Extraction:** For a given target conclusion ($s_t$), `TrustGeoGen` traces backward through the complete reasoning graph to extract the most efficient and necessary goal-oriented reasoning path ($P$). This path consists of triplets $(S_{i-1}, r_s, s_i)$, showing how each state is derived.

#### 3.2. Hierarchical Evaluation Framework Construction

Inspired by the van Hiele model, GeoBench categorizes geometric problem-solving into four hierarchical levels, which are operationalized through six distinct multiple-choice tasks. Each task is constructed using the detailed information (premises, states, rules, reasoning paths) from `TrustGeoGen`-generated problems:

- **Level 1: Visual Perception**
 - **Numerical Perception (N.P.):** Assesses MLLMs' ability to extract numerical information from diagrams. Correct options are actual numerical premises ($p_n^j$). Distractors are created by altering values or changing labels to non-existent elements, testing for hallucinations.
 - **Structural Perception (S.P.):** Evaluates MLLMs' ability to discern geometric relationships from diagrams. Correct options are actual structural premises ($p_r^i$). Distractors are formed by negating other given relationships (e.g., "collinear" becomes "not collinear").

- **Level 2: Goal-Oriented Planning**
 - **Irrelevant Premise Filtering (I.P.F.):** Measures MLLMs' ability to identify and discard redundant premises. Problems are selected where a subset of initial premises ($P \setminus S_0$) is not used in the goal-oriented reasoning path. The correct answer is an unused premise; distractors are randomly selected *necessary* premises.
 - **Sub-Goal Decomposition (S.D.):** Tests MLLMs' capacity to break down complex problems into necessary intermediate steps. For a target conclusion $s_t$, the correct option is the set of preceding states $S_{r_t} \setminus P$ required to derive $s_t$ by applying the final rule $r_t$. Distractors combine subsets of necessary conditions with irrelevant intermediate states.

- **Level 3: Rigorous Theorem Application**
 - **Theorem Selection (T.S.):** Assesses MLLMs' ability to correctly identify and apply geometric theorems. Questions are filtered to involve at least three rules. The correct option is a rule *not* used in the problem's solution, chosen from the entire rule repository. Distractors are three randomly selected *used* rules.

- **Level 4: Self-Reflective Backtracking**
 - **Faulty Branch Localization (F.B.L.):** Evaluates MLLMs' self-correction capabilities by identifying where a flawed reasoning path deviates from a correct one. A wrong reasoning path ($P_{wrong}$) is constructed, sharing initial steps with the correct path ($P$) but leading to an erroneous conclusion. The task is to pinpoint the *first* step where $P_{wrong}$ deviates, testing for the ability to trace errors.

#### 3.3. Data Distribution and Difficulty Assessment

- **Scale:** GeoBench comprises 1,021 question-answer pairs across the six tasks.
- **Diversity:** TrustGeoGen utilized 76 geometric constructions, 42 deduction rules, and 40 base scenarios.
- **Complexity:** A t-SNE analysis of image and solution embeddings shows GeoBench problems are more diverse and complex (longer token lengths for solutions) than GeoQA.
- **Difficulty:** Empirical evaluation against real-world benchmarks (GeoQA, Geometry3K, OlympiadBench-Geo) demonstrates that GeoBench problems generally fall within "elite problem-solving tiers," surpassing middle-school difficulty.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

The authors evaluated a range of general MLLMs (e.g., LLaVA, Qwen2-VL, GPT-4o) and reasoning MLLMs (e.g., QvQ, OpenAI-o1/o3, Gemini-2.5-pro, Claude-3.7-sonnet), as well as an agent model (CodePlot). Metrics included accuracy and accuracy gain over random choice. Ablation studies were conducted for Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, minor image perturbations, and text-only inputs to thoroughly analyze model behavior. Human evaluation by Ph.D. experts served as a performance ceiling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on GeoBench yielded several critical insights into the capabilities and limitations of state-of-the-art MLLMs in geometric reasoning:

1. **Declining Performance with Increasing Task Complexity:** A consistent trend across all evaluated MLLMs is that performance generally degrades as the tasks move up the hierarchical reasoning levels. Models perform best on Level 1 (Visual Perception) tasks and worst on Level 4 (Self-Reflective Backtracking) tasks.
2. **Superiority of Reasoning MLLMs (with caveats):** Proprietary reasoning-focused MLLMs, particularly OpenAI-o3, OpenAI-o1, and Gemini-2.5-pro, consistently demonstrated superior capabilities compared to general MLLMs. OpenAI-o3 achieved the highest accuracies in Goal-Oriented Planning (S.D., 91.00%) and Rigorous Theorem Application (T.S., 54.39%).
3. **Severe Limitations in Self-Reflective Backtracking (F.B.L.):** All MLLMs, including the top-performing reasoning models, showed profound limitations in the Faulty Branch Localization (F.B.L.) task. The highest accuracy achieved (OpenAI-o1 at 27.89%) was only marginally better than random guessing (25.71%), and some models even performed below random. Human experts, in contrast, achieved 52.94% accuracy, highlighting a significant gap in this crucial self-correction ability. The failure analysis suggests models struggled with directional errors (deviation from goal) versus factual errors.
4. **Key Reasoning Bottlenecks Identified:**
 - **Strong Correlation with Problem-Solving:** Sub-Goal Decomposition (S.D.) and Irrelevant Premise Filtering (I.P.F.) tasks exhibited the strongest positive Spearman correlation coefficients (0.88772 and 0.97902 respectively) with final problem-solving accuracy on GeoBench-solving. Theorem Selection (T.S.) also showed a strong correlation (0.82954). This finding highlights that the ability to decompose problems into sub-goals and filter irrelevant information are critical determinants of complex geometric problem-solving success.
 - **Weak Correlation of F.B.L.:** The Faulty Branch Localization (F.B.L.) task showed the weakest correlation (0.49561), further underscoring that current MLLMs' struggle with self-reflection does not translate proportionally to their overall problem-solving success, perhaps because their current solutions don't rely heavily on iterative error correction.
5. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Limitations:** Contrary to conventional wisdom, CoT prompting did not universally improve performance across all tasks. While it showed marginal benefits in some Level 2 tasks (S.P., I.P.F., S.D.), it yielded inconsistent results in Level 1 and 3, and critically, *degraded* performance in the F.B.L. task. The authors hypothesize that the presence of erroneous reasoning steps within the F.B.L. task's prompt, when combined with CoT, might mislead or interfere with the models' reasoning processes, revealing a nuanced perspective on CoT's applicability.
6. **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization:** The correlations observed between GeoBench tasks and final-answer accuracy remained consistent when evaluated on OOD datasets like GeoQA and Geometry3K. This robust generalization confirms that GeoBench effectively measures genuine geometric reasoning capabilities, not merely overfitting to specific dataset patterns.
7. **Robustness to Perturbations and Importance of Multimodality:** Minor image perturbations (±20° rotation) did not significantly impact model performance, suggesting a degree of visual robustness. However, text-only inputs generally led to inferior evaluation performance compared to multimodal inputs (image + text), affirming the importance of visual perception in geometric reasoning tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GeoBench represents a significant advancement in the evaluation of multimodal large language models for complex mathematical reasoning, particularly in geometry. Its significance and potential impact can be articulated as follows:

1. **Diagnostic Framework for AI Development:** The most impactful contribution of GeoBench is its establishment as a novel, hierarchical diagnostic framework. By breaking down geometric reasoning into four levels and six tasks, it moves beyond superficial, answer-only assessments. This fine-grained diagnosis allows researchers to pinpoint specific weaknesses in MLLMs—whether in visual perception, strategic planning, theorem application, or self-correction—thereby providing concrete, actionable insights for targeted model improvements. This shift is crucial for developing more robust and reliable AI systems.

2. **Guiding Principles for Model Enhancement:** The identification of "Sub-Goal Decomposition" and "Irrelevant Premise Filtering" as critical bottlenecks (due to their strong correlation with overall problem-solving success) provides invaluable guidance for future MLLM development. Researchers can now prioritize these areas, focusing on architectures or training methodologies that explicitly foster these strategic planning capabilities. This could lead to more intelligent agents that can effectively navigate complex, multi-step reasoning challenges.

3. **Challenging Conventional Prompting Strategies:** The unexpected finding regarding Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting—that it can degrade performance in certain self-reflective tasks—challenges the prevailing assumption of CoT's universal efficacy. This insight is critical for the broader NLP and AI community, suggesting that prompting strategies need to be nuanced and task-specific, especially in domains requiring rigorous logical deduction or error correction where misleading information can be detrimental. It opens avenues for research into more sophisticated, context-aware prompting or reasoning architectures.

4. **Mitigating Data Contamination and Overfitting:** By utilizing `TrustGeoGen` for the generation of formally verified, synthetic problems, GeoBench significantly reduces the risk of test data contamination that plagues many existing benchmarks. This ensures that models are evaluated on their genuine reasoning abilities rather than their capacity for memorization or pattern exploitation, fostering the development of truly intelligent systems.

5. **Advancing Trustworthy AI and Explainability:** The detailed reasoning graphs and hierarchical structure of GeoBench contribute to the pursuit of more interpretable and trustworthy AI. By scrutinizing the steps of reasoning, GeoBench helps in understanding not just *what* an MLLM knows, but *how* it knows it. This aligns with the broader push for explainable AI, making MLLMs more transparent and their mathematical conclusions more verifiable.

6. **Educational and Curricular Implications:** The alignment with the van Hiele model of geometric thinking provides a strong cognitive foundation. Insights from GeoBench could inform the design of educational technologies, intelligent tutoring systems, and even mathematical curricula by highlighting the stages of geometric understanding where AI models, and by extension possibly human learners, face the most significant challenges.

7. **Foundation for Future Research:** GeoBench lays a robust foundation for future research directions. The authors themselves point to extending the benchmark to 3D geometric structures, which would require developing new spatial rules and formal definitions. Moreover, the detailed failure analysis and the identified limitations of MLLMs in self-reflection invite further research into advanced error correction mechanisms, counterfactual reasoning, and more sophisticated self-supervised learning for geometric proofs.

In conclusion, GeoBench is more than just another dataset; it's a meticulously crafted framework that redefines how geometric reasoning in AI is understood and evaluated. Its findings offer actionable intelligence for building the next generation of MLLMs capable of genuine, rigorous, and verifiable mathematical intelligence.
