# SR-Platform: An Agentic Pipeline for Natural Language-Driven Robot Simulation Environment Synthesis

> [!summary] Summary
> SR-Platform is a production-deployed agentic pipeline that turns free-form English workspace descriptions into executable MuJoCo robot scenes. Four stages handle planning, cache-first asset retrieval or LLM-to-CadQuery mesh generation, industrial-constraint layout, and MJCF assembly with robot merging. Production telemetry shows a median of about 50 s for a five-object scene, and about 30-40 s when assets come from the cache.

## Problem

- Building a training-ready MuJoCo scene by hand needs expertise in 3D asset modeling, MJCF specification, collision-free layout, and robot integration, which limits the environment diversity robot learning needs.
- Prior LLM-for-robotics work assumes a fixed object library, stops at symbolic scene or task descriptions, or needs manual steps before the scene runs in a physics simulator.
- Text-to-3D methods optimize visual appearance and often give geometry that is not watertight, scale-consistent, or simulator-compatible, and scene generators rarely check industrial clearance and safety constraints.

## Method

- A four-layer pipeline run by a **PipelineCoordinator**: an ==LLM orchestrator== makes a ==JSON scene plan==, an ==asset forge== resolves objects, a ==layout architect== places them, and a bridge assembles ==MJCF==.
- A ==cache-aware asset forge== queries a ==Qdrant vector store== first, and on a miss has an LLM write ==CadQuery code== that becomes an ==STL mesh==, with validation, retry, ==fallback geometry==, and re-indexing for reuse.
- An **IndustryRulesChecker** in the layout architect checks electrical clearance, fire-safety egress, machinery distance, and ventilation, returning ==structured violations==. A **RobotRegistry** and **RobotMerger** insert the chosen robot at a computed ==spawn pose==.

## Results

- Over **30 days** of production telemetry (**611** successful LLM calls), CadQuery generation had a median latency of **17.9 s** and a p95 of **65.4 s**, making the asset forge the dominant latency cost.
- Estimated end-to-end latency is about **50 s** (p50) for a five-object all-miss scene and about **68 s** for eight objects. All-cache-hit scenes take about **30-40 s**, and L4 assembly takes under **1 s**.
- Of **300** first-attempt asset generations, **34** needed a retry (**11.3%**), and automatic retry or simplified-geometry fallback kept scene jobs from aborting. The deployment supports **5** concurrent jobs, with a queue of **100**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treat LLM-to-CAD generation as a recoverable subsystem, not a deterministic compiler. Validation, retry, caching, and graceful degradation give robustness despite an 11.3% first-attempt failure rate.
> - Splitting scene synthesis into typed, auditable stages with a shared data contract localizes failures and makes caching possible, unlike one monolithic LLM prompt.
> - Cache-first retrieval compounds over time, since each generated asset cuts future LLM calls and latency. Caveat: the end-to-end latencies are estimated from LLM-call distributions, not measured request traces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a recognized bottleneck in simulation-based robot learning: the manual and labor-intensive process of generating diverse, physically plausible simulation environments. Current practices for constructing training-ready MuJoCo scenes necessitate specialized expertise in 3D asset modeling, MJCF specification, spatial layout design, collision avoidance, and robot-model integration. This requirement often limits the scalability and diversity of environments critical for advanced robot learning methodologies such as reinforcement learning, imitation learning, manipulation benchmarking, and sim-to-real evaluation.

Existing platforms for simulation environment generation either require substantial manual effort for new scene construction (e.g., ThreeDWorld) or offer curated, predefined environment collections tied to specific benchmarks (e.g., BEHAVIOR-1K). While some generative approaches exist (e.g., RoboGen), they frequently rely on curated object libraries and are not specifically focused on production deployment for industrial scene synthesis. SR-Platform differentiates itself by targeting on-demand generation of industrial robot workspaces from natural language input, producing directly executable MuJoCo scenes rather than merely task descriptions or benchmark-specific environments.

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated capabilities in structured output generation, code synthesis, and spatial reasoning, leading to their application in various robotics contexts, including reward function generation (Eureka), task planning (SayCan), and robot control program synthesis (Code as Policies). However, many of these LLM-based approaches either assume a fixed object library, produce only symbolic scene descriptions, or require manual intervention before simulation execution. SR-Platform aims to bridge this gap by providing an end-to-end solution that converts unconstrained natural language prompts into physically valid, simulator-ready robot scenes without significant manual intervention.

In the domain of LLM-based code and asset generation, traditional text-to-3D methods primarily focus on visual appearance, often lacking the watertight, scale-consistent geometry required for physics simulation. SR-Platform employs LLM-to-CadQuery synthesis to generate simulator-compatible STL assets, integrating validation and retry mechanisms for robustness. The system also aligns with the paradigm of agentic AI systems (e.g., ReAct, LangGraph) by decomposing complex scene generation into modular, tool-mediated reasoning steps, enhancing debuggability, validation, and deployment compared to monolithic LLM approaches.

Furthermore, SR-Platform contributes to overcoming the sim-to-real gap by focusing on structural diversity in environments. While domain randomization often addresses visual variations, this work emphasizes generating varied workspace layouts, object combinations, and task-relevant spatial constraints, which are critical for developing robust robot policies. It operates at the environment-synthesis layer, complementing high-fidelity physics backends like MuJoCo, Newton, and Isaac Lab.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind SR-Platform is to alleviate the significant bottleneck presented by the manual construction of robot simulation environments, which is currently a prerequisite for large-scale robot learning. This process demands specialized knowledge across multiple domains, including 3D modeling, simulator-specific file formats like MuJoCo's MJCF, spatial planning to avoid collisions, and adherence to workspace constraints. Such requirements make it challenging to generate the diverse and physically plausible environments necessary for robust robot policy training, benchmarking, and sim-to-real transfer. While MuJoCo is a prominent simulator, its MJCF format is designed for precise control rather than intuitive authoring by non-specialist users. The gap between a high-level natural language description of a workspace and an executable simulation environment is substantial.

To address these challenges, the key objectives of SR-Platform are:

1. **Develop an end-to-end agentic pipeline:** The primary objective is to create a fully automated system that transforms free-form natural language descriptions of robot workspaces into executable MuJoCo simulation scenes. This pipeline is designed to include generated or retrieved assets, precisely assigned object poses, and an integrated robot model with spawn coordinates. The system aims to achieve this by embedding LLM calls within a structured, auditable, and cache-aware pipeline, rather than relying on a single, monolithic LLM for scene generation.
2. **Implement a cache-aware asset forge:** A specific objective is to efficiently resolve requested objects into simulator-compatible 3D meshes. This involves designing an asset forge that first attempts semantic retrieval from a vector database of previously generated assets. If no suitable match is found, the system then leverages LLM-to-CadQuery synthesis to generate new 3D geometry. This cache-first strategy is intended to reduce redundant, computationally expensive LLM calls for asset generation and improve overall system efficiency and reliability.
3. **Integrate industrial layout verification:** A crucial objective is to ensure the physical and industrial plausibility of generated environments. The system aims to incorporate industrial constraint checking into the layout generation process, verifying compliance with factors such as electrical clearance, fire-safety egress, machinery safety distances, and ventilation clearance. This integration aims to produce layouts that are not only collision-free but also reflect real-world industrial safety and operational standards.
4. **Evaluate system performance with production telemetry:** The paper aims to provide empirical validation of the system's operational characteristics under real-world conditions. This includes reporting production telemetry data from a deployed system, covering metrics such as per-stage latency, asset generation retry rates, system throughput limits, and estimated end-to-end generation times for cache-accelerated and cache-miss scenarios. This objective seeks to quantify the practical utility and robustness of the agentic pipeline.

Collectively, these objectives are motivated by the goal of making robot simulation environment generation more scalable and accessible, thereby accelerating robot learning and deployment in complex real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SR-Platform is conceptualized as a production-deployed agentic system, designed around a four-layer scene-synthesis pipeline orchestrated by a PipelineCoordinator. This modular architecture ensures that semantic planning, asset resolution, spatial layout, and MJCF assembly are handled independently, enhancing validation, caching, debugging, and extensibility. The system is deployed as a nine-service Docker stack, featuring a backend, worker, frontend, Qdrant for semantic search, PostgreSQL for metadata, Redis for job state, MinIO for mesh storage, InfluxDB for telemetry, and pgAdmin. Communication between the frontend and backend is facilitated via a WebSocket interface, enabling real-time progress streaming for long-running generation jobs.

The pipeline comprises four distinct stages:

**L1: Orchestrator:**
This initial stage translates a user's free-form natural language prompt, describing a robot workspace (e.g., an "assembly line"), into a structured JSON scene plan. The plan includes essential details such as room dimensions, a list of semantic object labels, the selected robot identity, and optional parameters for anomaly generation. Implemented as a lightweight LangGraph workflow, the orchestrator utilizes an LLM for plan generation, followed by a parsing step to validate the JSON structure. This separation of planning from downstream asset generation and layout reasoning simplifies individual LLM calls and facilitates failure localization. The L1 output serves as the primary data contract for subsequent stages.

**L2: Asset Forge:**
The asset forge is responsible for converting each requested object in the structured scene plan into a 3D mesh compatible with the simulator. For each object label, the forge first performs a semantic retrieval query against a Qdrant vector database. Object labels are embedded using a high-dimensional text embedding model, and the most similar cached assets are retrieved. If a match exceeding a predefined similarity threshold is found, the corresponding STL mesh is loaded from MinIO, bypassing further generation. In the event of a cache miss, an LLM-based generation agent is invoked to synthesize CadQuery code for the desired object. This code is then executed to produce an STL mesh, which is subsequently stored in MinIO and indexed into Qdrant for future reuse. This cache-first strategy is critical for reducing the computational expense associated with LLM-to-CAD generation. The asset forge also incorporates validation and retry mechanisms, allowing it to check generated meshes for geometric validity and automatically regenerate failed CadQuery programs, thereby improving robustness.

**L3: Layout Architect:**
Following asset resolution, the layout architect assigns precise spatial poses (positions and rotations) to all objects within the specified room dimensions. This stage receives the enriched scene plan, including object identities and mesh metadata. An LLM-based layout model proposes object placements that are both semantically plausible (e.g., tables on the floor, control panels near walls) and physically consistent (e.g., no object overlap, clearance from walls). Crucially, the layout architect integrates an IndustryRulesChecker, which evaluates generated layouts against industrial constraints inspired by real-world standards for electrical clearance, fire-safety egress, machinery safety distances, and ventilation clearance. Detected violations are returned as structured entries, enabling the system to resample layouts, notify the user, or generate anomaly-labeled scenes if anomaly injection is requested.

**L4: Bridge and MJCF Assembly:**
The final stage, the bridge, converts the complete, laid-out scene plan into an executable MuJoCo scene represented in MJCF XML format. This involves constructing the scene's foundational elements, including the floor, walls, object bodies, mesh references, materials, and global simulation settings. Each generated or retrieved STL asset is inserted as a mesh-backed body with its position and rotation determined by the L3 output. Robot integration is also performed here: a RobotRegistry resolves the selected robot model, and a RobotMerger inserts the robot's MJCF into the generated environment at the designated spawn position, ensuring coordinate-frame consistency for correct rendering and simulation execution. This stage is designed to be deterministic and fast, relying on local XML construction and mesh processing rather than further LLM calls. The final outputs, including the complete MJCF XML and robot spawn coordinates, are stored as per-user scene state and sent to the frontend for visualization.

**Request Lifecycle and Deployment:**
The system operates asynchronously. User prompts from the frontend initiate a WebSocket request to the backend, which authenticates it, enqueues a generation job via ARQ, and streams progress updates. Worker processes execute the L1-L4 pipeline, interacting with Qdrant, MinIO, Redis, and PostgreSQL for data management. Telemetry is recorded in InfluxDB. The frontend visualizes the final MJCF scene using MuJoCo WASM and Three.js. This architecture supports horizontal scaling and ensures fairness through per-user job limits.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SR-Platform utilized production telemetry collected over a 30-day deployment period, encompassing 611 successful LLM calls recorded in InfluxDB. The generation stages primarily used `venice/deepseek-v4-flash`, while `venice/text-embedding-3-large` was used for asset-retrieval embeddings. The CadQuery generation timeout was set to 120 seconds.

**Per-Stage Latency:**
Analysis of LLM call latency by pipeline stage revealed that the L2 asset forge is the dominant contributor to overall runtime.
- **L2 `skill_generator` (CadQuery generation):** Showed a median latency of 17.9 seconds and a p95 latency of 65.4 seconds across 300 calls.
- **L2 `skill_generator_retry`:** Calls were slower, with a median latency of 24.4 seconds and a p95 latency of 77.9 seconds across 34 calls, indicating the increased complexity of correcting initial failures.
- **`unknown` (L1 Orchestration + L3 Layout):** These calls, grouped due to an instrumentation gap, had a median latency of 15.9 seconds and a p95 latency of 73.9 seconds across 275 calls.
- **L4 Bridge:** This stage, which performs local XML construction and mesh processing without LLM involvement, was not included in LLM-call latency measurements and is reported as very fast (<1 second).

**End-to-End Scene Generation Latency (Estimated):**
Based on the LLM call distributions and pipeline structure, estimated end-to-end latencies for typical scenarios were calculated:
- For a five-object scene where all objects result in a cache miss (requiring one L1, five L2 generation calls, and one L3 call), the estimated median end-to-end latency was approximately 50 seconds.
- An eight-object scene with all cache misses was estimated to have a median latency of approximately 68 seconds, attributed to the need for multiple parallel batches of L2 generation calls given a worker limit of five.
- Conversely, for scenes where all requested objects were retrieved from the semantic cache, L2 generation calls were entirely skipped, reducing the estimated median latency to approximately 30-40 seconds.

**Asset Generation Reliability:**
The L2 asset forge demonstrated a first-attempt retry rate of 11.3%, with 34 out of 300 initial `skill_generator` calls requiring at least one retry. Failures primarily stemmed from malformed CadQuery code, geometrically invalid outputs, or meshes failing validation. The system's automatic retry mechanism and fallback to simplified geometry ensured that errors were corrected without aborting the entire scene generation job, prioritizing pipeline robustness.

**System Throughput:**
The deployed system configuration supported five concurrent scene-generation jobs. Throughput was primarily limited by the L2 asset generation stage due to the computational cost of LLM calls and CadQuery execution. However, the architecture is designed for horizontal scaling, allowing for increased throughput by launching additional workers while sharing core services. An improved cache hit rate also contributes to higher effective throughput by reducing the demand for new asset generation.

**Qualitative Examples:**
The paper includes visual examples of generated simulation scenes (Figure 4) that illustrate the system's capability to produce diverse environments such as classrooms, computer labs, kitchens, and laboratories. These examples demonstrate the integration of retrieved or synthesized assets, spatially assigned layouts, and merged robot models within MuJoCo-compatible geometry, rendered in a browser viewer. Figure 5 showcases the Asset Studio's ability to generate complex 3D objects like a barrier fence or an industrial lathe from text prompts, synthesizing CadQuery/OpenSCAD geometry, rendering to STL, and registering for future reuse when semantic retrieval fails.

**AI Model Performance for Asset Generation (Appendix):**
Benchmarks of LLM backends for CadQuery asset generation on `standard_100` (mechanical CAD) and `abstract_45` (free-form) task sets provided insights for model routing. `claude-opus-4-6-fast` achieved the highest composite score (86.8) for mechanical CAD but at a high token cost. `qwen3-coder-480b-a35b-instruct-turbo` presented a strong cost-performance balance (83.1 score, 4.3s latency). For free-form objects, `claude-opus-4-7` ranked highest (85.1), with `qwen3-coder-480b` again identified as a strong open-weight option. Comparisons also indicated that routing through a proxy added approximately 6 seconds of latency while largely preserving geometric quality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SR-Platform represents a development towards automating the creation of robot simulation environments, addressing a significant bottleneck in scaling robot learning. Its primary significance lies in making the generation of diverse, physically plausible MuJoCo scenes more accessible and efficient.

One of the key impacts is the **democratization of environment creation**. By enabling users to generate executable MuJoCo scenes from natural language prompts, the system reduces the necessity for deep expertise in 3D modeling, simulator-specific formats (MJCF), and spatial planning. This can allow a broader range of researchers and engineers to create custom training environments, fostering innovation in robot learning.

The **agentic pipeline and cache-first asset generation strategy** significantly enhance scalability and throughput. The modular design, combined with semantic asset retrieval and LLM-to-CadQuery synthesis, allows for efficient reuse of assets and reduces the computational burden of repeated LLM calls. This compounding advantage, where each successful generation enriches the asset database, suggests that the system's efficiency can improve over time, accelerating future environment creations.

The integration of **industrial layout verification** distinguishes SR-Platform from systems that prioritize visual plausibility over functional constraints. By checking for industrial constraints such as electrical clearance, fire safety, and machinery distances, the platform contributes to generating environments that are more representative of real-world industrial settings. This is crucial for improving the sim-to-real transferability of robot policies trained in these simulated environments, enhancing their practical utility in industrial applications.

Furthermore, the system’s **robustness features**, including automatic retry mechanisms for LLM-generated assets and graceful degradation through fallback geometry, ensure higher reliability. These capabilities mitigate the inherent variability and potential failures associated with LLM-based code generation, allowing scene generation to complete even when initial asset synthesis attempts are unsuccessful.

SR-Platform also lays a **foundation for future synthetic data pipelines**. Its ability to generate diverse environments from natural language can be extended to produce large datasets for anomaly detection, robustness evaluation, and various robot learning tasks. Future integrations, such as image-to-3D asset generation or direct export to robot-learning dataset formats, could further amplify its impact on automated data generation for robotics.

Finally, the **production-oriented features** (e.g., JWT authentication, role-based access control, persistent job state, telemetry) indicate the system's readiness for deployment as a multi-user service, moving beyond a typical research prototype. This enterprise-grade architecture supports reliable, secure, and monitorable operation in real-world settings.

In summary, SR-Platform contributes to advancing robot learning by alleviating a critical bottleneck in environment generation, enhancing accessibility, improving efficiency, promoting structural diversity for sim-to-real transfer, and providing a robust, scalable framework for synthetic data generation in robotics.
