# Learning to Fly in Seconds

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at NYU and TII developed an end-to-end deep reinforcement learning controller for quadrotors that trains in 18 seconds on a laptop, exhibiting robust zero-shot Sim2Real transfer and outperforming classical methods for agile trajectory tracking. The open-sourced framework enables reliable real-world deployment of policies trained with just 300,000 environment steps.

## Problem

- Classical quadrotor controllers require extensive, platform-specific engineering and lack generalizability.
- Deep RL policies for real-world quadrotor control suffer from a significant "Sim2Real gap" and prohibitively long training times, impeding practical deployment.
- Existing RL methods often simplify quadrotor control by using higher-level abstractions, avoiding the complexities of direct motor command (RPM) control.

## Method

- An asymmetric actor-critic deep ==reinforcement learning== architecture directly maps quadrotor states to motor RPM commands (end-to-end control).
- A custom, highly optimized C++ simulator allows for unprecedented training speed (1284 million steps/second).
- The training paradigm integrates curriculum learning, exploration noise decay, observation noise, and an action history for the actor to enhance sample efficiency and Sim2Real transfer.

## Results

- End-to-end control policies were trained in 18 seconds on a laptop, using only 300,000 environment steps, an order of magnitude faster and more sample-efficient than previous methods.
- The trained policies achieved robust zero-shot Sim2Real transfer, successfully controlling a real Crazyflie quadrotor across over 300 flights and generalizing to agile trajectory tracking tasks.
- For fast, agile trajectories, the learned controller outperformed classical PID, Geometric, Nonlinear, and INDI controllers, as well as other RL baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robust end-to-end quadrotor control, directly outputting motor commands, is achievable with deep reinforcement learning through a specialized asymmetric actor-critic architecture and action history.
> - An extremely fast, custom simulator is essential for reducing RL training times from hours to seconds, making rapid iteration and deployment feasible.
> - Curriculum learning and proper noise scheduling significantly improve training reliability and sample efficiency for real-world robotic tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The control of autonomous multirotor aerial vehicles has traditionally relied on classical, cascaded control hierarchies. While effective, these methods often require significant domain expertise and platform-specific engineering for adaptation, limiting their generalizability and ease of deployment. The emergence of machine learning, particularly deep reinforcement learning (RL), has presented a promising alternative for streamlining deployment, enhancing performance, and achieving generalization in complex systems like quadrotors.

However, despite impressive successes in simulation, RL-based control faces significant challenges in real-world deployment. The "simulation-to-reality" (Sim2Real) transfer is notoriously difficult due to model inaccuracies, partial observability, sensor noise, and external disturbances. Furthermore, RL algorithms are often plagued by prohibitively long training times and sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning and reward function design, which creates a high barrier to entry for researchers and developers.

This paper addresses these critical challenges by focusing on **end-to-end quadrotor control**, where the policy directly maps the quadrotor's state to motor commands (Revolutions Per Minute, RPMs). This is a significantly more challenging problem than controlling higher-level abstractions (e.g., velocity, angular rates, or even individual rotor thrusts), as it directly contends with all non-linearities and uncertainties in the vehicle's dynamics, including motor delays and thrust curves. The authors introduce a novel taxonomy classifying different levels of control abstractions, explicitly highlighting the increasing difficulty, non-linearities, and domain parameters encountered at lower control levels. Their work explicitly targets the lowest level (5.1 Motor commands/RPM setpoints), where the reality gap and system complexity are highest.

Previous works in RL for quadrotor control often simplify the problem by:
- Using higher-level control inputs (e.g., velocity, angular rate, or rotor thrusts, which abstract away motor dynamics).
- Employing complex exploration schemes or extensive domain randomization to aid Sim2Real transfer.
- Requiring prohibitively long training times (hours to days, millions to hundreds of millions of environment steps).
- Lacking open-source implementations, hindering reproducibility and further research.

This paper distinguishes itself by:
- Achieving end-to-end control at the lowest possible level (direct RPM control), which is less explored for robust real-world deployment.
- Demonstrating unprecedentedly fast training times (18 seconds on a laptop) and significantly lower sample complexity (0.3 million interactions) compared to state-of-the-art methods, directly addressing the "long training times" issue.
- Successfully performing Sim2Real transfer without explicit domain randomization, leveraging a highly optimized simulator and a carefully designed training paradigm.
- Proposing an asymmetric actor-critic architecture and incorporating an action history to mitigate partial observability, along with curriculum learning and noise scheduling to enhance training reliability.
- Open-sourcing their complete setup, including the high-fidelity simulator, which aims to democratize research in this area by lowering barriers to entry.

By tackling low-level, end-to-end control with rapid training and reliable Sim2Real transfer, this work pushes the boundaries of practical RL application in robotics, making advanced control accessible for widespread adoption and further development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the acknowledged limitations of existing quadrotor control paradigms. While classical cascaded controllers are effective, their platform-specific engineering and limited generalizability highlight the need for more adaptable solutions. Reinforcement Learning offers a promising avenue, but its widespread adoption in real-world multirotor control is hindered by two significant challenges:
1. **The Sim-to-Real Gap:** Policies trained in simulation often struggle to perform robustly in the real world due to discrepancies between simulation models and physical reality (e.g., model inaccuracies, observation noise, unmodeled disturbances). This gap is particularly pronounced for low-level, end-to-end control, where the policy directly interfaces with actuators and thus encounters more complex, non-linear dynamics.
2. **Prohibitively Long Training Times:** Deep RL algorithms typically require millions, if not billions, of environment interactions to learn effective policies. This translates to training times ranging from hours to days, which impedes rapid iteration, experimentation, and widespread adoption in research and development.

Given these challenges, the authors aim to push the boundaries of deep RL-based end-to-end quadrotor control. Their key objectives and contributions are:

- **RL-based End-to-End Controller Design:** To propose a novel asymmetric actor-critic-based architecture and a reliable training paradigm capable of true end-to-end quadrotor control, directly mapping quadrotor states to RPM outputs (Level 5.1 in their proposed taxonomy). This design explicitly accounts for the partial observability of real systems by incorporating an action history in the actor's observations while leveraging ground truth for the critic in simulation.
- **Achieve Best Sample Complexity:** To devise a curriculum learning strategy that gradually increases reward penalties, leading to improved sample efficiency and more reliable policies. The goal is to demonstrate training of an end-to-end quadrotor control policy with the fewest reported environment interactions using an off-policy RL algorithm (TD3), which is known for better sample complexity than on-policy methods typically used in this domain.
- **Achieve Fastest Training Time:** To implement a highly optimized multirotor dynamics simulator, enabling unprecedentedly fast training of an end-to-end control policy that is directly transferable to a real system.
- **Demonstrate Robust Sim2Real Transfer:** To conduct extensive real-world experiments (over 300 flights) across various configurations, seeds, and tasks, demonstrating that the trained policies for position control generalize effectively to other tasks like agile trajectory tracking without domain randomization.
- **Open-Source the Framework:** To make their entire setup, including the simulator and training code, open-source. The motivation here is to democratize research and development in learning-based quadrotor control by enabling anyone with a consumer-grade laptop to train and deploy state-of-the-art policies in seconds, thereby significantly lowering barriers to entry.

In essence, the paper seeks to demonstrate that robust, low-level, end-to-end RL control for quadrotors is feasible in the real world with minimal training time, challenging the long-held beliefs about the practical limitations of deep RL in robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors frame the quadrotor control problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to apply Reinforcement Learning. Their methodology combines a sophisticated RL architecture with a highly optimized simulation environment and carefully designed training strategies.

#### 3.1. Control Problem Formulation (MDP)

- **State Space:** The policy directly maps states to motor commands (RPMs). The state `s` is 17-dimensional, comprising position (`p`), orientation (`q`, represented as quaternions, but converted to rotation matrices for neural network input to avoid ambiguity), linear velocity (`v`), angular velocity (`ω`), and motor speeds (`ω_m`). The inclusion of `ω_m` is crucial because motors are modeled as first-order low-pass filters, meaning current motor speeds are part of the system's state.
- **Action Space:** Actions `a` consist of the four individual RPM setpoints for the quadrotor's motors (`ω_sp1`, `ω_sp2`, `ω_sp3`, `ω_sp4`). This positions their policy at Level 5.1 (Motor commands/RPM setpoints) in their proposed control taxonomy, representing the lowest and most challenging level of abstraction.
- **Initial State Distribution:** Episodes begin with diverse initial conditions for position, orientation, velocities, and rotor speeds to ensure the learned policy is a general position controller, not just a stabilizing controller for a specific state.

#### 3.2. Architecture: Asymmetric Actor-Critic

- The core of their RL architecture is an asymmetric actor-critic scheme. This design leverages the fact that the critic, which guides the learning process, can access "privileged information" from the simulator that is not available to the actor during real-world deployment.
- **Critic's Observation (`o_c`):** The critic observes a 28-dimensional vector including the full ground truth state (position, orientation as rotation matrix, linear velocity, angular velocity, rotor speeds) plus additional randomly sampled force (`f_r`) and torque (`τ_r`) disturbances. These disturbances are introduced at the beginning of each episode to enhance the robustness of the trained policies, making them more resilient to unmodeled real-world effects.
- **Actor's Observation (`o_a`):** The actor, designed for real-world deployment, has a more constrained observation space (18 + N_H * 4 dimensions, where N_H is action history length). It observes position, orientation (as rotation matrix), linear velocity, angular velocity, and crucially, an **action history** (`H`). The action history mitigates the significant partial observability caused by motor delays (where actions only impact the state after 5-25 control steps due to the motor's low-pass behavior). This proprioceptive measurement is easily implementable on real hardware without additional sensors.
- **Observation Noise:** To account for sensor imperfections in the real world, the actor's observations are additionally perturbed with noise during training.

#### 3.3. Reward Function Design

- The reward function `r(s, a, s')` uses a negative squared cost, penalizing deviations from the desired state (origin with zero velocity and zero angular velocity).
 - `- C_rp ||p||^2_2`: Penalty for position error.
 - `- C_rq (1 - q_w^2)`: Penalty for orientation error (q_w is the scalar component of the quaternion).
 - `- C_rv ||v||^2_2`: Penalty for linear velocity error.
 - `- C_rω ||ω||^2_2`: Penalty for angular velocity error.
 - `- C_ra ||a - C_rab||^2_2`: Penalty for control actions (RPMs), encouraging energy efficiency and smoother control.
 - `+ C_rs`: An additive constant incentivizing survival, combating the "learning to terminate" problem (where the agent might prefer to crash to maximize immediate reward).

#### 3.4. Training Paradigm Enhancements

- **Curriculum Learning:** A simple curriculum gradually transitions the reward constants from an initial, less restrictive set (`C_init,*`) to a more restrictive target set (`C_target,*`). This helps the agent learn basic stability first before refining its performance under stricter criteria, improving sample complexity and Sim2Real transfer. The weights are adjusted periodically (every 100,000 steps).
- **Exploration Noise Decay:** The exploration noise, vital for RL, is decayed using the same exponential scheme as the reward function curriculum, reducing randomness as the policy improves.
- **Off-Policy RL:** The Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic policy gradient (TD3) algorithm is used. TD3 is an off-policy algorithm, known for its better sample complexity compared to on-policy methods like PPO, which are common in related works.

#### 3.5. Highly Optimized Simulator

- A custom multirotor dynamics simulator is implemented, focusing solely on fast dynamics rather than visual fidelity.
- It leverages **C++ template metaprogramming** for compiler-level optimization, allowing tight integration with the RLtools deep RL framework (also developed by the authors). This optimization enables extremely high simulation speeds on consumer-grade GPUs, crucial for rapid training. The paper reports 1284 million steps/s on an Nvidia T2000 laptop GPU, equivalent to about 5 months of simulated flight per second.

By combining these elements, the methodology aims to create a fast, reliable, and deployable end-to-end quadrotor control system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling findings across simulation training, an extensive ablation study, and real-world trajectory tracking experiments.

#### 4.1. Simulator Performance and Training Speed

- **Unprecedented Simulation Speed:** The custom-built simulator achieves an astounding 1284 million steps per second on a laptop GPU. This translates to approximately 5 months of simulated flight time per real-time second. This is orders of magnitude faster (6420x) than comparable state-of-the-art simulators like Flightmare, making rapid iteration feasible.
- **Fastest Training Time:** Leveraging this highly optimized simulator and their training paradigm, the authors demonstrate that their end-to-end quadrotor control policy can be trained to reliably fly a real Crazyflie nano quadrotor in **only 18 seconds** on a 2020 MacBook Pro. This corresponds to a mere 300,000 environment steps, which is an order of magnitude less sample complexity compared to other learned policies at a similar low control level (Table I shows previous works requiring 10-100+ million steps and hours of training).

#### 4.2. Training Reliability

- The training approach exhibits remarkable reliability. Across 50 differently seeded training runs, even the 5 worst-performing runs (in terms of cumulative steps) learned to fly rapidly and robustly. This contrasts sharply with common observations in RL research, where cherry-picking across many seeds is often necessary to find a deployable policy.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

A comprehensive ablation study across 300 real-world trajectories (10 seeds, 3 tasks, various configurations) investigated the impact of different components of their training setup (Table II).
- **Overall Reliability:** The "All Components" (baseline) configuration demonstrated the highest reliability, with no crashes in any of the tasks or seed combinations during real-world deployment.
- **Impact of Minor Components:** Removing "smaller" modifications like observation noise, reward recalculation, exploration noise decay, and disturbances still yielded largely reliable policies, albeit with slight variations in performance.
- **Crucial Components:**
 - **Action History:** Training without action history led to a significant drop in reliability and positional accuracy, particularly for longer training periods (5/10 successful flights, high error after 3M steps), validating its necessity for mitigating partial observability due to motor delays.
 - **Rotor Delay Simulation:** Training without simulating rotor delay resulted in **no usable policies** (0/10 successful flights), confirming its critical importance for Sim2Real transfer at the RPM control level.
 - **Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC):** Removing AAC led to a noticeable drop in reliability (6/10 success early, 4/10 later), indicating its importance for stable training by providing the critic with privileged information.
 - **Curriculum Learning:** Without curriculum, policies struggled significantly in the early training phase (1/10 success early), though they could reach comparable performance after convergence. This highlights the curriculum's role in accelerating and stabilizing early learning.
 - **Combined Impact:** Removing both AAC and Curriculum resulted in extremely slow training and largely unusable policies (0/10 success early, 0/10 later, 2/10 for trajectory tracking), underscoring the synergistic importance of these two components.
- **Simulation vs. Real-world Correlation:** The study revealed only a loose correlation between simulation performance (episode returns/lengths) and real-world performance, with episode length appearing to be a better proxy for real-world reliability.

#### 4.4. Sim2Real Transfer and Trajectory Tracking

- **Zero-shot Sim2Real:** The policies successfully transferred to a real Crazyflie quadrotor for direct RPM control without requiring domain randomization, a notable achievement for low-level control.
- **Generalization to Trajectory Tracking:** Although trained as a position controller (returning to origin), the policies demonstrated impressive generalization capabilities, successfully tracking complex Lissajous trajectories with varying agility levels (slow, normal, fast) by simply offsetting the position/velocity setpoints (Fig. 5). The quadrotor achieved speeds up to 3 m/s and accelerations up to 0.9g.
- **Competitive Performance:**
 - Compared to classical controllers (PID, Geometric, Nonlinear, INDI) (Table III): Their method achieved competitive tracking errors for normal speeds and significantly **outperformed all other classical and learned baselines for fast, agile trajectories**. For slow trajectories, a geometric controller performed slightly better.
 - Compared to other RL baselines: Their method trained faster and more reliably. The replicated baselines either failed to fly the real drone (for their training duration) or exhibited major oscillations and crashes, especially for normal and fast trajectories. The authors note that required cherry-picking of policies and still struggled with agile trajectories, while exhibited poor yaw control and frequent crashes for faster movements.

In summary, the main findings confirm the unprecedented speed, high reliability, and robust Sim2Real transfer capabilities of their proposed RL framework for end-to-end quadrotor control, demonstrating its superiority for agile maneuvers compared to existing solutions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant leap forward in the field of autonomous multirotor aerial vehicle control, particularly in bridging the gap between sophisticated learning-based methods and practical real-world deployment. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Democratization of Advanced Quadrotor Control:** By demonstrating training of an end-to-end RL controller in mere seconds on a consumer-grade laptop, the paper drastically lowers the barrier to entry for developing and experimenting with advanced quadrotor control systems. The open-sourcing of their highly optimized simulator and code means that researchers, students, and enthusiasts can replicate, extend, and innovate upon this work without requiring high-performance computing clusters or extensive computational resources, effectively democratizing access to cutting-edge robotics research.
- **Accelerated Research and Development Cycles:** The unprecedentedly fast training times (18 seconds vs. hours/days for prior work) enable rapid iteration and experimentation. This allows researchers to quickly test new ideas, explore different reward functions, or adapt policies to new platforms, significantly accelerating the pace of research and development in RL for robotics.
- **Bridging the Sim2Real Gap for Low-Level Control:** The successful and robust Sim2Real transfer for direct RPM control without explicit domain randomization is a major achievement. This is particularly challenging given the numerous non-linearities and unmodeled dynamics at such a low control level. The findings provide valuable insights into architectural choices (asymmetric actor-critic, action history) and training strategies (curriculum learning, noise decay) that effectively mitigate the reality gap, paving the way for more reliable real-world applications of RL.
- **Pushing the Boundaries of End-to-End Control:** The work validates the feasibility and benefits of end-to-end RL for complex, agile quadrotor maneuvers. By directly controlling motor RPMs, the policy can potentially exploit the full dynamic capabilities of the platform, potentially surpassing the performance of classical cascaded controllers that often abstract away crucial low-level dynamics. Its competitive and often superior performance in agile trajectory tracking underscores this potential.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays a robust foundation for future research in several directions. The authors themselves suggest avenues such as automatic hyperparameter optimization, adaptive policies for changing system parameters (e.g., battery levels, wind), and integration with meta-RL techniques. The reliability and speed achieved mean that researchers can now tackle more complex tasks or robustness challenges on top of a well-performing, fundamental controller.
- **Reliability in RL Training:** The observed high reliability of their training paradigm (even the "worst" seeds learn effectively, eliminating the need for cherry-picking) addresses a critical pain point in RL research. This suggests that with careful design of the environment, reward, and architecture, RL training can be made significantly more robust and less susceptible to the inherent stochasticity of the algorithms.

In essence, "Learning to Fly in Seconds" is a landmark paper that addresses key practical limitations of deep RL in robotics. By making high-performance, real-world quadrotor control accessible, fast, and reliable, it opens up new possibilities for autonomous aerial vehicles in various applications, from search and rescue to package delivery and agile inspection in complex environments.
