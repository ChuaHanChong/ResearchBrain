# Prisma: An Open Source Toolkit for Mechanistic Interpretability in Vision and Video

> [!summary] Summary
> Prisma is an open-source toolkit that adapts mechanistic interpretability methods from language models to vision and video transformers, providing a unified interface and pre-trained sparse autoencoders. The toolkit enables researchers to analyze the internal workings of vision models and has been used to observe that vision sparse autoencoders exhibit lower sparsity than their language counterparts and that sparse autoencoder reconstructions can sometimes improve model loss.

## Problem

- Progress in mechanistic interpretability (MI) for vision and video models lagged significantly behind that for language models due to a lack of accessible frameworks and pre-trained interpretability artifacts.
- Existing MI tools for language models were not directly transferable to vision/video domains due to architectural and data-specific differences (e.g., continuous visual input, patch tokenization, self-supervised objectives).
- Researchers lacked unified toolkits to easily access activations, train sparse coders, and perform circuit analysis on a wide range of vision and video transformer architectures.

## Method

- Prisma provides a unified, open-source Python toolkit featuring a ==HookedViT class== for easy access to activations and weights across over **75** vision and video transformer models.
- The toolkit offers comprehensive support for training and evaluating ==sparse coder architectures==, including Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), transcoders, and crosscoders, with configurable training parameters.
- The project releases over ==80 pre-trained SAEs== for CLIP-B and DINO-B ViTs, along with tutorial notebooks and toy vision transformers, to lower the computational burden and entry barriers for vision mechanistic interpretability research.

## Results

- Vision Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) exhibit substantially higher activation density (lower sparsity) than language SAEs, with L0 values often exceeding **500** active features per patch in models like CLIP-B/32.
- Reconstructions from SAEs can sometimes decrease the original model's cross-entropy loss when reinjected into the forward pass, particularly in deeper layers of certain CLIP and DINO models, suggesting a potential denoising effect.
- Sparsity patterns and feature utilization in vision SAEs vary significantly between spatial patches and the CLS token, with the CLS token showing higher L0, especially in deeper layers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Successful mechanistic interpretability paradigms from language models, such as 'hooked' architectures and sparse autoencoders, can be adapted to vision and video transformers despite modality differences.
> - Providing comprehensive open-source tooling, pre-trained interpretability artifacts, and educational resources is crucial for accelerating and democratizing research in emerging AI interpretability subfields.
> - Preliminary analyses using newly developed tools can reveal fundamental and surprising properties about how vision models represent information, such as distinct sparsity patterns compared to language models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning, has made immense strides in performance across various domains, including natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV). However, this progress has often come at the cost of increased model complexity, leading to "black box" models whose internal workings are opaque. Mechanistic interpretability (MI) has emerged as a crucial subfield aiming to reverse-engineer these models to understand their internal algorithms, computations, and representational properties.

Prior to this work, mechanistic interpretability had seen significant advancements, particularly within the domain of large language models (LLMs). Tools like TransformerLens and the development of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) for decomposing activations into interpretable features have empowered researchers to identify and analyze "circuits" – specific computational pathways – responsible for particular behaviors in LLMs. This progress has been fueled by the availability of robust tooling, standardized implementation code, and publicly released pre-trained models and their interpretability artifacts (like pre-trained SAEs).

In stark contrast, mechanistic interpretability for vision and video models has lagged significantly. While traditional vision interpretability techniques like feature visualization, saliency maps, and attention analysis have existed, they often lack the causal circuit-tracing and feature decomposition capabilities that characterize modern language MI. The authors explicitly state that "similar progress in vision mechanistic interpretability has been hindered by the lack of accessible frameworks and pre-trained weights." This paper directly addresses this critical gap.

Prisma positions itself as a pioneering effort to bridge this modality-specific divide. It aims to port the successful methodologies and tooling paradigms from language MI to the vision domain, while acknowledging and addressing the fundamental differences between the two. These differences include the continuous nature of visual input versus discrete language tokens, distinct tokenization (e.g., spatial patches vs. canonical word tokens), the role of the CLS token in Vision Transformers (ViTs), and the prevalent use of contrastive learning or self-supervised objectives (like CLIP and DINO) instead of next-token prediction. Existing language interpretability libraries are not directly transferable due to these architectural and functional disparities.

By providing a unified toolkit, pre-trained sparse autoencoders, and support for various vision and video transformer architectures, Prisma directly facilitates research that was previously difficult or impossible. It enables researchers to apply and adapt techniques like SAE training, transcoder/crosscoder analysis, and circuit tracing to vision models, thereby accelerating the nascent field of vision mechanistic interpretability. Furthermore, its inclusion of video transformer support pushes the interpretability frontier into a domain that is even less explored due to higher computational costs and lack of dedicated tools. Prisma is designed to be a foundational piece of infrastructure, much like TransformerLens for language, that can foster a vibrant and productive research ecosystem for understanding vision and video models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind developing Prisma stems from a recognized disparity in the progress of mechanistic interpretability (MI) between language models (LMs) and vision/video models (VMs). The authors observe that the robust tooling and readily available pre-trained models and interpretability artifacts have significantly propelled MI research in LMs. However, a similar ecosystem has been conspicuously absent for vision and video transformers, hindering comparable advancements. This "lack of accessible frameworks and pre-trained weights" forms the core problem that Prisma seeks to resolve.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of the Prisma project are multifaceted:

1. **Accelerate Vision Mechanistic Interpretability Research:** The overarching goal is to remove existing barriers and enable faster, more scalable research into the internal workings of vision and video models.
2. **Provide a Unified and Comprehensive Toolkit:** To achieve acceleration, Prisma aims to offer a single, cohesive framework that integrates various essential MI techniques. This includes:
 - **Hooked Vision/Video Transformers:** Creating a standardized interface (similar to TransformerLens) to easily access and modify activations and weights across a wide range of models (75+ from `timm`, OpenCLIP, Huggingface). This allows for diverse downstream interpretability tasks.
 - **Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) Training Support:** Enabling researchers to train and evaluate SAEs, and their more advanced variants like transcoders and crosscoders, for decomposing model activations into interpretable features.
 - **Pre-trained SAE Weights:** Releasing a substantial suite of pre-trained SAEs (80+ for CLIP-B and DINO-B ViTs across all layers, and transcoders for CLIP), which significantly reduces the computational burden and entry barrier for new researchers.
 - **Interpretability Tools:** Integrating a suite of analytical tools, such as activation caching for efficient data access, circuit analysis tools (including compatibility with ACDC for automated circuit discovery), attention head visualization, and logit lens techniques.
 - **Educational Resources:** Providing tutorial notebooks and comprehensive documentation to facilitate rapid onboarding for researchers new to vision MI or the toolkit itself. This also includes "toy vision transformers" to enable interpretability work in compute-constrained environments.
3. **Lower Barriers to Entry:** By open-sourcing the framework, pre-trained weights, and educational materials, Prisma aims to democratize access to vision MI research, making it more approachable for a wider community of researchers, including those with limited computational resources.
4. **Enable New Research Directions:** Beyond providing tools, Prisma's release is intended to facilitate novel investigations into vision model internals. The paper itself demonstrates this by using Prisma to explore surprising findings regarding vision SAE sparsity and the impact of SAE reconstructions on model loss.

In essence, Prisma's motivation is to replicate the success of language MI by providing the necessary infrastructure, pre-trained artifacts, and educational resources, thereby fostering a vibrant and collaborative research ecosystem for understanding the complex internal mechanisms of vision and video transformer models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Prisma's methodology centers on developing an open-source Python library that systematically adapts and extends successful mechanistic interpretability (MI) techniques from the language domain to vision and video transformers, while explicitly accounting for the unique characteristics of visual data and architectures.

The core of Prisma is built around the `HookedViT` class, a unified interface inspired by TransformerLens. This class enables researchers to access and modify layer-wise activations and weights across a diverse range of 75+ pre-trained vision and video transformer models. These models are integrated from popular repositories like Hugging Face, OpenCLIP, and `timm`, ensuring broad compatibility with widely used architectures such as ViT, CLIP-VIT, DINO, ViViT, and V-JEPA. The "hooked" functionality is crucial for intercepting and manipulating intermediate activations, which is fundamental to most MI techniques. The model registry is designed for easy expansion to new models.

A significant component of Prisma's methodology is its comprehensive support for **sparse coder architectures**. This includes:
- **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs):** For decomposing single-layer representations into interpretable, sparse features.
- **Transcoders:** Which extend SAEs across layers to provide a more faithful approximation of feedforward computation and track features across layers.
- **Crosscoders:** Designed to track persistent features across different layers and even different models.
Prisma implements various activation functions for these sparse coders, including ReLU, Top-K, JumpReLU, and Gated variants, offering flexibility for researchers to experiment with different sparsity-inducing mechanisms.

The toolkit provides highly **configurable SAE training parameters** to enable systematic experimentation and optimization. These include:
- **Expansion Factor:** e.g., x64, mapping a 768-dimensional activation space to a 49,152-feature dictionary, allowing for a large number of potential features.
- **Weight Initialization:** Supporting best practices like initializing encoder weights as the transpose of decoder weights.
- **Optimizer and Scheduling:** Training on ImageNet1k for one epoch using the Adam optimizer, with learning rate sweeps (1e-5 to 1e-1) and a cosine annealing schedule preceded by a warmup.
- **Regularization and Stability:** Incorporating L1 coefficients (swept from 1e-11 to 1) for sparsity control, ghost gradients to prevent dead features, and dead feature resampling.
- **Efficiency:** Options for activation caching (for latency) or on-the-fly computation (for VRAM optimization).

To facilitate direct research and lower the barrier to entry, the authors **open-source a substantial suite of 80+ pre-trained SAEs**. These include SAEs for all twelve layers of CLIP-B-32 and DINO-B-32 models, as well as transcoders for CLIP. They are provided in multiple token configurations (all patches, CLS-only, spatial tokens) and with different sparsity variants (standard ReLU and Top-K with K=64, 128). Detailed evaluation metrics for these pre-trained SAEs are also provided in the appendix, covering aspects like explained variance, average L0 sparsity, cosine similarity, reconstruction loss, and percentage of alive features.

Prisma also integrates a suite of **interpretability tools**:
- **Circuit Analysis:** Support for ablation studies via forward pass interventions. The `HookedSAEViT` module enables sparse feature circuit analysis by replacing layers with multiple SAEs or sparse coders. The `HookedViT` module can integrate with ACDC (Automated Circuit Discovery) for more automated analysis.
- **Visualization:** Tools for logit lens (inspecting intermediate activations through the lens of the final output layer) and attention visualization.

Finally, Prisma emphasizes **educational resources**, offering tutorial notebooks and comprehensive documentation to onboard new users. Crucially, it provides **open-source toy vision transformers** (1-4 layers, with full transformer and attention-only variants, and multiple training checkpoints). These smaller models allow researchers to validate hypotheses and develop new interpretability methods in compute-constrained environments before scaling to larger, more computationally intensive models, mirroring a successful strategy in language MI research.

This comprehensive approach—from model integration and sparse coder training to evaluation, interpretability tools, and educational resources—aims to provide a complete ecosystem for advancing vision and video mechanistic interpretability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents two "surprising findings" derived from preliminary analyses conducted using the Prisma toolkit on CLIP Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), highlighting initial insights into vision transformer internals that warrant further investigation.

1. **Vision SAEs Exhibit Substantially Higher Sparsity (Lower Density) Compared to Language SAEs:**
 - **Observation:** Contrary to expectations from language models (where L0, the number of active SAE latents per token, typically ranges from 12–74 for GPT-2 Small), vision SAEs trained on CLIP-B/32 show significantly higher L0 values, often exceeding 500 active features per patch. This means vision SAEs are much *less sparse* (or more dense) than their language counterparts, despite achieving comparable explained variance. Even with Top-K SAEs (K=64, 128), polysemantic features (where a single feature activates for multiple, unrelated concepts) persist, suggesting that even higher K values might be necessary for achieving truly monosemantic features in vision. An extreme example cited is a pre-trained vision SAE for the Kandinsky encoder, which exhibited an L0 of 122,631 per CLS token.
 - **Proposed Explanations:** The authors put forth several hypotheses for this discrepancy:
 - **Information Density Differences:** Visual inputs may be inherently denser and lack the power-law distribution found in natural language, potentially requiring more features to represent.
 - **Patch Granularity:** Vision transformers like CLIP-B/32 process fewer, larger patches (approx. 50) compared to language models with many more tokens (e.g., 1024 for GPT-2). When L0 is normalized per *patch* (e.g., 25 per patch for vision), it becomes more comparable to language's L0 per token. This suggests the patch size and its relationship to L0 need deeper scrutiny.
 - **Patch Type Specialization:** Spatial patches and CLS tokens exhibit distinct distributional properties. The CLS token, which aggregates global image information, consistently shows a higher L0, especially in deeper layers, as it integrates more complex representations. Conversely, spatial patches show declining utilization of features in deeper layers as information migrates to the CLS token. This implies that optimal sparsity (Top-K value) might vary by token type and layer depth.
 - **Domain-Specific Optimization:** Current vision SAE training methods are largely adapted from language MI, suggesting that novel, vision-specific training techniques might be needed to achieve truly sparser representations.

2. **SAE Reconstructions Can Unexpectedly Improve Model Loss:**
 - **Observation:** In some instances, injecting the activations reconstructed by SAEs back into the model's forward pass (effectively replacing the original activations with their SAE-compressed and decompressed versions) can *decrease* the original model's cross-entropy loss. This surprising phenomenon was observed in certain layers of the Vanilla and Top K=64 CLS-only CLIP SAE sets, as well as the vanilla DINO set.
 - **Pattern and Implications:** For the Top K=64 CLS-only SAEs, this effect became more pronounced in deeper layers, suggesting that SAEs might be performing a "denoising" function on the CLS token's developing representation. This aligns with a prior observation of SAE reconstructions reducing loss in CLIP's final layer.
 - **Lack of Universality:** The improvement was not observed across all sparse coder types; specifically, CLIP transcoders, CLIP Vanilla Spatial Patch SAEs, and CLIP Vanilla CLS-only SAEs did not show significant improvements.
 - **Open Question:** The precise mechanism behind this loss reduction remains unclear, presenting a compelling avenue for future research. It could imply that SAEs, in some contexts, regularize or distill the model's internal representations in a beneficial way.

These preliminary findings, facilitated by the Prisma toolkit, not only validate its utility but also open up fundamental questions about the nature of representations in vision models and the efficacy of sparse coding techniques in this modality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Prisma toolkit represents a significant contribution to the burgeoning field of mechanistic interpretability, with profound implications for understanding and developing more transparent and trustworthy AI systems, particularly in the vision and video domains. Its significance and potential impact can be articulated as follows:

1. **Accelerating Vision and Video Mechanistic Interpretability:** Prisma directly addresses the critical gap in tooling and publicly available resources that has hindered progress in vision MI compared to its language counterpart. By providing a comprehensive, open-source framework, pre-trained sparse autoencoders, and a unified interface for various vision and video transformers, Prisma dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for researchers. This will undoubtedly accelerate the pace of discovery in understanding how vision models function internally.

2. **Enabling Deeper Scientific Understanding of Vision Models:** The toolkit empowers researchers to perform fine-grained analyses of vision transformer internals, moving beyond mere black-box performance metrics. Techniques like sparse autoencoder decomposition, circuit analysis, and activation visualization allow for the identification of interpretable features and the tracing of computational pathways, leading to a more mechanistic understanding of visual cognition in AI. This deeper understanding is crucial for diagnosing model failures, identifying biases, and improving robustness.

3. **Fostering a Collaborative Research Ecosystem:** By being open-source and providing extensive educational resources (tutorials, documentation, toy models), Prisma encourages community adoption and contributions. This collaborative environment can lead to faster development of new interpretability techniques, the discovery of novel phenomena, and the establishment of best practices within the vision MI community. The explicit invitation for researchers to "build on these findings and contribute to Prisma’s open-source development" underscores this aim.

4. **Bridging Modality-Specific Interpretability:** Prisma serves as a vital bridge between the mature field of language model interpretability and the nascent field of vision interpretability. By adapting successful paradigms from language (e.g., TransformerLens's hooked architecture, SAEs, transcoders) while carefully accounting for the unique characteristics of visual data, it facilitates the transfer of knowledge and methodologies across modalities. This cross-pollination of ideas can lead to more generalized interpretability frameworks applicable across different AI domains.

5. **Unveiling Fundamental Differences in Representations:** The preliminary findings themselves, particularly regarding the unexpected sparsity patterns of vision SAEs and the loss-reducing effect of SAE reconstructions, highlight the unique nature of vision model representations. These observations are not just curiosities; they represent fundamental differences that demand further investigation, potentially leading to new theories of how vision models process information and how interpretability methods need to be adapted accordingly. The "denoising" hypothesis for SAEs, if proven, could have implications for model training and robustness.

6. **Practical Implications for Model Development:** A deeper mechanistic understanding facilitated by Prisma can lead to more principled model design. If researchers can identify robust, interpretable features, this could inform architectures, training objectives, and even lead to methods for steering models towards desired behaviors (as hinted by the Kandinsky encoder example) or away from undesirable ones (e.g., biases). For video models, where understanding spatiotemporal reasoning is complex, Prisma provides the initial tools to tackle this challenge, which is critical for applications like autonomous driving and robotic control.

In conclusion, Prisma is more than just a software library; it is a foundational infrastructure project designed to democratize and accelerate the scientific understanding of complex vision and video AI models. By addressing a critical tooling gap and revealing intriguing properties of vision representations, it is poised to significantly advance the field of mechanistic interpretability and contribute to the development of more transparent, reliable, and controllable AI systems.
