# End-to-end training of object class detectors for mean average precision

> [!summary] Summary
> This work from the University of Edinburgh introduces a method for end-to-end training of object class detectors using mean Average Precision (mAP) as the direct optimization objective, including Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) in the training loop. It develops novel pseudo partial derivatives for non-differentiable functions, achieving performance comparable to Fast R-CNN on PASCAL VOC datasets while addressing the traditional training-test metric mismatch.

## Problem

- Object detectors were conventionally trained for surrogate tasks like window classification accuracy, leading to a mismatch with the ultimate evaluation metric of mean Average Precision (mAP).
- The crucial Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) post-processing step was ignored during training, creating a disconnect between the training and test-time pipeline.
- Directly optimizing non-differentiable, piecewise-constant functions like mAP and NMS using standard gradient descent is problematic due to zero or undefined gradients.

## Method

- The authors introduce ==pseudo partial derivatives (PPDs)== using ==Symmetric Difference Estimator (SDE)== and ==Mean Envelope Estimator (MEE)== to provide directional information for optimizing ==piecewise-constant functions==.
- ==NMS== and ==mAP== calculation are integrated as differentiable layers within the ==Fast R-CNN== framework, allowing for end-to-end gradient-like backpropagation.
- The training protocol is empirically modified with larger minibatches, a 5% foreground fraction, ==L4 regularization==, ==log-space gradients==, and ==gradient clipping== to stabilize optimization of the high-variance mAP loss.

## Results

- The end-to-end mAP training method achieved performance comparable to the Fast R-CNN baseline on PASCAL VOC 2007 and 2012 datasets (e.g., SDE **60.7%** vs. Fast R-CNN **62.4%** mAP with VGG16 on VOC 2007 test).
- The proposed method demonstrated improved performance gains when trained on larger datasets (VOC 2007+2012 trainval) compared to the Fast R-CNN baseline, indicating benefits from more diverse training examples.
- Ablation studies confirmed that proposed training modifications were critical; for instance, increasing the foreground fraction from 5% to 25% led to a **-6.1 mAP** drop, underscoring their importance for stable learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - It is possible to derive 'pseudo partial derivatives' that enable direct, gradient-based optimization of piecewise-constant, non-differentiable functions such as mAP and NMS.
> - The Mean Envelope Estimator (MEE) generally offers more stable and robust pseudogradient estimates compared to the Symmetric Difference Estimator (SDE).
> - Successful training with mAP loss requires careful tuning of training parameters and regularization techniques to handle the inherent challenges of high variance and sparse gradients.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper is situated at a critical juncture in the evolution of object detection, specifically within the paradigm shift brought about by convolutional neural networks (CNNs). In the mid-2010s, deep learning models like R-CNN, Fast R-CNN, and Faster R-CNN had revolutionized object detection performance. However, despite their success, a fundamental theoretical inconsistency persisted in their training methodology, which this paper directly addresses.

Historically, object detectors, including the early CNN-based ones, were trained for a *surrogate task*: typically, window classification accuracy. A CNN would classify individual object proposal windows as belonging to a specific object class or background. However, the ultimate performance of a detector at test time was (and still is) almost universally evaluated using **mean Average Precision (mAP)**, a metric that considers the global ranking of detection scores across an image and an entire dataset, and critically, is calculated *after* a post-processing step called **Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS)**. NMS prunes redundant overlapping detections, retaining only the highest-scoring ones.

The traditional approach thus suffered from a significant **training-test mismatch**:
1. **Different Metrics:** Training optimized for classification accuracy, while evaluation used mAP. While correlated, these metrics are not identical, and optimizing one does not guarantee optimal performance on the other.
2. **Ignored NMS:** The NMS process, which has a non-trivial impact on the final detections, was entirely absent during training. This meant the network never learned to explicitly optimize for the outcome of this crucial post-processing step.

This work aligns with the broader machine learning dictum: "the loss we minimize at training time should correspond as closely as possible to the evaluation metric used at test time." It also fits into the rapidly growing trend of **end-to-end training** in deep learning, where a single, unified model is trained for the ultimate task, rather than a pipeline of separately optimized components. Examples across vision (e.g., semantic segmentation, image captioning) and other fields (e.g., sequence-to-sequence learning) demonstrated the power of this paradigm.

Previous attempts to optimize directly for AP or incorporate NMS into training existed, but often came with significant limitations: they might focus on single-class AP, lack NMS integration, or require complex "loss-augmented inference" solvers (e.g., structured SVMs) which are computationally intensive and less general for deep learning. This paper aims to provide a more general, efficient, and "drop-in" solution that fully embraces multi-class mAP and NMS within the standard stochastic gradient descent (SGD) framework. By tackling the non-differentiability of mAP and NMS, it pushes the boundaries of what can be directly optimized in deep learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a method for **training CNN-based object class detectors directly using mean Average Precision (mAP) as the training loss, in a truly end-to-end fashion that includes Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) at training time.**

The motivation for pursuing this objective is multi-faceted and rooted in fundamental machine learning principles and practical considerations in object detection:

1. **Bridging the Training-Test Discrepancy:** The most significant motivation is to eliminate the inherent mismatch between the training objective (window classification accuracy) and the test-time evaluation metric (mAP after NMS). The authors argue that training for a surrogate task inevitably leads to suboptimal models, as the network is not directly optimizing for what truly matters for deployment.
2. **Principled Machine Learning Design:** Adhering to the core principle that the training loss should accurately reflect the ultimate performance metric. This ensures that the model's learning process is precisely guided towards the desired outcome.
3. **Incorporating NMS Effects During Training:** Traditional training pipelines completely ignore NMS. By integrating NMS into the training loop, the detector can learn to produce scores that are optimal *in the context of subsequent NMS*, potentially leading to better detection recall and precision when NMS is applied. This makes the model more robust and aligned with its operational environment.
4. **End-to-End Learning Paradigm:** The work is motivated by the success of end-to-end training in other deep learning applications. By treating the entire detection pipeline, including NMS and mAP calculation, as a single differentiable (or "pseudo-differentiable") computation graph, the model can learn to optimize all its components synergistically.
5. **Conceptual Simplicity and Elegance:** The authors highlight the conceptual appeal of using the "very same model and loss" at both training and test time. This unified approach reduces complexity and provides a more intuitive understanding of what the model is learning.
6. **Generalizability:** The underlying challenge—optimizing piecewise-constant, non-differentiable functions—is not unique to mAP. Developing a general method for defining "gradient-like quantities" for such functions could have wide applicability across various machine learning problems, particularly those involving ranking-based metrics common in information retrieval.

While not explicitly stated as an objective, an implicit motivation is the expectation that a more principled training approach *should* eventually lead to improved performance or, at the very least, a more robust and theoretically sound way to achieve current performance levels.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method lies in tackling the non-differentiability of mAP and NMS, both of which are piecewise-constant functions. Standard gradient descent algorithms cannot be directly applied because their partial derivatives are almost everywhere zero (in constant regions) or undefined (at steps). The authors introduce novel "pseudo partial derivatives" (PPDs) and efficient methods to compute them, enabling end-to-end training.

#### 3.1. Detection Framework

- **Base Model:** The method is built upon **Fast R-CNN**, a prominent object detection architecture.
- **Modifications:**
 - The softmax layer, which typically converts raw scores into probabilities, is omitted. The activations of the last fully-connected layer are used directly as raw window scores. This decision was empirical, finding that softmax saturation caused issues with gradient propagation.
 - Crucially, an **NMS layer** is introduced immediately after the scoring layer, making NMS an integral part of the model during *both training and testing*.
 - A custom **mAP loss layer** is added at the end, computing the mAP over the minibatch after NMS has been applied.

#### 3.2. Pseudogradients of General Piecewise-Constant Functions

The central technical innovation is the definition of PPDs. For a piecewise-constant function $f(x)$ with countably many discontinuities, the PPD ($\tilde{\partial}_{x_i} f$) is defined to convey useful directional information for optimization even where conventional gradients are zero or undefined. Two main definitions are explored:

1. **Symmetric Difference Estimator (SDE):**
 - This is an adaptive finite difference approach. For a given input $x$, it finds the *smallest non-infinitesimal perturbation* $\delta x$ such that $f(x + \delta x) \ne f(x)$.
 - The PPD is then computed as $\frac{f(x+\delta x) - f(x)}{\delta x}$.
 - To account for directionality, it performs this for both positive and negative perturbations ($\delta x^+$ and $\delta x^-$) and averages the resulting pseudogradients.
 - *Disadvantage:* Magnitude is sensitive to the exact distance to the nearest step.

2. **Mean Envelope Estimator (MEE):**
 - This approach fits piecewise-linear upper and lower envelopes to the steps of the piecewise-constant function.
 - The PPD is given by the slope of the envelope segment at point $x$. In practice, it's the average of the gradients of the upper and lower envelopes.
 - *Advantage:* Unlike SDE, MEE's magnitude does not become arbitrarily large as $x$ approaches a step, making it a more robust estimator.
 - SDE (with a lower bound on $\delta x$) is used for regions before the first step or after the last step where envelopes have zero gradient.

These PPDs are then used to define an analogue to the gradient ($\tilde{\nabla}f$) and Jacobian ($\tilde{J}_{ij} = \tilde{\partial}_{x_j} f_i$).

#### 3.3. Application to mAP and NMS

To apply these PPDs to mAP, the main challenges are: (i) efficiently finding the nearest step locations (minimal perturbations) for each window score that would change AP, and (ii) efficiently evaluating the resulting AP.
- **mAP Perturbations:** AP changes only when a true-positive (TP) and false-positive (FP) detection swap their relative ordering. Furthermore, the interpolated AP curve has specific conditions for changes (e.g., a FP moving past a TP only matters if that TP affects the filled-in precision curve).
- **Efficient Calculation:** For each class, two linear passes over the detections (descending then ascending score order) are performed.
 - The descending pass finds minimal *increases* to scores that would change AP (e.g., moving an FP past a TP).
 - The ascending pass finds minimal *decreases*.
 - Once step locations are found, the new AP is calculated by updating the relevant part of the PR curve.
- **Incorporating NMS Pseudogradients:** This is handled by considering NMS effects simultaneously with AP:
 - **Transitivity Approximations:** Two key approximations are made to simplify the non-local effects of NMS:
 1. **Ignore cascaded NMS:** Long-distance interactions (e.g., A suppresses B, B suppresses C, but A doesn't suppress C) are ignored, only local interactions are considered.
 2. **Ground-truth overlap assumption:** If detection A suppresses B, it's assumed B overlaps the same ground-truth instance as A (if any).
 - These approximations allow for linear-time computation of PPDs with respect to *pre-NMS* scores. This involves adding gradient contributions from:
 - Windows suppressed by TP/FP detections (their scores need to move similarly to their suppressors to affect AP).
 - Suppressed windows that would have covered missed ground-truth instances.
 - The detections that suppressed those previously mentioned windows.

#### 3.4. Training Protocol Modifications

The authors found that several empirical adjustments to the standard Fast R-CNN training protocol were essential for stable and successful training with mAP loss:
- **Larger Minibatches:** To mitigate the higher variance of mAP estimates compared to window classification accuracy.
- **Lower Foreground Fraction (5%):** In minibatches, this ratio of foreground-to-background windows is more representative of test-time conditions, improving gradient signal density.
- **L4 Regularizer on Scores:** A very small weight L4 regularizer ($\lambda \sum |s|^4$) prevents scores from exploding, which was observed to be a common instability issue.
- **Log-Space Gradients:** Following gradients of $\log(\text{mAP} + \epsilon)$ instead of mAP itself. This amplifies the effect of small changes when mAP is low, helping the network escape early training plateaus.
- **Gradient Clipping:** Clipping gradient elements to a fixed threshold to improve numerical stability, especially at higher learning rates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on the PASCAL VOC 2007 and 2012 datasets, using AlexNet or VGG16 as the base CNN architecture. The key findings are:

1. **Equivalent Performance to Fast R-CNN:** The most significant finding is that their end-to-end mAP training method achieves **comparable performance** to the traditional Fast R-CNN approach (trained with negative log-likelihood (NLL) for window classification, without bounding box regression). For instance:
 - On VOC 2007 test with VGG16, Fast R-CNN achieved 62.4% mAP, while their MEE method achieved 58.9% and SDE achieved 60.7%.
 - When trained on a larger dataset (VOC 2007+2012 trainval), Fast R-CNN reached 63.5% mAP, compared to MEE at 62.5% and SDE at 62.3%.
 This demonstrates that direct mAP optimization *can* work, even if it doesn't immediately lead to a performance breakthrough over the highly optimized NLL loss.

2. **Benefit from More Training Data:** The proposed method demonstrated a **more substantial performance gain** when trained on the union of VOC 2007 and VOC 2012 trainval sets, compared to training on VOC 2007 alone. This effect was notably stronger for their method than for Fast R-CNN (e.g., VGG16: 3.6% gain for their method vs. 1.1% for Fast R-CNN). This suggests that optimizing for mAP, which involves many comparisons between windows, particularly benefits from a richer and more diverse set of training examples.

3. **MEE vs. SDE Performance:** The **Mean Envelope Estimator (MEE) generally slightly outperformed the Symmetric Difference Estimator (SDE)** across most configurations. This is attributed to MEE being less sensitive to the precise distance from a data point to a "step" (discontinuity) in the piecewise-constant loss function, leading to more stable and robust gradient estimates.

4. **Cruciality of Training Protocol Modifications (Ablation Study):** The ablation study underscored the **critical importance** of the empirical training modifications proposed in Section 4.5.
 - Increasing the foreground fraction (from 5% to 25%) resulted in a **-6.1 mAP drop**.
 - Halving batch size led to a **-0.8 mAP drop**.
 - Using L2 instead of L4 regularization caused a **-1.0 mAP drop**.
 - Without regularization or gradient clipping, training quickly failed due to exploding scores and numerical instability.
 These results confirm that while the conceptual approach is sound, successful implementation requires careful tuning and specific techniques to manage the unique challenges of optimizing a high-variance, piecewise-constant loss.

5. **Comparison to Related Work:** Their method achieved **comparable performance** to Song et al. (which also optimizes for AP but with different techniques) on the VOC 2012 dataset (e.g., MEE 48.2% vs. Song et al. 48.5% with AlexNet). However, their method is significantly more general: it handles multi-class mAP, incorporates NMS, and does not require a complex, class-specific loss-augmented inference solver (max-oracle).

6. **Hypotheses for Lack of Significant Performance Boost:** The authors candidly discuss potential reasons why their principled approach did not yield a *significant performance gain* over the traditional method:
 - **Sparser Gradients:** The mAP loss yields much sparser gradients (only about 20% of scores have non-zero gradients in their setup) compared to a softmax loss (100% scores contribute). This could hinder efficient learning.
 - **Higher Variance:** mAP calculated over a minibatch is a higher-variance estimator of the true mAP over the entire dataset, making the optimization landscape noisier.
 - **Biased Estimator:** mAP over a minibatch of images is a biased estimator of mAP over the population of images.

Despite not achieving breakthrough performance, the authors emphasize that the "real advantage" of their method lies in its theoretical soundness and conceptual appeal, providing a principled foundation for future advancements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes several significant contributions with a broad potential impact on the field of machine learning and computer vision:

1. **Theoretical Advancement in Optimization:** The most profound contribution is the development of **generalizable "pseudo partial derivatives" (PPDs)** for piecewise-constant, non-differentiable functions. This is a fundamental theoretical advancement in optimization, addressing a class of functions that are ubiquitous in evaluation metrics but historically difficult to optimize directly with gradient-based methods. The SDE and MEE definitions provide practical tools for approximating gradients in such challenging scenarios.

2. **Principled End-to-End Training for Object Detection:** The work successfully demonstrates how to train a CNN-based object detector in a truly end-to-end fashion for its ultimate evaluation metric (mAP with NMS). By resolving the long-standing training-test discrepancy, it offers a more **theoretically sound and conceptually appealing** approach compared to traditional surrogate loss training. This aligns object detection training more closely with best practices in machine learning.

3. **Practical Drop-in Solution:** Crucially, the proposed mAP and NMS layers are designed to be **simple drop-in components** for existing deep learning frameworks (like Caffe, mentioned in the paper). They do not require modifications to the core SGD optimization algorithm or specialized, complex max-oracles, unlike some prior structured prediction methods. This significantly lowers the barrier to adoption for researchers and practitioners.

4. **Wider Applicability to Ranking-Based Metrics:** The general nature of the pseudogradient definitions means they can be applied to a wide array of other **ranking-based metrics** used in fields like information retrieval (e.g., discounted cumulative gain, simple Average Precision on document scores) or other structured prediction problems. This opens up new avenues for direct optimization in these domains.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** While the method achieved comparable performance to the established baseline rather than a significant leap, it lays a crucial foundation. Future work can build upon this principled approach to explore:
 - More sophisticated pseudogradient estimators or alternative optimization strategies for high-variance, sparse-gradient losses.
 - Refinements to the NMS approximations or alternative ways to integrate NMS more fully.
 - How to combine this mAP loss with bounding box regression (which was omitted for a fair comparison in this work).
 - The potential benefits of direct mAP optimization in more complex detection scenarios or with newer, more powerful architectures.

6. **Contribution to Robust Deep Learning:** The detailed ablation study, highlighting the necessity of careful training protocol adjustments (e.g., regularization, log-space gradients, gradient clipping), also contributes to the broader understanding of how to stabilize and effectively train deep networks with complex, non-standard loss functions.

In essence, this paper provides a robust theoretical and practical framework for directly optimizing non-differentiable evaluation metrics, pushing the field towards more consistent and principled deep learning model development, particularly in complex vision tasks like object detection.
