# Unsupervised Skill Discovery as Exploration for Learning Agile Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> The Skill Discovery as Exploration (SDAX) framework allows legged robots to autonomously acquire agile locomotion skills for tasks like leaping and wall-jumping by integrating unsupervised skill discovery with reinforcement learning. This approach reduces the need for extensive human engineering in reward design and curriculum development, leading to policies that successfully transfer to a real Unitree A1 robot.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning for agile legged robot locomotion often requires substantial human engineering, including complex reward functions, extensive demonstrations, or meticulously crafted curriculum strategies.
- Traditional exploration methods in high-dimensional robot control can struggle to efficiently discover complex, multi-stage agile behaviors.
- Human-engineered components limit the generalizability and autonomous learning capabilities of robots across diverse tasks and environments.

## Method

- **SDAX** integrates ==unsupervised skill discovery== techniques (e.g., ==METRA==) into a reinforcement learning objective, treating diverse skill acquisition as a high-level exploration mechanism.
- A ==bi-level optimization== framework dynamically adjusts a balancing parameter, λ, to optimally weigh the task-specific reward against the diversity reward throughout training.
- The policy is conditioned on a ==latent skill vector==, allowing it to learn a repertoire of diverse behaviors, with the aim that some skills will naturally lead to successful task completion.

## Results

- **SDAX** achieved superior sample efficiency and higher task performance (e.g., more obstacles passed) on leaping, climbing, and crawling tasks compared to task-only and naive exploration baselines.
- The framework enabled the Unitree A1 robot to learn the complex "wall-jump" maneuver, a behavior that traditional task-only rewards failed to produce.
- Learned policies successfully transferred to a real Unitree A1 robot, demonstrating agile behaviors like leaping over a **46 cm** gap, climbing a **25 cm** platform, and crawling under a **27 cm** obstacle with robust performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unsupervised skill discovery can serve as a more effective high-level exploration strategy than naive state-level exploration methods, facilitating the discovery of complex, multi-stage agile behaviors.
> - Dynamically balancing the contribution of diversity and task rewards through a learnable parameter significantly improves both sample efficiency and final task performance.
> - A "positive collapse" phenomenon occurs during training, where a high proportion of diverse skills converge to successful task solutions, simplifying skill selection for deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robot locomotion has observed considerable progress through the application of deep reinforcement learning (deep RL), enabling robots to achieve increasingly agile behaviors. However, this progress frequently relies on substantial human engineering efforts, which include intricate reward function design informed by domain expertise, the provision of extensive demonstration datasets, or the meticulous crafting of curriculum learning strategies. These requirements can limit the generalizability and autonomous learning capabilities of robots, as they introduce a dependence on human supervision for each new task or environment.

Unsupervised skill discovery, a subfield within reinforcement learning, offers an alternative approach. It aims to enable agents to autonomously learn a repertoire of diverse behaviors, or "skills," without explicit external reward signals. Each skill is typically associated with a latent variable, and the agent learns to produce distinct behavioral patterns when conditioned on different values of this variable. This paradigm is relevant to complex locomotion tasks where the optimal strategy might not be obvious or easily specified by human designers.

Prior work in agile locomotion has demonstrated capabilities such as high-speed running, jumping, and climbing. Some approaches, like that of Zhuang et al., achieved agile behaviors through manually designed rewards, which penalized overlap between the robot and imaginary obstacles. Other research has incorporated skill discovery into locomotion, but often with different objectives; for instance, Cheng et al. utilized skill discovery to diversify behaviors based on an already near-optimal policy, while Atanassov et al. focused on alternative skill discovery objectives.

This work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the challenge of reducing human engineering in learning agile locomotion. It proposes leveraging unsupervised skill discovery not merely to diversify known skills, but as a mechanism for *high-level exploration* to autonomously acquire novel and complex behaviors required for solving challenging tasks. Specifically, it aims to achieve a similar range of agile behaviors as seen in previous work, and extend to more complex "parkour-style" motions like wall-jumping, but with significantly less manual intervention in reward design or curriculum structuring. The key distinction lies in using skill discovery *in-the-loop* as an exploration strategy to optimize a task-specific reward, rather than as an independent objective or a post-hoc diversification tool.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel learning framework, named Skill Discovery as Exploration (SDAX), that enables legged robots to autonomously acquire agile locomotion behaviors for overcoming diverse obstacles. A central aim is to significantly reduce the human engineering effort typically associated with training such capabilities, specifically by minimizing reliance on extensive reward engineering, expert demonstrations, or carefully designed curriculum learning.

The motivation for this objective stems from several identified limitations in current deep RL approaches for legged locomotion:
- **Reliance on Human Engineering:** Existing methods often necessitate considerable human input in the form of detailed reward functions, which can be difficult to design for complex behaviors and may not generalize well to new tasks or environments. Similarly, collecting expert demonstrations or constructing progressive learning curricula is time-consuming and limits autonomous skill acquisition.
- **Challenges of Exploration:** Learning agile locomotion requires extensive exploration of the action space to discover effective strategies. Traditional exploration methods in RL can struggle with high-dimensional control problems and may not efficiently discover complex, multi-stage behaviors.
- **Generalizability:** The human-engineered components often tie learned policies to specific task configurations, thereby limiting their adaptability and generalizability to novel scenarios or changes in the environment.
- **Unsupervised Skill Discovery as a Solution:** The authors propose that unsupervised skill discovery can serve as an effective mechanism for autonomous, high-level exploration. By encouraging the agent to learn a diverse repertoire of skills, it is hypothesized that the agent will naturally explore a wider range of behaviors, some of which may prove instrumental in solving a given task.
- **Balancing Task and Exploration:** A critical motivation is to address the inherent exploration-exploitation trade-off. While skill discovery fosters exploration, an agent solely focused on diversity might fail to converge on a task solution. Conversely, an agent overly focused on a simple task reward might not explore sufficiently to discover optimal or more agile solutions. Therefore, a core motivation is to develop a method that dynamically balances the drive for diverse skill acquisition with the objective of maximizing task performance. This balance is crucial for ensuring that the exploration generated by skill discovery is directed towards task-relevant improvements.

In summary, the research aims to harness the exploratory power of unsupervised skill discovery to enable robots to learn complex, agile locomotion skills more autonomously and with less human intervention, thereby enhancing their generalizability and reducing development overhead.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Skill Discovery as Exploration (SDAX), integrates unsupervised skill discovery with reinforcement learning to facilitate autonomous acquisition of agile locomotion skills.

**Problem Formulation:** The problem is framed as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where the policy, $\pi_\theta$, is conditioned on a latent skill vector $z \in \mathbb{R}^n$, sampled from a prior distribution $p(z)$ at the beginning of each episode. The action space consists of joint position targets for a PD controller.

**Overall Objective:** The policy parameter $\theta$ is optimized to maximize the expected sum of discounted rewards, $J_{task+div} = E_{\pi_\theta} [\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t (r_{task_t} + \lambda r_{div_t})]$. This objective combines a task-specific reward ($r_{task}$) with a diversity reward ($r_{div}$), weighted by a learnable balancing parameter $\lambda$.

**Task Reward ($r_{task}$):** This reward is designed to be simple and directly specifies the goal of the task, such as encouraging forward velocity tracking. Details of task-specific coefficients are provided in Appendix C.2, also incorporating regularization terms for unrealistic motions.

**Diversity Reward ($r_{div}$):** The diversity reward is derived from unsupervised skill discovery methods. The paper states that SDAX is agnostic to the specific choice of skill discovery algorithm but evaluates two representative methods: DIAYN and METRA. For the main experiments, the formulation from METRA is adopted. METRA aims to maximize the Wasserstein Dependency Measure, $I_{WDM} = I_W(S; Z)$, which can be optimized by maximizing $r_{div} = (\phi(s_{t+1}) - \phi(s_t))^T z$. Here, $\phi: S \rightarrow Z$ is a learnable representation function that maps states into the latent skill space, and $z$ is the skill vector. A Lipschitz constraint on $\phi$ is enforced using dual gradient descent with a Lagrange multiplier $\kappa$.

**Bi-level Optimization for Balancing Parameter ($\lambda$):** A key component of SDAX is the dynamic adjustment of $\lambda$. This is achieved through a bi-level optimization framework:
1. **Policy Optimization:** The policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained to maximize the combined objective $J_{task+div}$.
2. **$\lambda$ Optimization:** The balancing parameter $\lambda$ is trained to maximize only the task reward $J_{task} = E_{\pi_\theta} [\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t r_{task_t}]$. The intuition is that $\lambda$ should increase when diversity helps solve the task and decrease when it hinders training.
 - The gradient $\nabla_\lambda J_{task}$ cannot be directly computed. The authors apply the chain rule to decompose it into $\nabla_\theta J_{task} \nabla_\lambda \theta$. The detailed derivation in Appendix A results in the approximation $\nabla_\lambda J_{task} \approx \alpha A_{task} \nabla_{\theta'} \log \pi_{\theta'}(a|s, z) \cdot A_{div} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s, z)$, where $\alpha$ is the learning rate, $\theta'$ is the policy parameter after one update, and $A_{task}$ and $A_{div}$ are advantage values computed from $r_{task}$ and $r_{div}$ respectively. This effectively increases $\lambda$ if the gradients from task and diversity rewards align, and decreases it otherwise.

**Skill Selection for Evaluation:** During the testing phase, rather than carefully selecting an optimal skill vector $z$, the authors report performance by simply sampling a random skill $z$. This is justified by an observed "positive collapse" phenomenon where, as training progresses, a significant proportion of learned skills become successful at the task.

**Implementation Details:**
- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used as the core RL algorithm.
- **Simulation Environment:** Isaac Gym is employed for parallel simulation.
- **Robot Hardware:** Experiments are conducted using the Unitree A1 quadruped robot.
- **Network Architecture:** Separate value networks ($v_{task}$ and $v_{div}$) are used for the two reward sources to maintain training stability, as the scale of combined rewards fluctuates with $\lambda$. Policy, value, and representation networks are multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) with specific configurations (Appendix C.6).
- **Observation Space:** The robot's observation space includes base position, rotation, velocities, joint states, gravity projection, velocity commands, and specific obstacle information, along with the sampled skill vector $z$ (Appendix C.3).
- **Real-world Deployment:** For transfer to real hardware, the simulated policy is fine-tuned by introducing observation noise and domain randomization (Appendix C.4, C.5). A limitation is noted regarding the order of noise application.
- **Manual Specification of Sub-dimensions:** Acknowledged limitation is the manual specification of sub-dimensions of the state space (e.g., base height for crawling) to guide the diversity objective effectively.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the efficacy of SDAX in enabling agile locomotion and its components:

#### 4.1. Learning Agile Locomotion Skills

- **Performance Comparison:** SDAX was evaluated on three parkour-style tasks (leaping, climbing, crawling) against several baselines: Task-only (RL with only task reward), Div-only (RL with only diversity reward), RND (RL with task reward and Random Network Distillation exploration bonus), and SDAX variants using DIAYN or METRA for diversity.
- **Superiority of SDAX:** SDAX, particularly when utilizing METRA for diversity, consistently demonstrated superior sample efficiency and achieved higher performance (measured by the number of obstacles passed) across all three tasks compared to all baselines (Figure 3).
- **Importance of Balance:** Task-only agents showed limited success, while Div-only agents failed to acquire meaningful skills, underscoring the necessity of balancing task and diversity rewards.
- **Diversity vs. Naive Exploration:** Diversity-based approaches (SDAX) outperformed RND. The authors attribute this to skill discovery methods facilitating "skill-level exploration" by encouraging distinct sets of states, which contrasts with RND's "state-level exploration" that focuses on visiting nearby unvisited states.
- **METRA vs. DIAYN:** METRA was found to be a more effective skill discovery module than DIAYN within SDAX, possibly because METRA aims for diversity without saturation, while DIAYN's reward can be maximized by small state differences.

#### 4.2. Skill Discovery Enables High-Level Exploration

- **Qualitative Evidence:** Visualizations (Figure 4) illustrated how different skill vectors ($z$) conditioned the policy to exhibit diverse behaviors for the same task. For instance, in the leaping task, some skills resulted in powerful jumps sufficient to clear obstacles, while others led to weaker, unsuccessful attempts. Similarly, for crawling, some skills enabled low body postures to pass under obstacles, while others resulted in ineffective high jumps.
- **Directed Exploration:** These observations indicate that skill discovery acts as a high-level exploration mechanism, allowing the agent to explore a range of strategies (e.g., varying heights or forces) and discover successful solutions.

#### 4.3. Learning Balancing Parameter $\lambda$

- **Adaptive vs. Fixed $\lambda$:** The adaptive $\lambda$ mechanism of SDAX outperformed experiments with fixed $\lambda$ values (0.01, 0.1, 1, 10) in the leaping task (Figure 5a), showing both better sample efficiency and final performance.
- **Dynamic Adjustment:** The learned $\lambda$ parameter was observed to dynamically adjust during training (Figure 5b), starting high and gradually decreasing over time. This suggests that SDAX effectively determines the appropriate weighting between diversity and task rewards throughout different stages of learning.

#### 4.4. Convergence of Different Skills into a Narrow Solution Space ("Positive Collapse")

- **Increased Success Rate:** Contrary to the potential challenge of needing to select a specific skill post-training, the study found that as training progressed, a significantly increasing proportion of randomly sampled skill vectors ($z$) led to successful task completion. For example, in the leap task, the success rate from random skills increased from approximately 43% early in training to nearly 97% later (Table 1). Similar trends were observed for climb and crawl tasks.
- **Mitigated Skill Selection Issue:** This "positive collapse" phenomenon, where different skill vectors converge towards similar successful behaviors, simplifies skill selection in deployment. The authors attribute this to task rewards propagating learning across the shared policy network, causing successful strategies to be adopted by multiple skills.

#### 4.5. Wall-Jump: Learning Super Agile Tasks

- **Complex Maneuver:** SDAX was applied to the "wall-jump" task, a complex parkour-style motion requiring a sequence of running, jumping, flipping, and landing.
- **Limitations of Task-Only Reward:** A guideline-based task reward alone was insufficient for solving the wall-jump, as the robot crashed into the wall without acquiring the necessary orientation (Figure 9b).
- **SDAX's Contribution:** By providing the robot's base roll, pitch, and yaw as input to the skill discovery algorithm, SDAX enabled the robot to explore and acquire specific orientations needed to kick off the wall, perform flips, and land successfully (Figure 6, 9c, 9d). This resulted in a significantly higher task return (Figure 9e).

#### 4.6. Hardware Experiments

- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Policies learned in simulation were successfully deployed on a real Unitree A1 robot after fine-tuning with observation noise and domain randomization.
- **Real-World Agility:** The robot demonstrated agile maneuvers on real hardware, including leaping over a 46 cm gap, climbing a 25 cm platform, and crawling under a 27 cm-high obstacle (Figure 1, 7). The crawling policy also exhibited robustness across different terrains (Figure 8).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, SDAX, with several implications for the development of autonomous legged robot locomotion:

- **Reduced Human Engineering Effort:** A significant impact of SDAX is its potential to substantially decrease the human engineering burden currently associated with training agile robot behaviors. By leveraging unsupervised skill discovery as an exploration mechanism, the framework reduces the need for manually designed curricula, extensive reward engineering, or large datasets of expert demonstrations. This can accelerate the development process for complex robot skills.

- **Enhanced Autonomous Exploration:** SDAX provides a method for high-level, skill-based exploration that is demonstrated to be more effective than naive state-level exploration methods for discovering complex, multi-stage behaviors. This enhanced exploratory capability allows robots to autonomously uncover diverse and potentially novel strategies for overcoming obstacles and performing agile maneuvers, leading to more versatile and adaptive agents.

- **Learning Complex Agile Behaviors:** The successful demonstration of SDAX on challenging tasks such as leaping, climbing, crawling, and particularly the complex "wall-jump," highlights its capability to enable robots to learn highly agile and sophisticated locomotion skills. The wall-jump task, which requires a precise sequence of coordinated motions and orientation control, exemplifies the framework's ability to push the boundaries of autonomously learned robot agility.

- **Robustness and Real-World Applicability:** The successful transfer of learned policies from simulation to real hardware (Unitree A1 robot), along with the demonstrated robustness to varying terrain conditions, indicates the practical utility and real-world applicability of SDAX. This enhances confidence in deploying policies trained with this framework in physical environments.

- **Flexible Framework:** The stated agnosticism of SDAX to the specific unsupervised skill discovery method used (demonstrated by testing with both DIAYN and METRA) offers flexibility. This allows future integration of more advanced or task-appropriate skill discovery techniques, potentially leading to further improvements in learning efficiency and behavioral diversity.

- **Addressing Exploration-Exploitation Trade-off:** The bi-level optimization strategy for dynamically adjusting the balancing parameter $\lambda$ addresses a fundamental challenge in reinforcement learning: the exploration-exploitation trade-off. By autonomously regulating the degree of exploration, SDAX ensures that the drive for diversity is aligned with the ultimate goal of task performance, leading to more robust and efficient learning. The "positive collapse" phenomenon further simplifies deployment by making skill selection less critical.
