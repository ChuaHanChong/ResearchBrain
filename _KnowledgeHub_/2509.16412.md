# Subteaming and Adaptive Formation Control for Coordinated Multi-Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UMass Amherst, North Carolina State University, U.S. Army DEVCOM, and the University of Denver developed SubTeaming and Adaptive Formation (STAF), a hierarchical learning framework enabling multi-robot teams to dynamically split into subteams and adapt their formations to navigate constrained environments. The STAF approach achieved a 100% success rate in diverse simulations and real-world scenarios, outperforming baselines in formation integrity while successfully navigating narrow passages and regrouping.

## Problem

- Existing multi-robot navigation methods struggle with maintaining rigid formations in complex, constrained environments like narrow corridors, leading to impasses.
- Traditional planning and optimization-based approaches incur high computational costs, limiting real-time adaptation in dynamic settings.
- Current subteaming methods focus mainly on team division and merging, lacking fine-grained control over subteam formations or individual robot navigation within challenging passages.

## Method

- Introduces **SubTeaming and Adaptive Formation (STAF)**, a hierarchical learning framework comprising a high-level ==deep graph cut== for dynamic team splitting.
- An intermediate-level ==graph learning== component coordinates subteams using a ==spring-damper model== for adaptive formation control.
- A low-level ==reinforcement learning== policy controls individual robot navigation, collision avoidance, and eventual team regrouping, trained using an alternating algorithm.

## Results

- STAF achieved a **100%** successful navigation rate in Gazebo and Unity 3D simulations across various formations, outperforming rigid Leader & Follower (**40-70%** SR) and matching Decentralized GNN in success.
- Demonstrated significantly higher Contextual Formation Integrity (CFI) than baselines, with STAF-full achieving **over 80%** (σ < 0.1) compared to DGNN's **58.91%** in circle formations.
- Successfully validated on physical differential-drive Limo robots in both indoor narrow passages and challenging outdoor environments (e.g., snowy terrain), showcasing real-world robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining deep graph cut for team division with graph learning for adaptive formation control enables robots to effectively navigate environments too narrow for a full team.
> - The integrated spring-damper model within the reward function facilitates flexible formation adaptation, balancing proximity for tight spaces with sufficient distance for collision avoidance.
> - A multi-objective loss function for subteam division, incorporating adjacency, balance, and goal distance, is crucial for forming compact, goal-oriented, and equally distributed subteams.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-robot systems offer advantages such as redundancy, parallelism, and scalability, making coordinated multi-robot navigation a fundamental capability for numerous real-world applications including search and rescue, space exploration, and transportation. A common requirement in these applications is for robot teams to maintain specific formations, such as circular formations for protection or line formations for coverage, while traversing diverse environments.

The existing research landscape for coordinated multi-robot navigation encompasses several approaches. Traditional methods, including classical planning, game-theoretic, and optimization-based techniques, often incur high computational costs, limiting their applicability in dynamic or complex environments. More recently, learning-based methods, such as deep neural networks and multi-agent reinforcement learning, have been employed for robot modeling, coordination, and navigation. While these approaches have advanced the field, the authors identify a gap: they typically have not addressed the specific challenge of *adaptive formation control* necessary for navigating complex, constrained environments like narrow corridors. In such scenarios, rigidly adhering to a predefined formation can impede progress or cause the team to become stuck.

Another relevant area is subteaming in multi-robot navigation and task allocation. Methods in this domain, such as graph cuts for team division or mixed-integer programming for task allocation, primarily focus on the division and merging of robot teams. However, these methods often lack mechanisms for fine-grained control over the formations of the resulting subteams or the navigation of individual robots within those subteams. Consequently, while they can divide a team, they do not inherently provide a solution for coordinated navigation and adaptive formation maintenance through challenging passages.

This work addresses these limitations by introducing a novel approach that integrates subteaming capabilities with adaptive formation control. It aims to bridge the gap between existing learning-based navigation techniques that lack formation adaptation and subteaming methods that do not provide control over subteams or individual robot navigation, particularly within the context of coordinated movement through constrained spaces. The paper positions itself as introducing one of the first problem formulations and learning-based solutions for this combined challenge.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable effective coordinated multi-robot navigation in complex scenarios where maintaining a full team's original formation is infeasible due to environmental constraints. Specifically, the authors aim to develop a system that allows robot teams to autonomously navigate through environments such as narrow corridors, which are too constricted for the entire team to pass simultaneously while preserving its predefined formation.

To achieve this overarching goal, the research focuses on developing capabilities for:
- **Formation Adaptation:** This objective involves enabling a robot team or its subteams to maintain a desired formation while dynamically adjusting their relative positions. The purpose of this dynamic adjustment is to allow safe and efficient navigation through unstructured or challenging environments (e.g., narrow corridors) towards their goal positions. The motivation here is to overcome the limitations of rigid formation adherence, which can lead to impasses in confined spaces.
- **Subteaming:** This objective entails the capability of a robot team, initially in a specific formation, to autonomously divide into smaller subteams. These subteams are intended to maintain the same *type* of formation (e.g., a smaller circle formation) but with adapted geometries to navigate through environments too narrow for the full team. Following successful traversal of the constrained area, the subteams must then be able to merge back into the original full team, restoring its initial formation. This capability is motivated by the need for flexible team configurations to overcome environmental bottlenecks.

The motivation for pursuing these objectives stems from the identified shortcomings of existing multi-robot navigation techniques. Traditional methods are often computationally intensive and struggle with dynamic adaptation. Current learning-based navigation approaches typically do not incorporate adaptive formation control, which is crucial for dynamic environments. Furthermore, dedicated subteaming methods focus solely on team division and lack the mechanisms to control subteam formations or individual robot movements during navigation. The authors argue that a unified approach capable of dynamically splitting teams, adapting formations, and controlling individual robots is essential for robust multi-robot operation in diverse and challenging real-world environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce SubTeaming and Adaptive Formation (STAF), a novel approach built upon a unified hierarchical learning framework comprising three interconnected levels: high-level deep graph cut for team splitting, intermediate-level graph learning for subteam coordination and adaptive formation control, and low-level policy learning for controlling individual robots to navigate and avoid collisions.

The robot team is represented as an undirected graph G = {V, E}. Each node v_i in V represents an individual robot, characterized by its position (p_i), goal position (g_i), and velocity (q_i). The edge matrix E = {a_i,j} defines spatial adjacency, where an edge exists (a_i,j = 1) if two robots are within a specified radius. The state of robot *i* is defined as s_i = [p_i, g_i, q_i, c_i], where c_i is the distance to the closest obstacle. The action for robot *i* is its velocity vector a_i = [v_x_i, v_y_i].

#### 3.1. High-Level Deep Graph Cut for Subteaming

At the highest level, STAF employs a deep graph cut approach to divide the full robot team into subteams when encountering a constrained environment.
1. **Graph Embedding:** A graph attention network (GAT) computes an embedding H = {h_i} for each robot. This involves projecting robot positions into a feature space (m_i = W_v p_i), computing attention coefficients (α_i,j) between adjacent nodes (reflecting their similarity), and then aggregating neighbor embeddings to generate the final node embedding h_i. A multi-head mechanism is utilized to enhance the richness of the embeddings.
2. **Team Division Classifier:** A classifier network, consisting of two fully connected layers followed by a SoftMax function, takes the robot embeddings H as input. It outputs a matrix Y = {y_i,j}, where y_i,j represents the probability of robot *i* belonging to subteam *j*. The number of subteams, *m*, is less than the total number of robots, *n*.
3. **Unsupervised Loss Function (L_st):** The network for team division is trained by minimizing an objective function L_st, which jointly optimizes three criteria:
 - **Subteam Adjacency:** This term, Y(1 − Y)^⊤ E, encourages robots within the same subteam to remain spatially adjacent. Minimizing this term, where Y(1 − Y)^⊤ quantifies the probability of robot pairs belonging to different subteams, forces the division to respect existing robot adjacencies.
 - **Subteam Balance:** This term, ∑_j ∑_i |y_i,j - n/m|, promotes an even distribution of robots across subteams. It minimizes the deviation of each subteam's size from the ideal balanced size (n/m).
 - **Subteam-Goals Distance:** This term, ∑_j || (∑_i y_i,j p_i / ∑_i y_i,j) - (∑_i y_i,j g_i / ∑_i y_i,j) ||^2, minimizes the distance between the calculated center position of each subteam and its aggregated goal position, ensuring that subteams are formed in a goal-oriented manner.

#### 3.2. Intermediate-Level Graph Learning for Multi-robot Formation Adaptation

The intermediate level focuses on coordinating robots to maintain a specific formation while dynamically adapting it to environmental constraints.
1. **Team State Embedding:** A graph network (ϕ) processes the individual robot state s_i and the team graph G to compute a team state embedding f_i for each robot. This network first projects s_i into an individual embedding z_i. Then, for each robot *i*, it aggregates the individual state embeddings of its teammates through message passing (specifically, the relative differences z_j - z_i) to form f_i. This embedding f_i captures both the robot's own state and its spatial relationships with its teammates, facilitating coordinated actions for formation maintenance.
2. **Spring-Damper Model for Formation Adaptation:** To enable adaptive formation control, a spring-damper model is integrated into the reward function (R_adp) used for training. This model has two components:
 - **Spring Component:** -λ|d_i,j - p_i,j|. This term encourages robot pairs to maintain a balance between proximity (for tight navigation in narrow spaces) and sufficient distance (for collision avoidance). d_i,j is the desired distance in the original formation, and p_i,j is the actual distance between robots *i* and *j*. The flexibility to adjust this balance enables formation adaptation.
 - **Damper Component:** -(1 - λ)q_i,j. This term, where q_i,j = ||q_i - q_j||_2 represents the relative velocity between robots, helps to smooth robot movements, preventing oscillations and overshooting during formation adjustments. The hyperparameter λ balances the influence of the spring and damper.

#### 3.3. Low-Level Individual Robot Control for Navigation

The lowest level of STAF is responsible for controlling individual robot movements to reach goals and avoid collisions.
1. **Navigation Control Network:** A network (ψ), comprising two linear layers and a ReLU activation function, takes the robot's state s_i and its team state embedding f_i as input. It outputs the action a_i, which are velocity commands for the individual robot.
2. **Reinforcement Learning Policy:** The network ψ is part of a control policy π_θ, parameterized by θ, which is trained using a reinforcement learning framework.
3. **Reward Function Design:** The reward function incentivizes:
 - **Goal Reaching:** Robots receive rewards for moving closer to their assigned goal positions.
 - **Obstacle Avoidance:** Penalties are imposed if a robot comes too close to obstacles or other robots, ensuring safe navigation.
4. **Team Recovery:** After subteams successfully navigate a narrow corridor and reach an open area sufficient for the full team, the goal positions of individual robots are updated. This prompts the subteams to merge back into the original full team formation, restoring the initial configuration.

#### 3.4. Training and Execution

The STAF framework is trained using an alternating algorithm, where the high-level graph cut component and the joint intermediate-low level components are iteratively trained. During deployment, the intermediate and low levels of the hierarchy execute in a decentralized manner, while the high-level team division is performed centrally.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The STAF approach was evaluated through extensive experiments across three distinct setups: Gazebo simulations, Unity-based 3D multi-robot simulations, and real-world physical robot teams.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setups

- **Gazebo Simulation (ROS1):** This standard simulation environment featured various numbers and types of robots configured in circular, wedge, and line formations. The scenarios included narrow corridors necessitating subteaming and adaptive formation control.
- **Unity-based 3D Multi-Robot Simulator (ROS1):** This high-fidelity simulator provided outdoor field environments with challenging narrow pathways and bridges. It employed differential-drive Warthog robots, which introduced complexities related to non-holonomic constraints and continuous formation adjustments over long, curved paths.
- **Physical Robot Teams (ROS2):** Real-world validation utilized differential-drive Limo robots with caterpillar tracks, equipped with onboard Intel NCU i7 processors and communicating via Wi-Fi. Experiments were conducted in both indoor narrow spaces and outdoor uneven terrain (including snowy conditions).

#### 4.2. Evaluation Metrics

The performance of STAF and comparative methods was assessed using three metrics:
- **Successful Rate (SR):** The percentage of robots in the full team that successfully reached their goal positions without collisions.
- **Travel Time (TT):** The total time taken for the entire robot team to reach its goal position.
- **Contextual Formation Integrity (CFI):** A real-time measure of how well robots adhered to their designated formation, with a shape threshold σ defining the strictness of formation maintenance. CFI ranges from 0 to 1, with smaller σ values indicating stricter requirements.

#### 4.3. Comparative Analysis with Baselines

STAF was compared against two baseline methods:
- **Leader and Follower (L&F):** A method where a designated leader robot guides follower robots while maintaining a rigid formation.
- **Decentralized GNN (DGNN):** A hierarchical learning framework generating velocity controls for individual robots, but without explicit team-level formation control.

#### 4.4. Results in Multi-Robot Simulations (Gazebo)

- **Qualitative Observations (Figures 3, 4):**
 - L&F often became stuck in narrow corridors due to its rigid formation adherence.
 - STAF demonstrated autonomous team division, adaptive formation adjustment within subteams to navigate narrow passages, and subsequent regrouping into the original full team formation.
 - Visualizations of robot trajectories confirmed the subteaming behavior (division into red and blue subteams) and individual robot paths adapting to the corridor.
 - For wedge formations, subteam division prioritized goal-distance objectives, leading to more compact subteams suitable for navigation.
- **Quantitative Results (Tables 1, 2):**
 - **Successful Rate (SR):** STAF-full achieved a 100% success rate across all tested formations (circle, wedge, line), outperforming L&F (40-70% SR) and matching DGNN (100% SR).
 - **Contextual Formation Integrity (CFI):** STAF-full showed significantly higher CFI values. For instance, with σ < 0.1, STAF-full achieved 80.12% for circle, 80.51% for wedge, and 80.06% for line formations, substantially higher than DGNN (e.g., 58.91% for circle) which lacks formation control, and L&F (e.g., 64.54% for circle) which uses rigid control.
 - **Travel Time (TT):** STAF-full generally exhibited slightly longer travel times compared to baselines. This was attributed by the authors to its more complex navigation strategy, which includes dynamic splitting and adaptation, indicating a trade-off between efficiency and robustness in complex scenarios.
 - **Subteam Performance:** Analysis of individual subteams (STAF-sub#) also showed 100% success rates and maintained high formation integrity, with CFI above 87% (σ < 0.5) for circle, wedge, and line formations.

#### 4.5. Results in High-Fidelity Unity3D Simulations (Figure 5)

STAF successfully addressed challenges in complex outdoor environments with differential-drive Warthog robots. It demonstrated the ability to divide full teams into subteams, adapt the actions of these subteams to navigate long, curved, narrow paths and bridges, and subsequently regroup. A scenario with 9 robots in a line formation successfully split into three subteams to cross a corridor too narrow for groups larger than 3.

#### 4.6. Results in Physical Robot Teams (Figure 6)

Real-world experiments with differential-drive Limo robots confirmed STAF's capabilities.
- **Indoor Environments:** Teams of 6 to 8 robots successfully divided into subteams and adapted formations to navigate narrow indoor passages.
- **Outdoor Environments:** The approach exhibited strong adaptability to unstructured terrain, including snowy and uneven surfaces. Subteaming and formation adaptation were performed effectively even under conditions with significant action uncertainty due to wheel slippage.

#### 4.7. Ablation Study on Subteam Division (Figure 7)

An ablation study on the components of the high-level objective function (L_st) for team division revealed the importance of each term:
- Optimizing only for subteam balance resulted in evenly sized but potentially non-adjacent groups.
- Optimizing only for subteam adjacency tended to assign all robots to a single subteam.
- Optimizing only for subteam-goals distance led to subteams aligned with goals but without consideration for balance or adjacency.
- Removing any single component (balance, adjacency, or goal-distance) from the objective function resulted in suboptimal divisions, leading to unbalanced, uncompact, or misaligned subteams, respectively. This confirms the necessity of jointly considering all three factors for effective team division.

#### 4.8. Generalizability (Figures 8, 9)

- **Different Team Sizes:** STAF demonstrated generalizability to varying team sizes (4, 6, 7, and 8 robots) while maintaining circle formations through narrow corridors. Quantitative CFI results showed formation integrity of at least 80% for 8 robots and 87% for 4 robots (σ < 0.03).
- **Different Numbers of Subteams:** The method effectively handled team divisions into 2, 3, and 4 subteams, as illustrated in various scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents STAF, a method that introduces novel capabilities for coordinated multi-robot navigation in complex and constrained environments.

The primary significance of STAF lies in its ability to enable *subteaming and adaptive formation control* within a unified framework, addressing a previously identified gap in multi-robot navigation research. By combining dynamic team division with adaptive formation maintenance and individual robot control, STAF allows robot teams to overcome environmental limitations that would otherwise render rigid formations ineffective. This capability is critical for expanding the operational envelopes of multi-robot systems.

Specifically, the work's contributions include:
- **Novel Problem Formulation and Solution:** It offers one of the first learning-based solutions for the combined problem of subteaming and formation adaptation, enabling robots to navigate scenarios like narrow corridors where the entire team cannot pass.
- **Hierarchical Learning Integration:** The proposed hierarchical framework, which integrates a high-level deep graph cut for dynamic team division, an intermediate-level graph learning for subteam coordination and adaptive formation control, and a low-level reinforcement learning policy for individual robot control, represents a structured approach to managing complexity in multi-robot tasks.
- **Empirical Validation:** The method's effectiveness has been demonstrated through comprehensive experiments across diverse environments, including standard Gazebo simulations, high-fidelity Unity3D simulations with non-holonomic robots, and real-world deployments with physical robots in both indoor and challenging outdoor settings. This extensive validation enhances confidence in the method's robustness and adaptability to various robot platforms and environmental conditions.
- **Performance Advancement:** STAF significantly outperforms baseline methods (Leader and Follower, Decentralized GNN) in terms of successful navigation rates and contextual formation integrity, particularly in scenarios requiring dynamic adaptation.

The potential impact of STAF is substantial, as it enhances the operational flexibility and robustness of multi-robot systems. This advancement could enable more sophisticated applications in fields such as:
- **Search and Rescue:** Robots could navigate collapsed structures or tight passages more effectively.
- **Exploration:** Teams could explore environments with varying accessibility, such as caves or industrial plants with narrow entry points.
- **Surveillance and Logistics:** Robot teams could maintain formation for coverage in open areas, then dynamically adapt to traverse gates or corridors without compromising mission objectives.
- **Defense and Security:** Enhanced coordinated movement could improve autonomous convoy operations or facility inspections in complex terrains.

While the paper acknowledges limitations, such as the centralized nature of the high-level team division and the alternating training algorithm, these also point to future research directions that could further decentralize, optimize, and automate the STAF framework, building upon the foundational capabilities established in this work.
