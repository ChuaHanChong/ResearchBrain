# HALO:Closing Sim-to-Real Gap for Heavy-loaded Humanoid Agile Motion Skills via Differentiable Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> HALO closes the sim-to-real gap for heavy-loaded humanoid locomotion via two-stage gradient-based system identification in the differentiable simulator MuJoCo XLA. Stage one calibrates the nominal robot model from unloaded real trajectories; stage two identifies payload mass and center-of-mass on torso and hands. Using only joint encoders with one foot mechanically fixed, identified dynamics enable zero-shot RL transfer, cutting walking drift 45-73% and achieving 10/10 success on agile maneuvers.

## Problem

- Domain randomization treats payload shifts as disturbances to hedge against, yielding conservative policies that cannot exploit the robot's full physical capability under heavy loads.
- Traditional system identification for floating-base humanoids needs joint torque sensors, motion capture, or partial disassembly, which is impractical on complex commercial platforms.
- Sampling-based identifiers like CMA-ES are derivative-free and sample-inefficient, converging to physically unreasonable local optima when payload-induced inertial shifts grow large.

## Method

- ==Two-stage gradient-based system identification== in MuJoCo XLA: stage one calibrates full model parameters on unloaded trajectories, stage two optimizes only payload mass and CoM on torso and hands.
- ==Trajectory-matching loss== over Cartesian body positions with an extra upper-body-weighted term, plus regularizers penalizing CoM, mass deviation and box-constrained joint damping and friction.
- ==Sensor-minimal data collection== fixing one foot with two vises, reconstructing full-body state by ==forward kinematics== and a per-timestep ==quadratic program== correcting foot-height drift.

## Results

- Under the heaviest perturbation (+**12 kg** torso), HALO recovered **11.9871 kg** versus ground truth **12.0000** with zero seed variance, while CMA-ES gave **12.4289 ± 0.9244** and diverged on CoM.
- Motion tracking beat wide-range domain randomization and calibrated-mass baselines: steady-state global MPJPE fell **94.91** to **52.47 mm**, high-agility **132.89** to **78.83 mm**, velocity error **0.157** to **0.098 m/s**.
- Real hardware cut forward position error **73.33%** and **45.45%** versus baselines, yaw-jump angular error **72.97%**, and scored **10/10** on swallow balancing, side and roundhouse kicking versus **0/10** and **5-7/10**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Removing structured model bias before policy training beats hedging against it: accurate dynamics let minimal randomization deliver agility that broad randomization trains away.
> - Decoupling identification into coarse base calibration then fine payload estimation prevents optimization from misattributing pre-existing wear and calibration drift to payload parameters.
> - Analytical gradients dominate derivative-free search precisely where the parameter shift is largest, and mechanical constraints can substitute for expensive sensing during data collection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of humanoid robots in real-world, unstructured environments necessitates their ability to perform complex physical tasks, often involving the manipulation of tools, carrying equipment, or integrating additional functional modules. Such scenarios frequently introduce unknown payloads that significantly alter the robot's system dynamics, including its total mass, Center of Mass (CoM), and inertia distribution. These dynamic shifts create a substantial "sim-to-real gap," causing policies trained with nominal dynamics in simulation to degrade when transferred to physical robots under load.

Existing strategies to bridge the sim-to-real gap include:
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** This approach trains policies by exposing them to diverse, randomized dynamics during simulation, aiming for robustness. While effective for small stochastic noise, DR often leads to conservative behaviors for large, structured payload variations and may not fully exploit the robot's capabilities. Adaptive DR schemes have also been developed, but still face the challenge of heuristic tuning and potential conservatism for heavy loads.
- **Adaptive Policy Learning:** Methods that attempt real-time policy adjustment or data-driven domain transfer during deployment. These often require high-quality supervisory signals and may not provide the seamless zero-shot transfer desired for immediate field operation.
- **Data-driven Dynamics Learning:** Approaches that integrate learned residual models to account for unmodeled dynamics. While these "patch" models can perform well in specific training scenarios, their generalization to diverse tasks is often limited.
- **Traditional System Identification (SysID):** This category focuses on directly estimating physical parameters (mass, CoM, inertia) from real interaction data to minimize model mismatch. However, traditional SysID for floating-base systems like humanoids faces practical limitations. It often requires specialized sensing equipment (e.g., joint torque sensors, external motion capture systems) or procedures that involve isolating subsystems or partial robot disassembly, making it impractical for complex platforms. Sampling-based evolutionary algorithms for SysID can handle non-differentiable dynamics but suffer from low sample efficiency.
- **Differentiable Simulation:** The emergence of differentiable physics engines (e.g., MuJoCo XLA, Brax) has offered a paradigm shift. By providing analytical gradients of system dynamics with respect to physical properties, differentiable simulators enable end-to-end gradient-based optimization for system identification. This approach has shown success in fixed-base manipulators, quadcopters, and quadruped robots. However, scaling this framework to heavy-payload humanoids, where large inertial shifts and complex non-linear dynamics are dominant, presents a significant challenge requiring robust identification strategies.

This work, HALO (HeAvy-LOaded humanoid motion control), positions itself within the differentiable simulation paradigm to address the specific and challenging problem of heavy-loaded humanoids. It aims to overcome the limitations of current approaches by providing a practical, sensor-minimal, and robust system identification framework that enables effective zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for agile motion skills under heavy payload conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable humanoid robots to execute agile motion skills robustly while carrying unknown and potentially heavy payloads in real-world environments. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and aims to address specific challenges:

- **Motivation 1: Bridging the Structured Sim-to-Real Gap caused by Payloads.** Humanoid robots are increasingly expected to operate in scenarios where they must carry tools or additional modules. These payloads introduce significant, structured changes to the robot's dynamics (mass, CoM, inertia), leading to a large discrepancy between the simulated nominal model and the physical robot. Existing methods like Domain Randomization (DR) are often insufficient, as they treat these systematic changes as disturbances rather than explicit model parameters, resulting in conservative and suboptimal policies. The motivation is to directly address this structured model bias.

- **Motivation 2: Overcoming Practical Limitations of Traditional System Identification for Floating-Base Humanoids.** While System Identification (SysID) is a direct method to reduce model mismatch, traditional techniques are often impractical for complex, floating-base robots. They typically rely on expensive and specialized external sensors (e.g., motion capture, joint torque sensors) or require cumbersome procedures like disassembling parts of the robot. The motivation is to develop a sensor-minimal and practical SysID method applicable to humanoids in deployment scenarios.

- **Motivation 3: Ensuring Stable and Accurate Parameter Estimation in the Presence of Nominal Model Mismatch.** A significant challenge in payload identification is the entanglement between intrinsic discrepancies in the robot's nominal model (due to wear, calibration drift, manufacturing variations) and the additional dynamic changes induced by the payload. If not separated, the optimization process might incorrectly attribute nominal model errors to payload parameters, leading to biased and inaccurate estimates. The motivation is to devise a strategy that decouples these effects for improved identification accuracy and stability.

- **Motivation 4: Enabling Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer for Agile Motion Skills.** The ultimate goal is to train locomotion policies in simulation with accurately identified dynamics and deploy them directly to the physical robot without requiring extensive real-world fine-tuning. Current methods often struggle to achieve this "zero-shot" transfer under heavy-load conditions due to persistent sim-to-real gaps, leading to degraded performance in agility and robustness. The motivation is to reduce the model bias sufficiently to allow for effective zero-shot transfer, thereby enhancing the robot's capabilities in real-world tasks.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of the HALO framework are:
1. **Develop a differentiable, trajectory-level, gradient-based system identification pipeline** that is efficient and accurate for estimating physical parameters under heavy-load conditions.
2. **Introduce a sensor-minimal data collection procedure** that eliminates the dependency on specialized external sensing equipment, leveraging only onboard joint encoders through a fixed-foot constraint.
3. **Implement a two-stage identification strategy** to explicitly decouple base-model calibration from payload parameter estimation, enhancing stability and accuracy by addressing inherent nominal model mismatch before identifying payload-specific parameters.
4. **Demonstrate that policies trained with the identified dynamics can be transferred zero-shot to hardware** to achieve high precision, agility, and robustness under heavy payloads across a range of challenging locomotion and manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HALO framework integrates differentiable simulation-based System Identification (SysID) into the reinforcement learning (RL) control pipeline for heavy-loaded humanoids. It comprises three main steps: Data Collection, Gradient-Based Identification, and Heavy-Load Skill Acquisition.

#### 3.1. A. Data Collection

The challenge of obtaining accurate global state trajectories for floating-base humanoids without external motion capture (MoCap) systems is addressed through a constrained data collection procedure.
1. **Fixed-Foot Constraint:** One foot of the robot is mechanically constrained using vises. This constraint provides a known reference point, removing ambiguity in global base drift and enabling the reconstruction of full-body trajectories solely from onboard joint encoder measurements via forward kinematics.
2. **Exploration Policy:** A heuristic robot trajectory is designed, and an exploration policy is trained to imitate it. This policy is trained with nominal model parameters and a wide range of Domain Randomization (DR) to ensure stability under varying payload conditions during data collection.
3. **Real-World Data Acquisition:** The trained exploration policy is deployed on the physical robot. Joint positions and corresponding control inputs (target joint positions tracked by a low-level PD controller) are recorded.
4. **Data Processing for Foot-Height Consistency:** Due to sensor noise, direct recovery of body states from joint measurements can introduce accumulated drift, leading to inconsistent left-right foot heights. To correct this, a constrained quadratic program is solved at each timestep. This program minimally adjusts the root and lower-body joint increments to eliminate foot-height discrepancies, ensuring physically consistent ground contact.
5. **Data Quantity:** The approach leverages that even small changes in mass or CoM position can cause significant simulated trajectory drift, thus only a small amount of data is needed to identify major differences between the model and the real robot.

#### 3.2. B. Gradient-Based Identification

System identification is formulated as a trajectory-matching optimization problem within the differentiable physics simulator MuJoCo XLA.
1. **Problem Formulation:**
 - `θ ∈ R^d` represents a set of physical parameters (link mass, CoM position, inertia, joint damping).
 - Real-world dynamics are `s^r_{i+1} = Φ_real(s^r_i, a^r_i; θ_r)`, where `θ_r` are the ground-truth parameters.
 - Simulated dynamics are `s^s_{i+1} = Φ_sim(s^s_i, a^r_i; θ)`. The real-world control sequence `a^r_i` is used, and the initial states are synchronized (`s^s_0 = s^r_0`).
 - The goal is to estimate `θ` such that the simulated trajectory `τ_s(θ)` matches the real trajectory `τ_r`.
2. **Optimization Objective:** The optimization problem is defined as minimizing `L_total(θ) = L_track(θ) + R(θ)`, where `L_track` is the trajectory tracking loss and `R` denotes regularization terms. This is solved using gradient-based methods (Gradient Descent) by leveraging the analytical differentiability of `Φ_sim`.
3. **Tracking Loss (L_track):**
 - To calculate the loss, a batch of `B` trajectory fragments is uniformly sampled from the complete real motion trajectory.
 - For each fragment, the robot's state in simulation is reset, and a rollout of `N` control steps is executed.
 - The loss consists of two components:
 - `L_all_track(θ)`: Measures the sum of squared Cartesian position differences between simulated and real positions for *all* robot bodies `j ∈ B`.
 - `L_upper_track(θ)`: An additional term, weighted by `α_u`, for a *subset of upper-body links* `j ∈ U` (e.g., torso, hands). This emphasizes accurate tracking of parts most influenced by the payload.
4. **Regularization Terms (R):** To ensure physical plausibility and prevent unreasonable parameter deviations, four regularization terms are introduced:
 - `R_com`: Penalizes squared differences between identified and nominal CoM positions for identified links (`i ∈ I_link`).
 - `R_mass`: Penalizes squared differences between identified and nominal masses for identified links (`i ∈ I_link`).
 - `R_damp` and `R_fric`: Softly constrain joint damping coefficients (`α_d,k`) and static friction (`α_f,k`) for identified joints (`k ∈ I_joint`) to remain within reasonable intervals `[l, u]` using a box-constraint function `ϕ`.
5. **Two-Stage System Identification:** This is a core component designed to address the entanglement of nominal model mismatch and payload-induced changes.
 - **Stage 1: Base Model Calibration:** Using trajectories collected *without payload*, the full set of model parameters (`θ`) is optimized, starting from nominal values. This process yields a calibrated base model, reducing the inherent sim-to-real gap of the robot itself.
 - **Stage 2: Payload Parameter Identification:** With the calibrated model from Stage 1 as the initialization, *only payload-related parameters* are optimized. Specifically, the mass and CoM positions of the links attached by the payload (e.g., torso and hands) are identified. This stage uses trajectories collected *under loaded conditions*. This restriction focuses the optimization on the most critical payload parameters while leveraging the pre-calibrated base model.

#### 3.3. C. Skill Acquisition Under Heavy-Load

Once the effective model parameters are identified, they are used to train RL policies for agile motion skills in simulation.
1. **Motion Imitation:** The method adopts a motion imitation approach based on mjlab, using pre-collected reference robot motion trajectories (e.g., from AMASS, LAFAN1, HuB) as imitation targets.
2. **Policy Training:** MLP-based policies are optimized using PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) to maximize a tracking reward. This reward encourages alignment of joint states and body poses with the reference trajectories.
3. **Zero-Shot Transfer:** Due to the accurately identified model parameters, only minimal Domain Randomization is required during policy training. This enables direct, zero-shot transfer of the learned skills from simulation to the physical heavy-loaded humanoid robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to evaluate HALO's performance in parameter identification, motion tracking accuracy, and real-world agility under heavy loads, comparing it against established baselines.

#### 4.1. A. HALO vs. CMA-ES: Performance under Extreme Payload Perturbations (Q1)

**Experiment:** Simulation comparison of HALO (gradient-based) against CMA-ES (sampling-based) for identifying payload parameters (mass and CoM offsets) under three different perturbation settings, representing increasing payload magnitudes. Regularization was not enabled.
**Findings:**
- Under light payload perturbations (Settings 1 and 2), both HALO and CMA-ES achieved comparable and satisfactory convergence accuracy.
- Under *extremely heavy-loaded conditions* (Perturbation Setting 3: +12kg torso mass, +3.5kg/+3.0kg hand masses), CMA-ES exhibited sensitivity to random seeds, sometimes converging to local optima with physically unreasonable parameter values and high standard deviations. In contrast, HALO robustly converged to the vicinity of the ground-truth parameters with minimal standard deviation.
**Conclusion:** HALO demonstrated superior optimization stability and accuracy for humanoid SysID under extreme payload conditions, overcoming the local optima convergence issues observed with CMA-ES.

#### 4.2. B. Impact of Accurate Payload Identification (Motion Tracking in Simulation) (Q2)

**Experiment:** Evaluation of motion tracking performance in simulation (using mjlab with AMASS, LAFAN1, HuB sequences) under a specific payload configuration (Perturbation Setting 1). Policies were trained using three methods: Wide Range DR (WDR), Calibrated Mass (CM - only mass identified, nominal CoM), and HALO. Policies were then validated on the ground-truth perturbed model.
**Metrics:** Global Mean Per-Joint Position Error (E_g-mpjpe), Mean Per-Joint Position Error (E_mpjpe), and Velocity Error (E_vel).
**Findings:**
- HALO consistently outperformed both WDR and CM across all metrics for both "Steady-state tasks" (e.g., walking) and "High-agility tasks" (e.g., side kicking).
- For steady-state tasks, HALO achieved E_g-mpjpe of 52.47 mm (compared to 94.91 for WDR and 85.87 for CM) and E_mpjpe of 44.87 mm (compared to 70.54 for WDR and 56.33 for CM).
- For high-agility tasks, HALO achieved E_g-mpjpe of 78.83 mm (compared to 132.89 for WDR and 92.82 for CM) and E_mpjpe of 59.54 mm (compared to 81.54 for WDR and 67.06 for CM).
**Conclusion:** HALO's precise identification of payload mass and CoM offsets led to superior motion tracking precision, indicating that explicit parameter identification (especially CoM) is more effective than broad randomization or partial calibration for handling payload-induced dynamic shifts.

#### 4.3. C. Effectiveness of the Two-Stage Strategy (Real-World SysID Accuracy) (Q3)

**Experiment:** Comparison of the two-stage HALO method against a one-stage baseline (direct identification of loaded links using only loaded trajectories) on a real Unitree G1 robot. Ground-truth CoM positions were not obtainable, so the focus was on identified masses compared to reference masses (nominal + counterweights).
**Findings:**
- The two-stage HALO method demonstrated superior convergence accuracy for mass estimates.
- For torso mass, HALO identified 13.83 kg (reference 13.82 kg), while the one-stage method identified 12.93 kg.
- For left-hand mass, HALO identified 2.13 kg (reference 2.25 kg), while the one-stage method identified 3.22 kg.
- For right-hand mass, HALO identified 2.71 kg (reference 2.25 kg), while the one-stage method identified 3.16 kg.
**Conclusion:** The two-stage strategy significantly improved the accuracy of real-world mass identification, supporting the hypothesis that decoupling global modeling errors from local payload variations enhances identification stability and precision.

#### 4.4. D. Real-World Validation Under Heavy Payload Conditions (Sim-to-Real Transfer Robustness) (Q4)

**Experiment:** Deployment of policies trained with HALO-identified parameters, WDR, and CM on a physical Unitree G1 robot with a 6kg torso payload and 2kg per wrist. Two scenarios were evaluated:
1. **Scenario 1: Quantitative Analysis of Fundamental Locomotion (Bidirectional Walking, In-Place 90° Yaw Jumping).**
 **Metrics:** Maximum Forward Position Error (E_fpos), End-point Residual Error (E_epos), and Angular Tracking Error (E_ang).
 **Findings:**
 - For walking, HALO reduced E_fpos by 73.33% (vs WDR) and 45.45% (vs CM), and E_epos by 70.79% (vs WDR) and 42.22% (vs CM).
 - For jumping, the WDR baseline failed to initiate takeoff due to inability to manage heavy payload dynamics. HALO reduced E_ang by 72.97% compared to the CM baseline (from 41.8° to 11.3°).
2. **Scenario 2: Evaluation of High-Agility Motion Tasks (Swallow Balancing, Side Kicking, Roundhouse Kicking).**
 **Metric:** Success rate (defined as executing the maneuver without significant divergence from the reference motion).
 **Findings:**
 - WDR failed all three tasks (0/10 success rate).
 - CM achieved partial success (5/10 for Swallow Balancing, 7/10 for Side Kicking, 5/10 for Roundhouse Kicking).
 - HALO consistently achieved a 100% success rate across all three challenging high-agility maneuvers.
**Conclusion:** The parameters identified by HALO substantially improved the robustness and fidelity of sim-to-real transfer for heavy-loaded humanoids. This enabled more precise locomotion and successful execution of complex, high-agility tasks on the physical robot, demonstrating the critical role of accurate system identification for transferring explosive whole-body maneuvers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, HALO, that addresses a notable challenge in humanoid robotics: robustly performing agile movements while carrying unknown and significant payloads. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Real-World Deployment of Heavy-Loaded Humanoids:** By effectively closing the sim-to-real gap under heavy-load conditions, HALO allows humanoid robots to move beyond controlled laboratory environments. This is critical for applications in industrial facilities, disaster response, logistics, and service robotics where robots are often required to transport or manipulate substantial equipment and tools.
- **Reduced Reliance on Specialized Hardware:** The proposed sensor-minimal data collection procedure, which utilizes only onboard joint encoders combined with a fixed-foot constraint, significantly lowers the barrier to entry for performing complex system identification. This eliminates the need for expensive and often impractical external motion capture systems or sophisticated joint torque sensors, making advanced SysID techniques more accessible and cost-effective for a wider range of robotic platforms and practitioners.
- **Enhanced Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The core contribution of accurate parameter identification directly translates to improved zero-shot transferability of reinforcement learning policies from simulation to physical hardware. This minimizes the need for extensive, often tedious, and potentially damaging real-world fine-tuning, accelerating the development and deployment cycle for agile humanoid behaviors.
- **Improved Stability and Accuracy in System Identification:** The two-stage gradient-based identification framework provides a robust and stable method for parameter estimation. By explicitly decoupling base-model calibration from payload-specific identification, the approach effectively handles the complex interplay of inherent robot model inaccuracies and load-induced dynamic shifts, leading to more reliable and physically plausible parameter estimates. This methodological advancement in SysID could be applicable to other forms of structured model discrepancies beyond payloads.
- **Advancement in Differentiable Simulation Applications:** This work demonstrates a successful and practical application of differentiable physics engines (MuJoCo XLA) for solving a complex, high-stakes problem in robotics. It showcases the power of analytical gradients for efficient and accurate optimization of physical parameters in high-dimensional spaces, furthering the adoption and development of differentiable simulation tools.
- **Potential for Broader Generalization:** While focused on heavy payloads, the principles of two-stage system identification and sensor-minimal data collection with physical constraints could be generalized to address other forms of robot dynamic variations, such as changes due to wear, configuration modifications, or dynamic environmental interactions.

In conclusion, HALO contributes to the field by providing a robust and practical solution for a critical limitation of humanoid robots. Its methodology offers a pathway for deploying more capable, agile, and reliable heavy-loaded humanoids in diverse and demanding real-world scenarios.
