# Rethinking the Trust Region in LLM Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Sea AI Lab and the National University of Singapore developed Divergence Proximal Policy Optimization (DPPO), a framework for LLM fine-tuning that replaces PPO's heuristic ratio clipping with a principled, divergence-based trust region. This approach delivers improved training stability and efficiency across various LLM architectures and tasks, often reducing the need for external stabilization techniques.

## Problem

- Proximal Policy Optimization's (PPO) heuristic ratio-clipping mechanism is structurally ill-suited for the vast, long-tailed vocabularies inherent to Large Language Models (LLMs), leading to suboptimal and unstable learning.
- LLM fine-tuning with Reinforcement Learning (RL) frequently suffers from training instability, often linked to 'bad updates' that cause catastrophic performance degradation, and inefficiency due to over-constraining critical token updates.
- Principled trust region methods, such as TRPO, are computationally prohibitive for LLMs because they require calculating full vocabulary probability distributions, leading to high memory and computational overhead.

## Method

- Derived new ==policy improvement bounds== tailored for the ==finite-horizon, undiscounted setting== of LLM generation, providing a rigorous theoretical basis for trust-region methods in this context.
- Introduced **DPPO** (==Divergence Proximal Policy Optimization==), which replaces PPO's ratio clipping with a ==dynamic mask applied to the policy gradient objective==, based on a direct measure of ==policy divergence== (e.g., ==Total Variation== or ==KL divergence==).
- Developed ==memory-efficient approximations== for policy divergence calculation (==Binary== and ==Top-K Approximations==) to ensure the method's scalability and negligible overhead for LLMs with massive vocabularies.

## Results

- DPPO consistently demonstrated superior training stability and efficiency, achieving faster reward optimization and better converged performance compared to baselines like GRPO-ClipHigher and CISPO across various LLM architectures.
- DPPO maintained stable training where baselines frequently exhibited instability or catastrophic collapse, and its inherent stability significantly reduced the reliance on complex external stabilization techniques like Rollout Router Replay (R3).
- The simpler 'binary approximation' of TV/KL divergence performed comparably to the richer 'Top-K approximation,' proving to be a sufficient and computationally efficient choice for scalable RL in LLMs across diverse reasoning tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A trust region is essential for stable LLM training even at low learning rates, and it must be correctly anchored to the original behavior policy (μ) rather than a recomputed policy distribution (π).
> - Training instability in LLM RL is primarily caused by a small fraction (≤ 0.5%) of 'bad updates' on negative samples that aggressively decrease the probability of a sampled token far outside the trust region.
> - PPO's ratio clipping mechanism 'over-constrains' updates to low-probability, high-entropy tokens, hindering exploration and efficiency, and Truncated Importance Sampling (TIS) can degrade stability rather than improve it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a method for fine-tuning Large Language Models (LLMs), enabling capabilities such as alignment with human preferences and performance on complex reasoning tasks. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is widely adopted for this purpose due to its simplicity and scalability. PPO's mechanism of ratio clipping is intended to constrain policy updates within a "trust region," thereby promoting stable learning.

However, the paper identifies a structural limitation of PPO's ratio clipping when applied to LLMs, which possess expansive and long-tailed vocabularies. The probability ratio of a single sampled token, used by PPO as a proxy for policy divergence, is presented as a noisy and potentially misleading estimate of the true distributional shift. This issue is exacerbated by the training-inference mismatch, a phenomenon where the policy used for gradient computation diverges from the policy used for data generation, even with identical model parameters. Previous research has attempted to address this mismatch through techniques like Truncated Importance Sampling (TIS) or by enhancing numerical precision and engineering alignment.

Existing efforts to improve PPO, such as Clip-Higher or CISPO, have recognized that low-probability tokens can be disproportionately penalized, hindering exploration. However, these methods are often heuristic or deviate from the core trust-region principle. This work positions itself as a fundamental re-evaluation of the trust region concept in LLM RL, proposing a method that directly estimates policy divergence rather than relying on single-sample ratio clipping, while also addressing computational feasibility for large vocabularies. It aims to provide a more theoretically grounded and empirically robust approach to LLM fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a perceived structural inadequacy of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)'s core ratio clipping mechanism when applied to Large Language Models (LLMs). The authors argue that this mechanism, which constrains policy updates based on the probability ratio of sampled tokens, functions as a noisy, single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of policy divergence, rendering it ill-suited for the vast and long-tailed vocabularies characteristic of LLMs.

Specifically, the authors identify two critical issues:
1. **Over-penalization of low-probability tokens:** When a low-probability token's likelihood increases even slightly, the resulting probability ratio can become substantial, triggering aggressive clipping in PPO. This occurs despite the absolute change in probability mass being minor, leading to the suppression of potentially valuable exploratory updates and slowing down the learning process.
2. **Under-constraining of high-probability tokens:** Conversely, a large absolute shift in the probability of a high-probability token might yield a probability ratio close to one, which falls within PPO's clipping range. This can allow significant, potentially destabilizing policy updates to occur unchecked, thereby risking training instability.

These issues are further complicated by the "training-inference mismatch," a common challenge in LLM fine-tuning where the policy distributions used for training and inference diverge. The paper demonstrates that this mismatch can lead to increased volatility in probability ratios, particularly for low-probability tokens, while true divergence measures remain more stable.

To address these limitations, the authors establish three key objectives for their work:
1. **Theoretical Formulation:** To derive policy improvement bounds specifically adapted for the finite-horizon, undiscounted setting prevalent in LLM generation. This objective aims to establish a robust theoretical foundation for trust-region methods within this domain, providing an alternative to PPO's heuristic.
2. **Stability and Efficiency Analysis:** To empirically investigate and isolate the primary factors contributing to training instability in LLM RL. This includes examining the necessity of a trust region, identifying the correct reference distribution for trust region constraints, and pinpointing the specific types of policy updates that primarily drive instability. Furthermore, this objective seeks to understand how low-probability tokens influence training efficiency and exploration.
3. **Algorithmic Performance:** To propose a novel algorithm, Divergence Proximal Policy Optimization (DPPO), that replaces PPO's ratio-based clipping with a constraint based on direct policy divergence. A key aspect of this objective is to introduce computationally efficient approximations (Binary and Top-K divergence) to make direct divergence estimation feasible for LLMs. The ultimate goal is to demonstrate that DPPO achieves superior training stability and efficiency compared to existing PPO variants, thereby offering a more reliable framework for RL-based LLM fine-tuning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research spans theoretical development, algorithmic design, and extensive empirical validation.

**Theoretical Foundation:**
The authors first adapt the standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework to the specific context of LLM fine-tuning, which is characterized by undiscounted (γ=1) episodic tasks with a finite horizon (T). They derive a performance difference identity (Theorem 3.1) and subsequent policy improvement bounds (Theorem 3.2) tailored for this setting. These bounds relate the performance difference between a target policy (π) and a behavior policy (μ) to a surrogate objective and an error term, with the error term bounded by either the maximum total variation (TV) divergence (quadratic in T) or the average token-level TV divergence (linear in T). This theoretical framework provides the justification for applying a trust region constraint based on policy divergence, similar to Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), but adapted for LLMs.

**Divergence Proximal Policy Optimization (DPPO) Algorithm:**
DPPO is proposed as an alternative to PPO's ratio clipping. Instead of using a fixed clipping threshold on the probability ratio, DPPO employs a dynamic mask within its objective function. This mask, denoted M_DPPO_t, is conditioned on a direct measure of distributional policy shift (D), such as TV or KL divergence, between the rollout policy and the training policy. The mask blocks updates only if they push the policy far outside the trust region, specifically when the advantage (Â_t) indicates a beneficial change but the policy ratio (r_t) is already deviating significantly, and the divergence D exceeds a predefined threshold (δ). This design retains the asymmetric structure of PPO's clipping, allowing unconstrained updates that move the policy towards the trusted region, while grounding the constraint in a more principled measure of divergence.

**Approximating Distribution Divergence:**
To address the computational challenge of calculating full policy divergence over large LLM vocabularies, two efficient approximations are introduced:
1. **Binary Approximation:** This method simplifies the categorical distribution into a Bernoulli distribution, focusing solely on the sampled token versus all other tokens. It allows for the computation of binary TV (D_Bin_TV) or KL (D_Bin_KL) divergences with minimal computational overhead.
2. **Top-K Approximation:** This approach considers the K most probable tokens under the behavior policy, augmented by the sampled token if not already included, and aggregates all other tokens into an "other" category. Divergence is then computed over this reduced vocabulary. This method aims to capture more distributional shift information than the binary approach while remaining computationally efficient. Both approximations are formally shown to be lower bounds of the true divergence.

**Empirical Evaluation:**
The methodology includes extensive empirical studies organized into three main phases:

1. **Analysis on Training Stability (Section 5):**
- **Setting:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B model fine-tuned on the MATH dataset, a scenario where a stable algorithm should achieve high accuracy.
- **Baselines:** Policy Gradient with Importance Sampling (PG-IS), Truncated Importance Sampling (PG-TIS/CISPO), PPO-like GRPO with Clip-Higher, and MiniRL (PPO variant using recomputed policy ratio for clipping).
- **Research Questions:** Investigate the necessity of a trust region, the correct anchor (rollout vs. recomputed policy) for the trust region, and the specific sources of training instability (e.g., negative samples with large divergence).
- **Metrics:** Training rewards, training-inference mismatch (mean absolute deviation between rollout and training policy distributions), and policy entropy.

2. **Analysis on Training Efficiency (Section 6):**
- **Setting:** Qwen3-1.7B-Base model trained on the DAPO dataset.
- **Experiment:** GRPO-ClipHigher baseline is modified to relax clipping for low-probability tokens (μ(y_t|s_t) < α) and to explore the impact of directional clip relaxation (e.g., relaxing upper or lower bounds).
- **Metrics:** Training reward curves, rollout probabilities of clipped tokens, and entropy of clipped tokens.

3. **Broader Evaluation (Section 7):**
- **Settings:** Five model configurations (MoE Base, MoE Base w/ R3, MoE Thinking, Dense Base, MoE Base w/ LoRA) based on Qwen3 models, fine-tuned on a filtered DAPO-Math subset.
- **Baselines:** GRPO-ClipHigher and CISPO.
- **Metrics:** Online evaluation of AIME24 and AIME25 scores, along with training rewards, training-inference mismatch, response length, and entropy.
- **Additional Tests:** RLHF-style alignment tasks using Gemma-2-9B-It and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 models with learned reward models, evaluated also on AlpacaEval 2.0. Ablation studies comparing Binary and Top-K divergence approximations. Generalization tests on Llama family models and other reasoning tasks (Arc1D, Acre, Sudoku-v0-easy). Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis for DPPO and GRPO.

All experiments are conducted using the VeRL framework in BFloat16 precision, and, as confirmed by the stability analysis, all methods utilize the original behavior policy for trust region construction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings across theoretical analysis, algorithmic performance, and empirical stability and efficiency:

**Theoretical Foundations:**
The paper establishes policy improvement bounds for LLM generation in finite-horizon, undiscounted settings. These bounds provide a rigorous theoretical justification for using divergence-based trust regions to ensure stable learning, moving beyond the heuristic nature of PPO's ratio clipping.

**Analysis on Training Stability:**
1. **Necessity of Trust Region:** Experiments demonstrated that algorithms without a trust region (PG-IS, PG-TIS/CISPO) suffered from accumulating training-inference mismatch, leading to performance collapse. In contrast, DPPO variants, which incorporate a principled trust region, maintained stable training and achieved high performance, even with very low learning rates.
2. **Correct Anchor for Trust Region:** The studies indicated that the trust region must be defined with respect to the *original behavior policy* (μ_θ') that generated the data. Using a recomputed policy distribution (π_θ') as the anchor, as in MiniRL or a decoupled DPPO variant, resulted in instability and performance collapse. This finding suggests a practical benefit by reducing training costs associated with recomputation.
3. **Source of Instability:** A detailed analysis revealed that a small proportion (typically ≤ 0.5%) of "bad updates" on negative samples (where the policy aggressively decreases the probability of a token) are the primary drivers of training instability. Implementing a minimal mask to block these specific updates was sufficient to restore stable training for otherwise unstable algorithms. This highlights the importance of managing negative feedback in LLM RL.
4. **Pitfalls of Truncated Importance Sampling (TIS):** The study found that TIS, often used to reduce variance, unexpectedly worsened training stability, leading to premature collapse and underperformance compared to untruncated counterparts. This effect is hypothesized to stem from TIS disproportionately down-weighting gradient signals from low-probability tokens that are crucial for exploration.

**Analysis on Training Efficiency:**
1. **Impact of Low-Probability Tokens:** Relaxing PPO's ratio clipping constraints for low-probability tokens (e.g., those with μ(y_t|s_t) < 0.1) led to substantial improvements in training efficiency. This supports the hypothesis that PPO's mechanism over-constrains updates for these tokens, hindering learning progress.
2. **Characteristics of Clipped Tokens:** Clipped tokens were observed to be predominantly low-probability and high-entropy, aligning with existing theories that high-entropy tokens are significant drivers of RL in LLMs.
3. **Directional Clipping Relaxation:** Exploring asymmetric clipping thresholds indicated that relaxing *both* the upper and lower clipping bounds (Relax-both) for low-probability tokens yielded the most efficient and stable training. While relaxing only the lower bound (Relax-low) showed faster initial learning, it eventually led to entropy collapse.

**Broader Empirical Evaluation:**
1. **Consistent Performance Superiority:** DPPO consistently demonstrated superior training stability and efficiency across all five large-scale experiments involving various Qwen3 model configurations (MoE, Dense, LoRA, with/without R3) and the DAPO-Math dataset. It optimized rewards at a faster rate and achieved better converged performance compared to GRPO-ClipHigher and CISPO.
2. **Robustness Against Instability:** DPPO maintained a stable training process even in scenarios where baseline methods (e.g., CISPO, GRPO-ClipHigher) exhibited training instability or catastrophic collapse.
3. **Orthogonality with R3:** DPPO variants, even without rollout router replay (R3), frequently outperformed R3-enhanced baselines. When R3 was combined with DPPO, additional performance gains were observed, suggesting that the benefits of DPPO and R3 are complementary.
4. **RLHF-style Alignment:** On open-ended alignment tasks (Gemma-2-9B-It with UltraFeedback and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 with HH-RLHF), DPPO improved learned rewards faster and reached higher final rewards than GRPO.
5. **AlpacaEval 2.0 Benchmarking:** A DPPO-fine-tuned Qwen3 model achieved 80.90% length-controlled and 79.93% raw win rates on AlpacaEval 2.0, establishing a new state-of-the-art on the community leaderboard, indicating transferability of gains to external alignment benchmarks.
6. **Divergence Approximation Ablation:** Both the Binary and Top-K (K=20) divergence approximations performed similarly well and significantly outperformed baselines, suggesting that the simpler Binary approximation is sufficiently effective and computationally efficient for scaled RL.
7. **Generalization and Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** DPPO demonstrated broad applicability across different model families (e.g., Llama-based OctoThinker) and reasoning tasks (e.g., Arc1D, Acre, Sudoku) and showed relative insensitivity to its divergence threshold (δ) within tested ranges, contrasting with GRPO's higher sensitivity to its clipping parameters.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuning with Reinforcement Learning (RL):

1. **Foundational Shift in Trust Region Design:** The work provides a theoretically grounded alternative to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)'s heuristic ratio clipping, which has been the de facto standard for LLM RL. By demonstrating the structural flaws of ratio clipping for large vocabularies and proposing a principled divergence-based constraint, the paper suggests a fundamental re-evaluation of how trust regions are defined in this domain. This could lead to the development of more robust and reliable RL algorithms for LLMs.

2. **Enhanced Training Stability and Efficiency:** Divergence Proximal Policy Optimization (DPPO) consistently demonstrates superior training stability and efficiency across a range of LLM architectures, sizes, and tasks. This translates to faster convergence, higher final performance, and reduced susceptibility to catastrophic collapse, which are critical for the resource-intensive process of fine-tuning large models. The improved stability could reduce the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning and specialized stabilization techniques often required in LLM RL.

3. **Practical Algorithmic Advancements:** The introduction of computationally efficient divergence approximations (Binary and Top-K) makes the principled trust-region approach feasible for LLMs with their immense vocabularies. This addresses a major practical barrier that previously limited the application of theoretically sound methods like TRPO. The empirical finding that the simpler binary approximation is often sufficient streamlines implementation and resource utilization.

4. **Actionable Insights for Practitioners:** The detailed analyses on training stability provide concrete guidelines for practitioners. Identifying the necessity of a trust region, confirming that it must be anchored to the original behavior policy, and pinpointing "bad updates" on negative samples as a primary source of instability offer valuable insights for designing and debugging LLM RL systems. The observed pitfalls of Truncated Importance Sampling (TIS) also warrant reconsideration of its widespread use.

5. **Broader Applicability and Generalization:** DPPO's demonstrated effectiveness across various LLM models (MoE, Dense, LoRA), families (Qwen, OctoThinker, Llama), and diverse reasoning/alignment tasks (math, open-ended RLHF, abstract reasoning) indicates its broad applicability. This suggests that the proposed method is not domain-specific but addresses a general challenge in LLM fine-tuning, potentially becoming a widely adopted component in future RL frameworks.

6. **Synergistic Improvements:** The finding that DPPO offers significant benefits even without techniques like rollout router replay (R3) for MoE models, and further gains when combined with it, implies that DPPO addresses a fundamental aspect of policy optimization that is orthogonal to other engineering-level optimizations. This can simplify the training of complex MoE architectures while enabling further performance stacking.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art in LLM reinforcement learning by proposing a theoretically sound and empirically validated alternative to PPO's trust region mechanism. Its impact could be substantial in making LLM fine-tuning more stable, efficient, and broadly applicable, thus accelerating progress in aligning and enhancing the capabilities of large language models.
