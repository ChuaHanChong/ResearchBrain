# Towards Socially Compliant Navigation in Deep Reinforcement Learning via Proxemics-Based Reward Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> A proxemics-based reward for deep RL social navigation. Each detected human's personal space becomes a radial Gaussian-mixture field over Hall's four interpersonal zones; these aggregate into a normalized social cost map, weighted by a robot-centered Gaussian kernel over the field of view, with the reward given as the negative temporal difference of that cost. Dropped into AttnGraph and MultiSoc, it improves social metrics while keeping competitive success rates.

## Problem

- Prior deep RL crowd navigation optimizes task-centric objectives, so policies reach goals while still invading personal space, jerking abruptly, and moving unpredictably.
- Existing comfort rewards are sparse threshold-based signals that are hard to learn from, or kinematic surrogates like relative velocity that supervise comfort only indirectly.
- Many reward formulations depend on global state rather than local perception, and lack principled grounding in Hall's proxemic zone theory.

## Method

- Per-human ==radial Gaussian-mixture proxemic field==: four components with zone radius, spread, and weight for intimate, personal, social, and public distances, ==isotropic== rather than anisotropic.
- Aggregated ==social cost map== via summed fields passed through a bounded normalizer, so overlapping personal spaces raise cost in congested regions and implicitly discourage cutting through groups.
- ==Robot-centered Gaussian kernel== integrated against the cost map over the instantaneous field of view via a ==Frobenius inner product==, with reward as the negative weighted ==temporal difference==.

## Results

- On circle crossing with AttnGraph, minimum distance rises from **0.48 m** to **0.65 m**, intimate-zone intrusion falls **3.78%** to **1.42%**, time-to-collision **1.83 s** to **2.38 s**, jerk **3.05** to **2.47**.
- In the corridor scenario success rate improves for both backbones, AttnGraph **0.72** to **0.74** and MultiSoc **0.60** to **0.80**, with minimum distance reaching **0.67 m** and **0.71 m**.
- Isotropic beats anisotropic shaping: AttnGraph personal-zone intrusion **6.55%** versus **11.65%**, while front/back intrusion ratios stay near balanced, roughly **51/49**, showing no directional preference emerges.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense, graded comfort signals over a perceived neighborhood teach social behavior far better than sparse threshold penalties or collision-risk surrogates that only fire near failure.
> - Anisotropic cost fields that help classical planners hurt policy learning, since directional discontinuities raise reward variance under stochastic exploration in dense crowds.
> - Reward shaping is a soft constraint: the weighting coefficient trades compliance against efficiency monotonically, and large values over-regularize into freezing-like caution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot navigation in human-populated environments has seen substantial advancements, particularly with the emergence of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) methods. Historically, robot navigation focused on achieving geometric or dynamic objectives, such as reaching a goal without collisions. However, as robots are increasingly deployed in real-world crowded settings (e.g., malls, hospitals), the necessity for "social navigation" has become paramount. Social navigation extends beyond mere collision avoidance, demanding that robots move in ways perceived by humans as safe, predictable, smooth, and minimally intrusive. This broader understanding emphasizes principles like comfort for surrounding agents, adherence to social norms, and legible motion.

Existing approaches to social navigation generally fall into two categories: optimization/model-based methods and learning-based methods. Optimization-based techniques often leverage hand-crafted mathematical models and explicitly encode comfort criteria, such as interpersonal distance zones derived from Hall's proxemics theory, into local navigation costmaps. These methods can offer interpretability and theoretical guarantees but may struggle with generalization across diverse crowd configurations or require extensive manual tuning.

Learning-based methods, particularly DRL, reduce the reliance on explicit modeling by training control policies in simulation. This allows policies to learn complex interaction patterns from rich observations. However, a significant challenge in DRL is that learned policies are heavily influenced by the reward function. If social compliance and human comfort are not adequately represented in the reward, DRL policies may achieve high navigation success rates while exhibiting socially inappropriate behaviors, such as personal space intrusion or abrupt movements. Prior work has attempted to integrate social considerations into DRL rewards, including proxemic elements. However, many existing reward formulations for social compliance are often sparse, rely on global state information, or lack a direct, principled grounding in established social theories like Hall's proxemics. This can lead to DRL agents that are collision-free but still socially awkward or uncomfortable for humans.

This paper positions itself within this context by addressing the identified gap in DRL-based social navigation. It aims to develop a novel reward modeling approach that provides a dense, interpretable social learning signal, grounded in proxemics theory, to improve human-aware behavior in DRL agents without sacrificing navigation efficiency. It focuses on the reward function itself rather than new policy architectures, suggesting that a refined social reward mechanism can enhance the social compliance of various DRL navigation backbones.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a novel proxemics-based reward formulation for Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) in social navigation, designed to enhance social compliance while maintaining navigation efficiency.

The motivation for this work stems from several observed limitations in current DRL-based social navigation approaches:

- **Insufficient Social Compliance:** While DRL has improved robot navigation in crowded environments by focusing on task-centric objectives like collision avoidance and goal reaching, the resulting policies often underrepresent or fail to explicitly optimize for social compliance. This leads to robots that might be collision-free but still exhibit behaviors considered intrusive, unpredictable, or uncomfortable by humans, such as entering personal space or making abrupt movements.
- **Limitations of Existing Reward Models:** Previous attempts to incorporate social compliance into DRL reward functions have encountered difficulties. Some approaches use sparse, threshold-based signals that are challenging for DRL agents to learn from effectively. Others rely on global state information, which is not always available in realistic deployments, or employ kinematic surrogates (e.g., relative velocity) that only indirectly supervise socially meaningful behaviors without explicit grounding in human social interaction theories.
- **Lack of Principled Proxemics Integration:** Despite Hall's proxemics theory being a widely adopted conceptual framework for social navigation, existing DRL reward formulations have not fully leveraged its potential to provide a dense, interpretable, and theoretically grounded signal for social learning. The direct integration of personal space concepts, like the four interpersonal zones (intimate, personal, social, public), into a continuous reward signal has been largely unexplored in a dense DRL context.
- **Need for Explicit Social Evaluation:** Current evaluation protocols for DRL navigation often primarily emphasize task success and efficiency metrics (e.g., success rate, travel time). There is a recognized need to explicitly assess human comfort and social acceptability through dedicated social metrics, rather than relying on navigation metrics alone as proxies for social behavior.

To address these motivations, the paper aims to:

1. **Develop a novel robot-centric, proxemics-based reward model:** This model is grounded in Hall's theory of interpersonal distance, representing each pedestrian's personal space as a radial Gaussian-mixture field. It computes a robot-centric local proxemic cost within the robot's field of view (FOV).
2. **Integrate and validate the proposed reward model:** The research seeks to integrate this reward into established DRL navigation methods (AttnGraph and MultiSoc) and evaluate its impact across multiple crowd scenarios, reward baselines, and crowd densities.
3. **Demonstrate consistent social metrics gains:** The objective is to show that the proposed reward formulation consistently improves social metrics (e.g., minimum distance to humans, time spent in intimate/personal zones, time to collision, motion jerk) while maintaining competitive navigation performance compared to other reward models.
4. **Provide insights into reward design factors:** Through systematic analysis of key reward design factors (e.g., human scalability, weighting sensitivity, field of view, Gaussian shape), the paper aims to offer guidance for future reward modeling in social navigation.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to bridge the gap between collision-free navigation and truly human-aware navigation in DRL, fostering the development of robots that can operate not only safely but also comfortably and appropriately in shared human spaces.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on developing a novel proxemics-based reward model and integrating it into existing DRL frameworks for social navigation, followed by a comprehensive evaluation.

**A. Problem Statement and Formulation**
The problem is framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) where a mobile robot navigates a crowded environment to reach a goal. The robot's state space includes its position and velocity, along with those of humans. At each timestep, the robot receives a local observation ($o_t$) comprising intrinsic information (robot's state, goal, orientation, radius) and positions of detected humans ($H_t$) within its Field of View (FOV). The robot executes continuous planar velocity actions ($a_t = (v_{x,t}, v_{y,t})$). The objective is to learn a policy that maximizes the expected discounted return, prioritizing safe and comfortable navigation. The paper assumes access only to detected human positions, reflecting realistic sensor capabilities.

**B. Proxemics-Based Reward Model**
The core contribution is a new reward term, $r_{prox,t}$, designed to promote social compliance:

1. **Per-Human Proxemic Field:** For each detected human $j$, a radially symmetric proxemic field $\phi_j(x, y)$ is defined. This field assigns a higher "cost" to regions closer to the human, decaying smoothly with distance. It is implemented as an isotropic Gaussian mixture over the radial distance:
 $\phi_j(x, y) = \sum_{k \in \{int,per,soc,pub\}} A_k \exp \left( - \frac{\| (x, y) - p^j_t \|_2 - \mu_k )^2}{2\sigma_k^2} \right)$.
 This formulation explicitly incorporates Hall's four interpersonal zones (intimate, personal, social, public), with parameters $\mu_k$ (characteristic radius), $\sigma_k$ (spatial spread), and $A_k$ (relative importance). The choice of an isotropic form is based on preliminary findings suggesting better performance in a DRL context.

2. **Aggregated Social Cost Map:** The individual proxemic fields from all detected humans are summed and normalized to create a social cost map $M_t(x, y)$:
 $M_t(x, y) = Norm( \sum_{j \in J_t} \phi_j(x, y))$, where $Norm(C) = 1 - e^{-C}$.
 This normalization maps costs to [0, 1), ensuring interpretability and boundedness, while naturally increasing costs where personal spaces overlap. The map is approximated by sampling on a grid.

3. **Robot-Centered Kernel:** A radial Gaussian kernel $\kappa(x, y)$ is defined to capture the robot's spatial footprint and influence:
 $\kappa(x, y) = \exp \left( - \frac{x^2 + y^2}{2\sigma_r^2} \right)$.
 $\sigma_r$ controls the spatial extent of the robot's contribution to proxemic intrusion. This kernel is also evaluated on a grid over the robot's instantaneous FOV ($\Omega_t$).

4. **Local Proxemic Intrusion Cost:** A scalar cost $C_t$ is computed by integrating the product of the robot kernel and the aggregated social cost map over the robot's FOV. In practice, this is approximated by the Frobenius inner product of the discrete kernel and cost map matrices: $C_t \approx \langle K_t, M_t \rangle_F$. This metric quantifies the spatial overlap between the robot's neighborhood and human-induced proxemic costs within its perception range.

5. **Proxemics-Based Reward Term:** The proposed reward term $r_{prox,t}$ is defined as the negative temporal change in the proxemic cost:
 $r_{prox,t} = -\lambda \cdot \Delta C_t$, where $\Delta C_t = C_t - C_{t-1}$.
 This temporal difference formulation encourages actions that reduce proxemic intrusion (rewarded) and penalizes actions that increase it (penalized), providing a dense, continuous learning signal.

**C. Overall Reward Structure**
The proposed proxemics-based reward term is integrated into a standard social navigation reward structure. The total reward $r_t$ is defined as:
$r_t = \begin{cases} r_c & \text{if collision} \\ r_s & \text{if goal reached} \\ r_{pot,t} + r_{soc,t} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
Where $r_c$ is a negative terminal penalty for collisions, $r_s$ is a positive terminal reward for reaching the goal, and $r_{pot,t}$ is a potential-based shaping term encouraging goal progress. The $r_{soc,t}$ term is the social interaction reward, which is varied across different reward baselines and includes the proposed $r_{prox,t}$.

**D. Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol**

1. **Simulation Environment:** The CrowdNav simulator is used. Humans are simulated with ORCA.
2. **Scenarios:** Two scenarios are employed: "Circle Crossing" (humans cross paths through a central region) and "Corridor" (humans and robot navigate in a corridor-like formation).
3. **Parameters:** Specific parameters are detailed for proxemic zones (Table I), robot kernel width, and reward weighting coefficients.
4. **Metrics:**
 - **Navigation Metrics:** Success Rate (SR), Collision Rate (CR), Timeout Rate (TO), Travel Length (TL), Travel Time (TT). These assess task effectiveness and physical safety.
 - **Social Metrics:** Social Compliance (SC, percentage of time in intimate/personal zones), Minimum Distance (MD) to a human, Time to Collision (TTC), Jerk (JE, smoothness of motion). These quantify human-aware behavior and comfort. Social metrics are computed over successful episodes only.
5. **Baselines and DRL Training:** The proposed reward model ($R_p$) is integrated into two DRL social navigation backbones: AttnGraph and MultiSoc. Performance is compared against:
 - **Base:** No human-aware reward ($r_{soc,t} = 0$).
 - **Distance-based Reward ($R_d$):** Uses the model from Nishimura et al..
 - **Velocity-based Reward ($R_v$):** Uses the model from Maddumage et al..
 The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used for training, with specific hyperparameters and training duration (10^7 timesteps). Experiments are repeated over three random seeds.

**E. Ablation Studies**
The methodology also includes several ablation studies to analyze the impact of key design choices on performance:
- **Human Scalability:** Varying crowd density (number of humans).
- **Weighting Sensitivity:** Adjusting the $\lambda$ coefficient for the proxemic reward.
- **Field of View:** Exploring different sensing ranges and angular apertures.
- **Gaussian Shape:** Comparing isotropic vs. anisotropic proxemic field formulations, including front/back intrusion ratios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation across two DRL navigation methods (AttnGraph and MultiSoc) and two crowd scenarios (Circle Crossing and Corridor) yielded several findings regarding the proposed proxemics-based reward model ($R_p$).

**A. Comparative Analysis (Table III)**

- **Overall Social Metric Improvement:** The proposed $R_p$ consistently achieved the strongest improvements across social metrics for both AttnGraph and MultiSoc, in both Circle Crossing and Corridor scenarios.
 - **Minimum Distance (MD) and Social Compliance (SC):** Policies trained with $R_p$ maintained larger minimum distances to humans (higher MD) and spent significantly less time within intimate (SC$_{0.25}$) and personal (SC$_{0.45}$) proxemic zones. For instance, in Circle Crossing with AttnGraph, MD increased from 0.48m (Base) to 0.65m ($R_p$), while SC$_{0.25}$ decreased from 3.78% to 1.42%. Similar trends were observed with MultiSoc and in the Corridor scenario.
 - **Time to Collision (TTC):** $R_p$ policies exhibited higher TTC values, suggesting more anticipatory and safer maneuvers, resolving potential conflicts earlier. With AttnGraph in Circle Crossing, TTC increased from 1.83 (Base) to 2.38 ($R_p$).
 - **Jerk (JE):** The mean magnitude of acceleration derivative (Jerk) was lower for $R_p$ policies, indicating smoother robot motion. This suggests that the proxemic shaping encourages more gradual trajectory adjustments rather than abrupt reactive movements.
- **Navigation Performance Maintenance:** While prioritizing social metrics, $R_p$ maintained competitive navigation performance (Success Rate, Collision Rate, Travel Length, Travel Time) relative to the compared reward models. For example, AttnGraph with $R_p$ had a slightly higher SR (0.87 vs 0.83 Base) and lower CR (0.12 vs 0.13 Base) in Circle Crossing, sometimes incurring a minor increase in Travel Length or Time due to more conservative paths.
- **Qualitative Behavior (Figure 3):** Sample trajectories illustrate that $R_p$-trained policies consistently selected wider, more peripheral passing arcs, effectively avoiding dense intersections of crowd flow. In contrast, policies trained with $R_d$ or $R_v$ often produced more central, closer pass-bys and exhibited more reactive trajectory changes.

**B. Ablation Studies**

1. **Human Scalability (Figure 4):**
 - As the number of humans increased, both AttnGraph and MultiSoc showed expected degradation in SR, MD, and TTC.
 - The incorporation of $R_p$ consistently shifted learned behavior towards social compliance across varying crowd densities. Policies with $R_p$ maintained larger MD and TTC, and reduced SC$_{0.45}$, demonstrating its effectiveness even in denser environments. This indicates that $R_p$ provides a robust signal for promoting socially compliant interaction geometries (e.g., wider passing arcs) rather than merely optimizing for goal coordinates.

2. **Weighting Sensitivity (Figure 5):**
 - Increasing the proxemic reward weighting coefficient $\lambda$ monotonically enhanced social compliance: MD and TTC increased, while SC$_{0.45}$ decreased. This confirms that $\lambda$ acts as a tunable parameter to control the robot's interaction margin and conservativeness.
 - However, excessively large $\lambda$ values led to a reduction in Success Rate, suggesting an over-regularization that can make the policy overly cautious and impede navigation efficiency in dense or constrained environments. This highlights a trade-off between social compliance and navigation efficiency.

3. **Field of View (Figure 6):**
 - Wider angular coverage and longer sensing ranges (larger FOV) generally improved task Success Rate for both methods. This suggests that rich local situational awareness enables earlier anticipation and less reactive conflict resolution.
 - Narrow, short-range FOVs consistently degraded SR, likely due to reduced observability and delayed responses to emerging conflicts.
 - The relationship with MD was nuanced; in some weak configurations, larger MD coincided with lower SR, possibly indicating simpler, less dense interactions rather than better social behavior. Conversely, broader FOV configurations often achieved higher SR with competitive MD, suggesting improved coordination in denser interactions.

4. **Gaussian Shape (Table IV):**
 - A comparison between anisotropic (direction-aligned) and isotropic (radial) proxemic field formulations showed that the isotropic reward consistently outperformed the anisotropic variant across both navigation and social metrics.
 - The isotropic formulation (R$_p$-Iso.) resulted in better MD, SC, TTC, and JE for both AttnGraph and MultiSoc.
 - The authors attribute this to the DRL learning dynamics, where isotropic shaping provides a smoother, denser, and direction-agnostic reward signal, offering more reliable feedback. Anisotropic fields, with their sharp directional discontinuities, create a higher-variance reward landscape that is harder for DRL to optimize under stochastic exploration.
 - Front/back intrusion ratios (SC$_{F0.45}$ and SC$_{B0.45}$) remained balanced for both variants, indicating no clear learned preference for front versus backside intrusions in this DRL setting, contrasting with model-based methods where anisotropic fields often exploit directional asymmetry.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method to enhance the social compliance of robots navigating in human environments, a critical challenge for real-world robotic deployment. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

1. **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** By explicitly integrating Hall's proxemics theory into the DRL reward function, the proposed method fosters more socially acceptable and comfortable robot behaviors. Robots trained with this model exhibit increased minimum distances to humans, reduced intrusion into personal space, and smoother trajectories. This directly contributes to building trust and acceptance of robots among humans in shared spaces, moving beyond merely collision-free operation to genuinely human-aware navigation.

2. **Methodological Contribution to DRL Reward Design:** The paper presents a novel, dense, and interpretable reward formulation that leverages a continuous discomfort cost field. This approach addresses limitations of existing sparse or indirectly supervised social rewards in DRL, providing a more effective learning signal. The use of a radial Gaussian-mixture field and a robot-centric kernel to compute local proxemic intrusion cost, along with temporal difference shaping, offers a principled way to incorporate social norms into DRL.

3. **Generalizability Across DRL Architectures:** The successful integration and consistent performance gains observed across two different DRL navigation backbones (AttnGraph and MultiSoc) demonstrate the modularity and general applicability of the proposed reward model. This suggests that the approach is not tied to a specific policy architecture, making it potentially transferable to other DRL-based social navigation systems.

4. **Actionable Insights for Reward Engineering:** The comprehensive ablation studies provide valuable insights for future research and development in social navigation.
 - The analysis of weighting sensitivity (λ) offers a clear understanding of the trade-off between social compliance and navigation efficiency, providing a tunable knob for designers to balance these objectives.
 - The findings on the field of view underscore the importance of sufficient local situational awareness for learning robust human-aware behaviors.
 - The comparison between isotropic and anisotropic proxemic fields highlights a DRL-specific effect, suggesting that smoother, more direction-agnostic reward signals can be more effective for DRL optimization despite theoretical advantages of anisotropic fields in classical planning.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work acknowledges its limitations, particularly that reward shaping is a soft constraint. This naturally paves the way for future research into integrating the proxemics model within constrained Reinforcement Learning (RL) frameworks, where strict adherence to safety and social norms could be guaranteed. Additionally, the explicit call for human preference studies and real-world deployment indicates a clear path towards validating and refining these socially compliant behaviors in practical settings.

In summary, this research significantly advances the state of social robot navigation by providing a robust, interpretable, and effective method for instilling proxemics-based social compliance into DRL agents. Its impact lies in fostering safer, more comfortable, and socially integrated human-robot coexistence.
