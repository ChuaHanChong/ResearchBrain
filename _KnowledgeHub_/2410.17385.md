# Do Vision-Language Models Represent Space and How? Evaluating Spatial Frame of Reference Under Ambiguities

> [!summary] Summary
> A new evaluation protocol assesses Vision-Language Models' spatial understanding, revealing that current models predominantly rely on English-centric egocentric perspectives for spatial reasoning and lack robustness and flexibility in adopting alternative Frames of Reference.

## Problem

- Existing VLM benchmarks for spatial reasoning largely overlook the inherent ambiguities in spatial expressions related to Frames of Reference (FoR).
- Current evaluations lack measures for spatial continuity, robustness, and consistency, treating spatial relations as fixed ground truths.
- VLMs need to align with the nuanced, ambiguous, and culturally diverse nature of human spatial cognition to truly exhibit human-like intelligence.

## Method

- The ==COMFORT== protocol uses ==synthetic 3D scenes== with controllable spatial relations and perspectives to simulate continuous spatial environments.
- It employs binary questions with explicit or implicit perspective prompts, translated into ==109 languages==, to test different ==Frames of Reference (FoR)== and cross-lingual understanding.
- Novel metrics are introduced, including ==Region Parsing Error (ε)==, Robustness (Standard Deviation, Prediction Noise), and Consistency (Spatial Symmetric, Spatial Opposite), to evaluate fine-grained VLM behavior.

## Results

- Most evaluated VLMs exhibit a preference for the reflected coordinate transformation and an egocentric relative Frame of Reference, aligning with English linguistic conventions.
- VLMs significantly struggle to adopt alternative Frames of Reference (intrinsic, addressee-centered) when explicitly prompted, often performing near chance levels, indicating inflexibility in perspective-taking.
- Models generally show a lack of robustness and consistency in their spatial representations, with high variability under minor visual changes and poor adherence to geometric principles; cross-lingual evaluation also indicates an English-centric bias.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spatial language in situated communication is inherently ambiguous, with meanings varying based on the adopted Frame of Reference (FoR) and cultural conventions.
> - Current VLM evaluations often overlook these fundamental ambiguities, focusing on fixed spatial relations rather than the flexibility and consistency required for human-like spatial cognition.
> - Cross-cultural analysis is essential as human conventions for resolving spatial ambiguities are not uniform across languages, potentially revealing biases in multilingual VLMs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Artificial Intelligence has witnessed remarkable progress, largely propelled by the success of Large Language Models (LLMs) and their multimodal extensions, Vision-Language Models (VLMs). These models have demonstrated impressive capabilities across various tasks, leading to excitement about their potential for human-like cognition. Researchers are increasingly focusing on evaluating VLMs beyond mere performance metrics, delving into aspects inspired by human cognitive capabilities such as referential grounding, compositional reasoning, visual illusions, and theory of mind.

Within this burgeoning landscape, **spatial language understanding and reasoning** has emerged as a crucial area of inquiry. Several benchmarks have been developed to assess VLM performance in this domain (e.g., Mirzaee et al., 2021; Kamath et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023a), and new models are being designed to enhance these capabilities (e.g., SpatialVLM, SpatialRGPT). This paper acknowledges these advancements but critically identifies a significant gap: the **under-exploration of ambiguities in spatial expressions**, particularly those related to **Frames of Reference (FoR)**.

The authors situate their work by emphasizing that human spatial cognition is inherently ambiguous and deeply intertwined with language. They cite classic linguistic and cognitive science literature (e.g., Levinson, 2003; Shusterman & Li, 2016) that highlights how the meaning of spatial terms like "right" or "behind" can vary dramatically depending on the adopted FoR (e.g., egocentric, intrinsic, absolute) and cultural conventions in coordinate transformations. Existing VLM benchmarks, as highlighted by the authors, largely overlook these fundamental ambiguities, treating spatial relations as fixed, unambiguous ground truths. Furthermore, these benchmarks often lack "spatial continuity" (testing only canonical axes) and specific metrics for robustness and consistency.

By focusing on the inherent ambiguity and cross-cultural diversity of spatial reasoning, this research directly addresses a critical oversight in current VLM evaluation. It aims to shift the focus from merely assessing whether models *can* understand spatial relations to *how* they represent space, particularly under conditions of ambiguity, and how their representations align (or misalign) with human cognitive and linguistic conventions across different cultures. This positions the work as a crucial step towards developing VLMs that possess more sophisticated, human-like spatial intelligence, moving beyond superficial pattern matching to deeper cognitive alignment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to systematically investigate **how Vision-Language Models (VLMs) represent space**, specifically focusing on their ability to handle the inherent **ambiguities in spatial expressions** related to **Frames of Reference (FoR)** and their consistency across various conditions.

The key motivations driving this work are:

- **Unexplored Ambiguity in VLMs**: While VLM spatial reasoning has gained attention, the potential ambiguities arising from different Frames of Reference (FoR) are largely underexplored. Spatial language in situated communication is inherently ambiguous; a single expression can have multiple interpretations depending on the speaker's and listener's adopted FoR. This ambiguity is further complicated by diverse conventions across languages and cultures. Current benchmarks do not adequately account for this.
- **Cognitive Alignment**: Spatial cognition is a fundamental human capability. For VLMs to truly exhibit human-like intelligence and interact effectively in the real world, they must grasp the nuances of spatial reasoning, including its ambiguous nature and the flexibility humans demonstrate in adopting different FoRs. This research is motivated by a growing effort to align VLMs with human cognitive intuitions.
- **Robustness and Consistency Assessment**: Beyond simple accuracy, the authors aim to evaluate the robustness and consistency of VLM spatial understanding in continuous spatial environments and under minor variations (e.g., object color, size, distractors). This is crucial because real-world spatial reasoning is not limited to discrete, canonical positions.
- **Cross-Lingual and Cross-Cultural Understanding**: Human conventions for resolving spatial ambiguities are not uniform across languages and cultures. The paper seeks to investigate whether multilingual VLMs can faithfully adhere to these language-specific and culture-specific preferences or if they are dominated by conventions from high-resource languages (e.g., English).
- **Bridging the Evaluation Gap**: The existing benchmarks for VLM spatial reasoning are deemed insufficient because they overlook FoR-related ambiguities, lack spatial continuity in their test cases, and do not provide metrics to evaluate robustness and consistency rigorously. This work aims to fill these critical evaluation gaps.

In essence, the research is driven by the need for a more comprehensive and cognitively informed evaluation of VLM spatial understanding, moving beyond simple input-output correctness to explore the underlying representational mechanisms and their alignment with complex human spatial cognition across diverse linguistic and cultural contexts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

To systematically address its objectives, the paper introduces a novel evaluation protocol called **COMFORT (COnsistent Multilingual F rame O f Reference Test)**. This framework is designed to assess VLM spatial reasoning under ambiguity, emphasizing consistency and cross-lingual diversity.

#### 3.1. Scene Setup and Dataset Generation

The core of COMFORT lies in its synthetic 3D images, rendered using Blender, providing precise control over spatial relationships and perspectives. Each scene contains a *referent* and a *relatum*, with the referent moving along a rotational trajectory around the relatum. This design explicitly supports **spatial continuity**, allowing for fine-grained analysis of how models perceive regions of acceptability for spatial terms.

COMFORT is split into two configurations based on the relatum's properties:
- **COMFORT-BALL**: Used when the relatum is **non-fronted** (e.g., a blue ball with a red ball). This configuration focuses on ambiguity related to **coordinate transformation conventions** in relative Frames of Reference (FoR). It includes randomized variations in object colors, sizes, shapes, camera positions, and distractors to test robustness.
- **COMFORT-CAR**: Used when the relatum is **fronted** (e.g., a car, horse). This setup involves a referent, a fronted relatum, and an additional human addressee. It explicitly tests how models handle ambiguity between different **reference systems** (Camera-egocentric, Addressee-centered, Object-centered intrinsic FoRs), where each can lead to a different interpretation of the same spatial expression. The relatum and addressee orientations are varied to disentangle FoR effects.

#### 3.2. Language Query Setup

Queries are formulated as binary questions: "Is [A] [relation] [B]?", focusing on four canonical spatial relations: "to the left of," "to the right of," "in front of," and "behind." The crucial aspect is the inclusion of **perspective prompts** to either explicitly specify or implicitly test for FoR preferences:
- `nop` (no perspective): "Is [A] [relation] [B]?" (ambiguous FoR).
- `cam` (camera perspective): "From the camera’s viewpoint, is [A] [relation] [B]?" (egocentric relative FoR).
- `add` (addressee perspective): "From the [addressee]’s viewpoint, is [A] [relation] [B]?" (addressee-centered relative FoR).
- `rel` (relatum perspective): "From the [relatum]’s viewpoint, is [A] [relation] [B]?" (object-centered intrinsic FoR).

For the multilingual analysis, English prompts are translated into **109 languages** (out of 132 supported by Google Translate, after filtering for quality control via back-translation), enabling cross-cultural evaluation.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics

Beyond traditional accuracy, COMFORT introduces a sophisticated suite of metrics to assess VLM competence:
- **Accuracy**: Standard correct/incorrect classification based on whether the predicted probability of "Yes" is > 0.5 within the "acceptable region" (a 180-degree hemisphere centered on the canonical direction).
- **Region Parsing Error (ε)**: To address VLM biases (e.g., affirmative responses) and the continuous nature of space, this metric uses a *normalized acceptance probability* `bp_i`. It calculates the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between `bp_i` and two analytically defined reference probabilities:
 - `λ_hemi(θ)`: Hemisphere-based reference (1 if within 90° of canonical, 0 otherwise).
 - `λ_cos(θ)`: Cosine-based reference, mapping the cosine of the deviation angle to.
 - Lower ε indicates better alignment with spatial ground truth regions.
- **Robustness Metrics**:
 - **Standard Deviation (σ)**: Measures the consistency of `bp_i` across images with identical spatial relations but different scene variations (color, size, distractors). Lower σ indicates higher robustness.
 - **Prediction Noise (η)**: Measures the smoothness of the probability curve across deviation angles, using RMSE against a Butterworth Low Pass Filter. Lower η indicates smoother, less noisy predictions.
- **Consistency Metrics**:
 - **Spatial Symmetric Consistency (c_sym)**: Measures if predictions for symmetric deviation angles (θ and -θ for 'front') are similar. Ideal models should exhibit geometric symmetry.
 - **Spatial Opposite Consistency (c_opp)**: Measures if the probability of accepting "left" is complement to rejecting "right" (i.e., `bp_left + bp_right ≈ 1`). Ideal models should respect geometric opposition.

#### 3.4. Evaluated VLMs

The study evaluates a diverse set of nine state-of-the-art VLMs, including:
- Supervised instruction-tuned models: InstructBLIP, LLaVA-v1.5, InternLM-XComposer2.
- Models with reinforcement learning alignment: MiniCPM-Llama3-V.
- Mechanistically grounded VLMs: GLaMM.
- Multilingual VLMs: mBLIP-BLOOMZ and GPT-4o.

This comprehensive methodological approach, combining synthetic data generation with fine-grained control, diverse linguistic prompts, and novel cognitive-inspired metrics, enables a deep and nuanced analysis of VLM spatial reasoning capabilities and limitations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical experiments conducted using the COMFORT framework revealed several significant insights into how Vision-Language Models (VLMs) represent space and handle ambiguities:

#### 4.1. Preference for Reflected Coordinate Transformation

When evaluating the ambiguity of coordinate transformations within an egocentric relative FoR (using COMFORT-BALL with `cam` prompt), most VLMs (e.g., LLaVA, InternLM-XComposer2, MiniCPM-V, GPT-4o) exhibited a clear preference for the **reflected transformation convention**, which aligns with English linguistic conventions. This means they tend to reverse the front-back axis relative to the viewer but maintain the left-right axis. The BLIP series models were exceptions, showing no significant preference. Some models, like GPT-4o and LLaVA-1.5-13B, also showed sensitivity to multiple conventions at canonical angles, indicating some underlying uncertainty.

#### 4.2. Preference for Egocentric Relative Frame of Reference

When presented with ambiguous spatial expressions without an explicitly specified FoR (using COMFORT-CAR with `nop` prompt), almost all VLMs demonstrated a significant preference towards the **egocentric relative FoR**, consistent with English conventions. This preference was less pronounced or inconsistent across sagittal (front/behind) versus lateral (left/right) directions, suggesting uneven spatial understanding. Again, the BLIP series did not show a strong preference.

#### 4.3. Inflexibility in Adopting Alternative Frames of Reference

A critical finding was that VLMs struggle significantly when explicitly prompted to adopt alternative FoRs (intrinsic or addressee-centered relative FoRs). Despite clear instructions in the prompts (`rel` or `add`), models performed close to chance (around 50% accuracy) for these non-egocentric perspectives. While there were marginal improvements in region parsing error (ε), overall accuracy often decreased compared to ambiguous (nop) prompts. This suggests that current VLMs predominantly default to an egocentric perspective and lack the flexibility for robust perspective-taking, a crucial aspect of human spatial cognition and Theory of Mind.

#### 4.4. Lack of Robustness and Consistency

Despite achieving decent accuracy in some scenarios, VLMs generally exhibited a lack of robustness and consistency in their spatial representations:
- **Object Hallucination**: BLIP models suffered from severe object hallucination, which directly impacted their performance. While other models (e.g., GLaMM, LLaVA, GPT-4o) performed well on object grounding, this did not automatically translate to better spatial understanding.
- **High Standard Deviation (σ) and Prediction Noise (η)**: Models showed considerable variation in predictions for identical spatial relations under minor visual variations (e.g., object colors, sizes, distractors), indicating poor robustness. The probability curves were often noisy, rather than smooth.
- **Poor Consistency Metrics**: Spatial symmetric consistency (`c_sym`) and spatial opposite consistency (`c_opp`) were generally poor across models. For instance, GPT-4o's spatial opposite consistency was not significantly better than random trials, implying that its internal representations do not consistently capture the complementary nature of opposite spatial relations. This indicates that while models might get individual answers right, their underlying spatial understanding is not geometrically coherent or consistent.

#### 4.5. English Dominance in Cross-Lingual Evaluation

The cross-lingual evaluation with GPT-4o across 109 languages revealed that the English convention of reflected projection predominantly influenced the FoR preference conventions of other languages. Even for languages like Tamil and Hausa, which have human-preferred coordinate transformation conventions different from English, GPT-4o defaulted to the English-like interpretation. This raises significant concerns about an "English-centric" bias in multilingual VLMs, likely stemming from training data heavily reliant on machine-translated English captions.

In summary, the results demonstrate that while state-of-the-art VLMs possess some form of spatial representation, they primarily align with English-specific conventions, lack the flexibility to adapt to alternative perspectives, and exhibit significant shortcomings in robustness and internal consistency, especially across continuous spatial variations and geometric principles.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the fields of Artificial Intelligence, Vision-Language Models, and Cognitive Science, with several potential impacts:

#### 5.1. Deeper Understanding of VLM Spatial Capabilities

The COMFORT framework provides a nuanced lens through which to analyze VLM spatial understanding, moving beyond simple accuracy to probe the underlying representations. The findings confirm that VLMs do represent space, but predominantly in a way that aligns with English egocentric-relative FoR and reflected projection. This clarifies the "how" in "Do VLMs represent space and how?", highlighting both their learned conventions and their limitations.

#### 5.2. Highlighting Key Deficiencies for Future Research

The identified shortcomings—lack of flexibility in adopting alternative FoRs, and poor robustness and consistency in continuous space—point to critical areas for future VLM development.
- **Perspective Taking**: The struggle of VLMs with non-egocentric perspectives (intrinsic and addressee-centered) is a major limitation for achieving human-like communication and interaction. The ability to infer and reason from others' viewpoints is foundational to the Theory of Mind. This implies a need for training data that goes beyond typical screen-based egocentric views, potentially incorporating 3D data, multi-view data, or real-world embodied interaction scenarios that naturally present diverse perspectives.
- **Robustness and Consistency**: The observed noise and inconsistency in spatial predictions suggest that current VLM spatial representations are brittle. Future work should focus on developing models that maintain coherent and geometrically consistent spatial understanding across minor scene variations and across complementary spatial relations. This might involve integrating more explicit spatial reasoning modules, neuro-symbolic approaches, or geometrically informed regularization during training.

#### 5.3. Addressing Cross-Cultural and Linguistic Biases

The finding that English conventions dominate multilingual VLMs' spatial reasoning preferences is a critical wake-up call for the AI community. It underscores the pervasive issue of data bias in multilingual model training, where reliance on machine-translated data can erase or suppress the unique linguistic and cognitive conventions of other languages. This has broad implications beyond spatial reasoning, affecting fairness, cultural sensitivity, and the applicability of AI models in diverse global contexts. The paper advocates for the collection and utilization of more naturally generated multilingual and multimodal data to foster genuine cross-cultural understanding in AI.

#### 5.4. Advancing Grounded Linguistic Analysis

The research reinforces the growing trend of grounding linguistic analysis in rich, real-world modalities. It demonstrates that the meaning of language, especially ambiguous terms like spatial prepositions, is deeply enriched when coupled with visual context and an understanding of Frames of Reference. This work encourages future linguistic analysis in AI to move beyond text-only paradigms, emphasizing the importance of embodied and situated understanding for true language intelligence.

#### 5.5. New Evaluation Protocol for Cognitive AI

COMFORT itself is a valuable contribution. Its systematic design, synthetic data, continuous spatial variation, and sophisticated metrics (region parsing error, robustness, and consistency) provide a more rigorous and cognitively informed framework for evaluating VLM spatial understanding. It sets a new standard for diagnostic evaluation, encouraging researchers to look beyond superficial performance and delve into the underlying competence of AI models.

In conclusion, this paper significantly advances our understanding of VLM spatial reasoning by meticulously dissecting its strengths and, more importantly, its weaknesses when confronted with the complex and ambiguous nature of human spatial language. Its findings serve as a crucial roadmap for future research, urging the community to develop more robust, flexible, and culturally sensitive VLMs capable of genuinely human-like spatial cognition.
