# BrickCraft-Duo: Efficient Dual-Arm Skill Learning and Refinement for Compositional Long-Horizon Assembly

> [!summary] Summary
> BrickCraft-Duo is a modular dual-arm framework for long-horizon interlocking-brick assembly. It learns reusable single-arm (UniAssembly) and cooperative dual-arm (CoAssembly) diffusion-policy skills, shares demonstrations across arms and assembly-support roles via bilateral symmetry alignment, and composes them through stability-aware planning into a skill execution graph. With targeted human corrections it reaches at least 60% long-horizon success and 95% step completion across five real tasks up to nine steps.

## Problem

- Task-and-motion planning for assembly relies on predefined primitives, accurate geometric models and calibration, so it needs heavy task-specific engineering and breaks under perception errors.
- Monolithic end-to-end policies and vision-language-action models need extensive demonstrations for state coverage and lack explicit mechanisms enforcing physical constraints like structural stability during execution.
- Prior skill-composition work for brick assembly covers only single-arm settings, cannot hold partially supported or overhanging structures, and ignores combinatorial diversity across dual-arm role assignments.

## Method

- **CoAssembly**: a cooperative dual-arm ==diffusion policy== where one arm inserts while the other supports, selected by ==stability-aware planning== that models insertion load as a virtual mass and runs force-distribution analysis.
- ==Bilateral symmetry alignment==: mirror-reflects observations, states, forces and actions, and swaps assembly-support streams, so one shared policy covers both arms and both role configurations.
- ==Skill execution graph== compiler turns the planned attachment sequence into dependency-linked Transit/Pick/**UniAssembly**/**CoAssembly** nodes with pre- and post-conditions; human SpaceMouse corrections are aggregated for targeted policy fine-tuning.

## Results

- On five partially supported single-step tasks, UniAssembly failed all **20** trials through structure detachment, while CoAssembly reached **75%** success, failures being misalignment rather than collapse.
- Symmetry-aligned UniAssembly hit **85%** on both arms versus **60%** and **65%** for single-arm-only policies (**5-20%** cross-arm zero-shot); aligned CoAssembly gained **30 percentage points** in both role settings.
- Long-horizon success rose from **1/5**, **0/5** and **0/5** to **4/5**, **4/5** and **3/5** on Gate, Scale and Eagle after refinement, with completion rates above **95%** and relevant demos per step rising only from **8.2-13.0** to **10.8-17.1**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Geometric symmetry of a hardware setup is free data augmentation: aligning demonstrations into one canonical frame beats per-arm policies, even when the mirror symmetry is only approximate.
> - Zero-shot mirrored transfer degrades sharply, so symmetry helps as a data-pooling device far more than as an exact equivariance assumption; installation differences leave a real domain gap.
> - High per-step success still yields near-zero long-horizon success under strong sequential dependencies; a few targeted corrections at failing steps buy far more than broad retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Interlocking bricks are hard for a robot to assemble because tightly fitting parts must be pressed together. On a stack that is only partly supported, that force can deform or detach the existing structure. BrickCraft-Duo addresses this by having one arm insert the brick while the other steadies the structure when support is needed. In real-robot tests on insertions the planner predicted would be unstable without support, this cooperative approach succeeded where the single-arm version did not. The authors frame the system as extending their earlier BrickCraft from single-arm assembly to dual-arm assembly with support for partially supported structures.

### 2. When insertion needs a second arm

An insertion can destabilize a partly supported or overhanging structure. BrickCraft-Duo represents each step relative to a reference brick. It then searches for an assembly sequence that satisfies connectivity, visibility, geometric operability, and stability constraints. The stability estimate relies on specific assumptions:

- The single-arm feasibility test assumes a downward insertion load.
- That load is experimentally selected.
- The dual-arm test uses prescribed nominal gripper poses.

When the analysis predicts instability, the planner assigns the other arm to support the structure. Otherwise, the step can use one arm.

The system has two skills for these cases. UniAssembly attaches a brick with one arm. CoAssembly inserts with one arm and provides support with the other. In both skills, motion planning brings the robot to a coarse pre-assembly position. A learned policy then uses visual and proprioceptive feedback for local alignment and insertion.

The decisive test used five single-step tasks predicted to be unstable without support, with 20 trials per method and a 20 N assumed insertion load. UniAssembly failed in all trials, while CoAssembly succeeded in 75%. The remaining CoAssembly failures were mainly misalignment or incorrect placement, not structural collapse. This comparison supports the practical value of adding active support in those tested conditions. It does not show that support is the only cause of every failure, or that all partially supported assemblies will succeed.

### 3. From local skills to long assemblies

Each step changes the structure, and one failure can end a long assembly. BrickCraft-Duo turns the planner's ordered attachment steps into a directed skill graph. A single-arm step uses transit, pick, transit, and UniAssembly. A dual-arm step prepares the assembly and support arms before CoAssembly. Preconditions and postconditions gate execution. The graph permits parallel nodes, but the current system only runs Transit nodes in parallel.

Five real-world tasks tested this composition. Each had four to nine assembly steps and included at least one partly supported brick. Each task was tested over five trials after a human placed the initial anchor brick. The same skills were reused without task-specific policy training.

Two measures describe the results:

- **Long-horizon success** means completing every planned step autonomously and correctly, without human intervention.
- **Completion rate** is the fraction of planned steps completed successfully.

On the eight-step Gate task, the system initially succeeded in 1/5 trials, with an 85.0% completion rate. The authors then made targeted SpaceMouse corrections at underperforming steps and fine-tuned the policies. After this refinement, Gate succeeded in 4/5 trials, with 97.5% completion. Because corrections are human interventions, the refined score is not the original unassisted result. Other longer tasks also remained imperfect after refinement: Scale reached 4/5 and Eagle 3/5.

For Gate ($N=8$, one CoAssembly step), long-horizon success (L-SR) went from 1/5 to 4/5 and completion rate (CR) from 85.0% to 97.5%. Scale went from 0/5 to 4/5 and Eagle from 0/5 to 3/5. ### 4. Support changes the plan

In BrickCraft-Duo, the second arm is used only where it is needed. Stability reasoning makes support a step-specific choice, and the skill graph lets that choice coexist with reusable single-arm skills. The scope has clear limits. The robot uses predefined assembly modes and standardized Duplo brick geometries. The stability decision relies on a modeled insertion load and nominal poses.
