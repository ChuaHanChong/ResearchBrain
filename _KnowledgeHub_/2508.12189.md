# Self-Guided Action Diffusion

> [!summary] Summary
> Self-Guided Action Diffusion (Self-GAD) is an efficient test-time inference method for diffusion-based robot policies that enhances performance in diverse and dynamic environments by guiding action generation with prior predictions. The approach achieves near-optimal success rates with fewer samples, demonstrating up to 71.4% higher success than random sampling and improving performance in foundation models like GR00T-N1 by up to 48.2% on RoboCasa tasks.

## Problem

- Robot learning from large-scale human demonstrations faces challenges due to inherent behavioral diversity and complex, long-range action dependencies.
- Existing methods for addressing temporal consistency, like bidirectional decoding, are computationally expensive, hindering real-time robotic deployment.
- Diffusion-based policies, while capable of multimodal action generation, struggle to produce smooth and temporally consistent trajectories in single-sample settings.

## Method

- **Self-Guided** ==Action Diffusion (Self-GAD)== modifies diffusion policy inference by integrating a gradient-based guidance mechanism that leverages prior action predictions.
- A trajectory deviation loss is defined to steer generated actions towards previously predicted trajectories, particularly in overlapping action horizons.
- This guidance mechanism is implemented as a weighted gradient update during the denoising process, controlled by a tuneable guidance weight (beta).

## Results

- Self-GAD achieved an average success rate **71.4%** higher than random sampling and maintained near-optimal performance with substantially fewer samples.
- The method demonstrated enhanced robustness in dynamic environments, improving success rates by up to **26.5%** with moving targets and by **14.3%** in high-variance demonstration datasets.
- When integrated into the GR00T-N1-2B foundation model, Self-GAD improved success rates by up to **48.2%** on RoboCasa and **17.2%** on DexMimicGen benchmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging prior action predictions to guide the diffusion process at inference time enhances temporal consistency and reactivity in robot policies.
> - Optimizing proposal distributions through gradient-based guidance improves sample efficiency, allowing for robust performance with significantly fewer samples than traditional search methods.
> - The guidance mechanism is effective in managing behavioral diversity and complex action dependencies in imitation learning from large datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot learning, specifically imitation learning from large-scale human demonstrations. A central challenge in this domain is managing the inherent behavioral diversity within demonstration datasets and addressing complex action dependencies that span multiple time steps. Existing approaches often model the distribution of action chunks to capture temporal dependencies, but they frequently encounter difficulties in maintaining consistency across these chunks when dependencies extend further.

Previous work, such as that by Liu et al., introduced test-time search techniques like bidirectional decoding to address cross-chunk dependencies, showing effectiveness in improving the consistency and reactivity of generative robot policies, particularly those based on diffusion models. However, a significant limitation of these methods is their computational expense, which increases proportionally with the diversity of sampled actions. Diffusion-based policies, while capable of capturing multimodal action distributions, have also been noted to struggle with generating smooth, temporally consistent trajectories in single-sample and single-action-horizon settings, which are crucial for closed-loop stability in robotic control.

The broader landscape also includes various policy steering techniques applied during inference time. These methods aim to adapt robotic policies to task objectives, such as aligning samples with user intent, preserving data manifold constraints, re-ranking actions using value functions, or guiding generation via gradient-based optimization in diffusion models. These approaches highlight the utility of dynamically adjusting policies during execution to mitigate distribution shifts or enhance specific performance metrics.

This paper positions itself as an advancement in this context by proposing a more efficient test-time inference method, Self-Guided Action Diffusion (Self-GAD), specifically for diffusion-based policies. It aims to overcome the computational inefficiency of prior test-time search strategies like bidirectional decoding while retaining or improving their performance, especially in scenarios characterized by high demonstration diversity or dynamic environments. By intervening in the proposal distribution through a guided diffusion objective, Self-GAD seeks to balance exploration and exploitation, offering a method to enhance temporal consistency and reactivity without incurring the high computational costs associated with extensive sampling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Self-Guided Action Diffusion (Self-GAD), an efficient test-time inference method designed to improve the performance of diffusion-based robot policies. This method aims to address the computational inefficiencies associated with existing inference-time search techniques while maintaining or enhancing policy performance.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and observations in contemporary robot imitation learning:

1. **Challenges with Large-Scale Demonstrations:** As the volume of human demonstrations used for imitation learning increases, two principal difficulties arise:
 - **Behavioral Diversity:** Large datasets often exhibit significant variations in demonstrated behaviors, making it complex for policies to learn a consistent, generalizable strategy.
 - **Complex Action Dependencies:** Robot tasks often involve action sequences where decisions at one time step critically influence future actions, leading to dependencies that span multiple time steps.
2. **Limitations of Existing Approaches for Temporal Consistency:**
 - While methods that model action chunks help capture temporal dependencies within those chunks, maintaining consistency across chunk boundaries remains a challenge.
 - Recent advancements, such as bidirectional decoding proposed by Liu et al., have demonstrated effectiveness in addressing these cross-chunk dependencies through test-time search, thereby improving consistency and reactivity. However, this strategy is computationally expensive, with its inefficiency increasing as the diversity of sampled actions grows. This cost renders such methods impractical for real-time deployment in many robotic applications.
 - Diffusion-based policies, despite their ability to capture multimodal action distributions, often struggle to generate smooth and temporally consistent trajectories in single-sample or single-action-horizon settings, which are critical for stable closed-loop robot control.
3. **Need for Efficient and Robust Inference:** There is a clear need for inference-time methods that can enhance policy performance by promoting temporal coherence and reactivity, especially in dynamic environments or with diverse training data, without incurring prohibitive computational costs. The authors hypothesize that leveraging prior decision-making to guide the proposal distribution during diffusion can achieve this balance, leading to improved sample efficiency and robustness.

In essence, the research is motivated by the desire to enable robot policies to leverage the benefits of generative models like diffusion policies, particularly their capacity to handle diverse behaviors and temporal dependencies, while simultaneously making their inference process more computationally tractable and robust to varying environmental conditions and data characteristics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented involves developing Self-Guided Action Diffusion (Self-GAD), which modifies the inference process of diffusion-based policies using a gradient-based guidance mechanism.

**Problem Formulation:**
The work considers an imitation learning setup where a robot learns from a dataset of expert demonstrations $D = \{\tau^i\}_{i=1}^N$. Each trajectory $\tau^i$ comprises state-action pairs $(s_t, a_t)$. The action $a_t$ is influenced by the observed state $s_t$ and latent variables $z_t$. The policy learns to predict a chunk of future actions $a_{t:t+l}$ conditioned on a context of past states $s_{t-c:t}$, denoted as $\pi(a_{t:t+l} | s_{t-c:t})$. The policy is trained to minimize the divergence between its predictions and the expert policy $\pi^*$. During deployment, only the first $h$ steps of the predicted action sequence are executed, forming a $(c, h)$-policy.

**Denoising Diffusion Models Background:**
The core of the generative model is a denoising diffusion process, which aims to generate samples from a data distribution $p_{data}(s, a)$. This is achieved by iteratively denoising a sample of pure white noise. The process involves gradually diffusing $p_{data}(s, a)$ into a sequence of smoothed densities $p(s, a; \sigma)$ where $\sigma$ represents the noise level. For large $\sigma$, the distribution approximates a Gaussian. Samples are then generated by reversing this process, evolving backward from a high-noise distribution to lower noise levels using a probability flow Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE). The key component for this reverse process is the score function, $\nabla_{x_{t:t+l}} \log p(x_{t:t+l}; \sigma)$, which is approximated by a neural network $D_\theta(x_{t:t+l}; \sigma)$ trained to denoise noisy inputs.

**Self-Guided Action Diffusion (Self-GAD):**
The central hypothesis is that integrating prior reusability estimates can improve decoding strategies by optimizing proposal distributions and enhancing sample efficiency. Self-GAD intervenes in the proposal distribution at each diffusion step by guiding the generation process based on prior action predictions.

1. **Guidance Mechanism:** The method leverages gradients from a policy's likelihood function to steer generated actions towards previous action predictions (the "prior").
2. **Trajectory Deviation Loss:** A loss function $L$ is defined to penalize deviations between the currently predicted trajectory ($\hat{a}_i$) and the prior trajectory ($a_{prior,i}$). This loss is applied to the overlapping region between the generated trajectory and the prior, specifically for actions from $i=t+h$ to $t+l$, where $h$ is the number of executed steps and $l$ is the prediction length.
 - $L = \sum_{i=t+h}^{t+l} w_i \cdot \|\hat{a}_i - a_{prior,i}\|^2_2$
 - Exponentially decaying weights $w_i = 0.5^{i-(t+h)}$ are used, giving more importance to actions closer to the execution horizon.
3. **Weighted Gradient Update:** A weighted gradient update is applied to the predicted states and actions $(\hat{s}_t, \hat{a}_t)$.
 - $(\hat{s}_t, \hat{a}_t) \leftarrow (\hat{s}_t, \hat{a}_t) + \beta \nabla_{(\hat{s}_t, \hat{a}_t)} L$
 - The guidance weight $\beta$ modulates the influence of the prior and is tuned via grid search to balance adherence to prior trajectories with flexibility for adaptation.
4. **Integration with Foundation Models:** Self-GAD is designed as a plug-in guidance method, compatible with existing diffusion flow-matching architectures and robotic foundation models. The paper specifically demonstrates its integration with GR00T-N1, a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) model. In this setup, denoising tokens are conditioned on proprioceptive state and action history and cross-attended with multimodal visual and textual embeddings from a vision-language model (Eagle-2 VLM) to predict denoised motor actions.

**Experimental Design:**
The experiments were structured to evaluate Self-GAD across various inference settings, addressing specific research questions:
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against random sampling (equivalent to a single random draw in coherence sampling) and coherence sampling.
- **Single-Sample Closed-Loop Settings:** Evaluation on Robomimic benchmark tasks (BlockPush, Franka Kitchen, Lift, PushT) to assess baseline performance.
- **Sample Efficiency:** Investigation of the impact of varying sample counts ({1, 4, 8, 12, 16}) on Self-GAD versus Coherence Sampling, particularly on the PushT task.
- **Robustness to Unseen Dynamics:** Introduction of environmental perturbations in the PushT task, where a target object moves with constant velocity ({0, 1, 1.5}) in the (x, y) plane. Performance was evaluated across different action horizons ({1, 4, 8, 16}) to test adaptability in dynamic settings.
- **Robustness to Dataset Variability:** Evaluation on the RoboMimic pick-and-place square task, using datasets with incrementally varying trajectories (low, medium, high variance) introduced through perturbations in object positions, grasp offsets, and peg/nut placements.
- **Foundation Model Enhancement:** Integration and evaluation of Self-GAD within the GR00T-N1-2B robotic foundation model, fine-tuned on tasks from the RoboCasa benchmark and DexMimicGen Cross-Embodiment Suite.

Across all experiments, success rate was the primary performance metric. The guidance weight $\beta$ was manually tuned for optimal performance in each setting through grid search.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted provided several key findings regarding the performance and characteristics of Self-Guided Action Diffusion (Self-GAD).

**A. Self-GAD Outperforms Baseline (Single-Sample Settings):**
In closed-loop, single-sample inference settings across various Robomimic benchmark tasks (BlockPush, Franka Kitchen, Lift, and PushT), Self-GAD consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to random sampling. With an optimally tuned guidance weight $\beta$, Self-GAD achieved an average success rate 71.4% higher than random sampling across all tested benchmarks. This indicates that even with a single action sample, the proposed guidance mechanism significantly enhances policy efficacy.

**B. Sample Efficiency of Self-GAD:**
Self-GAD exhibited notable sample efficiency when compared to Coherence Sampling. While Coherence Sampling required up to 16 samples to achieve comparable success rates, Self-GAD attained near-optimal performance with significantly fewer samples, including a single sample. For instance, in the PushT task, Self-GAD's single-sample performance was maintained even up to 16 samples, demonstrating that it can achieve robust performance with substantially reduced computational overhead, leading to faster inference. Policies operating with fewer samples were observed to be particularly sensitive to overly constrained guidance weights.

**C. Robustness to Unseen Dynamics:**
Self-GAD demonstrated enhanced robustness in temporally noisy and dynamic environments. When tested on the PushT task with a dynamically moving target object (speeds of 0, 1, and 1.5), Self-GAD, particularly in a single-action-horizon setting, proved effective in enabling closed-loop execution with guided benefits. The performance gains were more pronounced in environments with stronger dynamic shifts:
- At a target object speed of 1.5, Self-GAD achieved a 26.5% performance improvement.
- In the static PushT setting (speed 0.0), performance improved by 9%.
In contrast, vanilla coherence sampling struggled with dynamic movements and diverse demonstrations, showing significantly lower success rates until longer action horizons (e.g., action horizon 8), where strategy consistency became more critical. At the longest horizon (16), Self-GAD and unguided Coherence Sampling achieved comparable performance.

**D. Robustness to Dataset Variability:**
The study investigated Self-GAD's ability to adapt to varying levels of dataset variability and scripted multimodality in the RoboMimic pick-and-place square task. As the complexity of learned trajectories increased due to higher dataset variance (introduced by perturbations in object positions, grasp offsets, etc.), Self-GAD's advantage amplified:
- It improved performance by 6.4% in low-variance settings.
- It improved performance by 11.2% in medium-variance settings.
- It improved performance by 14.3% in high-variance settings.
This indicates that the guided consistency offered by Self-GAD becomes increasingly significant in datasets exhibiting greater diversity.

**E. Self-GAD Enhanced Robotic Foundation Models:**
Self-GAD was integrated as a plug-in guidance method into GR00T-N1-2B, a large-scale robotic foundation model. This integration demonstrated compatibility with generalized frameworks and yielded improved performance on fine-tuned tasks across two benchmarks:
- **RoboCasa:** Self-GAD boosted success rates of vanilla diffusion by 48.2% and improved the fine-tuned GR00T-N1 by 28.4% in task success.
- **DexMimicGen (DexMG):** Self-GAD improved vanilla diffusion success rates by 17.2% and the fine-tuned GR00T-N1 by 12%.
Across both benchmarks, Self-GAD consistently outperformed the vanilla diffusion and GR00T-N1 baselines, demonstrating enhanced sample efficiency and robustness even under limited data when integrated into foundation models.

Overall, the findings indicate that Self-GAD enables near-optimal performance with significantly reduced sample requirements, particularly excelling in dynamic environments and when dealing with diverse or variable demonstration data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Self-Guided Action Diffusion (Self-GAD) carries several significances and potential impacts within the field of robot learning and control:

1. **Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** Self-GAD addresses a critical bottleneck in generative robot policies: the computational expense of inference-time search methods like bidirectional decoding. By achieving near-optimal performance with significantly fewer samples (even a single sample), Self-GAD makes these advanced policy improvements more computationally tractable and suitable for real-time robotic applications where rapid decision-making is essential. This can lead to faster policy deployment and reduced energy consumption during operation.
2. **Improved Robustness in Dynamic Environments:** The demonstrated ability of Self-GAD to perform effectively in environments with unseen dynamics, such as moving target objects, is significant. This enhanced robustness is crucial for deploying robots in real-world scenarios, which are inherently dynamic and unpredictable. The method's capacity to maintain closed-loop adaptability and consistency under perturbations makes robot policies more reliable.
3. **Better Handling of Dataset Variability:** Imitation learning from large, diverse human demonstration datasets is prone to challenges stemming from behavioral variations. Self-GAD's ability to enhance consistency and performance with increasing dataset variability suggests a practical solution for training more generalizable robot policies from complex and multimodal demonstrations. This could lead to robots that can operate effectively even when trained on less uniform or meticulously curated data.
4. **Generalizability Across Models:** The successful integration of Self-GAD as a plug-in guidance method into a large-scale robotic foundation model like GR00T-N1 highlights its broad applicability. This suggests that Self-GAD is not limited to specific diffusion policy implementations but can be used to enhance various state-of-the-art robot learning architectures, potentially accelerating the development of more capable generalist robots.
5. **Advancement in Inference-Time Policy Adaptation:** Self-GAD represents an advancement in inference-time policy steering. By dynamically adjusting the proposal distribution based on prior knowledge, it offers a refined approach to balancing exploration and exploitation during action generation. This contributes to the broader research area of making policies more reactive and adaptive without retraining.
6. **Future Development of Adaptive Control:** The discussion about the constraint of manual tuning of the guidance weight ($\beta$) and the prospect of developing adaptive, on-the-fly tuning mechanisms indicates a clear path for future research. If successful, such adaptive mechanisms could unlock even greater performance and efficiency in highly dynamic and non-uniform environments, potentially leading to more autonomous and intelligent robotic systems that can self-optimize their control strategies.

In summary, Self-GAD offers a practical and effective method for improving the performance, efficiency, and robustness of diffusion-based robot policies, especially relevant for the emerging generation of generalist robots learning from vast and diverse datasets.
