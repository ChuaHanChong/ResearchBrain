# COLLAGE: Adaptive Fusion-based Retrieval for Augmented Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called COLLAGE, developed at The University of Texas at Austin, enhances few-shot robotic imitation learning by adaptively fusing demonstration data retrieved using multiple modalities. This approach yielded an 11.2% relative performance increase in simulation and a 57.4% relative improvement in real-world tasks compared to single-modality retrieval methods.

## Problem

- Robotic imitation learning requires extensive task-specific demonstration data, which is often impractical to collect for novel tasks in real-world environments.
- Generalist multi-task policies trained on large, diverse datasets can suffer from 'negative transfer,' where irrelevant demonstrations degrade performance on specific tasks.
- Prior retrieval-augmented methods rely on single-modality similarity measures (e.g., visual, motion, language), which can be brittle and suboptimal as different tasks benefit from distinct types of information.

## Method

- **COLLAGE** performs ==retrieval== across multiple modalities (visual, motion, shape, language) to gather distinct subsets of relevant trajectories from a large prior dataset.
- It estimates an ==importance weight== for each modality-specific retrieved dataset by training a lightweight reference policy and evaluating its log-likelihood on the small set of target demonstrations.
- A language-conditioned policy is then trained via ==behavior cloning==, sampling from the combined target and retrieved datasets with a bias determined by the adaptive importance weights.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **50.5%** on the LIBERO-10 benchmark in simulation, an **11.2%** relative improvement over the best single-modality baseline (STRAP/Visual).
- Demonstrated an average success rate of **45.5%** in real-world tasks using a FRANKA Panda robot and the DROID dataset, representing a **57.4%** relative improvement over the best single-modality baseline.
- Ablation studies confirmed that adaptive weighting in **COLLAGE** outperformed non-adaptive uniform fusion (NA-Fusion) in both simulation (**50.5%** vs. **46.9%**) and real-world experiments, validating the benefit of task-specific relevance estimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - No single modality is universally optimal for retrieving helpful demonstrations; different tasks benefit from distinct types of similarity (e.g., visual for 'open book', motion for 'stir bowl').
> - Adaptively fusing demonstrations retrieved from multiple modalities significantly improves few-shot imitation learning performance by leveraging complementary information.
> - Estimating the task-training relevance of each retrieved modality using a lightweight policy's performance on target demonstrations provides an effective, data-driven importance weighting mechanism.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic imitation learning (IL) focuses on training robot policies by observing expert demonstrations. Recent advancements, particularly with transformer and diffusion models, have demonstrated high performance in complex manipulation tasks when extensive task-specific demonstration data is available. However, a significant challenge in real-world environments is the impracticality of collecting sufficient demonstrations for every novel task or variation.

To address this data scarcity, two primary research directions have emerged:
1. **Generalist Multi-task Policies:** Training policies on large, diverse datasets (e.g., DROID, Open X-Embodiment) that encompass hundreds of tasks and environments. The aim is for these policies to generalize to unseen tasks with minimal adaptation. However, these policies often exhibit "negative transfer," where irrelevant demonstrations degrade performance on specific tasks compared to task-specific expert policies.
2. **Retrieval-Augmented Few-Shot Imitation Learning:** Augmenting a small set of task-specific "target" demonstrations with relevant data retrieved from large pre-existing datasets. This approach aims to provide sufficient training data for a particular task without requiring extensive new data collection.

Prior methods in retrieval-augmented learning typically rely on a single-modality similarity measure (e.g., visual features, optical flow, or language embeddings) to select relevant trajectories. These methods assume that the most helpful trajectories are those most similar in a specific feature space. The paper identifies a limitation in this approach: single-modality heuristics can be brittle and fail in specific cases, as different tasks may benefit from different types of similarity (e.g., visual similarity for "Open the book" vs. motion similarity for "Stir the bowl"). This work aims to overcome the limitations of single-modality retrieval by developing a strategy to combine data retrieved using multiple similarity measures.

COLLAGE is positioned as a method that generalizes prior single-modality retrieval techniques and relates to research in multi-modal representation learning, particularly late fusion methods. While other multi-modal approaches exist, they often require manual tuning or fixed sequences of filtering. COLLAGE distinguishes itself by proposing a data-driven, feature-agnostic adaptive weighting of retrieved subsets, drawing parallels to importance-based data selection and importance sampling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for adaptive, multi-modal data retrieval that improves few-shot imitation learning performance. Specifically, the authors aim to address the limitations of existing retrieval-augmented methods that rely on single-feature distance heuristics.

The motivation stems from the observation that different types of similarity (e.g., visual, semantic, motion) capture distinct aspects of task relevance, and relying on only one can lead to suboptimal or "harmful" retrieval. Harmful demonstrations might include visually similar but semantically unrelated tasks, or motions from tasks with divergent goals. For example, "opening a book" might be visually distinct from "stirring a bowl," where motion is more indicative of the action.

The central question motivating COLLAGE is: "Can we design a strategy to automatically combine data retrieved using different similarity measures for higher performance in retrieval-augmented few-shot policy learning?" The authors propose that such a strategy should synergistically aggregate subsets of data preselected by various single-modality heuristics, assigning weights based on their estimated task-training relevance. This adaptive fusion mechanism is intended to guide the selection of relevant demonstrations by leveraging a task-specific combination of multiple cues, thereby enhancing the robustness and effectiveness of augmented policy learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

COLLAGE (COLLective data AGgrEgation) is designed as an adaptive late fusion mechanism for few-shot imitation learning. It operates in three main stages: retrieving modality-specific subsets, estimating their task relevance, and using these relevance scores for importance sampling during policy training.

**Problem Formulation:** The work considers a few-shot imitation learning setting where a policy needs to be learned from a small target dataset ($D_{target}$) of $n$ expert trajectories, each consisting of state-action pairs and a task instruction. To overcome the limited size of $D_{target}$, a large offline prior dataset ($D_{prior}$) with $m \gg n$ trajectories is available to support augmented policy learning. The goal is to train an augmented policy ($\pi_{aug}$) that outperforms one trained solely on $D_{target}$ by effectively combining $D_{target}$ with a relevant subset of $D_{prior}$. Observations ($o$) are multi-modal, including RGB images ($I$), depth maps ($d$), and robot state ($r$). The approach assumes access to $k$ feature encoders ($F_1, \ldots, F_k$), each corresponding to a different modality (e.g., vision, motion, shape, language).

**Overview of COLLAGE:**
1. **Retrieval Across Multiple Modalities:** For each feature encoder $F_i$, subsets of relevant trajectories ($D_{f_1}^{retrieved}, \ldots, D_{f_k}^{retrieved}$) are retrieved from $D_{prior}$ by measuring similarity to demonstrations in $D_{target}$.
2. **Estimating Weights for Retrieved Datasets:** An importance weight ($w_f$) is estimated for each retrieved subset based on its task-training relevance.
3. **Retrieval Augmented Policy Learning:** A language-conditioned policy is trained using the union of $D_{target}$ and all retrieved datasets, where trajectories are sampled according to the estimated importance weights ($w_f$).

#### 3.1. Retrieval Across Multiple Modalities

The method primarily adopts sub-trajectory level data retrieval, inspired by prior work, to allow for more flexible and semantically meaningful matching.
- **Sub-trajectory based Data Retrieval:** Target demonstrations in $D_{target}$ are first segmented into sub-trajectories using an action-based heuristic (end-effector velocity magnitude). For each feature modality $F_i$, retrieval is performed independently. Given a target sub-trajectory $t'$ and a prior trajectory $t$, a pairwise cost matrix is computed based on the squared Euclidean distance between features ($C_{ij} = \|F_i(O_i) - F_i(O_j)\|^2$). Subsequence Dynamic Time Warping (S-DTW) is then applied to align $t'$ with contiguous sub-sequences of $t$. For each target sub-trajectory, the top-K lowest-cost matches from $D_{prior}$ are retrieved. This process results in modality-specific retrieval datasets, each sub-trajectory inheriting its corresponding language instruction.
- **Feature Modalities:** COLLAGE instantiates four feature modalities:
 - **Visual Similarity:** Uses DINOv2 to extract features from RGB images ($F_v(s_i) = DINO(I_i)$).
 - **Motion Similarity:** Uses Optical Flow (specifically GMFlow) between consecutive images. A custom encoder-decoder model is pretrained on $D_{prior}$ to reduce dimensionality and learn a compact representation ($F_m(s_i) = p_\theta(o_i)$).
 - **Shape Similarity:** Converts depth maps into point clouds (after background segmentation) and processes them through a pretrained PointNet++ model to compute a 3D geometric feature ($F_p(s_i) = PointNet(p_i)$).
 - **Language Similarity:** Uses OpenAI embeddings to encode the task instruction associated with each demonstration ($F_l(l_d) = OpenAIEmbed(l_d)$). This is applied at the demonstration level, retrieving demonstrations with instruction similarity above a threshold.

#### 3.2. Estimating the Weights for Retrieved Datasets

To address the suboptimality of uniform merging, an adaptive weighting strategy is proposed. For each modality $f$:
1. A lightweight behavior cloning (BC) policy ($\pi_{ref_f}$) is trained using only the modality-specific retrieved data ($D_{f}^{retrieved}$).
2. The relevance of each modality is evaluated by computing the log-likelihood of the target demonstrations under the corresponding reference policy: $S_f = \sum_{(s,a,\ell)\in D_{target}} \log \pi_{ref_f}(a | s, \ell)$. This score indicates how well the reference policy, trained on data from modality $f$, explains the target task.
3. These scores are normalized using a softmax function to produce modality weights: $w_f = \frac{\exp(S_f/\tau)}{\sum_{f'} \exp(S_{f'}/\tau)}$. A higher weight implies greater task relevance, akin to importance weights in importance sampling.

#### 3.3. Retrieval Augmented Policy Learning

A language-conditioned visuomotor policy ($\pi$) is trained using behavior cloning. The policy is a transformer-based model, taking past observations and a task instruction as input to predict future actions using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) head. The training objective is a multi-step prediction loss with $L_2$ regularization.
- **Sampling Strategy:** For each modality $f$, an augmented dataset ($D_f^{aug} = D_f^{retrieved} \cup D_{target}$) is formed. During policy training, examples are sampled from these augmented datasets using the estimated importance weights $w_f$. This biases training batches towards examples retrieved by modalities deemed more useful for the target task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of COLLAGE was conducted in both simulated and real-world environments, comparing it against non-retrieval, single-modality retrieval, and non-adaptive fusion baselines.

#### 4.1. Simulated Experiments (LIBERO-10 Benchmark)

- **Dataset:** $D_{target}$ consisted of 5 demonstrations from each of 10 tasks in LIBERO-10. $D_{prior}$ comprised 4500 demonstrations from LIBERO-90.
- **Overall Performance:** COLLAGE achieved an average success rate of 50.5% across 10 tasks, representing an 11.2% relative improvement over the best single-modality baseline (STRAP/Visual, 45.6%) and a 41.1% relative improvement over multi-task learning (MT, 35.8%).
- **Single-Modality Limitations:** While individual modalities performed well on some tasks (e.g., Visual on "Bowl-Cabinet," Language on "Stove-Moka"), they often struggled with others. For instance, Visual retrieval underperformed on tasks with significant appearance changes, while Motion-based retrieval often ignored critical semantic information.
- **Benefit of Multi-modality:** COLLAGE showed notable improvements on tasks where single modalities were insufficient. For the "Book" task, COLLAGE improved by 23.3% over Visual-modality-alone retrieval, by leveraging complementary information from Motion and Language.
- **Adaptive Weighting:** The ablation study comparing COLLAGE (adaptive weighting) with NA-Fusion (non-adaptive, uniform sampling) demonstrated COLLAGE's superiority, with a 50.5% average success rate versus 46.9% for NA-Fusion. This indicates that adaptively weighing the modalities is beneficial.
- **Impact of Retrieval Size:** An experiment comparing COLLAGE with a single-modality (DINO) retrieval approach using an identical total number of retrieved samples (K=400) showed COLLAGE still outperformed the DINO-only setup (50.5% vs. 46.9%), indicating that combining diverse modalities provides benefits beyond simply increasing the quantity of retrieved examples.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments (DROID Dataset)

- **Dataset:** A FRANKA Panda robot was used for real-world tasks. $D_{prior}$ was a set of 30k successful episodes from the DROID dataset, which presented a significant visual domain shift from the target setup. 5 demonstrations were collected for each of 6 real-world tasks (e.g., Pen-in-Cup, Scrub-Plate).
- **Overall Performance:** COLLAGE achieved an average success rate of 45.5%, representing a 57.4% relative improvement over the best single-modality baseline (Visual, 28.9%). The BC baseline (training only on D_{target}) performed poorly (6.7%), highlighting the difficulty of few-shot learning in this challenging real-world setting.
- **Robustness to Variations:** The method demonstrated robustness to real-world variations such as lighting and object pose. It successfully retrieved meaningful and helpful demonstrations from DROID despite the visual differences.
- **Complementary Behaviors:** COLLAGE outperformed individual modalities by combining complementary behaviors. For example, on the "Spatula-Stir" task, COLLAGE achieved 9/15 successes compared to Language's 5/15.
- **Adaptive Weighting in Real-World:** The importance weights predicted by COLLAGE for real-world tasks correctly down-weighted less informative modalities (e.g., Motion for "Lego" and Shape for "Pen-in-Cup"), mirroring the findings from the simulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

COLLAGE represents a contribution to the field of robotic imitation learning, particularly in few-shot settings. Its main significance lies in providing a robust and flexible framework for data retrieval that overcomes the limitations of single-modality approaches.

**Key Contributions:**
- **Adaptive Multi-modal Fusion:** COLLAGE introduces an adaptive late fusion mechanism that intelligently combines data retrieved from multiple similarity measures (visual, motion, shape, language). This moves beyond fixed or manually tuned retrieval pipelines, offering a data-driven approach to leverage complementary information.
- **Improved Few-Shot Performance:** The method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art retrieval and multi-task learning baselines in both simulation (11.2% relative improvement) and real-world scenarios (57.4% relative improvement), demonstrating its effectiveness in augmenting limited target demonstrations.
- **Robustness to Domain Shift:** By successfully retrieving and leveraging data from large, diverse offline datasets like DROID, COLLAGE shows promise in addressing real-world challenges such as substantial visual and task domain shifts without requiring manual curation. This is particularly impactful for deployment in unstructured environments.
- **Feature-Agnostic and Scalable:** The design of COLLAGE is agnostic to the specific type of retrieval features, allowing for flexible integration of any number or type of modalities. Its adaptive weighting mechanism automatically identifies and prioritizes the most useful retrievals for a given task, enhancing scalability.

**Potential Impact:**
The ability of COLLAGE to effectively leverage diverse data sources for few-shot learning could significantly reduce the data collection burden in robotics. This could accelerate the deployment of intelligent robotic systems in dynamic environments such as homes, where rapid adaptation to new tasks is essential. It contributes to making generalist robots more practical by improving their performance on specific tasks when only a few demonstrations are available.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge a few practical limitations. The performance of COLLAGE is ultimately dependent on the quality of the retrieved data; if all modalities fail to identify relevant examples, performance gains will be limited, though this was found to be rare in experiments. Additionally, training a lightweight reference policy for each modality introduces computational overhead.

Future research directions include exploring more efficient ways to approximate these reference policies or developing proxy metrics that can predict the utility of retrieved data without full policy training. This would further enhance the method's efficiency and applicability. The release of the code will facilitate further research and development in this area.
