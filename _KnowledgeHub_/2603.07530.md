# ICLR: In-Context Imitation Learning with Visual Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The ICLR framework advances robotic in-context imitation learning by integrating embodied visual reasoning, training a transformer to jointly predict visual reasoning traces and low-level actions. This approach leads to improved task generalization and robustness in complex and ambiguous environments, consistently outperforming existing baselines in both simulated and real-world manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation policies struggle to generalize from limited demonstrations, often requiring extensive and costly data collection for new tasks.
- Existing in-context imitation learning methods rely heavily on low-level state-action conditioning, which is insufficient for inferring high-level task intent in complex or ambiguous environments where multiple objectives might align with similar actions.
- The absence of explicit reasoning about task objectives hinders generalization to novel tasks or unseen object configurations and limits the interpretability of a robot's decision-making process.

## Method

- The **ICLR (In-Context Imitation Learning with Visual Reasoning)** framework augments robot trajectories with structured ==visual reasoning traces==, defined as polylines representing anticipated gripper pixel positions in camera images.
- A ==Llama2-style causal transformer== architecture is employed to jointly and autoregressively predict both the visual reasoning traces and corresponding low-level robot actions within a unified sequence.
- The model is trained with a combined ==L1 loss== and a ==reasoning trace masking== regularization technique to improve robustness, enabling iterative prediction of traces and actions during inference in a closed-loop manner.

## Results

- In simulation experiments on the LIBERO benchmark, the ICLR 'Dropout' model achieved the highest average success rates, with the full 'Ours' model consistently ranking second, both outperforming the ICRT baseline across various task suites.
- Real-world robot experiments across **12** unseen manipulation tasks demonstrated that the full 'Ours' ICLR model largely outperformed all baselines, achieving higher average success rates on both poking and pick-and-place tasks with novel object configurations.
- Ablation studies indicated that efficient reasoning intervals (e.g., generating traces every **8** or **16** steps) could achieve performance comparable to full reasoning with significantly reduced computational cost (up to **8x** faster inference), highlighting a practical trade-off for deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating explicit embodied visual reasoning, represented as anticipated gripper trajectories, enables robots to infer higher-level task intent, moving beyond direct state-action mapping and enhancing generalization.
> - A causal transformer can effectively learn to jointly generate both visual reasoning traces and low-level actions, leading to more robust and adaptable policies in novel and diverse scenarios.
> - Structured intermediate reasoning, akin to chain-of-thought prompting, provides crucial guidance for robot action prediction and increases the interpretability of the robot's decision-making process, aiding in understanding task failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a prominent challenge in robotics: data scarcity. Traditional robot learning methods often require extensive datasets, which are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and potentially unsafe to collect in real-world scenarios. This has motivated the development of approaches that enable robots to acquire new skills from a limited number of demonstrations.

In-context imitation learning has emerged as a promising paradigm in this context. Existing methods typically train a model to condition its behavior on a few "prompt" demonstrations consisting solely of state-action trajectories. During inference, the robot infers the task intent from these prompts and executes the task without further training. However, these methods primarily rely on direct action imitation based on observed states and actions. This limitation becomes pronounced in complex or ambiguous environments where identical actions might correspond to different underlying task objectives, making it difficult for the robot to infer the demonstrator's true intent.

The present research draws inspiration from advances in large language models (LLMs) and large vision-language models (VLMs), specifically chain-of-thought prompting, which has demonstrated the value of explicit intermediate reasoning steps in complex tasks. While some prior work has explored incorporating reasoning processes into robot policy learning (e.g., via subgoals or plans), applying embodied visual reasoning to *in-context imitation learning* specifically remains an area that has not been extensively investigated. This paper seeks to bridge this gap by integrating structured visual reasoning directly into the in-context learning framework, aiming to provide a more robust and generalizable mechanism for task intent inference than state-action conditioning alone. It builds upon and extends existing transformer-based in-context imitation learning architectures like ICRT by adding a visual reasoning component.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness and generalization capabilities of robotic in-context imitation learning systems, particularly in complex and visually ambiguous environments. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **Addressing Data Scarcity**: A fundamental challenge in robotics is the high cost and labor associated with collecting large-scale, high-quality demonstration data. In-context imitation learning offers a pathway to enable robots to learn new skills rapidly from a small number of demonstrations, thereby mitigating the data scarcity problem.
- **Inferring High-Level Task Intent**: Existing in-context imitation learning approaches primarily condition on low-level state-action trajectories. While effective for simpler tasks, this approach often lacks an explicit representation of the demonstrator's higher-level task intent or underlying reasoning process. In scenarios with multiple objects or plausible objectives, the same sequence of actions could be consistent with different goals. Without access to explicit reasoning, the robot may struggle to correctly infer the desired task.
- **Improving Generalization to Unseen Tasks and Configurations**: The inability to explicitly capture task intent can hinder a robot's ability to generalize to novel tasks or new object configurations that deviate from training data. A system that can reason about *why* certain actions are taken, rather than just *what* actions are taken, is hypothesized to be more adaptable.
- **Leveraging Advances in Reasoning Models**: The success of chain-of-thought prompting in LLMs and VLMs for eliciting complex reasoning has demonstrated the value of structured intermediate steps. The authors hypothesize that a similar principle, applied to embodied visual reasoning, could provide crucial guidance for robot action prediction by grounding it in explicit task understanding.
- **Developing an Interpretable Framework**: By explicitly generating reasoning traces, the proposed method aims to offer increased transparency into the robot's decision-making process, which can be valuable for debugging, understanding failures, and building more reliable autonomous systems.

To address these motivations, the authors propose "In-Context Imitation Learning with Visual Reasoning (ICLR)" as a novel framework. The core aim is to augment demonstration prompts with structured visual reasoning traces, which represent anticipated future robot trajectories in image space, and to enable the model to jointly learn to generate these reasoning traces and low-level actions within a unified autoregressive transformer architecture.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ICLR framework extends existing in-context imitation learning paradigms by integrating embodied visual reasoning. The methodology involves specific data augmentation, a tailored transformer architecture, and a distinct training and inference strategy.

**Problem Statement and Data Formulation:**
The research considers a single-arm robot with a gripper, equipped with a third-view camera and a wrist-mounted camera. The objective is to train an in-context imitation learning method capable of performing unseen tasks in novel environment configurations by conditioning on a few prompt demonstrations. A key challenge is that testing configurations differ from prompt demonstrations, preventing naive action copying, and each testing environment permits multiple possible tasks, requiring the model to infer the correct objective from the prompts.

The training dataset `T` comprises visuomotor robot trajectories, `T_i = {s_1, a_1..., s_t_i, a_t_i}`. Each state `s` includes multi-view camera observations and robot proprioception (absolute end-effector pose), while actions `a` are defined as delta end-effector poses. The dataset is semantically categorized into disjoint subsets, `S_k`, each corresponding to a specific task. Training sequences are formed by concatenating trajectories from the same subset, where a randomly selected `n` trajectories serve as "prompt demos" and the remainder as "target episodes."

**Visual Reasoning Trace Generation:**
To integrate reasoning, each trajectory `T_i` is augmented with visual reasoning traces `r`, forming `T_i_aug = {s_1, r_1, a_1..., s_t_i, r_t_i, a_t_i}`. The visual reasoning trace `r` at each time step is defined as a polyline of five points. These points correspond to the robot gripper's pixel positions in the third-view camera images, sampled evenly from the current time step to the end of the trajectory. Five points were selected to represent key stages of common manipulation behaviors like pick-and-place (e.g., moving to object, grasping, transporting, placing).

- **In Simulation**: The gripper's 3D position (derived from proprioceptive state) and known camera parameters are used to determine pixel locations.
- **In Real-World**: The Molmo2 VLM is employed. It is prompted with "Point to the robot gripper" to detect the gripper's pixel coordinates in the third-view images. The visual traces are represented as numerical vectors for input to the model.

**In-Context Imitation Learning with Visual Reasoning (ICLR) Method:**

- **Model Architecture**: ICLR uses a Llama2-style causal transformer architecture, similar to ICRT. It incorporates modality-specific encoders for states, reasoning traces, and actions.
 - **State Encoder**: Visual observations from both cameras are processed by a pretrained Vision Transformer, and proprioceptive information by an MLP. Attention pooling aggregates these into a state token `f_s`.
 - **Reasoning Encoder**: The visual traces (flattened keypoint vectors) are encoded by an MLP into reasoning tokens `f_r`.
 - **Action Encoder**: Actions are encoded by an MLP into action tokens `f_a`.
 All `f_s`, `f_r`, and `f_a` tokens are interleaved into a single sequence and fed into the causal transformer, which performs autoregressive next-token prediction. The key architectural distinction from ICRT is the explicit inclusion of reasoning tokens `f_r` in both the input and output sequences, enabling the model to jointly learn to generate reasoning and actions.

- **Loss Functions**: The model is trained using a standard next-token prediction objective. Losses are computed only for predictions following the prompt demonstrations to enforce in-context learning. The combined loss is `L = L_action + 0.3 * L_reasoning`, where both are L1 losses for action and reasoning trace prediction, respectively. A weight of 0.3 for `L_reasoning` was empirically chosen.

- **Training**:
 - The pretrained Vision Transformer weights are frozen.
 - Action chunking is applied, where the model predicts the next 16 actions simultaneously.
 - A regularization technique, reasoning trace masking, is used: a random subset (0-100%) of visual reasoning trace tokens in the *target* trajectories are masked. All reasoning tokens in the *prompt* demonstrations are preserved. This encourages the model to be robust to imperfect or partially missing reasoning traces, preventing over-reliance.
 - Teacher forcing is used, meaning ground-truth reasoning and action tokens are used for conditioning during training.

- **Inference**:
 - Given augmented prompt demonstrations and the current robot state, the model first predicts the next visual reasoning trace.
 - Subsequently, it predicts the corresponding action chunk. The immediate action to be executed is derived from this chunk via temporal ensembling.
 - This process is iterative: after each action execution, the robot's updated state is used to generate the next reasoning trace and subsequent actions in a closed-loop manner.
 - Key-value caching is utilized to accelerate transformer inference, allowing for efficient incremental decoding. The model can predict a trace or action in approximately 0.0278 ms on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU.
 - **Reasoning Dropout Variant**: An alternative inference mode, "reasoning dropout," is also evaluated. In this variant, the visual trace generation step for the target trajectory is skipped, and a zero vector is used in place of the reasoning trace to condition action prediction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The ICLR method was extensively evaluated in both simulation and real-world manipulation tasks, comparing it against competitive baselines and ablation variants.

**A. Models Evaluated:**
- **Ours**: The complete ICLR model, which generates visual reasoning traces during inference.
- **Ours Dropout**: The ICLR model trained with reasoning trace masking, but during inference, it omits visual trace generation for the target trajectory and uses a zero vector instead.
- **ICRT**: A state-of-the-art transformer-based in-context imitation learning baseline, which our method builds upon but does not incorporate explicit reasoning.
- **TO (Target-Only) Reasoning**: An ablation where visual reasoning traces are omitted from the prompt demonstrations, but the model is still trained to generate reasoning traces for target trajectories.
- **TO Dropout**: The target-only reasoning model with reasoning dropout during inference.

**B. Simulation Experiments (LIBERO Benchmark):**
- **Setup**: Experiments were conducted using a Franka Panda robot in the LIBERO-Object and LIBERO-90 task suites. Tasks included a mix of seen tasks for training and unseen tasks for testing, with varying object configurations. Each test task involved 3 prompt episodes and 50 distinct initializations, totaling 150 rollouts per task per model.
- **Results (Table I)**:
 - The `Ours Dropout` model achieved the highest average success rates across all LIBERO settings (LIBERO-Object, Kitchen, Living, Study, and overall average).
 - The `Ours` (complete ICLR) model consistently secured the second-best performance in most settings and on the overall average.
 - The `TO` and `TO Dropout` models generally ranked third and fourth, respectively.
 - `ICRT` exhibited the lowest performance across all evaluated scenarios.
 - **Key Finding**: The results indicate that incorporating explicit reasoning significantly improves performance in simulated in-context imitation learning. The superior performance of the `Ours Dropout` variant suggests that the model learns to internalize the reasoning process effectively, and skipping explicit trace generation at inference can lead to more stable actions in environments with limited diversity in configurations.

**C. Real Robot Experiments:**
- **Setup**: A Franka Research 3 robot arm with a UMI gripper was used in a tabletop manipulation setup. Two RealSense cameras provided visual observations. Data collection and prompt recording utilized a GELLO teleoperation system. Training involved 825 demonstrations for 5 poking and 10 pick-and-place tasks, designed to prevent shortcut learning by ensuring multiple feasible tasks from the same initial configuration.
- **Evaluation**: Models were tested on 12 unseen tasks (6 poking, 6 pick-and-place), including both tasks with objects seen during training (but in unseen task combinations) and tasks with completely unseen objects. Each task had five difficulty levels with increasing numbers of distractor objects. 10 rollouts were performed per task per model, using one of three prompt demonstrations (varying distractors).
- **Results (Table II)**:
 - The `Ours` (complete ICLR) model largely outperformed all other baselines on both poking and pick-and-place tasks, achieving the highest average success rates.
 - `Ours Dropout` was generally the second-best performer.
 - `TO` and `TO Dropout` followed, with `ICRT` again performing the worst.
 - **Key Finding**: The real-world results reaffirm the effectiveness of incorporating explicit visual reasoning into in-context imitation learning for generalization to unseen tasks and novel object configurations.
 - **Discrepancy (Simulation vs. Real)**: In contrast to simulation, the complete `Ours` model achieved higher success rates than `Ours Dropout` in real-world settings. This discrepancy is attributed to the higher diversity and larger configuration differences in real-world training and testing data compared to LIBERO. The authors hypothesize that in these more challenging real-world scenarios, explicit generation of visual traces becomes more crucial for guiding action prediction, making the full reasoning-enabled model more effective. In simulation, with less diversity, dropout variants might exhibit more stable actions due to fewer potential errors in generated visual traces.

**D. Ablation Studies (Real-world):**
- **Prompt Demonstrations (Table III)**: Varying the number and type of prompt demonstrations (0, 1, or distractor receptacle; 2 random; or 3 total demos) for a pick-and-place task showed that the complete `Ours` model consistently maintained the highest success rates, indicating its stability across different prompt conditions. It was also noted that increasing the number of prompt demos did not always lead to improved performance, which aligns with findings in other robot in-context learning work but contrasts with LLMs/VLMs.
- **Failure Analysis (Figure 6)**: Analysis of failure modes for pick-and-place and poking tasks revealed that visual trace errors (e.g., targeting the wrong object) accounted for 40-45% of failures. However, downstream execution failures, such as grasp failures (45% in pick-and-place) or mis-poking (55% in poking), represented the majority of failures when visual traces were correct. This suggests that the ICLR framework is largely effective at capturing task intent, and future improvements could focus on enhancing low-level control robustness.
- **Efficient Reasoning (Figure 7)**: Experiments on different reasoning intervals (how often reasoning traces are generated during inference) showed a general decrease in performance as the interval increased. However, variants generating traces every 8 or 16 steps achieved performance comparable to the full model, with the 8-step variant being approximately 8 times faster. This suggests a potential trade-off between reasoning frequency and computational efficiency without substantial performance degradation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ICLR framework represents a notable advancement in the field of robotic in-context imitation learning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness**: By incorporating explicit embodied visual reasoning, ICLR moves beyond simple state-action conditioning. This allows robots to infer higher-level task intent, leading to improved generalization capabilities and robustness when faced with unseen tasks, novel object configurations, and visually ambiguous environments. The consistent outperformance over baselines in both simulation and real-world settings supports this claim.
- **Increased Interpretability**: The visual reasoning traces provide an interpretable intermediate representation of the robot's anticipated future trajectory. This "chain-of-thought" for robot actions can make the robot's decision-making process more transparent, facilitating debugging, failure analysis, and potentially fostering greater trust in autonomous systems.
- **Addressing Limitations of In-Context Learning**: The work demonstrates a method to mitigate the shortcomings of existing in-context imitation learning methods that struggle with inferring intent when multiple plausible objectives align with similar low-level actions.
- **Practical Efficiency**: The ablation study on efficient reasoning highlights that while explicit reasoning is important, its computational frequency can be optimized. This suggests that ICLR or similar approaches could be deployed in real-world applications with adjustable trade-offs between performance and computational resources.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: ICLR opens several promising avenues for future research. This includes exploring other forms of reasoning representations (e.g., bounding boxes, affordances, depth information), extending the framework to a wider range of complex tasks (e.g., bimanual, dexterous, long-horizon manipulation), and investigating more data-efficient training methods that leverage human video demonstrations or cross-embodiment data.
- **Inspiration from LLMs/VLMs**: The successful application of chain-of-thought-inspired reasoning from large language and vision-language models to embodied robotics underscores the potential for cross-domain inspiration in AI research.

In conclusion, ICLR demonstrates that integrating explicit embodied visual reasoning into in-context imitation learning can substantially improve the adaptability, robustness, and interpretability of robotic systems, contributing to the development of more capable and generalizable robots.
