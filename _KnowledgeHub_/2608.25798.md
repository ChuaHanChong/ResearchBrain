# TacForcing: Streaming Action Generation with Execution-Time Tactile Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> TacForcing is a streaming action-generation framework for contact-rich manipulation that replaces a VLA's standard flow-matching action expert with a Streaming Action Expert generating action blocks progressively, refreshing tactile input after each executed block, plus Execution-Aware Tactile Attention restricting each tactile update to the next block. It reaches 65% average success in UniVTAC simulation and 69% on three real tasks.

## Problem

- Chunk-based vision-language-action models condition an entire action chunk on tactile observations captured before execution, so tactile conditioning goes stale mid-chunk.
- Tactile representations shift far faster than visual ones within one horizon, giving 0.55 versus 0.005 cosine drift over 35 control steps in a dropper-squeeze episode.
- Existing tactile-reactive policies bolt on separate high-frequency controllers or explicit tactile prediction modules, raising architectural and training complexity.

## Method

- **Streaming Action Expert** with ==block-wise flow scheduling==: the horizon splits into K blocks of B actions with ==position-dependent flow times==, so blocks complete and dispatch sequentially.
- **Block-level tactile refresh**: task context is encoded once, while fingertip deformation maps are re-encoded after each executed block and used to refine ==retained intermediate states== of unfinished blocks.
- **Execution-Aware Tactile Attention**: an ==additive attention mask== lets tactile tokens condition only the block scheduled to execute next, enforced identically at training and inference.

## Results

- On six UniVTAC simulation tasks TacForcing averages **65%** success, beating FTP-1 (**59%**), vision-only pi-0.5 (**51%**), RDP (**42%**), and UniVTAC-ACT (**37%**).
- Across three real contact-rich tasks it averages **69%**, exceeding FTP-1, GR00T N1.7, and pi-0.5 by **17**, **27**, and **42 points**; Transfer Liquid hits **50%** versus at most **19%**.
- Ablations: fixed initial tactile conditioning hurts (real average **42%** to **31%**); block-wise refresh lifts it to **48%**, and adding EATA reaches **69%** real, **60%** simulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Timing of sensor conditioning matters as much as sensor presence: a stale tactile observation held across a chunk can degrade performance below a vision-only policy.
> - Reactivity can come from restructuring the generative schedule itself rather than adding a separate high-frequency controller or predictive tactile module.
> - Attention masking that mirrors execution order is a cheap, general way to align fast-changing modalities with the action segments they actually govern.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This work operates within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically addressing the challenges of contact-rich tasks through enhanced perception and control. The broader landscape is dominated by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which have demonstrated generalization capabilities in robotics by generating actions from language instructions and visual observations. However, a known limitation of these models, particularly in contact-rich scenarios such as precision assembly or part insertion, is their reliance on visual input alone, which often fails to capture crucial information like forces, slip events, or occluded contact states.

Recent advancements have attempted to integrate tactile perception into VLA models to overcome these visual limitations. Existing approaches generally fall into two categories:
1. **Tactile-conditioned VLA models:** These models integrate tactile feedback but often predict entire action "chunks" based on observations collected *before* execution. This leads to a temporal mismatch, as tactile states can evolve significantly during the execution of a pre-planned action chunk, making the initial tactile conditioning "stale."
2. **Tactile-reactive approaches:** These methods typically employ separate high-frequency controllers dedicated to tactile feedback, which operate in conjunction with a lower-frequency visuomotor policy. While effective at real-time adaptation, this architecture increases both the complexity of the system and the challenges associated with training and integrating multiple controllers.

TacForcing positions itself as an alternative that addresses the temporal mismatch of chunk-based tactile conditioning without introducing the architectural and training complexity of separate reactive controllers. It extends streaming generation techniques, commonly used in diffusion models for sequence generation, to robotics by interleaving action generation and execution with updated tactile feedback. This approach aims to bridge the gap between static, pre-execution conditioning and real-time, high-frequency reactive control, offering a unified framework for dynamic adaptation during contact-rich manipulation.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties encountered by current robotic systems in performing contact-rich manipulation tasks. Such tasks, including precision assembly and dexterous handling, necessitate not only a semantic understanding of the task but also continuous adaptation to contact states that can change rapidly and substantially during an action's execution.

The key objectives and motivations can be detailed as follows:

1. **Address the limitations of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in contact-rich scenarios:** While VLA models exhibit strong generalization, they primarily rely on visual observations. Visual cues are often insufficient to reliably detect subtle but critical changes in force, slip, or latent contact states, especially when parts of the interaction are occluded. This perceptual gap compromises the precision and robustness required for effective contact-rich manipulation.

2. **Mitigate temporal mismatch in tactile conditioning:** Existing VLA models that incorporate tactile feedback typically predict complete action chunks from observations (including tactile) gathered at the beginning of the action horizon. The paper demonstrates, using an example of dropper squeezing, that tactile representations can evolve significantly (e.g., a cosine distance of 0.55 over 1.17 seconds) within an action chunk, even when visual representations remain relatively stable. Consequently, an initial tactile observation becomes increasingly "stale" as the action chunk is executed, leading to misalignment between the assumed and actual contact states. The research aims to develop a mechanism to update tactile conditioning dynamically throughout execution.

3. **Avoid the complexity of separate reactive controllers:** Current methods that address execution-time adaptation often implement dedicated high-frequency tactile pathways or controllers distinct from the main VLA policy. While these reactive components provide real-time adjustments, they introduce additional architectural complexity (e.g., slow-fast hierarchies) and increase the challenges of training and orchestrating multiple control loops. A core objective is to achieve effective execution-time adaptation within a unified action-generation framework.

4. **Enable streaming action generation with continuous tactile feedback:** The overarching goal is to introduce a framework that allows actions to be generated progressively, responding to tactile feedback acquired *during* execution. This involves breaking down the generation of a full action chunk into smaller, sequential blocks, with the ability to integrate new tactile observations after each block's execution to refine the generation of subsequent actions.

5. **Ensure temporal alignment between tactile updates and action execution:** A specific objective is to prevent newly acquired tactile feedback from prematurely influencing actions that are far in the future within the action chunk. Since contact states can change rapidly, a tactile observation relevant for the *next* immediate action block might be outdated for blocks much later in the execution horizon. The research aims to introduce a mechanism (Execution-Aware Tactile Attention) to align tactile conditioning precisely with the actions nearing execution, thereby maximizing the relevance of the tactile input.

In summary, the motivation is to enhance the robustness and precision of VLA models for contact-rich tasks by developing an architecturally simple, streaming action generation method that intelligently incorporates dynamic, execution-time tactile feedback while minimizing temporal misalignment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TacForcing introduces a streaming action-generation framework designed to integrate execution-time tactile feedback without a separate reactive controller. The approach consists of three main components: a Streaming Action Expert, block-wise flow scheduling, and Execution-Aware Tactile Attention (EATA).

#### 3.1. Streaming Action Expert

The conventional approach in flow-based action experts is to generate an entire action chunk synchronously from initial observations. TacForcing replaces this with a **Streaming Action Expert** that generates action chunks progressively. An action chunk of horizon $H$ is partitioned into $K$ consecutive blocks, each containing $B$ actions. Near-term actions (i.e., the current block) are generated and dispatched for execution first. The intermediate states of subsequent blocks are retained and then refined using newly acquired tactile feedback after the preceding block has been executed. This design allows for dynamic adaptation to evolving contact states within a single, continuous generation process.

#### 3.2. Block-Wise Flow Scheduling

Building upon Flow Matching (FM) principles, which learn a conditional velocity field to transport samples from a Gaussian prior to the data distribution, TacForcing adapts the concept of flow time. Instead of a single flow time shared across the entire action chunk, it employs **position-dependent flow times**.
- **Flow Time for Blocks:** All actions within a block $k$ share a common block flow time, $\lambda_k$, meaning they complete simultaneously.
- **Sequential Completion:** Given $N$ total sampling steps, with $K$ blocks and $S$ sampling steps between successive block completions (so $N = KS$), block $k$ completes at sampling step $n_k = kS$. As $n_1 < n_2 < \dots < n_K$, blocks become ready for execution sequentially.
- **Progressive Refinement:** At each sampling step, the learned velocity field advances every unfinished block. Once a block $A^{(k)}_t$ completes and is executed, its intermediate states are retained and further refined using updated tactile feedback.

#### 3.3. Conditioning on Execution-Time Tactile Feedback

TacForcing implements a block-level tactile update strategy combined with a novel attention mechanism.
- **Block-Level Tactile Updates:** The task context (visual observation, proprioceptive state, language instruction) is encoded once and reused for the entire action chunk generation. In contrast, the tactile condition is refreshed after the execution of each action block. If $T^{(k)}_t$ denotes the tactile observation acquired after $k$ blocks have been executed (with $T^{(0)}_t$ being the initial tactile observation), this new observation is encoded into tactile tokens $Z^{(k)}_t$ and used to condition the subsequent refinement of the remaining, unfinished blocks. During the sampling steps for block $k$, $Z^{(k-1)}_t$ serves as the latest available tactile representation.
- **Execution-Aware Tactile Attention (EATA):** A key challenge is that a tactile update (e.g., $Z^{(k-1)}_t$) might be highly relevant for the *next* block to be executed ($A^{(k)}_t$), but potentially outdated for blocks much further in the execution horizon. To prevent this temporal mismatch, EATA restricts direct access to the latest tactile representation $Z^{(k-1)}_t$ only to the action tokens within the block scheduled for immediate execution ($A^{(k)}_t$). This is achieved using an additive attention mask. Unfinished blocks beyond $A^{(k)}_t$ continue to evolve based on the block-wise flow schedule without directly attending to the current tactile tokens. They will be conditioned on newer tactile feedback when their turn as the "next block to execute" arrives. This constraint is applied consistently during both training and inference.

#### 3.4. Training Procedure

The training methodology is designed to align with the streaming inference process:
- **Intermediate State Training:** The expert is trained on block-wise intermediate states, paired with the tactile feedback available at the corresponding execution stage.
- **Progress Sampling:** For each demonstration, a generation progress $p$ is sampled.
- **Tactile and Attention Mask Application:** Based on this sampled progress, the corresponding tactile representation $Z^{(k^*-1)}_t$ (where $k^*$ is the block currently being processed) and the EATA mask $M^{(k^*)}$ are used.
- **Loss Calculation:** The training objective, $L_{train}$, calculates the $L_2$ norm between the predicted velocity and the target velocity, but it is applied only to the *unfinished* action positions. This ensures that the model learns to refine actions progressively, consistent with the streaming inference process.

By combining these elements, TacForcing aims to provide a responsive, tactile-aware policy that adapts to changing contact states during execution without requiring a complex multi-controller architecture.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TacForcing was conducted across six simulated UniVTAC tasks and three real-world contact-rich manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against various baselines.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Overall Performance:** TacForcing achieved an average success rate of **65%** across the six UniVTAC simulation tasks.
- **Baseline Comparison:**
 - It outperformed the vision-only $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline by **14 percentage points** (65% vs 51%).
 - It surpassed the tactile-reactive RDP baseline by **23 percentage points** (65% vs 42%).
 - It also exceeded UniVTAC-ACT (37%) and FTP-1 (59%).
- **Task-Specific Performance:** TacForcing recorded the highest or tied-highest success rates on five out of the six simulated tasks: Lift Bottle (90%), Pull-out Key (48%), Put Bottle in Shelf (43%), Insert Hole (69%), and Insert Tube (79%). For the Lift Can task, TacForcing achieved 63%, which was close to the best-performing method (FTP-1 at 66%).
- These results suggest the effectiveness of TacForcing in diverse simulated contact-rich manipulation scenarios.

#### 4.2. Real-World Results

- **Overall Performance:** In real-world experiments, TacForcing demonstrated an average success rate of **69%** across the Stand Bottle, Transfer Liquid, and Wipe Board tasks.
- **Baseline Comparison:**
 - It outperformed FTP-1 by **17 percentage points** (69% vs 52%).
 - It surpassed GR00T N1.7 by **27 percentage points** (69% vs 42%).
 - It significantly exceeded the vision-only $\pi_{0.5}$ baseline by **42 percentage points** (69% vs 27%).
- **Task-Specific Performance:** TacForcing achieved the highest success rates on Stand Bottle (81%) and Transfer Liquid (50%). It tied with FTP-1 for the highest success rate on Wipe Board (75%).
- **Specific Task Impact:** The performance gap was particularly notable on the Transfer Liquid task, where TacForcing's 50% success rate far exceeded the baselines (max 19%). This indicates that execution-time tactile feedback, as incorporated by TacForcing, provides significant benefits for tasks demanding precise contact regulation during execution.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study was conducted to quantify the contribution of execution-time tactile updates and EATA across three simulation tasks and three real-world tasks.

- **Base (No Tactile Conditioning):** This configuration, using a standard Action Expert without tactile input, achieved average success rates of 43% in simulation and 42% in the real world.
- **Fixed Tactile (Initial Tactile Conditioning):** Conditioning the entire action chunk on a single initial tactile observation showed no consistent benefit. The average success rate slightly decreased to 42% in simulation and significantly to 31% in the real world compared to the Base configuration. This finding motivated the necessity of dynamic tactile feedback during execution.
- **TacForcing without EATA (Streaming with Refreshed Tactile Feedback):** This configuration utilized the Streaming Action Expert and refreshed tactile feedback after each block execution but lacked EATA. It improved the average success rate from 42% to **51%** in simulation and from 31% to **48%** in the real world compared to the Fixed Tactile baseline. These gains demonstrated the value of allowing subsequent actions to leverage tactile information acquired during execution.
- **TacForcing (Streaming with Refreshed Tactile Feedback and EATA):** Incorporating EATA into the streaming architecture further enhanced performance across all six evaluated tasks. The average success rate increased from 51% to **60%** in simulation and from 48% to **69%** in the real world. Compared to Fixed Tactile, TacForcing achieved gains of 18 percentage points in simulation and 38 percentage points in real-world experiments. The consistent improvements over 'TacForcing without EATA' indicate that aligning tactile conditioning with block execution, via EATA, further optimizes the utility of execution-time tactile feedback.

In summary, the results indicate that TacForcing's streaming action generation, combined with execution-time tactile updates and Execution-Aware Tactile Attention, contributes to substantial and consistent performance improvements in contact-rich manipulation tasks in both simulated and real-world environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in TacForcing offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving physical contact.

**Addresses a Core Challenge in Contact-Rich Manipulation:** The research directly tackles the fundamental problem of adapting to rapidly evolving contact states during robot execution. This is a critical hurdle for tasks requiring precision, such as assembly, insertion, or dexterous object handling, where visual information alone is often insufficient or unreliable due to occlusion or subtle force dynamics. By effectively incorporating execution-time tactile feedback, TacForcing provides a method for robots to respond to real-time physical interactions, enhancing precision and robustness.

**Simplifies Architecture for Real-Time Adaptation:** A notable impact is the demonstration that effective execution-time adaptation can be achieved *without* resorting to a separate, high-frequency reactive controller. Traditional approaches often build complex "slow-fast" hierarchies, which increase architectural complexity and training overhead. TacForcing's approach integrates this adaptability directly into a single streaming action-generation framework, potentially simplifying policy design, training, and deployment for tactile-aware robots.

**Introduces a Novel Streaming Generation Paradigm for Robotics:** By adapting the concept of position-dependent flow times and block-wise scheduling from diffusion models, TacForcing contributes to the emerging field of streaming policies in robotics. This paradigm allows for actions to be generated and executed progressively, with continuous refinement based on updated sensory input. This can lead to more responsive and less brittle robot behaviors compared to methods that generate entire action sequences before execution.

**Enhances Temporal Alignment of Multimodal Feedback:** The introduction of Execution-Aware Tactile Attention (EATA) is a specific methodological contribution that has broader implications for multimodal learning in robotics. It highlights the importance of not just integrating multiple sensory modalities but also considering their temporal relevance. EATA ensures that tactile feedback conditions actions that are temporally aligned with its acquisition, preventing "stale" information from negatively impacting future actions. This principle could be extended to other fast-changing sensory modalities.

**Demonstrates Robust Performance Across Diverse Tasks and Environments:** The consistent and substantial performance gains observed across a range of six simulated tasks and three real-world contact-rich manipulation tasks underscore the practical utility of TacForcing. Its superior performance over strong baselines, including vision-only and existing tactile-reactive methods, in both simulation and real-world settings, indicates its potential for broad applicability. The significant improvement in tasks like "Transfer Liquid" highlights its impact on scenarios demanding fine-grained contact control under occlusion.

**Potential for Broader Generalization and Application:** By improving the fundamental capability of robots to handle physical contact with dynamic adaptation, TacForcing could pave the way for more generalist robot manipulation systems. Such systems could perform a wider variety of tasks, particularly those in unstructured or partially known environments, where precise interaction with objects is paramount. It could also influence the design of future tactile sensing and processing architectures in robotics.
