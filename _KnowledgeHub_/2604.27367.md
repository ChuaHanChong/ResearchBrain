# DOT-Sim: Differentiable Optical Tactile Simulation with Precise Real-to-Sim Physical Calibration

> [!summary] Summary
> DOT-Sim introduces a differentiable optical tactile simulation framework that accurately models the complex physical deformation and optical responses of soft sensors. The framework achieves high fidelity in both physical and visual aspects, enabling robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for tasks like object classification, tumor detection, and precise trajectory following.

## Problem

- Accurately simulating soft, deformable optical tactile sensors presents challenges due to their complex mechanical behavior and intricate internal light transport mechanisms.
- Existing simulation frameworks often simplify physical and optical aspects, limiting realism and generalization capabilities for complex contacts and large deformations.
- The lack of high-fidelity differentiable simulation hinders robust data generation, algorithm training, and reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for robotic perception and control tasks.

## Method

- **DOT-Sim** employs the ==Material Point Method (MPM)== for physically accurate modeling of soft gel deformation under large nonlinear displacements.
- Leverages ==differentiable physics== to rapidly calibrate sensor parameters (Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio) using few real-world demonstrations and FEA pseudo-ground-truth.
- Proposes a neural network to predict a ==residual image== (contact minus idle), added to a real-world idle image for high optical fidelity.

## Results

- Improved physical accuracy: L2 Chamfer Distance **1.71 mm vs. 1.74 mm** (Taxim); F-Score at 1mm **69.89 vs. 64.69**.
- Optical simulation: **PSNR 30.48** in challenging settings, **17.34%** average improvement over baselines.
- Zero-shot sim-to-real: **90.48%** indenter classification, **96.55%** tumor detection, **0.896 mm** average trajectory error.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining a physically grounded deformable body model (MPM) with a data-driven residual optical rendering approach effectively captures both the mechanical and visual complexities of optical tactile sensors.
> - Differentiable physics enables efficient and accurate calibration of soft sensor material properties, significantly reducing the manual effort and time typically required for system identification.
> - Predicting residuals, rather than full raw images, improves the fidelity and sharpness of simulated optical tactile feedback, which is crucial for capturing fine-grained contact features.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Tactile sensing is a critical component for robots to interact effectively with their environments. Optical tactile sensors, such as GelSight, DIGIT, and DenseTact, have gained prominence due to their high spatial resolution and detailed contact feedback, finding applications in object recognition, manipulation, and shape reconstruction. However, simulating these sensors accurately presents significant challenges. The sensors are characterized by soft, deformable materials and intricate internal light transport mechanisms, which lead to highly nonlinear responses to contact.

Existing tactile simulation frameworks often simplify the physical and optical aspects. Some approaches rely on analytical geometry approximations for contact or fixed mappings for visual appearance, limiting their realism and generalization capabilities, particularly for complex contacts and large deformations. Other methods might forgo full optical simulation, instead tracking simplified proxies like surface markers for sim-to-real transfer. While computationally efficient, these simplifications can compromise the rich expressive signal provided by optical tactile sensors, thereby restricting the range and complexity of tasks that can be trained effectively in simulation and transferred to the real world. Previous learning-based optical simulators have also not consistently achieved the visual accuracy required for robust policy conditioning.

This work addresses the gap for a physically accurate and visually realistic differentiable simulation framework for optical tactile sensors. By providing a method that precisely models both the deformation dynamics of soft materials and the complex optical responses, DOT-Sim aims to overcome the limitations of prior simulators. This enables more robust data generation, algorithm training, and ultimately, more reliable zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for various robotic perception and control tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a differentiable optical tactile simulation framework, named DOT-Sim, that accurately models the physical behavior and optical responses of soft tactile sensors. This framework is designed to enable physically accurate simulation and support robust sim-to-real transfer for downstream robotic applications.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and needs in the field of robotic tactile sensing:

1. **Challenges in Simulating Deformable Sensors**: Optical tactile sensors employ soft, deformable gels, making their mechanical behavior complex and highly nonlinear under contact. Existing simulators often use simplified physical models that fail to capture these intricate deformations, especially for larger displacements. An accurate physical model is necessary to reflect real-world interaction dynamics.

2. **Complexity of Optical Rendering**: The visual output of optical tactile sensors results from complex internal light transport, reflections, and gel properties. Directly simulating these optical effects with high fidelity is computationally intensive and often sensor-specific. Simplified optical models in prior work frequently lack the visual accuracy required for training robust vision-based policies that can transfer to real sensors.

3. **Need for Accurate Sim-to-Real Transfer**: Accurate simulation is fundamental for generating synthetic training data, developing learning algorithms, and facilitating zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. Without high-fidelity simulation, policies trained in a simulated environment often perform poorly when deployed on physical robots, necessitating extensive and costly real-world data collection. The current state of tactile simulation often fails to provide the necessary realism for successful transfer across a broad range of tasks.

4. **Efficient Calibration**: Calibrating physical parameters of soft materials is typically a time-consuming process. There is a need for a simulation framework that allows for rapid and efficient calibration of sensor parameters using minimal real-world data, integrating seamlessly with differentiable physics methods.

To address these motivations, DOT-Sim aims to:
- Accurately replicate the physical dynamics of optical tactile sensors, accommodating large and non-linear deformations, by modeling them as elastic materials using the Material Point Method (MPM).
- Enable rapid and precise calibration of the simulation's physical parameters using differentiable physics and a small number of real-world demonstrations.
- Generate realistic optical outputs in various contact scenarios by proposing a novel residual image learning approach, rather than predicting raw images directly.
- Facilitate robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for downstream tasks such as object classification, tumor detection, and precise trajectory following, demonstrating the practical utility of the high-fidelity simulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DOT-Sim operates as a two-stage framework, integrating a physically grounded model for sensor deformation with a data-driven approach for optical rendering.

**A. Calibration of the Physical Properties of Tactile Sensors**

1. **Physical Modeling**: The tactile sensor is modeled as a collection of particles, and its mechanical behavior is simulated using the Material Point Method (MPM). MPM is a particle-based continuum simulation technique suitable for simulating soft, elastic materials. Each particle represents a small volume with properties such as mass, position, velocity, and deformation gradient. The simulation involves standard MPM operations (particle-to-grid and grid-to-particle transfer) and a contact model for sensor-indenter interaction.
2. **Differentiable Physics**: The MPM simulation is differentiable, allowing for gradient-based optimization of physical parameters. The key physical properties targeted for calibration are Young’s modulus (E) and Poisson’s ratio (ν), denoted collectively as θ = (E, ν).
3. **Data Collection and Pseudo-Ground Truth**: To calibrate θ, a small set of real-world demonstration sequences (19 videos) are collected. During these demonstrations, an indenter interacts with the sensor, and its position is tracked using an OptiTrack marker tracking system. Direct capture of ground-truth mesh deformation is challenging due to occlusions and sensor limitations. Therefore, pseudo-ground-truth mesh deformations are generated using the Abaqus 2024 Finite Element Analysis (FEA) simulator. Abaqus, while computationally expensive, is considered accurate for complex material behaviors when provided with parameters derived from standardized material tests (e.g., silicone-plastic friction coefficient, Yeoh hyperelastic constants). This process provides paired data of indenter poses and corresponding detailed sensor deformations.
4. **Calibration via Optimization**: The MPM simulator is then calibrated to match the Abaqus outputs. This is achieved by minimizing the Chamfer distance between the point clouds generated by the differentiable MPM simulation and the Abaqus-generated pseudo-ground-truth point clouds. The optimization process uses gradient descent (Adam optimizer) on the re-parameterized log(E) and ν, with a learning rate of 0.1, for 30 iterations. This calibration is efficient, completing within minutes on a single A5000 GPU. The median of estimated parameters across sequences is taken for robustness.

**B. Optical Simulation: A Residual Approach**

1. **Geometric Input from Physics**: Once the physical model is calibrated, the MPM simulation provides accurate 3D deformation of the sensor under various contact forces.
2. **Virtual Camera Rendering**: From the simulated deformed sensor mesh, depth maps and surface normal maps are rendered. A virtual camera is positioned at the center of the sensor base, mimicking the internal imaging setup of real-world optical tactile sensors. These maps constitute a 4-channel input (3 for normals, 1 for depth).
3. **Neural Rendering with Residuals**: Instead of directly learning a mapping from depth and normals to a raw RGB tactile image, DOT-Sim employs a neural network (U-Net architecture with a DeepLabV3 ResNet50 backbone) to predict a *residual image*. This residual image represents the difference between a contact-induced frame and a real-world idle (contact-free) reference frame. The final optical output is obtained by adding this predicted residual image to the actual real-world idle image. This approach leverages the observation that much of the tactile image remains static during contact, and significant signals appear as localized residuals.
4. **Training**: The neural network is trained using a pixel-wise ℓ2 loss on RGB values. Optimization is performed with the Adam optimizer (learning rate 3e-4, weight decay 1e-4, batch size 8) for 100 epochs. Input images are resized to 640x480.

The DenseTact 2.0 sensor is chosen for experiments due to its soft nature, ability to undergo significant deformation, and challenging randomized surface pattern.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates DOT-Sim across physical accuracy, optical simulation fidelity, and performance in downstream tasks, including classification, tumor detection, and sim-to-real control.

**A. Physical Accuracy of 3D Deformation**
The physical realism of the simulated sensor deformation was assessed by comparing the MPM-simulated surface against observed DenseTact point clouds generated by Abaqus FEA.
- **Metrics**: L2 Chamfer Distance (CD), Significant L2 Chamfer Distance (Sig. L2 CD), Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD), and F-Score at 1mm. These metrics quantify geometric similarity and discrepancies between point clouds.
- **Comparison**: DOT-Sim was compared against Tacto and Taxim. DiffTactile was noted as not being reproducible for comparison due to reported stiffness issues in its FEM simulation.
- **Results**: DOT-Sim demonstrated improved fidelity compared to Taxim and Tacto. It achieved lower L2 CD (1.71 mm vs. 1.74 mm for Taxim), lower Sig. L2 CD (3.82 mm vs. 3.97 mm for Taxim), lower EMD (1.29 mm vs. 1.31 mm for Taxim), and a higher F-Score at 1mm (69.89 vs. 64.69 for Taxim). The improvements in Sig. L2 CD and F-Score are particularly relevant as they emphasize accuracy in highly deformed regions, which are critical for tactile sensing.

**B. Optical Simulation Accuracy**
The accuracy of DOT-Sim’s optical outputs was quantified by comparing simulated and real tactile images across various scenarios and indenters.
- **Metrics**: Mean L2 Norm, Significant Pixel L2 Norm, and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR).
- **Settings**: Evaluation was performed under Easy (in-domain indenters), Medium (mixed indenters), and Hard (unseen indenters) settings.
- **Comparison**: DOT-Sim was compared against DiffTactile and Tacto (calibrated).
- **Results**: DOT-Sim exhibited significant improvements in visual accuracy. In the challenging Hard setting, DOT-Sim achieved a PSNR of 30.48, compared to 26.79 for Tacto and 21.31 for DiffTactile. It also achieved lower Mean L2 Norm (3.50e-2 vs. 5.04e-2 for Tacto) and Significant Pixel L2 Norm (4.53e-2 vs. 6.35e-2 for Tacto). This represents an average PSNR improvement of 17.34% over the strongest baseline. Qualitative comparisons indicated that DOT-Sim captures fine-grained contact features, including anisotropic responses from complex indenter geometries, more faithfully.
- **Ablation Study on Residual Prediction**: An ablation study confirmed the effectiveness of the proposed residual image prediction. Predicting residuals resulted in a higher PSNR (30.48 vs. 28.89 for direct regression) and lower significant L2 error (4.53e-2 vs. 5.39e-2), indicating improved image fidelity and reduced blurriness compared to directly regressing the full optical frame.

**C. Performance in Downstream Classification, Detection, and Sim-To-Real Applications**

1. **Sim-To-Real Zero-Shot Indenter Classification**: A classifier trained solely on simulated tactile images from DOT-Sim was evaluated on real-world tactile images to identify indenter types.
 - **Results**: DOT-Sim achieved 90.48% accuracy in an in-domain setting and 81.18% in a challenging out-of-domain setting (where test indenters were not used during the optical training). This performance significantly surpassed DiffTactile (65.88% in-domain, 52.94% out-of-domain) and Tacto (50.59% for both settings), indicating strong sim-to-real generalization.
2. **Tumor Detection via Tactile Feedback**: The task involved classifying the presence or absence of a tumor (simulated as convex/concave shapes) by pressing on simulated skin. A classifier trained exclusively on DOT-Sim generated data was tested on real-world images across three different skin stiffness levels.
 - **Results**: DOT-Sim achieved high accuracy rates of 80.56% (Skin 1), 92.31% (Skin 2), and 96.55% (Skin 3), outperforming DiffTactile and Tacto consistently across all skin types.
3. **Precise Trajectory Following with Sim-to-Real Transfer**: An agent was trained using behavior cloning in simulation (ResNet-18 policy outputting 6-DoF velocity commands from synthetic tactile images) and directly deployed on an xArm 7 robot for trajectory following.
 - **Results**: The transferred policy successfully tracked the demonstrations in the physical environment, achieving an average action error of 0.896 ± 0.031 mm over 10 trials, relying solely on synthetic tactile images.

**D. Reinforcement Learning**
DOT-Sim’s utility for control was also demonstrated in a box-repositioning task using reinforcement learning.
- **Task**: An agent had to rotate a box to a target yaw of 10 degrees while maintaining stable contact, using only DenseTact images as input.
- **Method**: A PPO agent was trained within the differentiable DenseTact environment.
- **Results**: The policy converged within 15 minutes, successfully driving the box towards the target yaw.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DOT-Sim represents a step forward in the simulation of optical tactile sensors, addressing critical challenges related to physical realism and optical fidelity. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

1. **High-Fidelity Simulation**: The framework provides a physically and visually consistent simulation of optical tactile sensors. By coupling the Material Point Method (MPM) for deformation modeling with a residual-based neural rendering pipeline, it accurately captures the complex nonlinear deformations of soft gels and their intricate optical responses. This level of fidelity is crucial for tasks where precise contact information is necessary.
2. **Efficient Calibration**: The use of differentiable physics enables rapid and precise calibration of sensor physical parameters (e.g., Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio) using a minimal set of real-world demonstrations. This efficiency significantly reduces the manual effort and time typically required for system identification, making the simulation framework more practical for researchers and developers.
3. **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer**: A core strength of DOT-Sim is its demonstrated capability for robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The success in indenter classification, tumor detection, and precise trajectory following without further real-world training highlights the high alignment between the simulated and real-world domains. This reduces the reliance on costly and time-consuming real-world data collection, accelerating the development and deployment of tactile-enabled robotic systems.
4. **Enabling Advanced Robotic Applications**: By providing realistic tactile feedback, DOT-Sim opens new avenues for training robots in complex manipulation and interaction tasks that require fine-grained perception. This is particularly relevant for handling delicate objects, performing dexterous tasks, or interacting with unknown environments where soft contact is prevalent.
5. **Foundation for Future Research**: The modular design of DOT-Sim allows for its integration with any MPM-based engine and offers flexibility for developing lightweight optical rendering plugins. This provides a robust foundation for future research into tactile perception, control, and new sensor designs.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
Despite its capabilities, DOT-Sim currently faces limitations. It may struggle to generalize to highly out-of-distribution geometries, particularly those with sharp edges or fine surface details, leading to potential alignment issues between simulated and real contacts. Addressing this could involve improving MPM efficiency for denser point sampling, incorporating residual models for local deformation refinement, and expanding the indenter dataset to enhance geometric diversity.

Furthermore, the current simulation pipeline is computationally demanding, operating at approximately 3 FPS on an NVIDIA A6000 GPU, which restricts its use in real-time control applications. However, the authors note that runtime can be significantly improved by tuning MPM parameters (such as voxel resolution, contact density, and substeps per frame) without substantial increases in error, suggesting that the computational efficiency can be optimized for specific applications.
