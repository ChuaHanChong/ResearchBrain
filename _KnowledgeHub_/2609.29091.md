# From Passive Execution to Active Exploration: Agentic Embodied Manipulation in Realistic Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> An agent-based active exploration framework turns embodied manipulation from passive instruction following into a closed loop of planning, perception, and execution. A planner picks the next exploratory or manipulative subtask, a perception module grounds the scene via recognition, localization, and OCR, and a fine-grained perception-action interleaving strategy re-perceives after each atomic operation. On a real-world Find-and-Place task with hidden targets, it reaches 95.7% success.

## Problem

- Most embodied manipulation frameworks execute predefined plans passively, assuming targets are visible, layouts uncluttered, and initial observations already sufficient for direct execution.
- Real scenes break those assumptions: targets sit occluded inside containers, sit among visually similar distractors, or are identifiable only through indirect textual and semantic cues.
- Prior planning-based search methods handle concealment but depend on carefully engineered task-specific modules and paradigms that generalize poorly to new settings.

## Method

- A three-module closed-loop architecture: **planning module** reasons over instruction, observation, and interaction history to pick the next subtask; perception and execution modules ground and act.
- **Perception module** yields structured scene understanding through object recognition, target localization, ==OCR== of cabinet category labels, and a judgment of whether observations suffice for execution.
- ==Perception-action interleaving== decomposes the task into atomic operation units (open drawer, close drawer, pick), re-perceiving after each so the planner verifies, replans, and explores incrementally.

## Results

- On the real-world Find-and-Place task with a Franka arm, overall success reached **95.7%** without distractors and **94.3%** with visually similar lemon-shaped distractors.
- Across three pear-toy variants differing in color, size, material, and texture, success held at **95.0-96.0%**, indicating generalization beyond a single fixed visual instance.
- The full system runs on **one** consumer-grade NVIDIA RTX 3090, showing the pipeline needs no data-center hardware or large-scale inference to operate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Perception granularity should match action granularity: re-perceiving after every atomic operation, not per task, is what makes exploration under partial observability reliable.
> - Separating semantic planning from low-level skill invocation keeps long-horizon execution stable while letting a general agentic planner handle reasoning and task progress.
> - Per-step visual verification corrects earlier assumptions before errors compound, which is the main defense against distractors and semantic ambiguity in cluttered scenes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot is asked to find and fetch an object. The object is hidden, and the instruction does not say which drawer contains it. A request to fetch something does not tell a robot where an out-of-sight object is, and the robot cannot safely assume that the first plausible object is the right one. The authors take a different approach from a fixed sequence of actions: the robot acts to reveal information, checks the changed scene, and adjusts its next step. Their robot treats finding an object as part of the task, not as a prerequisite supplied by the user. The authors emphasize this shift from passive execution to active exploration and a tighter perception–action loop. In their specific drawer-search task, the system reports high end-to-end success even with visually similar distractors.

### 2. From instruction to exploration

A fixed action sequence cannot reliably find an object that is initially out of sight, may be in an unknown drawer, and must be identified from semantic clues among look-alikes. The system addresses this with three collaborating roles:

- The planner uses the instruction, the current visual observation, and the interaction history to choose a search or manipulation subtask.
- Perception extracts scene information, including object identity and location and textual or semantic cues.
- Execution turns the selected subtask into robot actions.

The key mechanism runs at a finer granularity than a whole task. The robot executes one operation, such as opening a drawer. It then perceives the changed scene and decides whether to pick, close, or search elsewhere. As a result, a revealed object or a mistaken assumption can affect the next decision, rather than leaving the robot committed to its initial plan.

Active search itself is not new. The earlier system [RoboRetriever: Single-Camera Robot Object Retrieval via Active and Interactive Perception with Dynamic Scene Graph](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.12916) already addresses hidden objects by combining camera viewpoint changes, physical interaction, and persistent scene memory. This paper does not report a head-to-head comparison with it.

### 3. What the test establishes

The real-world setup uses two multi-drawer cabinets with different category labels. The target is in an unknown drawer and is initially invisible. The instruction asks the robot to find a target object and put it in a box. The paper evaluates three pear-toy variants that span color, size, material, and texture, with visually similar lemon-shaped distractors.

A trial succeeds only when the robot selects the correct cabinet, searches until the target is found, grasps it, and places it in the box. Recognizing or grasping the object alone is not enough.

Under these conditions, the reported overall success rate is 95.7% without similar distractors and 94.3% with them. The “overall” values are the paper’s aggregate across the three variants. The paper does not state trial counts or uncertainty estimates. The results establish high end-to-end success in this particular real-world setup. They do not show superiority over a passive baseline or prove that interleaving alone caused the results, because the paper reports no baseline or mechanism ablation.

The overall row reads 95.7% without distractors and 94.3% with distractors. The variant rows read 96.0/94.0, 96.0/95.0, and 95.0/94.0. The table reports success rates, not trial counts, uncertainty intervals, or a comparison against another method.

### 4. Closing

When an object is hidden, a robot needs more than a plan for handling it. It also needs a way to learn what the scene contains as it acts. The paper’s observe–act–verify–replan design comes with high end-to-end success in the tested Find-and-Place setting, including under distractor conditions. The evidence does not isolate how much the interleaving itself contributes to that success.
