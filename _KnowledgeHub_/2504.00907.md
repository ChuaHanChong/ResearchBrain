# Grounding Multimodal LLMs to Embodied Agents that Ask for Help with Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces AUTOASK, a method for training embodied agents to interpret ambiguous human instructions in household environments. It leverages Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) and online Reinforcement Learning (RL) with rewards generated by another LLM to enable agents to ask clarifying questions and resolve ambiguities under partial observability, achieving significant improvements over baseline models in success rate and ambiguity resolution efficiency.

## Problem

- Embodied AI agents often struggle to interpret and act upon ambiguous or under-specified human instructions in partially observable, real-world-like environments.
- Training interactive embodied agents that can both act and ask clarifying questions is challenging due to the high cost of human demonstrations for imitation learning or the impracticality of manually engineering dense reward functions for complex reasoning tasks.
- Existing zero-shot LLM approaches for embodied tasks frequently assume full observability or over-rely on assistance, failing to effectively handle partial observability and strategic ambiguity resolution.

## Method

- The paper introduces the ==ASK-TO-ACT== task, which requires an embodied agent to perform multi-object rearrangement based on ambiguous natural language instructions in a partially observable Habitat 3.0 environment, with the ability to ask clarifying questions.
- The proposed AUTOASK method adapts a LLaVA-OneVision MLLM into a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy trained using online Reinforcement Learning (DD-PPO).
- A Llama-3 LLM is utilized as a process reward model (PRM) to generate dense, per-step rewards that incentivize both task completion and strategically useful clarification questions, thereby circumventing the need for extensive human demonstrations or manual reward engineering.

## Results

- AUTOASK significantly outperformed all baselines operating under partial observability, achieving a **45.9%** success rate and **40.6%** Ambiguity-Resolution Efficiency Score (ARS) on UNSEEN SCENES, and **38.7%** SR / **35.2%** ARS on UNSEEN TASKS.
- The method demonstrated an absolute improvement of **24.4**-**30.2%** in success rate compared to the best zero-shot visual MLLM baseline and **6.5**-**12.3%** over a supervised fine-tuned MLLM.
- Ablation studies confirmed that LLM-generated rewards are indispensable for the ASK-TO-ACT task, as policies trained using only sparse success or subgoal rewards resulted in near-random or very low success rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs can effectively serve as process reward models, providing dense, high-level common sense and deductive reasoning rewards crucial for training complex interactive embodied agents without human demonstrations.
> - Integrating MLLMs with online RL enables agents to actively explore, ground questions visually in partially observable environments, and strategically resolve ambiguities, leading to robust performance on novel tasks and scenes.
> - Sparse or programmatically defined subgoal rewards alone are insufficient for complex ambiguity resolution tasks, highlighting the critical role of precise, LLM-generated rewards in guiding agents to ask meaningful questions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the vibrant intersection of **Embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI)**, **Large Language Models (LLMs) and Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs)**, **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, and **Human-Robot Interaction (HHRI)**. It addresses a critical challenge in making embodied agents truly useful and robust in real-world environments: interpreting and acting upon ambiguous human instructions.

Historically, embodied AI tasks often featured well-defined goals, allowing agents to rely on clear instructions or pre-programmed objectives (Batra et al., 2020; Weihs et al., 2021). However, real-world human communication is inherently vague and context-dependent. Prior work in dialogue-guided embodied agents (e.g., Padmakumar et al., 2021; Thomason et al., 2019) has explored agents asking questions to improve task performance. This paper extends this by focusing on ambiguity resolution in multi-object rearrangement tasks within partially observable settings.

The rise of LLMs has profoundly influenced embodied AI, with many approaches leveraging LLMs for high-level task planning and reasoning in a zero-shot manner (Ren et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2022). These methods often assume full observability or require extensive prompt engineering to manage the agent's tendency to over-rely on assistance. This paper pushes beyond these limitations by placing agents in *partially observable environments*, forcing them to *actively explore* and *ground their questions* in the visual and environmental context.

Another significant trend is the use of **Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)**, which integrate visual information with language processing (Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). This paper adapts MLLMs into **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models** (Szot et al., 2024b; Black et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024), enabling them to perceive, understand, and act. The novel aspect here is their training methodology.

The training of complex embodied agents traditionally relies on expensive human demonstrations for imitation learning or manually engineered reward functions for reinforcement learning. Both are challenging for tasks involving natural language interaction and complex reasoning. This work addresses this by tapping into a burgeoning area: **LLMs as reward models for RL** (Ma et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2023b; Chen et al., 2025). While previous efforts used LLMs to generate code-based rewards for low-level control, this paper pioneers using LLMs to generate *dense, per-step rewards* that encourage high-level *common sense and deductive reasoning* for interactive ambiguity resolution in embodied settings.

Therefore, this work makes a significant step toward more capable and autonomous human-robot interaction by integrating advanced MLLMs, state-of-the-art RL techniques, and LLM-driven reward generation within a realistic, partially observable embodied environment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop **embodied agents capable of interpreting and acting upon ambiguous and under-specified human instructions in household environments**. The authors recognize that current embodied AI systems often struggle with the inherent vagueness of human language, leading to incorrect assumptions and task failures.

The core motivation stems from the observation that a truly capable household robot should possess the following abilities:
1. **Recognize ambiguity:** Identify when a user's instruction is unclear due to multiple possible interpretations (e.g., "Bring the cup" when several cups are present).
2. **Ask relevant clarification questions:** Strategically pose minimal, yet informative, questions to resolve ambiguities and infer the user's precise intent or preferences (e.g., "Are you looking for a red cup?" or "Is it on the kitchen counter?").
3. **Ground questions in the environment:** Ensure that clarification questions are relevant to the observed state of the environment, not just generic queries.
4. **Balance multiple objectives:** Navigate partially observable environments, explore to gather information, deduce the minimum questions needed, and determine the optimal time to ask them.

To systematically study these challenges, the authors introduce a novel task called **ASK-TO-ACT**. This task forces an embodied agent to perform single or multi-object rearrangement tasks based on under-specified instructions.

The motivation for their specific methodological approach, **AUTOASK**, arises from the significant limitations of existing training paradigms for such interactive agents:
- **Human Demonstrations (Imitation Learning):** Collecting large-scale human demonstrations that include natural language interaction interleaved with robot actions is exceedingly expensive and time-consuming.
- **Manually Engineered Rewards (Reinforcement Learning):** Designing dense, task-specific reward functions that effectively encourage deductive reasoning, commonsense understanding, and strategic questioning is practically impossible to scale for complex, open-ended interactive tasks.
- **Zero-shot LLM Approaches:** While powerful, these often require careful prompt engineering, struggle with partial observability, and can be prone to over-reliance on asking for help rather than intelligent action.

Motivated by these difficulties, the authors aim to **bootstrap the learning signal** needed for interactive agents by leveraging the rich contextual commonsense and deductive reasoning abilities of LLMs themselves. Their objective is to adapt MLLMs into VLA policies using **online reinforcement learning with LLM-generated rewards**, thereby eliminating the need for extensive human demonstrations or laborious manual reward engineering, and enabling agents to operate effectively under partial observability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the **ASK-TO-ACT task** and proposes a novel method called **AUTOASK** to train embodied agents capable of solving it.

#### 3.1. ASK-TO-ACT Task Definition

The ASK-TO-ACT task requires an embodied agent to perform single or multi-object rearrangement using an ambiguous natural language instruction within a home environment. The agent operates under **partial observability**, meaning it only perceives its immediate surroundings. Its core challenge is to strategically ask minimal, yet relevant, clarification questions to resolve ambiguities and infer user intent before executing the task.

The task considers various types of ambiguity:
- **No Ambiguity:** Baseline tasks with clear instructions.
- **Attribute Recognition:** Disambiguating objects based on appearance (e.g., "red cup" vs. "green cup").
- **Spatial Reasoning:** Differentiating objects by location (e.g., "cup on the kitchen counter" vs. "cup on the table").
- **Object Size:** Distinguishing objects of the same type and attribute by size (e.g., "large red bowl" vs. "small red bowl").
- **Compositional Ambiguity Reasoning:** Combinations of attribute, spatial, and size ambiguities.
- **Clean Clutter:** Multi-object tasks requiring agents to identify "clutter" and follow hidden placement preferences.
- **Preference-based:** Tasks requiring agents to inquire about user preferences for target locations of multiple objects.

**Simulation Environment:**
The task is instantiated in **Habitat 3.0**, a highly realistic simulator for embodied AI. It uses 83 scenes from the **ReplicaCAD** dataset and 42 object categories from **Google Scanned Objects**. The agent uses a Spot robot embodiment.

**Agent's Perceptions and Actions:**
At each timestep, the agent receives:
- A 480x640 resolution RGB image (egocentric visual observation).
- Robot joint positions and base egomotion.
- An indicator of whether it's holding an object.
- The task instruction.
The agent's action space consists of 125 predefined high-level oracle skills (e.g., `pick(apple)`, `place(sink)`, `navigate(cabinet)`, `open(drawer)`) and an open-ended **`ask_question("...")`** action, allowing it to generate natural language queries.

**Simulation Answers:** To enable programmatic evaluation and training, an LLM-based answering module simulates diverse user preferences and language responses. This module takes the task instruction, privileged environment state, target object metadata, and expected target location to answer agent-generated questions.

#### 3.2. AUTOASK: Policy Architecture and Training

**Policy Architecture:**
AUTOASK adapts the **LLaVA-OneVision (0.5B)** Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) into a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model.
- **Input:** Task instruction, sequence of past observations, previous actions, and user responses to questions.
- **Visual Input Processing:** To handle long sequences of visual observations in partially observable settings (which would lead to prohibitively large token counts if each visual observation was fully embedded), a **Perceiver model** downsamples visual tokens from each observation to a compact representation (k=4 tokens).
- **Token Interleaving:** Downsampled visual tokens from the current observation are interleaved with tokenized task instructions, past actions, and user responses.
- **Output:** The MLLM autoregressively predicts a sequence of language tokens corresponding to either a discrete high-level action (skill) or a natural language question. Invalid action sequences are treated as no-ops.
- **Action Length Normalization:** To prevent actions with more tokens (like questions) from being penalized by lower overall probabilities during training, a token normalization technique is applied to action probabilities.

**Training with Reinforcement Learning using LLM-Generated Rewards:**
The core innovation is training the MLLM policy using **online Reinforcement Learning (RL)** with **LLM-generated rewards**.
- **RL Algorithm:** DD-PPO (distributed version of PPO) is used for training.
- **LLM as Reward Model:** A **Llama-3 (or Llama-3.1 8B Instruct)** LLM serves as a process reward model (PRM). This LLM is given privileged information (true environment state, task instruction, target object metadata, expected location, history of questions asked) and evaluates the agent's actions and generated questions.
- **Reward Function (Equation 1):** The per-step reward ($r_t$) is a composite of several terms:
 - $r_1 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{success}$: Sparse reward for successful task completion.
 - $r_2 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{subgoal}$: Reward for achieving intermediate objectives (e.g., picking the target object, navigating to a location), programmatically generated using privileged simulator state.
 - $r_3 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{useful\_question}$: Crucially, this reward is generated by the *LLM reward model*. It evaluates whether a question asked by the agent is valid and makes progress towards resolving ambiguity or identifying user preference.
 - $-r_4 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{exceed\_budget}$: Penalty if the agent asks more questions than a predefined budget $K$ (minimum required questions).
 - $-r_5$: Timestep penalty to encourage faster task completion.
- **Implementation Details:** The training involves 50 million steps on 8 A40 GPUs, with 8 parallel vLLM servers handling asynchronous reward generation to maintain high throughput.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates AUTOASK against several strong baselines across two generalization axes: **UNSEEN SCENES** (seen tasks in novel environments) and **UNSEEN TASKS** (novel task instances with new ambiguity compositions in novel environments).

#### 4.1. Evaluation Metrics

- **Success Rate (SR):** Percentage of successfully completed tasks.
- **Ambiguity-Resolution Efficiency Score (ARS):** Measures successful task completion while penalizing asking more or fewer questions than the minimum required ($K$). It's calculated as $SR / (1 + |q_{relevant} - K| + q_{irrelevant})$.
- **Question Ratio (QR):** Ratio of total questions asked to the minimum required questions, $(q_{relevant} + q_{irrelevant}) / K$.

#### 4.2. Baselines

The study compares AUTOASK against five baselines:
1. **Fully Observable Text WorldGraph + ReAct (Zero-shot):** GPT-4o with a full textual description of the environment, reasoning via ReAct prompting.
2. **Fully Observable Text WorldGraph + ReAct (Few-shot):** Same as (1) but with in-context examples.
3. **Partially Observable Text WorldGraph + ReAct (Few-shot):** Same as (2) but with limited environmental knowledge, requiring active exploration.
4. **Vision GPT-4o + SoM + ReAct:** GPT-4o receiving egocentric visual observations annotated with Set-of-Marks (SoM) and a textual history.
5. **LLaVA-OneVision SFT:** An open-source MLLM fine-tuned with supervised learning on heuristically generated, successful trajectories.

#### 4.3. Key Results (Table 1)

- **AUTOASK Outperforms Baselines under Partial Observability:** AUTOASK achieves a success rate of 45.9% and ARS of 40.6% on UNSEEN SCENES, and 38.7% SR / 35.2% ARS on UNSEEN TASKS. This significantly surpasses all baselines operating under partial observability.
 - Compared to the best zero-shot visual MLLM (Vision GPT-4o + SoM + ReAct), AUTOASK shows an absolute improvement of 24.4-30.2% in success rate.
 - Compared to the SFT-trained MLLM (LLaVA-OneVision SFT), AUTOASK outperforms by an absolute margin of 6.5-12.3% in success rate and 8.8-20.0% in ARS. This indicates that direct RL training with LLM rewards is more effective than imitation learning on synthetic expert data, particularly in encouraging efficient ambiguity resolution.
- **Difficulty of Zero-Shot LLMs:** Even with privileged, fully observable world graphs, zero-shot GPT-4o (baseline 1) shows limited performance (SR 41.7-39.9%), highlighting the inherent difficulty of task planning and ambiguity resolution for LLMs in this setup. Few-shot examples (baseline 2) improve this, but performance drops considerably under partial observability (baseline 3).
- **Importance of Dense Rewards (Table 2):** An ablation study demonstrates that sparse success rewards or even programmatically defined subgoal rewards alone are insufficient for training an effective policy for ASK-TO-ACT. These simpler rewards result in near-random or very low success rates, underscoring the critical need for precise, dense, per-step rewards (like those generated by the LLM reward model) that incentivize both acting and asking meaningful questions to resolve ambiguity.
- **Trade-off with Question Budget (Fig. 3 a, b):** Experiments with variable question budgets show a direct correlation: increasing the allowed number of questions an agent can ask (up to a certain point) leads to higher success rates. However, this comes with a trade-off of a higher Question Ratio, meaning agents ask more questions than strictly necessary. This insight allows for flexible policy design based on user preference for success vs. conversational brevity.
- **Behavioral Analysis (Fig. 3 c, d):** The performance of both RL and SFT agents decreases as the number of *required* questions for a novel task increases. Interestingly, RL-trained agents tend to ask *more* questions than SFT-trained counterparts, which often leads to higher success rates, suggesting that SFT agents are more conservative, potentially due to the nature of the training data.

Qualitative examples (Fig. 4 and Fig. 6) illustrate AUTOASK's ability to navigate, identify ambiguous objects, ask a sequence of clarifying questions, interpret user responses, and successfully complete the rearrangement task.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of embodied AI, particularly in human-robot interaction and instruction following. Its contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Novel Task and Training Paradigm:** The introduction of the **ASK-TO-ACT** task provides a standardized benchmark for studying ambiguity resolution and interactive reasoning in embodied agents. More importantly, the **AUTOASK** method represents a novel paradigm for training such agents by effectively combining MLLMs, online RL, and LLM-generated rewards. This is highlighted as the *first demonstration* of adapting MLLMs as VLA agents that can both *act and ask for help* using *LLM-generated rewards* with *online RL* under *partial observability*.

2. **Overcoming Training Bottlenecks:** The work directly addresses two major limitations in embodied AI research:
 - **Expensive Human Demonstrations:** By using LLM-generated rewards, the method largely bypasses the need for massive, costly human demonstrations, which are especially difficult to collect for interactive tasks involving interleaved actions and natural language dialogue.
 - **Impractical Manual Reward Engineering:** It overcomes the challenge of manually designing dense reward functions for complex behaviors like deductive reasoning and strategic questioning, instead leveraging the LLM's inherent reasoning capabilities for reward generation. This makes the training process more scalable and applicable to a wider range of nuanced tasks.

3. **Enhanced Ambiguity Resolution and Generalization:** The significant empirical improvements achieved by AUTOASK over strong zero-shot and SFT baselines demonstrate its effectiveness in learning to resolve various types of ambiguities (attribute, spatial, size, compositional, preference-based). The strong generalization performance on both UNSEEN SCENES and UNSEEN TASKS is crucial, indicating that the learned policies are robust to novel environments and previously unseen forms of ambiguity.

4. **Advancing Human-Robot Interaction:** By enabling robots to recognize ambiguity and proactively seek clarification, this research moves us closer to more natural, intuitive, and reliable human-robot collaboration. Robots can become more helpful by avoiding incorrect assumptions and confirming user intent, thereby increasing user trust and satisfaction. This is particularly relevant for household robots or assistive agents where instructions are frequently informal and incomplete.

5. **Pioneering LLMs as Reward Models for Embodied Reasoning:** This paper validates the use of LLMs as process reward models (PRMs) for high-level, common sense, and deductive reasoning tasks in embodied settings. This opens up a promising avenue for future research into using LLMs to guide complex learning processes beyond simple code-based rewards, potentially accelerating the development of intelligent agents.

**Potential Future Impact and Limitations:**
While groundbreaking, the authors acknowledge limitations: the current system uses a constrained set of question templates to simplify learning for a smaller MLLM. Future work aims to explore larger MLLMs for unconstrained, open-ended question generation, tackle more diverse and complex ambiguous tasks, and further investigate the reliability and accuracy requirements for LLM-generated rewards in less constrained real-world scenarios.

In summary, this research not only introduces an innovative solution to a critical problem in embodied AI but also lays foundational groundwork for future interactive intelligent agents, paving the way for robots that can truly understand and respond to the complexities of human communication.
