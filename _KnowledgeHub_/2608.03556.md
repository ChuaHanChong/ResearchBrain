# Human Centric Embodied Intelligence for Soft Wearable Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper from the National University of Singapore introduces a Human-Centric Embodied Intelligence (HCEI) paradigm and a Perception-Cognition-Actuation-Augmentation (PCAA) framework for designing and understanding soft wearable robots. The work synthesizes advancements across multidisciplinary areas, identifies critical challenges, and proposes a roadmap to accelerate the translation of these devices toward personalized, predictive, and human-centric wearable intelligence.

## Problem

- Existing wearable robotics research often focuses on specific device components or applications, lacking a unified framework to integrate diverse advancements in soft materials, AI, and human adaptation.
- Traditional rigid exoskeletons suffered from bulkiness, misalignment, and discomfort, while current soft wearable robots face challenges in seamlessly integrating sensing, intelligence, and user co-adaptation over long periods.
- The field requires moving beyond fixed assistive forces to systems that can sense, interpret, and continuously adapt with individual users, considering human function, comfort, and agency as primary objectives.

## Method

- The authors propose the **Human-Centric Embodied Intelligence (HCEI)** paradigm, defining five fundamental principles (==morphological intelligence==, multimodal perception, adaptive control, human-robot co-adaptation, and societal embedding) that guide the design and evaluation of coupled human-robot systems.
- They introduce the **Perception-Cognition-Actuation-Augmentation (PCAA)** framework, which organizes the interdependent functions of soft wearable robots and reorients the design philosophy from an "actuator-first" approach to one driven by augmentation objectives and human-integration constraints.
- The paper synthesizes advances across the PCAA components (e.g., multimodal sensing, learning-based control, soft actuation, varied augmentation use cases) and introduces constructs like the ==Human Digital Signature (HDS)== and the ==Human Digital Twin== to facilitate personalized, longitudinal adaptation.

## Results

- The review provides a comprehensive synthesis of progress in soft wearable robotics, identifying key advancements in soft materials, wearable sensing, AI for control, and diverse actuation methods.
- It articulates critical grand challenges impeding real-world deployment, including robust perception, reliable intelligence, digital-twin fidelity, and clinical/regulatory harmonization.
- A capability roadmap is proposed, outlining **five generations** of soft wearable robots from assistive to context-aware, personalized, human digital twins, and ultimately human-AI symbiotic systems, providing a guide for future research and development.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Intelligence in soft wearable robotics is most effectively achieved when it emerges from the coupled human-robot system, distributed across device morphology, multimodal sensing, adaptive cognition, and the wearer's physiological and behavioral adaptation.
> - The PCAA framework highlights that all functional components of a soft wearable robot are mutually dependent and must be considered concurrently from the outset of design, with augmentation objectives driving perception and cognition, not just actuation.
> - Scalable personalization and long-term real-world deployment of soft wearable robots require the development of robust human digital signatures and digital twins to manage individual variability, physiological changes, and continuous adaptation over time.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Wearable robotics has emerged as a significant area addressing human needs that fixed laboratory equipment cannot meet, by providing robotic assistance directly on the body during natural movement. This field is driven by demands in rehabilitation (e.g., stroke recovery), occupational assistance (e.g., reducing musculoskeletal injuries from repetitive tasks), and human augmentation (e.g., lowering the metabolic cost of walking). Historically, rigid exoskeletons provided substantial joint support but introduced issues such as bulk, joint misalignment, and discomfort.

The research landscape is characterized by a shift towards soft wearable robots. These systems prioritize body-conformal interfaces, compliant materials, and textiles, integrating with the body as an assistive layer. This transition represents a move from device-centric mechatronic assistance to systems that sense, interpret, and co-adapt with individual users over time, enabled by advancements in soft materials, compliant actuation, flexible on-body sensing, and conformal electronics.

This review positions itself within this evolving landscape by arguing that the next stage of the field requires a "human-centric embodied intelligence" (HCEI) paradigm. Existing literature often focuses on specific device classes, body regions, actuation methods, or clinical applications, but a unified narrative connecting historical evolution, soft embodiment, on-body artificial intelligence, personalized user representations, digital twins, shared human-robot intelligence, and translational readiness has been largely absent. This paper aims to integrate these diverse elements, thereby providing a comprehensive framework for understanding and guiding the future trajectory of soft wearable robotics. The paper also contextualizes this evolution through four historical eras of wearable robotics, from early rigid exoskeletons (Era I, 1960-2000) to the current integration phase (Era III, 2015-2025), and projects into a prospective Era IV (2025-2040) focused on personalized, human-centric embodied systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the evolving challenges in soft wearable robotics. As the field progresses beyond foundational development in materials and actuators, the central challenge shifts towards the integration of sensing, intelligence, and human adaptation into systems that users can wear comfortably, trust, and benefit from over extended periods. This requires moving beyond fixed assistive forces to platforms that sense, interpret, and co-adapt with an individual user over time.

The paper's key objectives are:

1. **Advance the Human-Centric Embodied Intelligence (HCEI) Paradigm:** The review aims to establish HCEI as a central lens for the field. This paradigm conceptualizes intelligence as emerging from the coupled human-robot system, distributed across morphology, multimodal sensing, adaptive cognition, compliant actuation, and the wearer's physiological and behavioral adaptation. It contrasts with traditional approaches by treating the wearer, the soft device, and their continuous co-adaptation as a single, integrated system, with objectives defined by human function, comfort, agency, and long-term well-being. The authors define HCEI through five fundamental principles: morphological intelligence, multimodal body-linked perception, adaptive and anticipatory control, human-robot co-adaptation and agency, and contextual and societal embedding.

2. **Propose the Perception-Cognition-Actuation-Augmentation (PCAA) Framework:** The paper introduces PCAA as an organizing framework to synthesize and structure advances in various interdependent components of soft wearable robots. This framework positions perception and cognition as primary drivers of design, explicitly challenging the conventional "actuator-first" paradigm. PCAA decomposes a soft wearable robot into four coupled functions—Perception (sensing human-robot state), Cognition (converting state to action), Actuation (delivering mechanical output), and Augmentation (realized change in wearer’s function)—emphasizing their mutual dependence and the necessity of concurrent consideration from the outset of design.

3. **Synthesize Advances and Identify Grand Challenges:** Using the PCAA framework, the review synthesizes progress across diverse areas, including soft materials, wearable sensing, artificial intelligence, actuation, human-robot interaction, digital twins, clinical translation, manufacturing, regulation, and ethics. This synthesis aims to highlight how these components collectively shape long-term personalization and real-world deployment. Furthermore, the paper identifies the coupled grand challenges that impede real-world deployment and proposes a capability roadmap, thereby guiding future research and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration.

In essence, the overarching objective is to provide a unified conceptual framework and design perspective to accelerate the translation of next-generation soft wearable robots toward personalized, predictive, and human-centric wearable intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper adopts an integrative, narrative review methodology rather than a systematic review. This approach involves selecting and synthesizing representative literature to construct a conceptual argument, without claiming exhaustive or protocol-driven coverage.

**Literature Search and Inclusion:**
Sources were identified through academic databases including IEEE Xplore, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. This was supplemented by backward and forward citation tracing from seminal primary studies and key reviews. Search terms combined device and domain descriptors (e.g., "soft exosuit," "robotic glove," "textile actuator") with function and application descriptors (e.g., "embodied intelligence," "wearable sensing," "human-in-the-loop optimization," "digital twin," "rehabilitation," "gait assistance," "occupational exoskeleton"). Studies were included if they addressed soft or soft-rigid hybrid wearable systems, their sensing, control, augmentation, or clinical translation. Purely rigid exoskeletons, prosthetics, implantable robots, and non-wearable soft robots were generally excluded, except where they provided historical context or conceptual contrast. Priority was given to peer-reviewed primary research and authoritative reviews. The literature spanned roughly 1995 to 2026, with an emphasis on developments from the past decade, while foundational works were retained for historical context. The selection process was purposive, aiming to offer a structured interpretation rather than a quantitative meta-analysis.

**Conceptual Frameworks:**

1. **Human-Centric Embodied Intelligence (HCEI) Paradigm:** This is presented as the central thesis and organizing lens. The paper defines HCEI as the capacity of a human-robot ensemble (soft wearable robot and wearer) to achieve assistance or augmentation by distributing intelligence across morphology, multimodal body-linked sensing, adaptive control, and the wearer's physiological and behavioral adaptation. Its objectives are human-centric, focusing on function, comfort, agency, and long-term well-being. HCEI builds on the concept of embodied intelligence from soft robotics, but specifically extends it to the coupled human-robot system, emphasizing the human as an active, adaptive partner.
 The definition of HCEI is operationalized through five proposed principles:
 - **Morphological intelligence:** Encoding assistive competence in garment geometry, anchoring, and spatial compliance.
 - **Multimodal, body-linked perception:** Continuous, multimodal sensing (kinematics, forces, physiology) embedded in the worn structure, including self-perception of the combined human-robot state.
 - **Adaptive and anticipatory control:** Moving beyond reactive parameter tuning to predictive assistance based on wearer intent.
 - **Human-robot co-adaptation and agency:** Bidirectional adaptation over time, ensuring wearer understanding, control, and consent.
 - **Contextual and societal embedding:** Incorporating all-day comfort, sustainability, privacy, and compatibility with real workflows as constitutive requirements.

2. **Perception-Cognition-Actuation-Augmentation (PCAA) Framework:** This framework provides the organizational structure for the review, decomposing any soft wearable robot into four mutually dependent functions. The core claim is that these functions are coupled and must be considered concurrently from the outset of design, challenging the conventional "actuator-first" design sequence.
 - **Perception:** Encompasses all sensing of the human and environment (e.g., inertial measurement, load sensing, EMG, physiological monitoring).
 - **Cognition:** The layer that translates perceived state into decided action (e.g., control policies, intent estimation, learning-based policies).
 - **Actuation:** The mechanical output, including principle, force delivery, and control bandwidth (e.g., pneumatic, cable-driven, shape-memory alloy systems).
 - **Augmentation:** The realized effect on the human (e.g., restored function, reduced effort, enhanced performance).
 The framework posits that the augmentation target dictates what must be sensed and decided, and actuation is selected downstream. The paper uses the PCAA framework to map existing devices and identify gaps, also introducing a five-level intelligence ladder to assess cognitive maturity.

**Supporting Constructs:**
Within the HCEI-PCAA spine, several other original constructs are introduced as analytical tools:
- **Human Digital Signature (HDS):** A personalized, multimodal, time-evolving representation of the wearer, derived from fused sensor data.
- **Wearable Intelligence Stack:** A layered view within the Cognition pillar, from morphological intelligence to a projected personal AI agent.
- **Actuator Comparison Matrix:** An ordinal rating of actuation families across wearable-relevant axes (e.g., force, portability, textile integration).
- **Clinical Readiness Scorecard:** Qualitative assessment of translational readiness across application areas (e.g., safety, evidence quality, usability).
- **Five-Generation Roadmap:** A capability-based periodization of the field's future, from assistive wearables to human-AI symbiosis.

The methodological approach emphasizes the interdependencies among these components, arguing that a holistic, human-centric design perspective is necessary for addressing the complex challenges of real-world deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper synthesizes numerous advancements and identifies critical challenges across the PCAA framework, contextualized by the HCEI paradigm.

**Perception: Understanding the Human and Environment (Section 4)**
- **Multimodal Sensing:** Soft wearables integrate sensing directly into their compliant materials. Five key modalities are identified: kinematic and biomechanical (inertial units, strain/capacitive sensors, optical waveguides, load cells), bioelectrical (surface electromyography, force myography), physiological (cardiovascular, respiration, temperature, biochemical markers), and environmental. Sensor fusion is crucial for transforming raw measurements into state estimations like intent, effort, and fatigue.
- **Human Digital Signature (HDS):** Proposed as a personalized, time-evolving representation of the wearer. The HDS comprises kinematic/biomechanical, strength/activation/fatigue, physiological-response, and behavioral/environmental-exposure profiles. It enables personalized assistance, detection of physiological changes, and supports the human digital twin.
- **Challenge of Robust Perception:** Daily use conditions introduce challenges like sensor hysteresis, creep, drift, electrode shift, motion artifacts, and distribution shift (signal-state relationship changes over time). Robust, self-calibrating, all-day perception remains an open requirement, limiting downstream cognition and augmentation.

**Cognition: Intelligence, Learning, and Personalization (Section 5)**
- **Learning for Estimation, Prediction, and Intent:** Conventional machine learning (regression for joint moments, classification for activity/intent) and deep learning (recurrent/convolutional networks for gait phase, locomotion mode) are employed. Intent prediction is crucial for anticipatory assistance. Human-in-the-loop optimization, often using reinforcement learning, is a key tool for personalization based on physiological objectives, though constrained by safety and sample efficiency.
- **Continual Learning and Personalized Adaptation:** Wearers change over time, necessitating policies that adapt across sessions without catastrophic forgetting or unsafe exploration. The human digital twin, integrating HDS, is proposed to manage these longitudinal adaptations.
- **Hybrid Control and Wearable Intelligence Stack:** Hybrid architectures combine analyzable structured controllers with adaptive learned components, ensuring safety through model-based safety envelopes. The "Wearable Intelligence Stack" conceptualizes layers from morphological intelligence to an interaction layer for intent and a prospective personal AI agent.
- **Shared Control, Co-adaptation, and Trust:** The device and wearer form a shared loop. Wearable haptics provide feedback. Shared control distributes authority between user, controller, and device morphology. Co-adaptation occurs as humans optimize gait in response to assistance, requiring continuous device adaptation. Trust, based on mechanical, behavioral, and informational transparency, is critical for acceptance and adherence.

**Actuation: Delivering Intelligent Physical Assistance (Section 6)**
- **Soft Materials and Morphological Intelligence:** Constituent materials (elastomers, hydrogels, textiles/fibers) simultaneously form load paths, sensing media, and comfort interfaces. Architected materials and smart geometries (origami, kirigami) embed tunable stiffness and logic, enabling morphological intelligence to offload control complexity.
- **Actuation Families and Trade-offs:** No single optimal actuator exists. Pneumatic actuation offers compliance and large deformation but requires bulky pressure sources. Hydraulic/electrohydraulic systems offer high force density but add fluid mass. Tendon-driven systems separate motor weight from transmission, using cables for force routing. Shape-memory systems are compact but slow and inefficient. Electroactive polymers and magnetic actuation are emerging but face voltage/durability or niche limitations. The paper emphasizes the critical role of interfaces and anchoring for effective force delivery.
- **Actuator Comparison Matrix:** Provides an ordinal synthesis of actuation families across ten wearable-relevant axes (e.g., force, portability, textile integration, noise), highlighting application-conditioned selection and the promise of hybridization and quasi-passive designs.

**Augmentation: Expanding Human Capability (Section 7)**
- **Use Cases:** Augmentation is distinguished from rehabilitation and assistance, focusing on reducing fatigue, preventing injury, or enhancing performance in unimpaired users.
- **Industrial and Logistics Work:** Evidence supports biomechanical and metabolic effectiveness in reducing musculoskeletal load for tasks like overhead work and lifting, but thermophysiological burden and worker acceptance are critical for real-world success.
- **Other Domains:** Construction, field work, defense, sport, and extreme environments present diverse demands on robustness, portability, energy autonomy, and minimal interference. Teleoperation extends wearer capability by coupling to remote agents, requiring robust sensing and bilateral feedback.
- **Cross-domain Synthesis:** Augmentation objectives and success criteria vary significantly across domains (e.g., fatigue reduction in industry, endurance in defense, precision in sport). Common cross-cutting constraints include thermophysiological burden, energy autonomy, and user acceptance.

**Clinical Translation and Real-World Deployment (Section 8)**
- **Translational Gap:** Technical feasibility often outpaces clinical readiness, with challenges in evidence quality, standardization, portability, and implementation feasibility.
- **Clinical Evidence:** Most mature evidence is in gait applications (e.g., soft exosuits for post-stroke walking), with emerging evidence in pediatric, older adult, and upper-limb/hand applications. High-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain scarce, particularly for upper-limb and hand devices.
- **Translational Barriers:** Weak evidence design (small cohorts, inconsistent protocols), regulatory maturity lagging adaptive systems, usability (setup time, donning/doffing), and workflow integration/reimbursement are significant bottlenecks.
- **Clinical Translation Scorecard:** Table 3 provides a qualitative assessment of translational readiness across lower-limb gait, upper-limb, and hand/glove applications, highlighting current status across dimensions like safety, evidence quality, usability, and regulatory status.
- **Manufacturing, Regulatory, Commercialization:** Scalability of multi-material soft systems, regulatory frameworks for adaptive devices, and health-economic evidence for reimbursement are underdeveloped.
- **Human Digital Twin:** Proposed as a mechanism for scalable personalization, turning the HDS into a predictive engine. It manages longitudinal changes in joint mechanics, fatigue, and adaptation. Challenges include balancing individual fidelity with population scalability under privacy and cost constraints.
- **Ethics and Governance:** Wearable robots raise concerns regarding privacy (raw data, inferred states, contextual information), fairness (bias in data/deployment), agency (behavioral shaping, dependence), explainability, accountability, and cybersecurity. Current regulation is fragmented, necessitating risk-tiered, lifecycle-based governance.

**Grand Challenges and Future Roadmap (Section 9)**
The paper identifies system-level coupling problems as grand challenges, encompassing wearability, energy autonomy, reliable intelligence, digital-twin fidelity, trust and shared-control reliability, manufacturing scalability, clinical adoption, and benchmarking/regulatory harmonization. These challenges inform a "Capability Roadmap" of five generations:
1. **Assistive Wearables (current foundation):** Task-specific, reactive assistance.
2. **Context-Aware Wearables:** Adds situational awareness, assistance varies with activity.
3. **Personalized Wearable Intelligence (first true threshold):** Maintains individualized models, adapts across sessions (connecting to HDS).
4. **Human Digital Twins (emerging target):** Predictive, model-based coordination anticipating state changes and individualizing therapy.
5. **Human-AI Symbiosis (long-horizon projection):** Persistent cooperative systems with shared, bidirectional adaptation, preserving human agency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance of this work lies in its integrated perspective on the rapidly evolving field of soft wearable robotics, moving beyond component-level advancements to systemic integration.

1. **Unified Conceptual Framework:** By introducing Human-Centric Embodied Intelligence (HCEI) as a paradigm and the Perception-Cognition-Actuation-Augmentation (PCAA) as an organizational framework, the paper provides a cohesive structure for understanding and navigating the complexities of soft wearable robotics. This framework offers a shared language and analytical toolset for researchers across disciplines, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration.

2. **Reorientation of Design Philosophy:** The paper explicitly challenges the traditional "actuator-first" design approach, advocating for a human-centric, objective-driven design sequence. By placing augmentation objectives and human-integration constraints at the forefront, followed by perception, cognition, and then actuation, it aims to address root causes of common failures such as lack of generalization, interface-induced sensing issues, and device abandonment despite technical efficacy.

3. **Guidance for Future Research:** By synthesizing advancements across diverse domains (materials, sensing, AI, HRI, digital twins, clinical translation) and identifying specific, coupled grand challenges, the review provides a clear agenda for future research. The proposed 50 open questions and the capability roadmap offer concrete directions, from improving wearability and energy autonomy to developing robust, explainable AI and validating digital twin fidelity.

4. **Acceleration of Translational Impact:** The paper directly addresses the bottlenecks in translating research prototypes into real-world clinical and occupational applications. By emphasizing clinical readiness, regulatory challenges, manufacturing scalability, and ethical considerations (privacy, fairness, agency), it highlights crucial non-technical factors that dictate adoption and societal benefit. The "Human Digital Twin" concept is presented as a mechanism to achieve scalable personalization, which is vital for widespread deployment.

5. **Emphasis on Human Outcomes and Experience:** A central contribution is the redefinition of success metrics beyond mere technical performance (e.g., torque or task completion). The HCEI paradigm places comfort, trust, co-adaptation, user agency, and long-term well-being as primary design objectives and evaluation criteria. This focus on the human experience is critical for developing devices that are not only effective but also accepted and sustained in daily life.

In summary, this paper offers a comprehensive synthesis and a prescriptive framework that can guide the development of next-generation soft wearable robots. By prioritizing the human wearer and viewing intelligence as an emergent property of the entire human-robot system, it aims to accelerate the realization of personalized, predictive, and truly human-centric wearable intelligence.
