# A Hierarchical Framework for Humanoid Locomotion with Supernumerary Limbs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Imperial College London introduce a hierarchical control framework for humanoid robots equipped with supernumerary limbs, enabling stable bipedal locomotion by actively managing dynamic disturbances. This framework integrates a learning-based locomotion policy with a model-based dynamic balancing controller, demonstrating a 47% reduction in Center of Mass trajectory deviation and enhanced intra-cycle stability.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots experience significant stability challenges during bipedal locomotion, inherently being underactuated and unstable systems.
- The integration of Supernumerary Robotic Limbs (SLs) introduces substantial and continuous dynamic perturbations, severely compromising the robot's locomotion stability.
- Existing control methods, whether traditional model-based or monolithic deep reinforcement learning, struggle to simultaneously control complex locomotion and manage the expansive state-action space and conflicting objectives posed by heavy, dynamic SLs.

## Method

- A hierarchical control framework was developed, decoupling the control problem into a low-level locomotion policy and a high-level dynamic balancing controller.
- The low-level policy, using ==Deep Reinforcement Learning (PPO)== with imitation and curriculum learning, was trained to generate a stable walking gait for the humanoid's legs and lower torso, progressively adapting to SL mass and challenging poses.
- A high-level, model-based controller independently modulated the poses of the SLs based on real-time feedback of the robot's Center of Mass (CoM) and Center of Support (CoS) to actively counteract instabilities.

## Results

- The DRL agent successfully adapted to increasing payload mass and challenging SL arm poses during 500 million training steps, with episode returns and lengths consistently improving.
- The dynamic balancing strategy achieved a **47%** reduction in Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance (**65.54**) of the CoM trajectory from the baseline compared to the static payload scenario (**123.71**), indicating better preservation of the fundamental gait characteristics.
- The 'Dynamic Balancing' scenario consistently yielded the lowest median Gait Cycle Stability Minimum (GCSM) values, demonstrating more effective and complete re-stabilization at critical points within each gait cycle.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the control of complex locomotion and dynamic balancing into a hierarchical framework effectively addresses the stability challenges posed by supernumerary limbs.
> - Supernumerary limbs can be transformed from a source of dynamic perturbation into functional assets for active re-stabilization during dynamic bipedal locomotion.
> - Curriculum learning is an effective strategy for training deep reinforcement learning policies to adapt to increasing dynamic complexity and varied payload conditions introduced by articulated limbs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of versatile humanoid robots capable of operating in human-centric environments presents a significant challenge in robotics, particularly regarding stable bipedal locomotion. This foundational problem is complicated by the inherent underactuated and unstable dynamics of legged systems. The integration of Supernumerary Robotic Limbs (SLs) aims to enhance robot capabilities, enabling complex manipulation, carrying, and support tasks. However, these articulated and often heavy SLs introduce substantial and continuous dynamic perturbations to the main robot body, severely compromising locomotion stability.

Current research in humanoid locomotion control has primarily focused on two areas: traditional model-based control, such as methods leveraging the Zero-Moment Point (ZMP) criterion, and more recent Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) approaches. While ZMP-based controllers are effective in structured environments, they often struggle with variable terrain or external disturbances due to their reliance on precise models. DRL has shown promise in generating complex gaits for legged systems, often utilizing high-fidelity physics simulators and techniques like imitation learning and algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). However, applying a single DRL policy to simultaneously control both complex locomotion and heavy SLs presents difficulties due to the expansive state-action space and potentially conflicting objectives. Hierarchical reinforcement learning has been suggested as a way to decompose such problems.

Regarding balance augmentation with SLs, existing strategies include using limbs as static anchors for bracing in quasi-static scenarios or specialized appendages like robotic tails for angular momentum modulation or extra legs for broader support during load carriage. While effective for their specific functions, these solutions lack generalizability for maintaining dynamic walking stability in the presence of continuous, high-magnitude disturbances from multi-purpose SL arms, which generate unpredictable, task-driven movements.

This research positions itself by addressing this gap. It proposes a novel approach that leverages general-purpose, anthropomorphic robotic arms for *dynamic* balance assistance during locomotion. This is distinct from prior work that focused on static bracing or specialized appendages. The methodology integrates learning-based control (for locomotion) with model-based control (for balancing), aligning with a contemporary trend in robotics that combines the strengths of both paradigms. This hybrid approach aims to demonstrate a practical solution for tackling multifaceted robotics challenges by allowing the same limbs used for manipulation to also function as active balancing aids, thereby enhancing system versatility and utility.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the significant stability challenges introduced when augmenting humanoid robots with Supernumerary Robotic Limbs (SLs). While SLs offer substantial potential for enhancing a robot's capabilities in areas like manipulation and payload carriage, their mass and movement introduce dynamic perturbations that can destabilize the underlying bipedal locomotion. The inherent instability and underactuated nature of humanoid robots further magnify this problem. The complexity of simultaneously controlling locomotion and managing these dynamic disturbances with a single monolithic controller, particularly with learning-based approaches, is a recognized limitation in the field.

To address these challenges, this research posits that a decoupled, hierarchical control strategy can provide an effective solution. By separating the complex control problem into two more manageable layers—one focused on generating the primary locomotion gait and another dedicated to dynamic balancing—it aims to achieve stable walking for humanoids equipped with heavy SLs. This modular approach is intended to facilitate independent development and optimization of each component, leading to a more effective overall system.

The overarching aim of this project is to develop and validate a novel hierarchical control framework that enables a humanoid robot to maintain stable bipedal locomotion while effectively managing the significant self-induced dynamic effects imposed by heavy, articulated supernumerary limbs.

To achieve this aim, the following specific objectives were established:

1. **Locomotion Gait Development:** To develop a stable walking gait for the Unitree H1 humanoid robot using imitation-guided Deep Reinforcement Learning. This objective establishes the fundamental mobile base upon which the SLs are integrated.
2. **Robustness Enhancement through Curriculum Learning:** To enhance the resilience and adaptability of the learned locomotion policy by implementing a curriculum learning strategy. This strategy progressively introduces the mass and dynamic poses of the SLs during training to prepare the agent for varied and challenging conditions.
3. **Dynamic Balancing Controller Design:** To design and implement an independent, model-based dynamic balancing controller. This controller actively utilizes the SLs to counteract instabilities by modulating their configuration based on real-time feedback from the robot's Center of Mass (CoM) and Center of Support (CoS).
4. **Quantitative Framework Evaluation:** To quantitatively evaluate the performance of the full hierarchical framework in a high-fidelity physics simulation. This evaluation involves comparison across three distinct and increasingly complex scenarios:
 - **Baseline Walking:** To establish a performance benchmark with an unladen humanoid, defining the characteristics of an ideal, unperturbed gait.
 - **Static Payload:** To assess the DRL policy’s ability to manage a constant, challenging load by having the humanoid walk with the SLs locked in a fixed pose. This isolates the effect of the learned policy under a predictable disturbance.
 - **Dynamic Balancing:** To evaluate the complete hierarchical framework with the high-level controller providing active balance modulation, comparing its performance against the other two scenarios to validate the effectiveness of the decoupled control strategy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopted a hierarchical control framework implemented within a high-fidelity physics simulation environment. The methodology primarily comprised the development of a low-level locomotion policy, a high-level dynamic balancing controller, and a structured experimental evaluation.

#### 3.1. System Architecture and Environment

The study was conducted using the MuJoCo physics engine, known for its efficiency and accuracy, and leveraging the JAX framework for computations due to its just-in-time (JIT) compilation and automatic differentiation capabilities. The robotic platform modeled was a composite system:
- **Unitree H1 Humanoid:** Served as the mobile base, with 12 degrees of freedom (DoF) for its legs and torso.
- **Supernumerary Limbs (SLs):** Two Kinova Gen3 robotic arms, each with 7 DoF, mounted on a custom backpack on the H1’s torso. Each arm was equipped with a 2F-85 gripper.
The total system had 26 actuated DoF. A simplification in the simulation was that actuator torques were not saturated to physical limits, allowing focus on algorithm performance.

The **hierarchical control framework** decoupled the control problem into two layers:
- **Low-Level Locomotion Policy:** A Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)-based policy responsible for generating the fundamental walking gait, controlling the humanoid's legs and lower torso.
- **High-Level Balancing Controller:** A model-based controller dedicated to active stability augmentation, dynamically adjusting the SLs' poses to counteract perturbations.

#### 3.2. Low-Level Control: DRL for Locomotion

The humanoid’s walking policy was trained using **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, an actor-critic DRL algorithm. The policy network consisted of three hidden layers with neurons and a Tanh activation function, aiming to maximize the expected total discounted reward.
- **Imitation Learning:** To guide the learning, an expert walking trajectory was sourced from the Ubisoft La Forge Animation Dataset (LAFAN1). The agent was rewarded for mimicking this motion.
- **Reward Function:** A weighted sum of components followed the "Survive First, Imitate Later" principle. This included a high survival reward (5.0 per step) and stability penalty (4.0 for falling), alongside imitation terms for joint position, velocity, and key body site orientations and velocities.
- **Curriculum Learning:** Over 500 million training timesteps, task difficulty was gradually increased through two parallel ramps:
 1. **Payload Randomization:** The mass of the SLs' payload incrementally increased from negligible to 19-30 kg.
 2. **Arm Pose Curriculum:** The static pose of the SLs transitioned from a neutral, close-to-body position to a challenging forward-reaching posture over four phases.

#### 3.3. High-Level Control: Model-Based Dynamic Balancing

This controller operated independently of the DRL agent, using a simplified rigid-body dynamics model for active stability.
- **State Estimation:** Real-time metrics were calculated:
 - **Center of Mass (CoM):** Weighted average of all body links' positions.
 - **Center of Support (CoS):** Calculated based on ground contact forces. During single support, it was the geometric center of the stance foot. During double support, it was the force-weighted average of individual Centers of Pressure (CoP) for each foot.
- **Balancing Controller:** A reactive strategy used the vector difference `d_xy = CoM_xy - CoS_xy` as a dynamic stability indicator. The target joint angles (`q_arm_target`) of the SLs were modulated from a constant neutral base pose (`q_arm_base`) proportionally to `d_xy` via a gain matrix `K_arm_p`. A Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller then generated the required torques (`τ_arm`) to drive the SL joints towards `q_arm_target`. Gains were empirically tuned for critically damped behavior.
- **Control Fusion:** The calculated `τ_arm` for the SLs overrode the DRL policy's output for those actuators, while the DRL policy directly controlled the humanoid’s leg and torso actuators.

#### 3.4. Experimental Evaluation

The framework's effectiveness was validated through quantitative analysis across three experimental scenarios:
1. **Baseline Walking:** Unladen humanoid, controlled solely by the DRL policy trained on a base curriculum (no payload, neutral arms). This served as the unperturbed gait benchmark.
2. **Static Payload:** Humanoid walking with SLs locked in a fixed, forward-reaching pose. The DRL policy was fully trained with the complete curriculum, but the high-level balancing controller was disabled. This scenario isolated the DRL policy's performance under a constant, challenging load.
3. **Dynamic Balancing:** The full hierarchical framework was active, with the fully trained DRL policy controlling locomotion and the high-level controller dynamically actuating the SLs for balance.

Key performance metrics included:
- **CoM Trajectory Similarity:** Quantified using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance against the baseline.
- **Stability Recovery:** Assessed by the CoM-CoS distance and a new metric, the Gait Cycle Stability Minimum (GCSM), which measures the minimum CoM-CoS distance during the recovery phase of a gait cycle.
- **Bipedal Coordination:** Analyzed through a phase-plane plot of vertical Ground Reaction Forces (GRF), with "Orientation Error" quantifying deviation from an ideal anti-phase relationship.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the hierarchical control framework yielded several key findings regarding training performance, Center of Mass (CoM) trajectory, dynamic balance modulation, and gait coordination.

#### 4.1. DRL Training Performance

The Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent was trained for 500 million environment steps. The curriculum learning strategy successfully guided the agent’s adaptation to increasing task difficulties, which involved progressive increases in payload mass and adjustments to the SL arm poses. Both the mean episode return (task achievement) and mean episode length (stability) showed consistent upward trends. Periodic dips observed in these metrics corresponded directly to the scheduled increases in curriculum difficulty, followed by rapid recovery and continued improvement. This indicated that the curriculum strategy was effective in enabling the policy to adapt to the challenging dynamics introduced by the supernumerary limbs (SLs).

#### 4.2. Center of Mass Trajectory Analysis

To assess the impact of different control strategies on the overall walking pattern, the robot’s CoM trajectory was analyzed across the three scenarios. The "Baseline Walking" scenario, without SLs, served as the reference. Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance was used to quantify the similarity of the "Static Payload" and "Dynamic Balancing" trajectories to this baseline, with lower values indicating higher similarity in dynamic pattern.
- The "Static Payload" scenario resulted in a DTW distance of 123.71 from the baseline.
- The "Dynamic Balancing" scenario achieved a DTW distance of 65.54 from the baseline, representing an approximate 47% reduction compared to the static payload condition.
This finding indicated that the active balancing controller effectively mitigated the high-frequency disturbances introduced by the SLs. While all trajectories diverged in absolute path over time, the dynamic balancing strategy preserved the fundamental dynamic characteristics and rhythm of the unladen gait more effectively than merely carrying a static payload.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Dynamic Balance Modulation

Walking involves continuous modulation of balance, characterized by oscillations in the horizontal CoM-CoS distance, which increases during the swing phase and decreases upon foot-strike. The effectiveness of re-stabilization within each gait cycle was quantified using the Gait Cycle Stability Minimum (GCSM), defined as the minimum CoM-CoS distance achieved during the recovery phase of a cycle. A lower GCSM value indicates more effective and complete recovery.
- The "Dynamic Balancing" scenario consistently achieved the lowest median GCSM value.
- Analysis of averaged, normalized recovery cycle dynamics showed that the "Dynamic Balancing" curve reached a significantly lower trough compared to the other scenarios.
These results demonstrate that the active SL controller enables the robot to manage dynamic fluctuations more effectively, achieving a more stable state at the critical recovery point within each gait cycle. This indicated a transformation of the SLs from a destabilizing element to a functional asset for active re-stabilization.

#### 4.4. Exploratory Analysis of Gait Coordination

An exploratory phase-plane analysis of vertical Ground Reaction Forces (GRF) was conducted to examine gait coordination. An ideal bipedal gait exhibits an anti-phase relationship between left and right foot GRFs, corresponding to a 135° orientation in the phase plane. "Orientation Error" quantified the deviation from this ideal.
- The baseline gait, learned through imitation, exhibited an Orientation Error of 8.37°, indicating inherent sub-optimality.
- The "Static Payload" scenario showed a reduction in error to 5.59°. This was hypothesized to be due to the fixed, sloping-downward pose of the SLs lowering the robot’s overall center of mass, thereby increasing passive stability and allowing the DRL policy to execute a more coordinated gait.
- The "Dynamic Balancing" scenario achieved the lowest Orientation Error among the three conditions at 1.95°.
This trend suggested that by offloading the primary balancing task to the high-level controller, the low-level locomotion policy could better adhere to its learned coordination pattern. While consistent with the study’s main findings, the authors noted that more rigorous statistical analysis would be required to confirm the significance of these coordination differences.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a hierarchical control framework for humanoid robots equipped with supernumerary limbs (SLs), demonstrating a viable approach to managing the complex interplay between locomotion and dynamic stability. The study's significance lies in its quantitative validation that a decoupled control strategy can effectively mitigate internal dynamic disturbances arising from the mass and movement of SLs, thereby enabling stable locomotion for such augmented humanoids.

The key findings highlight several important implications:
- **Enhanced Stability and Gait Preservation:** The significant reduction in Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance for the Center of Mass (CoM) trajectory and the lower Gait Cycle Stability Minimum (GCSM) achieved under active dynamic balancing demonstrate that the hierarchical framework enhances intra-cycle stability and preserves the fundamental dynamic characteristics of the learned gait. This indicates that the SLs can be transformed from a destabilizing payload into functional assets for active re-stabilization.
- **Practical Framework for Complex Robotics:** The successful integration of learning-based locomotion (DRL with curriculum learning) and model-based balancing exemplifies a practical strategy for tackling multifaceted robotics challenges. This hybrid approach leverages the strengths of both paradigms: DRL for complex gait generation and model-based control for reliable, real-time balance regulation.
- **Increased Versatility of Supernumerary Limbs:** By enabling SLs to perform dual functions—potentially manipulation and active balance assistance—the research contributes to the development of more versatile and capable humanoid robots. This could significantly enhance a robot's utility in human-centric environments, allowing it to perform complex tasks requiring both stability and dexterity.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The study establishes a foundational baseline for stable locomotion with heavy, dynamic loads. It identifies the "sim-to-real" gap and the current scope limitation (SLs solely for balancing) as critical areas for future work, paving the way for the deployment of such systems on physical hardware and the development of more sophisticated, unified controllers that integrate manipulation and balance objectives.

In summary, this work provides a demonstrable solution for a critical challenge in advanced robotics, moving closer to the realization of highly versatile humanoid platforms capable of stable and robust operation in dynamic environments. The hierarchical framework offers a principled approach to managing complexity and opens avenues for future advancements in intelligent robot control.
