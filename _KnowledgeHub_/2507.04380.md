# Transferring Visual Explainability of Self-Explaining Models through Task Arithmetic

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at STAIR Lab and ZOZO Research developed a method using task arithmetic to transfer visual explainability to self-explaining models without requiring target domain explanation data. The approach achieves explanation quality comparable to Kernel SHAP with 150 times fewer inferences, while maintaining classification accuracy at 90% of a prediction-only baseline model.

## Problem

- Developing and deploying visual explainability in AI systems faces high computational and labeling costs.
- Training self-explaining models, while efficient at inference, demands expensive ground-truth explanation data or numerous model passes.
- Post-hoc explanation methods are computationally intensive during inference, limiting their use in real-time or resource-constrained applications.

## Method

- The method uses ==task arithmetic== to algebraically extract and transfer an =='explainability vector'== from a source self-explaining model to a target domain model.
- It extends vision-language pretrained models (e.g., CLIP ViT-B/32) to generate both predictions and ==patch-level attributions== in a single forward pass.
- Target model parameters are constructed by combining a target-specific ==classification task vector== with the source-learned explainability vector, bypassing the need for explanation data in the target domain.

## Results

- The transferred models maintained high classification accuracy (average **0.876**) close to target-finetuned prediction-only models (**0.890**), while significantly improving explanation quality (e.g., E-RMSE decreased from **1.599** to **0.943**).
- Explanation generation achieved IoU@10 scores comparable to Kernel SHAP with approximately **150 times** fewer model inferences, demonstrating a substantial reduction in computational cost.
- An explainability vector learned from ImageNet+X proved robust, improving explanation quality across 9 out of 10 diverse target datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An 'explainability vector' can be mathematically isolated and transferred between models using task arithmetic, representing specific knowledge for explanation generation.
> - This explainability knowledge can be effectively transferred to models in a target domain that were only trained for prediction, without requiring ground-truth explanations for the target tasks.
> - The similarity between classification-only task vectors from source and target domains can moderately predict the success of explainability transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual explainability for machine learning models is a field of increasing importance, providing insights into model decision-making across various applications such as medical image diagnosis, autonomous driving, and model debugging. Current approaches to generating visual explanations generally fall into two categories: post-hoc explanation methods and self-explaining models.

Post-hoc methods, such as SHAP and Grad-CAM, are applied after a model has been trained. While effective, they often incur significant computational costs, requiring numerous model inferences (e.g., hundreds for SHAP) or gradient calculations (for Grad-CAM), which can be resource-intensive. Self-explaining models, conversely, are designed to produce both predictions and explanations within a single inference, offering efficiency during the inference phase. However, their training typically demands substantial resources, including the generation of ground-truth explanation data or extensive forward computations to learn these explanations. This training overhead presents a barrier for users who already possess trained prediction-only models and wish to augment them with explainability without retraining from scratch.

Existing research has explored model editing techniques like "task arithmetic," which allows for the composition or removal of learned capabilities by manipulating task vectors in a model's parameter space. This framework has been applied to various challenges, including adding mathematical reasoning, improving fairness, and mitigating bias. However, its potential for transferring visual explainability across different domains has not been thoroughly investigated.

In the realm of self-explaining vision models, various architectures have been proposed to improve the faithfulness and computational efficiency of explanations. Some methods directly aim to produce Shapley value-based explanations through objective function design or specific network architectures, while amortized explainers learn to mimic post-hoc explainers efficiently. While these approaches enhance explanation efficiency, they often specialize in certain types of explanations or still require significant initial training costs.

Regarding visual explainability transfer, methods like Transferrable Vision Explainer (TVE) pretrain encoders for meta-attributes, aiming to transfer pretrained explainability, but TVE operates as an amortized explainer, addressing a different problem. Other studies have explored transferring attention patterns in Vision Transformers (ViTs), which can be interpreted as explanations. However, such attention transfer has shown limited effectiveness when teacher and student models are trained on different domains.

This research addresses the gap between the computational efficiency of self-explaining models and their high training costs, particularly for users with existing prediction-only models. It proposes to leverage the task arithmetic framework to transfer visual explainability from a source domain, where explanation supervision is available, to prediction-only models in a target domain, *without requiring additional training* in the target domain. By defining an "explainability vector," the work aims to enable efficient and generalizable explainability for existing models, overcoming the limitations of prior domain-specific transfer methods and the expense of training new self-explaining models from scratch.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing demand for transparent and interpretable artificial intelligence systems, especially in high-stakes domains where understanding model decisions is critical. Visual explainability, which provides human-understandable insights into model outputs, is vital for applications ranging from medical diagnostics and autonomous driving to manufacturing quality control and cybersecurity analysis. Explanations facilitate trust, enable effective debugging, and support informed decision-making.

Despite the recognized value of explanations, their generation frequently entails significant computational overhead. Traditional post-hoc explanation methods, while versatile, can be resource-intensive. For example, methods like SHAP often require hundreds to thousands of model inferences for a single image explanation, leading to high latency. Grad-CAM, while faster, necessitates calculating gradients, which demands backpropagation resources. These computational demands can render post-hoc explanations impractical in real-time or resource-constrained environments.

Self-explaining models offer an alternative by integrating explanation generation into the model's forward pass, thus achieving high inference-time efficiency. However, the development of these models presents its own set of challenges. Training self-explaining models typically requires vast amounts of data with ground-truth explanations, which are often costly and labor-intensive to acquire or generate. This substantial upfront cost, both in terms of data labeling and computational training, dissuades many users who already possess well-performing prediction-only models from investing in new self-explaining architectures.

Given these challenges, the key objective of this study is to efficiently equip prediction-only models with visual explainability *without the need for additional training or ground-truth explanation data in the target domain*. The researchers aim to enable the conversion of existing, trained prediction-only models into self-explainable models with minimal cost and effort.

To achieve this, the research proposes to:
1. **Develop a mechanism to transfer visual explanation capabilities** learned in a source domain (where explanation supervision is available) to prediction-only models operating in a target domain.
2. **Utilize the task arithmetic framework** as the underlying principle for this transfer, which allows for algebraic manipulation of model parameters to compose new capabilities.
3. **Define an "explainability vector"** within this framework, representing the specific knowledge required to generate explanations, isolated from general classification knowledge.
4. **Design a self-explaining model architecture** that is an extension of common Vision Transformer-based prediction-only models, ensuring broad compatibility and the ability to leverage existing pretrained models.

By pursuing these objectives, the study aims to lower the barrier for integrating explainability into deployed AI systems, providing a computationally efficient and practically viable solution for enhancing model transparency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method leverages the task arithmetic framework to transfer visual explanation capabilities. It introduces a self-explaining model architecture and defines an "explainability vector" to facilitate this transfer across different domains.

#### 3.1. Model Architecture

The self-explaining model is designed as an extension of Vision Transformer (ViT)-based vision-language models (VLMs), such as CLIP, to enable zero-shot classification and the inference of patch-level attributions. The model consists of two main components:
- **Feature Extractor ($f_\theta$)**: A Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture that processes input images divided into $M$ patches. It outputs $M+1$ K-dimensional feature vectors, including one for a special [CLS] token. The learnable parameters $\theta$ reside within this extractor.
- **Domain-Specific Head ($g_W$)**: A linear function that maps the K-dimensional feature vectors to task-specific outputs. The weights $W$ of this head are derived from text embeddings of class names (e.g., using a VLM's text encoder) and are fixed during training.
 - The [CLS] token's feature vector is mapped to class logits ($l \in R^C$).
 - Each patch's feature vector is mapped to unnormalized patch-level attributions ($\tilde{\phi}_m \in R^C$).
The key distinction from a prediction-only VLM-based classifier is that the domain-specific head is utilized for predicting both logits and patch-level attributions. This design allows the model to reuse pretrained VLM parameters as initial base parameters ($\theta_{base}$).

#### 3.2. Learning Classification Capability and Explainability

Explainability is acquired by fine-tuning the self-explaining model in a source domain where ground-truth patch-level attributions are available.
- **Source Dataset ($D_S$)**: Comprises labeled images with ground-truth class labels ($y^{(n)}$) and corresponding ground-truth patch-level attributions ($\phi^{(n)}$). These attributions can be generated by human annotators or computationally expensive post-hoc methods like Kernel SHAP or Grad-CAM. The availability of such supervision is assumed only in the source domain due to its cost.
- **Optimization Objective**: The model parameters are learned by minimizing a combined loss function:
 $L_\alpha(\theta; D_S) = \alpha L_{cls}(\theta; D_S) + (1-\alpha)L_{exp}(\theta; D_S)$
 - $L_{cls}$: Standard cross-entropy loss for classification.
 - $L_{exp}$: An L2 loss between normalized predicted patch-level attributions (for the ground-truth class) and normalized ground-truth attributions. Normalization involves subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation.
 - $\alpha$: A hyperparameter balancing the importance of classification loss and explanation loss.
- **Resulting Parameters**:
 - When $\alpha=1$, the model is fine-tuned only for classification, yielding parameters $\theta_{S,ft}$.
 - When $0 < \alpha < 1$, the model is fine-tuned for both classification and explanation, yielding parameters $\theta_{S,ft\star}$.

#### 3.3. Transferring Visual Explainability

For the target domain, the crucial assumption is the *unavailability* of ground-truth patch-level attributions. However, a prediction-only model, fine-tuned solely for classification in the target domain, can be obtained, yielding parameters $\theta_{T,ft}$. The objective is to transfer the explainability from the source domain to $\theta_{T,ft}$ to approximate a model $\theta_{T,ft\star}$ (which would have both classification and explanation capabilities in the target domain) without direct supervision.

The method employs task arithmetic based on an analogy:
"prediction-only is to prediction-with-explanation in the source domain as prediction-only is to prediction-with-explanation in the target domain."
This translates to an approximate relationship between task vectors:
$\tau_{S,ft\star} - \tau_{S,ft} \approx \tau_{T,ft\star} - \tau_{T,ft}$
where $\tau_X = \theta_X - \theta_{base}$ represents a task vector.
Defining *explainability vectors* as $\tau_{S,\star} = \theta_{S,ft\star} - \theta_{S,ft}$ (source) and $\tau_{T,\star} = \theta_{T,ft\star} - \theta_{T,ft}$ (target), the core idea is that if these explainability vectors are similar ($\tau_{S,\star} = \tau_{T,\star}$), then the target model with explainability ($\theta_{T,ft\star}$) can be constructed.

The proposed method obtains the transferred model parameters $\tilde{\theta}_{T,ft\star}$ using the following equation:
$\tilde{\theta}_{T,ft\star} = \theta_{base} + \lambda_1 \tau_{T,ft} + \lambda_2 \tau_{S,\star}$
Here, $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are scaling coefficients for classification and explainability capabilities, respectively. $\lambda_1$ is typically set to 1.0 to preserve the target model's classification performance, while $\lambda_2$ is a tunable hyperparameter. This framework allows for injecting the learned explainability vector from the source domain into a target-domain prediction-only model without requiring additional training or explanation data in the target domain.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Interpretation

The explainability vector $\tau_{S,\star}$ can be interpreted as an optimal perturbation to $\theta_{S,ft}$ that minimizes the combined loss under a local quadratic approximation. Specifically, $\tau_{S,\star} \approx - \frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha} (H_S)^{-1} g_S$, where $H_S$ is the Hessian of the classification loss and $g_S$ is the gradient of the explanation loss at $\theta_{S,ft}$. This indicates that $\tau_{S,\star}$ modifies the model parameters to improve explanation quality while regularizing against significant classification performance degradation. The risk of classification accuracy drop in the target domain is related to the alignment of $\tau_{S,\star}$ with the eigenvectors of the target domain's Hessian matrix $H_T$. The similarity between $\tau_{S,ft}$ and $\tau_{T,ft}$ can serve as an indirect predictor for the success of explainability transfer, although empirical analysis suggests this correlation is moderate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed explainability transfer method across various image classification datasets and model scales.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

Experiments primarily utilized CLIP with a ViT-B/32 backbone, initialized with parameters pretrained on DataComp-1B. The input images were 224x224 pixels, divided into 49 patches (32x32 pixels each). The classification scaling coefficient ($\lambda_1$) was fixed at 1.0, while the balance between classification and explanation losses ($\alpha$) and the explainability scaling coefficient ($\lambda_2$) were tuned. Evaluation metrics included classification accuracy, Explanation Root Mean Squared Error (E-RMSE), Kendall Rank Correlation Coefficient (Rank Corr.), and Intersection over Union (IoU) at top-K patches (IoU@1, IoU@5, IoU@10). Ground-truth patch-level attributions for all datasets were generated using Kernel SHAP with 500 perturbations.

#### 4.2. Transferring Explainability Between Various Domain Datasets

The first set of experiments involved 56 pairs of datasets from a selection of eight benchmark image classification datasets (Cars, DTD, EuroSAT, GTSRB, RESISC45, SUN397, SVHN, MNIST).
- **Accuracy and Explanation Error Trade-off**: For the majority of dataset pairs, the proposed method successfully reduced E-RMSE (indicating improved explanation quality) while largely maintaining classification accuracy. Specifically, most transfers preserved at least 90% of the accuracy achieved by models fine-tuned solely on the target domain.
- **Domain Relation**: The effectiveness of transfer was observed to be sensitive to the relatedness of the source and target domains. For instance, using SUN397 (a large, diverse scene image dataset) as a source consistently led to successful transfers, suggesting it learns generalizable explanatory capabilities. Conversely, transfers between less-related domains (e.g., MNIST to Cars) sometimes resulted in a noticeable decrease in accuracy. However, transfers between highly related domains (e.g., MNIST to SVHN) showed substantial improvements in E-RMSE while maintaining or improving accuracy.

#### 4.3. Transferring Explainability Learned on ImageNet

To investigate the potential for a universal explainability vector, an ImageNet+X dataset was constructed by augmenting ImageNet-1k with Kernel SHAP-generated patch-level attributions. The transfer was then evaluated on 10 diverse target datasets (the initial eight plus CIFAR100 and STL10).

- **Quantitative Results (ViT-B/32)**:
 - The proposed method ($\tilde{\theta}_{T,ft\star}$) achieved the second-best average accuracy (0.886 $\pm$ 0.113) among all evaluated models, closely trailing the target-finetuned prediction-only model ($\theta_{T,ft}$, 0.890 $\pm$ 0.106).
 - Crucially, the proposed method consistently achieved the *best average explainability performance* across all metrics (lowest E-RMSE: 1.125 $\pm$ 0.116; highest Rank Corr., IoU@1, IoU@5, IoU@10).
 - This performance represents a notable improvement in explainability compared to models without explanation supervision ($\theta_{base}$, $\theta_{S,ft}$, $\theta_{T,ft}$) while preserving high classification accuracy.
 - The model fine-tuned on ImageNet+X ($\theta_{S,ft\star}$) showed good explainability but suffered from significantly lower classification accuracy (0.353 $\pm$ 0.243), rendering it impractical for combined tasks. The proposed method effectively combined the high accuracy of $\theta_{T,ft}$ with the explanation capability of $\theta_{S,ft\star}$.

- **Impact of Scaling Coefficient $\lambda_2$**: Analysis of $\lambda_2$ (the explainability scaling coefficient) revealed that increasing its value generally improved explainability scores (lower E-RMSE, higher IoU@10) across target datasets, often plateauing around $\lambda_2 = 0.8$. This increase in $\lambda_2$ also tended to slightly decrease classification accuracy, demonstrating a controllable trade-off. However, accuracy degradation was typically kept within 5% for $\lambda_2 \leq 1.0$, allowing for practical tuning to achieve a balance.

- **Qualitative Evaluation**: Visualizations of the top-10 highlighted patches demonstrated that the proposed method produced attributions that were qualitatively more similar to the ground-truth explanations compared to baseline models lacking explanation supervision. This supported the quantitative findings regarding improved explanation quality.

- **Comparison with Kernel SHAP**: The proposed self-explaining model, which generates explanations in a single forward pass, was compared against Kernel SHAP (used to generate ground truth, requiring hundreds to thousands of inferences).
 - For most explainability metrics (E-RMSE, Rank Corr., IoU@5, IoU@10), the proposed method substantially outperformed Kernel SHAP, even when Kernel SHAP used 1,000 perturbations. This highlights the significant computational efficiency advantage.
 - For IoU@1, the proposed method achieved performance equivalent to Kernel SHAP with approximately 300 perturbations, demonstrating its particular effectiveness in identifying the most crucial patch locations efficiently.

#### 4.4. Experiments with ViT-L/14 (Appendix D)

Experiments with a larger ViT-L/14 backbone (using Grad-CAM for ground truth attributions) yielded consistent results.
- The proposed method achieved the second-best accuracy (0.928 $\pm$ 0.072) and either the best or second-best explainability performance, similar to the ViT-B/32 results.
- Classification accuracy was generally higher than with ViT-B/32 due to increased model capacity.
- Explainability scores were slightly lower for ViT-L/14, attributed to potentially lower quality of Grad-CAM-generated ground truths and the increased difficulty of attribution prediction with higher patch resolution (256 patches vs. 49).

#### 4.5. Impact of Similarity of Explainability Vectors (Appendix C)

A statistically significant negative correlation (Pearson's r = -0.51, p < 0.01) was observed between the cosine similarity of source and target explainability vectors ($\tau_{S,\star}$ and $\tau_{T,\star}$) and E-RMSE, indicating that higher vector similarity leads to better explanation transfer. While the similarity of classification task vectors ($\tau_{S,ft}$ and $\tau_{T,ft}$) showed a significant positive correlation (r = 0.73, p < 0.01) with the explainability vector similarity, its correlation with E-RMSE was weaker (r = -0.32, p < 0.01). This suggests that while $\tau_{S,ft}$ and $\tau_{T,ft}$ similarity can be an indicator, it should be used cautiously for predicting transfer success.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel and practical method for endowing prediction-only models with visual explainability using task arithmetic, a significant contribution to the field of interpretable AI. The work's primary significance lies in its ability to circumvent the traditionally high costs associated with training self-explaining models from scratch or generating explanations via computationally intensive post-hoc methods.

Key aspects of its significance and potential impact include:

1. **Reduced Barrier to Explainable AI Adoption**: By demonstrating that visual explainability can be transferred to existing prediction-only models without additional training in the target domain, the method significantly lowers the entry barrier for organizations and researchers to integrate explainable AI into their deployed systems. This is particularly valuable for users who have already invested in training high-performance prediction models but lack the resources or expertise to retrain for explainability.
2. **Computational Efficiency**: The proposed self-explaining models generate patch-level attributions in a single forward pass, providing a substantial computational advantage over post-hoc methods like Kernel SHAP, which can require hundreds to thousands of inferences. This efficiency makes real-time explanation generation feasible for applications such as autonomous driving, medical diagnostics, or manufacturing anomaly detection, where immediate insights are critical.
3. **Generalizability of Explainability**: The finding that an explainability vector learned from a large, diverse source dataset like ImageNet+X can effectively transfer explainability to models across a wide range of target domains suggests the potential for "universal" explainability knowledge. This could lead to pre-trained explainability modules that are broadly applicable, reducing the need for domain-specific explanation data collection.
4. **Practical Trade-off Management**: The ability to tune the scaling coefficient ($\lambda_2$) for explainability allows practitioners to manage the inherent trade-off between explanation quality and classification accuracy. This flexibility ensures that the method can be adapted to various application requirements, prioritizing either high fidelity explanations or robust classification performance as needed.
5. **Foundation for Future Research**: The introduction of the "explainability vector" as a specific type of task vector opens new avenues for research within the task arithmetic framework. Future work could explore the construction of more robust or specialized explainability vectors, the theoretical properties governing their transferability, or their application to other modalities and explanation types beyond visual patch-level attributions. The potential to construct a truly universal explainability vector from even larger-scale datasets is a direct implication of this work.

In summary, this research offers a pragmatic and efficient solution for bringing explainability to existing deep learning models, making AI more transparent and accessible in practical deployments.
