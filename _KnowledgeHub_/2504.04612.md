# Tool-as-Interface: Learning Robot Policies from Observing Human Tool Use

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Illinois, UT Austin, and Columbia University developed the "Tool-as-Interface" framework, enabling robots to acquire complex tool-use skills from natural human video demonstrations. This approach leverages 3D scene reconstruction and tool-centric action representations to bridge human-robot embodiment and viewpoint gaps, achieving higher success rates, smoother trajectories, and up to 81% faster data collection compared to teleoperation methods across tasks like hammering and scooping.

## Problem

- Robot manipulation often relies on specialized, costly, and slow hardware for data collection, limiting scalability and accessibility for learning complex tasks.
- Learning from natural human videos introduces challenges like significant viewpoint variations and embodiment gaps between human demonstrators and robotic platforms.
- Transferring dynamic, high-precision, and contact-rich tool-use skills from human demonstrations to robots remains difficult with existing imitation learning paradigms.

## Method

- The **Tool-as-Interface** framework uses low-cost RGB cameras to collect natural human tool-use demonstrations, abstracting actions to the tool's pose to simplify skill transfer across embodiments.
- A perception pipeline incorporates ==Grounded-SAM== for visual alignment by masking embodiment-specific elements and uses ==3D-aware view augmentation== (==MASt3R== and ==Gaussian splatting==) to synthesize robust, varied viewpoints.
- A task-frame, tool-centric 6D action representation, invariant to camera and robot base movements, is learned by a ==diffusion model==, enabling direct command generation for robot end-effectors.

## Results

- The framework achieved consistently higher success rates (e.g., **13/13** for nail hammering) and produced smoother, more efficient trajectories compared to teleoperation and handheld gripper baselines across five real-world tool-use tasks.
- Policies demonstrated robustness to severe camera shaking, robot base movements, and real-time human perturbations, maintaining task success in challenging, dynamic environments.
- Data collection was significantly accelerated (up to **81%** faster than teleoperation) and made more affordable by using only ubiquitous RGB cameras, eliminating the need for specialized, expensive hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Utilizing the tool as a common 'interface' between humans and robots effectively simplifies the transfer of complex manipulation skills by abstracting away morphological differences.
> - Combining 3D scene reconstruction with perception alignment and view augmentation is crucial for training visuomotor policies that are robust to diverse camera viewpoints and embodiment disparities.
> - A task-space, tool-centric action representation ensures policies are invariant to variations in camera pose or robot base configuration, enhancing generalization in dynamic environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Current State of Robot Manipulation and Tool Use

Robot manipulation typically involves fundamental operations such as grasping and pick-and-place tasks. While these foundational skills are important, expanding robot capabilities to perform more complex, real-world tasks often necessitates the use of tools. Tool use allows robots to extend their physical interactions and engage with environments in more sophisticated ways. The ability of robots to effectively use diverse tools in dynamic environments is a significant area of research, moving beyond basic object handling.

#### 1.2. Challenges in Imitation Learning

Imitation Learning (IL) has emerged as a method for robots to acquire skills by observing human demonstrations. Prior approaches to collecting demonstration data for IL include teleoperation platforms and specialized hand-held grippers. These methods typically offer precise supervision but often require expensive, specialized hardware, 3D-printed tools, or expert calibration. Such requirements can limit scalability across different users and environments, making data collection slow and susceptible to issues like delay sensitivity, particularly in dynamic tasks.

An alternative is to leverage natural human manipulation videos, which are abundant and do not require specialized hardware for collection. However, learning from such raw human video data introduces distinct challenges for robot learning. These include:
- **Viewpoint Variations**: Differences in camera angles and perspectives between human demonstrations and robot deployment can lead to policies that are not robust to changes in observation.
- **Embodiment Gaps**: The morphological and kinematic differences between human demonstrators and robotic systems create a discrepancy in how actions are executed and perceived. This embodiment mismatch can hinder the direct transfer of skills.

#### 1.3. Gaps Addressed by This Work

This research addresses the aforementioned challenges by proposing a framework that aims to bridge the gap between natural human video demonstrations and effective robot policies for tool use. Specifically, it seeks to:
1. **Improve Scalability and Cost-Effectiveness of Data Collection**: By using widely available, low-cost cameras (e.g., RGB cameras) instead of specialized teleoperation equipment or custom grippers, the framework aims to reduce the financial and logistical barriers to collecting large, diverse datasets.
2. **Enhance Policy Robustness to Viewpoint Variations**: The work integrates 3D scene reconstruction and novel view synthesis to train policies that are invariant to changes in camera viewpoint, thereby improving generalization in dynamic environments.
3. **Mitigate Human-Robot Embodiment Gaps**: It employs specific perception and action representations—namely, segmented observations and tool-centric, task-space actions—to minimize the impact of morphological differences between humans and robots, enabling more seamless policy transfer.
4. **Enable Learning of Dynamic and High-Precision Tool-Use Skills**: The framework aims to leverage the natural fluidity and speed of human demonstrations to teach robots complex, dynamic, and contact-rich tool-use tasks that are often difficult for teleoperation systems to capture effectively.

By addressing these gaps, the work contributes to making robotic tool-use learning more accessible, efficient, and robust for a wider range of real-world applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to develop a scalable data collection and policy learning framework that enables robots to acquire diverse tool-use skills from observing human manipulation. The core motivations driving this work include:

1. **Efficient and Scalable Data Collection**: The current landscape of robot learning often relies on teleoperation or specialized hardware for data collection, which is typically slow, costly, and limited in scalability. The authors aim to establish a method that allows for rapid, intuitive, and low-cost data collection from natural human manipulation, thereby facilitating the creation of extensive datasets necessary for complex robot learning.
2. **Robustness to Environmental and Observational Variations**: Real-world robot operation often involves dynamic conditions, such as moving cameras or robot bases, and varying object configurations. A key objective is to train policies that are robust to these perturbations, ensuring consistent performance even under challenging and unconstrained environments.
3. **Bridging the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap**: Human demonstrations, while rich in skill information, come from a different embodiment than a robot. This mismatch can prevent direct skill transfer. The motivation is to devise representations for both observations and actions that minimize this gap, allowing robots to effectively learn from human videos without requiring extensive robot-specific demonstrations.
4. **Enabling Complex and Dynamic Tool Use**: Many real-world tasks involving tools require high precision, dynamic movements, and complex interactions (e.g., contact-rich manipulation, fast actions). The goal is to enable robots to learn and execute these types of demanding tasks, which are often difficult to teach through conventional teleoperation methods due to latency or lack of tactile feedback.
5. **Democratization of Robot Learning**: By reducing the reliance on specialized, expensive hardware and expert users, the framework aims to make robot learning more accessible and affordable, allowing a broader community to contribute to and benefit from advancements in robotic manipulation. This involves utilizing ubiquitous equipment, such as RGB cameras, for data collection.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for a practical, robust, and scalable solution to train robots for complex tool-use tasks by effectively harnessing the wealth of human demonstration data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Tool-as-Interface" framework addresses the challenges of learning robot policies from human videos by proposing specific mechanisms for data collection, perception alignment, 3D-aware view augmentation, and tool-centric action representation.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where a robot learns a policy $\pi: O_r \to A$. The robot's observation space ($O_r$) includes a single-view RGB image ($I_r$) and proprioceptive data ($x_r$). The policy is trained using an imitation dataset of human demonstrations, $D = (O_h^0, O_h^1, \dots)_N^{n=1}$, where each $O_h = \{I_h^{v1}, I_h^{v2}\}$ consists of two RGB images from different viewpoints. Actions ($a$) are 6D poses ($SE(3)$) inferred from the human data. A key assumption is that the tool is rigidly attached to the human hand (implicitly) and the robot end-effector (explicitly), with a fixed transformation estimated before deployment. This allows the robot to reproduce human-demonstrated tool trajectories.

#### 3.2. Tool-Centric Demonstrations

The framework leverages the commonality of physical tools between humans and robots. Tools serve as an "interface" for interaction, simplifying the transfer of human demonstrations. This approach focuses on abstracting actions to the tool's pose, thereby reducing dependence on the specific morphology of the demonstrator (human hand) or the robot. This abstraction promotes policy generalization across different embodiments and simplifies data collection, as humans can naturally manipulate tools without additional instrumentation. During deployment, the robot either rigidly grasps the tool or attaches it using a custom fast tool changer.

#### 3.3. Perception Alignment Across Embodiments

To bridge the visual differences between human and robot demonstrations, the framework aligns observations within a shared visual space. This is achieved using a feature extraction function $g: I_h \cup I_r \to I_s$. Grounded-SAM is instantiated as $g$, employing prompts like "human hand" during training to mask out human hands and "robot arm" during deployment to mask out the robot's arm. This masking ensures that only task-relevant visual information, such as the tool and objects, remains visible across both phases, thereby minimizing embodiment-specific visual biases and improving generalization.

#### 3.4. 3D-Aware View Augmentation

To address viewpoint variations and the limitations of single-camera setups (scale ambiguity, limited 3D perception), the framework incorporates 3D-aware view augmentation:
- **3D Reconstruction**: The MASt3R model is used to reconstruct accurate 3D environments from just two RGB images, eliminating the need for depth sensors or known camera intrinsics/extrinsics. Two cameras are used to resolve scale ambiguity.
- **View Synthesis and Augmentation**: 3D Gaussian splatting is then applied to synthesize novel viewpoints from the reconstructed scene. This process augments the training data, providing the policy with a broader distribution of visual perspectives and enhancing robustness to viewpoint changes.
- **Random Cropping**: To further improve robustness and generalization, random cropping is applied to the images, exposing the policy to minor visual perturbations and partial views.

#### 3.5. Tool-Centric Action Representation and Policy Deployment

A task-frame, tool-centric action representation, $T_{tool}^{task}$, is proposed. This representation describes the tool's motion independently of the human or robot morphology, camera pose, or robot base configuration, making it invariant to these variations.
- **Action Inference**: The tool's 6D pose is first estimated in the camera frame ($T_{tool}^{camera}$) using a 6D pose estimation model like FoundationPose. This pose is then transformed into the task frame: $T_{tool}^{task} = T_{camera}^{task} T_{tool}^{camera}$, where $T_{camera}^{task}$ is the transformation from the camera to the task frame.
- **Policy Learning**: A diffusion model is trained as the visuomotor policy, mapping a single-view RGB image (after perception alignment) to the predicted 6D action $T_{tool}^{task}$.
- **Robot Deployment**: During deployment, the robot command is computed by converting the predicted $T_{tool}^{task}$ into the end-effector frame. For stationary robots, the task frame aligns with the base frame. For mobile platforms, base movement is compensated using $T_{task}^{base}$. The final end-effector pose is given by: $T_{eef}^{base} = T_{task}^{base} T_{tool}^{task} T_{eef}^{tool}$, where $T_{eef}^{tool}$ is the known fixed transform between the tool and the robot end-effector.

This methodological combination aims to enable scalable, robust, and generalizable robot policy learning for diverse tool-use tasks from easily acquired human video data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the "Tool-as-Interface" framework across five real-world tool-use tasks: nail hammering, meatball scooping, pan flipping (egg, bun, patty), wine balancing, and soccer ball kicking, using Kinova Gen3 and UR5e robots with RealSense D415 cameras.

#### 4.1. Policy Effectiveness and Task Performance

The framework demonstrated superior performance compared to baseline methods:
- **Success Rates**: The proposed method achieved consistently higher success rates across all tasks compared to diffusion policies trained on teleoperation data (SpaceMouse or Gello). For instance, in nail hammering, it achieved 13/13 successes, while the teleoperation-based policy failed all 13 trials. For pan flipping, wine balancing, and soccer kicking, teleoperation policies were not feasible.
- **Comparison with Hand-Held Grippers (UMI)**: The method also outperformed UMI, a handheld gripper-based approach. For nail hammering, UMI failed all 13 trials with 25 demonstrations (comparable to the proposed method's data collection time) and only succeeded with 100 demonstrations. The proposed method achieved 13/13 successes with 40 demonstrations. UMI was found inapplicable or struggled with dynamic tasks (wine balancing, pan flipping, soccer kicking) due to challenges like contact, inertia, or localization failures.
- **Task Capabilities**: The framework enabled accurate spatial detection (nail hammering, meatball scooping), high-speed motions (pan flipping), precise insertion (wine balancing), and swift reactions (soccer kicking).

#### 4.2. Policy Execution Efficiency

The proposed framework demonstrated higher execution efficiency:
- **Task Completion Time**: It achieved shorter task completion times and smoother action trajectories compared to baselines. For meatball scooping, the proposed method completed tasks in an average of 12.4 seconds, compared to 42.0 seconds for the teleoperation-based diffusion policy.
- **Trajectory Smoothness**: Qualitative analysis (Figure 12) indicated significantly smoother end-effector trajectories with approximately 10x fewer waypoints compared to policies trained on robot-collected data, leading to more fluid execution and reduced cumulative errors.

#### 4.3. Robustness and Generalization

##### 4.3.1. Camera Pose Robustness

- **View Augmentation (VA) and Random Cropping (RC)**: The combination of random cropping and 3D-aware view augmentation significantly enhanced policy robustness to camera pose variations. The policy trained with both RC and VA successfully operated under a wider range of camera configurations for the nail hammering task (Figure 4), outperforming policies trained with only RC or without either.
- **Camera Shaking**: The policy consistently completed tasks (meatball scooping, nail hammering, pan flipping) despite intentional camera shaking, a robustness attributed to random cropping during training.

##### 4.3.2. Robot Base Robustness

- **Base Movement Compensation**: The task-space, tool-centric action representation enabled the policy to compensate for manual shaking of the robot base, maintaining successful task completion even when shaking frequency exceeded the robot's control frequency.
- **Combined Shaking**: The policy maintained effectiveness under simultaneous camera and robot base shaking.
- **Chicken Head Stabilization**: At lower base movement frequencies, the end-effector exhibited a stabilizing behavior, similar to a chicken's head, maintaining steady control during mild base movements.

##### 4.3.3. Human Perturbation Robustness

The policy demonstrated resilience to real-time human interventions (Figure 6):
- **Dynamic Tracking**: The robot successfully tracked a manually moved nail during hammering.
- **Adaptive Manipulation**: It located and scooped new meatballs thrown mid-task by a human.
- **Recovery from Intervention**: It recovered and re-flipped an egg after a human repositioned it in the pan.

##### 4.3.4. Spatial, Object, and Tool Generalization

- **Spatial Generalization**: The policy performed successfully across varied initial spatial configurations of objects in tasks such as nail hammering, meatball scooping, pan flipping, and soccer ball kicking.
- **Object Generalization**: In the pan-flipping task, the policy generalized effectively to different objects (egg, burger bun, 3D-printed meat patty), having only seen the egg and bun during training.
- **Tool Generalization**: The policy, trained on large, medium, and square pans, achieved high success rates on the large and medium pans during evaluation. Performance declined on smaller pans due to limited surface area and on the square pan due to shallow edges affecting object containment.

#### 4.4. Data Collection Efficiency and Affordability

- **Throughput**: Leveraging natural human dexterity, the method achieved significantly higher data collection throughput. It reduced data collection time by 73% for nail hammering and 81% for meatball scooping compared to teleoperation methods. It was 41% faster than UMI for nail hammering and enabled dynamic tasks where teleoperation and handheld grippers consistently failed.
- **Reliability**: Human demonstrations provided smoother, more consistent trajectories, avoiding issues like safety stops, collisions, tool slippage, and latency common in teleoperation and handheld gripper methods (Figure 7).
- **Cost and Usability**: The framework requires only two monocular RGB cameras (e.g., smartphones or webcams), incurring zero additional hardware cost compared to expensive teleoperation devices, handheld grippers, or VR/AR equipment. This makes data collection intuitive, accessible, and eliminates the need for specialized hardware design, printing, or manufacturing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Tool-as-Interface" framework represents a notable advancement in robotic manipulation by addressing several critical limitations of existing imitation learning paradigms.

#### 5.1. Advancements in Robot Learning

- **Robustness to Dynamic Environments**: The framework's ability to learn policies robust to camera motion, robot base movement, and human perturbations is a significant step towards deploying robots in less constrained, real-world environments. This resilience is supported by 3D-aware view augmentation, random cropping, and a task-space, tool-centric action representation.
- **Enhanced Skill Transfer**: By effectively bridging the human-robot embodiment gap through segmented observations and invariant action representations, the research demonstrates a more seamless transfer of complex, dynamic, and contact-rich manipulation skills from human demonstrations to diverse robotic platforms (6-DOF and 7-DOF robots).
- **Improved Policy Quality**: The use of natural human demonstrations results in policies that exhibit not only higher success rates but also more efficient and fluid execution, with smoother trajectories and fewer redundant waypoints compared to teleoperation-based methods. This leads to policies that are more likely to succeed in tasks requiring precise timing and continuous control.

#### 5.2. Addressing Scalability and Accessibility

- **Democratization of Data Collection**: A primary impact of this work is the significant reduction in the barriers to collecting large-scale, high-quality demonstration datasets. By eliminating the need for expensive, specialized hardware (like teleoperation devices or custom grippers) and relying on ubiquitous RGB cameras, the framework makes data collection affordable and accessible to a much broader user base. This democratization can accelerate research and development in robot learning by enabling larger and more diverse datasets.
- **Efficiency in Data Acquisition**: The method dramatically reduces data collection time compared to traditional approaches, increasing throughput and making it practical to gather sufficient data for learning complex behaviors. This efficiency is crucial for scaling robot learning to a wider array of tasks and environments.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

While the framework offers substantial improvements, the authors identify several areas for future work:
- **Pose Estimation Reliability**: The perception pipeline's reliance on FoundationPose for tool pose extraction can be a source of errors, potentially requiring data recollection. Future work could focus on more robust pose estimation algorithms or self-correction mechanisms.
- **Realism of Augmented Views**: Novel view augmentation using Gaussian splatting can introduce noise and reduced realism when views deviate significantly from the collected camera positions. Advanced rendering techniques could enhance the realism of augmented views and improve policy generalization.
- **Rigid Tool Assumption**: The current method assumes a rigid attachment between the tool and the robot end-effector. Future efforts could explore incorporating tactile sensing to address minor shifts in contact-rich tasks or using flexible representations for deformable tools.

In conclusion, this research establishes a foundational framework for scalable, accessible, and robust robot learning from human tool-use videos. By overcoming key challenges related to data collection, embodiment gaps, and viewpoint variations, it lays groundwork for advancing robotic manipulation in complex, real-world scenarios, while also reducing the entry barriers for participation in robotics research and development.
