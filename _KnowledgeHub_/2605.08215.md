# Test-Time Training for Visual Foresight Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A test-time training approach called T3VF enhances the out-of-distribution robustness of Visual Foresight Vision-Language-Action (VF-VLA) models by leveraging self-supervision from predicted-attained image pairs, guided by an adaptive action variance filtering mechanism. This method demonstrated average success rate improvements of up to 2.8% on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark with minimal additional inference cost.

## Problem

- Visual Foresight Vision-Language-Action (VF-VLA) models exhibit amplified performance degradation under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions due to their sequential visual prediction and action generation stages.
- Errors in predicting future visual states under OOD shifts directly compromise subsequent action generation, leading to compounded failures.
- Current test-time adaptation methods for VLA models are often computationally demanding or require external reward signals, not specifically addressing VF-VLA's unique challenges.

## Method

- The **T3VF** framework integrates a ==test-time training== mechanism into ==VF-VLA== models by leveraging the correspondence between predicted future images and observed actual images as a ==self-supervision signal==.
- Updates are applied only to ==learnable query tokens==, which are a small subset of parameters in the image prediction pathway, to minimize computational overhead.
- An ==adaptive update filtering== mechanism uses ==action variance== and a running percentile buffer to selectively apply updates, filtering out noisy signals and adapting to varying task difficulties.

## Results

- T3VF improved the average success rate of the Mantis VF-VLA model on the LIBERO-Plus OOD benchmark by **2.8%** (from **49.3%** to **52.1%**) in a partially adapted training setting.
- The adaptive update filtering mechanism proved crucial, yielding a **31.8%** success rate on the Robot perturbation, outperforming indiscriminate test-time training (**29.8%**) and fixed-threshold filtering (**28.6%**).
- The method achieved these performance gains with a contained computational overhead, increasing average episode time by approximately **1.3 times** compared to the base model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The sequential nature of visual foresight in VF-VLA models amplifies OOD vulnerability, making accurate future visual state prediction crucial for robust action generation.
> - A natural self-supervision signal is inherently available during robot operation through predicted-attained image pairs, which can be leveraged for test-time adaptation.
> - An adaptive filtering mechanism, based on action variance, is vital for reliable test-time training, as it addresses the ambiguity of error sources and adapts to fluctuating task difficulties, preventing noisy updates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a central paradigm in the development of generalist robotic manipulation systems. These models integrate visual perception, language understanding, and action generation to enable robots to perform complex tasks based on human instructions. A prominent architectural design within VLA research is the Visual Foresight VLA (VF-VLA) framework. VF-VLA models operate in a two-stage manner: they first predict a future visual state that the robot is intended to achieve, and then they generate the corresponding actions conditioned on this predicted future visual information. This design has demonstrated notable performance in various tasks, leading to its widespread adoption in recent VLA literature.

Despite their effectiveness, VLA models, and particularly VF-VLA architectures, are known to exhibit vulnerability to out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts. OOD conditions arise when the operational environment or task parameters at test time differ significantly from the data distribution encountered during training. For VF-VLA models, this vulnerability is amplified due to their dual-stage nature. Inaccurate predictions of future visual states under OOD conditions directly impair the quality of subsequent action generation, as the action module relies on these potentially erroneous visual forecasts. This results in a compounded performance degradation. Previous research has consistently shown that existing VLA models, despite high in-distribution success rates, perform poorly when faced with such perturbations.

Various approaches have been explored to adapt VLA models during deployment, including test-time reinforcement learning. However, these methods often necessitate separate reward models, incur substantial computational overhead from online reinforcement learning, and are generally designed for VLA models rather than specifically targeting the unique challenges of VF-VLA. The current work positions itself to address the amplified OOD vulnerability specific to VF-VLA through a lightweight, self-supervised test-time training mechanism, without requiring architectural modifications or external supervision signals.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method that mitigates the amplified out-of-distribution (OOD) vulnerability inherent in Visual Foresight VLA (VF-VLA) models during test time.

The motivation for this objective stems from several observations regarding VF-VLA models:

1. **Dual-Stage Vulnerability:** VF-VLA models are designed with a two-stage process: predicting a future visual state and then generating actions based on that prediction. This sequential dependence means that if the initial visual prediction is inaccurate, the subsequent action generation is also likely to be suboptimal. Under OOD conditions, errors can propagate and amplify across both stages, leading to a significant degradation in overall task success. For example, if a robot encounters an object with an unfamiliar texture (an OOD visual condition), its ability to accurately foresee the visual outcome of an interaction (e.g., grasping the object) might be compromised, subsequently leading to incorrect grasping actions.

2. **Empirical Evidence of OOD Performance Drop:** Existing VF-VLA models, such as WorldVLA and Mantis, demonstrate substantial drops in success rates when evaluated on OOD benchmarks like LIBERO-Plus compared to their in-distribution performance on LIBERO. This empirical evidence underscores the practical challenge posed by OOD shifts to the reliability of VF-VLA models in real-world scenarios.

3. **Natural Self-Supervision Opportunity:** The core intuition driving the proposed solution is the existence of a natural self-supervision signal within the VF-VLA operational loop. At any given step, the model predicts a future image. After executing the corresponding action and a fixed number of steps, the actual image at that future time is observed from the environment. This observed image effectively serves as a ground truth or "oracle" for the earlier prediction, forming a predicted-attained image pair. This pair offers a direct and free source of supervision to improve the visual prediction component of the model without requiring additional data collection or human annotation.

4. **Practical Challenges with Indiscriminate Test-Time Updates:** While the predicted-attained pair provides a valuable supervision signal, its indiscriminate use for test-time training presents practical challenges:
 - **Ambiguity of Error Source:** A large discrepancy between a predicted and an attained image does not always indicate an error in the visual prediction pathway. It could also arise from an incorrect action being executed, even if the visual prediction was accurate. Applying updates blindly based on such discrepancies risks introducing noise into the model parameters rather than beneficial adaptation.
 - **Varying Task Difficulty:** The difficulty of robot manipulation tasks can vary significantly both across different episodes and even within a single episode. A fixed threshold for determining when to apply an update would be ineffective. If the threshold is too lenient, it could allow excessive noisy updates in easier segments. If it is too strict, it might prevent useful learning signals from being utilized in harder segments or episodes.

By addressing these challenges, the research aims to develop a robust, efficient, and practical test-time training approach that can effectively leverage the inherent self-supervision in VF-VLA models to enhance their OOD robustness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Test-Time Training Visual Foresight VLA (T3 VF), builds upon the standard VF-VLA architecture and introduces a test-time training mechanism complemented by an adaptive update filtering strategy.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries of VF-VLA Architecture

A typical VF-VLA model comprises a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone $P$, an image head $I_h$, and an action head $A_h$. Given a language instruction $l$, a current observation $o_t$, and learnable query tokens $q$, the backbone extracts various representations: $(h_{inst_t}, h_{img_t}, h_{act_t}) = P(l, o_t, q)$. The image head $I_h$ then predicts a future observation $\hat{o}_{t+n}$ (at $n$ steps ahead) from $h_{inst_t}$ and $h_{img_t}$, while the action head $A_h$ generates an action $\hat{a}_t$ from $h_{act_t}$. During training, the model is optimized using a combined loss function $L_{train} = L_{img}(\hat{o}_{t+n}, o_{t+n}) + \lambda L_{act}(\hat{a}_t, a_t)$, where $o_{t+n}$ is the ground-truth future observation and $a_t$ is the demonstration action. The learnable query tokens $q$ are a small subset of parameters that participate in the image prediction pathway.

#### 3.2. Test-Time Training with Predicted-Attained Image Correspondence

The core intuition for T3 VF is that the predicted future image $\hat{o}_{t+n}$ and the subsequently observed actual image $o_{t+n}$ form a natural self-supervision pair. At test time, as the robot executes actions, these predicted-attained pairs are collected along the trajectory of an episode into a buffer $B$. Once the size of $B$ reaches a predefined batch size, a single gradient update step is performed to minimize $L_{TTT} = \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{(\hat{o}_{t+n}, o_{t+n}) \in B} L_{img}(\hat{o}_{t+n}, o_{t+n})$. This loss function is identical to the image loss used during the initial training phase. Crucially, this update is applied only to the learnable query tokens $q$, keeping the majority of the model parameters frozen. This design choice minimizes computational overhead, as $q$ is identified as the smallest module contributing to the image prediction pathway. The update process is designed to proceed in parallel with the robot's execution, aiming for minimal disruption to inference time.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Update Filtering

To address the practical challenges of indiscriminate test-time updates, T3 VF introduces an adaptive update filtering mechanism.

**Addressing Ambiguity of Error Source:**
The method uses the variance of sampled actions as a proxy metric to determine the reliability of a supervision signal. At each step $t$, $K$ action samples $\{\hat{a}^{(k)}_t\}_{k=1}^K$ are drawn from the action head. The variance $\sigma^2_t$ is computed as the averaged squared L2 deviation of these samples from their mean $\bar{a}_t$: $\sigma^2_t = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \|\hat{a}^{(k)}_t - \bar{a}_t\|^2_2$. A low $\sigma^2_t$ indicates high internal consistency in the model's intended action. In such cases, if a significant discrepancy between $\hat{o}_{t+n}$ and $o_{t+n}$ is observed, it is more plausibly attributed to an error in the visual prediction pathway, making the predicted-attained pair a useful signal for updating $q$. Conversely, high variance suggests ambiguity in the action itself, implying the error source cannot be reliably attributed to visual prediction, and thus the step is skipped to avoid injecting noise. This variance calculation is efficient, requiring a single forward pass through the backbone followed by parallel decoding.

**Addressing Varying Task Difficulty:**
Instead of a fixed threshold for $\sigma^2_t$, the method employs an adaptive variance buffer $V_t$. This buffer maintains a running window of variance values collected over the most recent $|V|$ steps. An update is permitted for step $t$ only if its calculated variance $\sigma^2_t$ falls below a specified $\rho$-percentile of the values currently in $V_t$ (i.e., $\sigma^2_t \leq \text{Quantile}_\rho(V_t)$). This relative ranking criterion allows the filtering mechanism to naturally adapt to the varying scale of variance observed within an episode and across episodes of different difficulties. It ensures that a consistent proportion of the "most confident" (lowest variance) steps are selected for training, regardless of the absolute magnitude of variances, thereby providing a stable flow of supervision signals.

The overall T3 VF algorithm proceeds by first calculating action variance and updating the variance buffer. If the variance meets the adaptive filtering criterion, the predicted future image is stored along with its corresponding observed future image once available. When enough such filtered pairs accumulate in batch $B$, a test-time training step is executed to update $q$. This integrated approach allows T3 VF to leverage self-supervision effectively while managing noise and adapting to environmental variability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of T3 VF utilized Mantis, a representative VF-VLA model, as the baseline and was conducted on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark. LIBERO-Plus assesses OOD robustness across seven perturbation dimensions (Robot, Language, Noise, Layout, Background, Camera, Light). Experiments were performed in two settings: "w/ Perturbed Train" (model fine-tuned on LIBERO-Plus, partially adapted) and "w/o Perturbed Train" (official LIBERO checkpoint, fully OOD).

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

T3 VF consistently improved the average success rate across both experimental settings (Table 1):
- **w/ Perturbed Train:** Mantis achieved an average success rate of 49.3%. Mantis + T3 VF improved this to 52.1%, representing a **+2.8%** increase. Improvements were observed across most perturbation dimensions, with notable gains in Camera (+4.8%) and Light (+4.6%).
- **w/o Perturbed Train:** Mantis achieved an average success rate of 39.8%. Mantis + T3 VF improved this to 40.3%, a **+0.5%** increase. While the overall gain was smaller in this more challenging setting (where the base model was not adapted to perturbations), T3 VF still yielded a positive improvement, demonstrating its ability to provide some adaptation even with an entirely OOD base model.

These results support the central hypothesis that T3 VF's targeted adaptation of the visual prediction pathway, using predicted-attained image pairs, effectively mitigates the dual-stage OOD vulnerability of VF-VLA models. The improvements in visual prediction are observed to propagate positively to action generation.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study was conducted on the Robot perturbation in the "w/ Perturbed Train" setting to determine the contribution of each T3 VF component (Table 2):
- **Base Model (Mantis):** Achieved a success rate of 29.0%.
- **Indiscriminate Test-Time Training (TTT) without filtering:** Adding only the test-time training procedure, without any filtering, improved the success rate to 29.8% (+0.8% over base). This indicates that the predicted-attained image pairs carry useful supervision signal even when applied without careful selection.
- **TTT with Fixed-Threshold Variance Filter:** Incorporating a fixed-threshold filter based on action variance led to a decrease in success rate to 28.6% (-1.2% from indiscriminate TTT, -0.4% from base). This result confirms the paper's motivation that a fixed threshold is insufficient to consistently distinguish informative from noisy steps across varying task difficulties, potentially hindering learning.
- **Full T3 VF (TTT with Adaptive Variance Buffer):** Replacing the fixed threshold with the adaptive variance buffer (the full T3 VF method) resulted in the highest success rate of 31.8% (+3.2% from fixed-threshold filter, +2.8% from base). This outcome validates the effectiveness of the adaptive variance buffer in providing a reliable proxy for selecting useful supervision signals by adapting to the relative difficulty of different steps and episodes.

#### 4.3. Efficiency Analysis

The efficiency of T3 VF was analyzed by measuring the average time per episode on the Robot perturbation setup (Fig. 3):
- **Base Model (Mantis):** Average time per episode was 65.2 seconds.
- **Indiscriminate Test-Time Training:** Increased the time to 113.4 seconds (approximately 1.7 times the base time). This overhead is attributed to the continuous computation of $L_{img}$ and parameter updates at every step.
- **Full T3 VF (with Adaptive Update Filtering):** Reduced the overhead significantly compared to indiscriminate TTT, bringing the average time per episode to 85.4 seconds (approximately 1.3 times the base time). This reduction is due to the adaptive filtering mechanism, which triggers updates only on a fraction of the total steps, thereby reducing computational cost.

The efficiency analysis, in conjunction with the ablation study, demonstrates that T3 VF effectively balances performance improvements with contained additional inference cost, achieving robust adaptation while remaining practical for deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a relevant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation and VLA models by specifically addressing the previously unaddressed amplified out-of-distribution (OOD) vulnerability in Visual Foresight VLA (VF-VLA) architectures. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Addressing a Specific Vulnerability:** The paper is the first to systematically identify and propose a solution for the dual-stage exposure to OOD shifts inherent in VF-VLA models. By targeting the visual prediction pathway, T3 VF aims to improve the foundational component upon which action generation relies, thereby offering a focused approach to enhancing robustness.

2. **Practical and Lightweight Test-Time Adaptation:** T3 VF introduces a practical and lightweight test-time training approach. It leverages a natural self-supervision signal (predicted-attained image pairs) that is intrinsically available during robot operation, eliminating the need for additional training pipelines, auxiliary modules, external reward signals, or offline adaptation. This design principle makes it highly amenable for deployment in real-world robotic systems where computational resources and additional data collection might be constrained.

3. **Novel Adaptive Filtering Mechanism:** The adaptive update filtering mechanism, which utilizes action variance and a running percentile buffer, is a methodological contribution that enhances the reliability of self-supervision. It effectively discriminates between useful and noisy supervision signals and adapts to varying task difficulties, addressing critical challenges in online adaptation. This mechanism contributes to more stable and effective learning during deployment.

4. **Improved Robustness for Robotic Systems:** By mitigating OOD vulnerability, T3 VF can contribute to the development of more robust and reliable robotic manipulation systems. Robots operating in dynamic and unpredictable real-world environments frequently encounter conditions that differ from their training data. T3 VF offers a method for VF-VLA models to partially adapt to these unforeseen shifts, potentially leading to higher success rates in diverse and unconstrained settings.

5. **Contained Overhead:** The efficiency analysis demonstrates that T3 VF achieves performance gains at a modest additional inference cost (approximately 1.3 times the base inference time). This contained overhead, along with the strategy of updating only a small subset of parameters (learnable query tokens), makes it a feasible option for online adaptation without significantly impeding the robot's real-time operational capabilities.

In summary, T3 VF provides a principled and empirically validated method for enhancing the OOD robustness of VF-VLA models. Its practical nature and contained computational demands position it as a valuable tool for improving the deployment readiness and generalization capabilities of current and future vision-language-action robotic systems.
