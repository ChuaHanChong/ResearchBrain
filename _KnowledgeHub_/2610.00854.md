# Are Frontier VLM Agents Ready to Be Robot Generalists? An Empirical Study with the Embodied Agent Arena

> [!summary] Summary
> Embodied Agent Arena is a 1,000-case evaluation built from 32 established sources plus GeoProbe, a new Blender-and-real-image geometric benchmark. It spans Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, Affordance, Task Planning and Manipulation. A minimal persistent-Python harness scores metric precision, contact grounding and native goal completion separately across seven frontier VLM agents. The top agent, Astra, excels at estimation and contact localization, but coordinated goal completion remains the main gap.

## Problem

- Control suites measure only whether goals are achieved, while visual diagnostics isolate spatial or grounding skills, so no evaluation links local competence to complete robotic task success.
- Aggregate scores hide whether errors come from metric scale, reference frames, or final placement conditions, and terminal failures conceal useful partial progress made along the way.
- Prior embodied benchmarks such as EmbodiedBench, Embodied Arena and VLABench rarely combine fixed probes, region grounding, robot manipulation and camera calibration or pose diagnostics in one study.

## Method

- **Embodied Agent Arena**: 1,000 cases organized into five capability domains, each specifying observations, a query or goal, source-provided operations, and the source's native scoring criteria.
- ==GeoProbe benchmark==: 168 cases varying camera motion, object motion and scale references in controlled Blender scenes plus real images, targeting calibration, pose, depth, displacement and scale estimation.
- ==Unified agent harness== with a ==persistent Python runtime== and native manipulation loops that scores geometric precision, affordance grounding and terminal success separately, plus RGB-expanded and multi-round tool-assisted review studies.

## Results

- Astra leads with **17.1** degree rotation error, **60.3%** contact validity, **80.3%** planning success and **41.8%** manipulation success versus Fable's **24.7%**, giving a five-domain index of **56.2** versus **47.3**.
- Astra traces 3D paths most closely (RMSE **0.382** m), yet its joint success is **33.9%** versus Gemini's **37.5%**, and **36** of its **37** failures come from endpoint initiation or arrival.
- Adding per-round RGB under matched budgets flips CALVIN success, with Astra falling from **30%** to **0%** and Fable rising from **0%** to **40%**. All agents fail BEHAVIOR-1K, and Astra solves 1 of **10** RoboCasa tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Local competence such as accurate estimation or valid contacts does not add up to goal success, so evaluations should score terminal goal conditions separately from intermediate precision.
> - Richer observations do not always help: under fixed budgets they can use up inference, delay building a reusable controller, and reverse model rankings on control tasks.
> - Which model leads depends on the metric, since task success and numerical precision favor different VLMs, so a single aggregate leaderboard misrepresents how ready a model is to be a robot generalist.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Predicting a route for an object helps a robot only if the route starts at the object, reaches its target, and stays collision-free. Estimating an object's position, choosing a usable contact, or tracing a route can each look competent while still failing to get an object where it needs to go. Huang et al. contribute an evaluation framework, not a new robot controller, that measures these local capabilities separately from complete task success. In spatial tracing, Astra drew the lowest-error 3D paths, but Gemini had the higher rate of paths that met all the task's constraints. This comparison concerns a predicted spatial trace. It does not show that a robot physically executed the plotted path.

Panel (c) compares single-run 3D trajectory RMSE in meters (lower is better) with 3D joint-success percentage (higher is better) for seven agents. Astra has the lowest RMSE, 0.382 m, versus Gemini's 0.426 m, but its joint success is 33.9% versus Gemini's 37.5%.

### 2. A benchmark for the whole job

Embodied Agent Arena contains 1,000 cases from 32 established sources plus GeoProbe, a new 168-case geometry benchmark. GeoProbe uses controlled Blender scenes, supplemented by real images, to vary camera motion, object motion, depth, and scale. The cases span Geometry, Spatial Reasoning, Affordance, Task Planning, and Manipulation. Of these, 660 cases use fixed inputs and 340 are interactive.

A shared harness preserves source observations and operations. The agent uses source-provided tools or native interaction, and scoring checks outputs or terminal states. The key design choice is to score a local estimate or a valid contact separately from completing all required goal conditions.

The closest prior work, [[2509.15273|Embodied Arena]], already combines fixed probes and interactive tasks. The new paper extends that approach by combining and diagnosing these requirements. It separately scores geometric estimates, action-conditioned contact grounding, and terminal outcomes, including direct robot manipulation evaluation.

### 3. What a close path misses

Two measures define the trace comparison. RMSE is the average deviation of a path from a reference. Each predicted and reference path is resampled at 100 equally spaced positions, and the 3D measure is in meters. Joint success requires a trace to pass all three offline checks:

- start within 20 cm of the object point cloud or reference start;
- reach within 20 cm of the target 3D box;
- keep sampled-position collision overlap at or below 20%.

Under these definitions, Astra's paths are closer to the reference, yet fewer of them satisfy every check. Of Astra's 37 failed traces, 36 fail initiation or arrival. The sets of initiation, arrival, and collision failures can overlap. The authors' main diagnosis is boundary placement, and collision-free motion is not identified as the sole cause. A path's average distance from a reference does not establish that the path starts in the right place, reaches the target, and avoids collisions.

### 4. More views, mixed results

The authors also changed what agents could see. Baseline uses each source's native observations and tools. RGB-expanded adds current RGB views at each model decision. Both conditions match inference budgets, interaction allowances, and native execution-step limits.

On CALVIN's ten five-goal sequences, the ranking reverses. Astra goes from 30% full-sequence success and 3.10 consecutive goals on average to 0% and 1.10. Fable goes from 0% and 0.90 to 40% and 2.90. Across the common nine-suite, 153-task subset, Astra records 63 successes in both conditions, while Fable rises from 34 to 65.

These results show that outcomes depend on model and task under the tested protocol. They do not identify RGB as the only cause of the differences, and they do not establish a general benefit or harm from richer observations.

Panel (b) compares seven agents' average consecutive CALVIN goals, on a 0–5 scale, before the first failure. Filled markers show Baseline and open markers show RGB-expanded. Astra falls from 3.10 to 1.10, and Fable rises from 0.90 to 2.90.

### 5. The remaining gap

The arena shows when measurable local progress fails to satisfy a goal. The authors conclude that completing coordinated, goal-directed actions remains the key gap to generalist readiness.

The evidence has clear limits. It covers seven models on 1,000 bounded cases from five domains, using source-specific budgets and interfaces. It does not prove performance on physical robots, in unseen environments, or during extended operation. The authors name the central test for future work: whether agents can coordinate capabilities and reliably complete goals over extended operation.
