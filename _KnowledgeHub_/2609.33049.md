# Is Online Interaction Necessary for Recovery? A Minimalist Approach to Robust Planning via Perturbation

> [!summary] Summary
> PARS (Perturbation-Augmented Recovery Supervision) is a behavior-cloning training scheme for action-sequence policies. It perturbs the robot's proprioceptive state and supervises only the later suffix of the predicted trajectory against the demonstration, leaving the early prefix free to produce corrective motion. Using only existing demonstrations, it raises mean closed-loop success for six flow, transformer, and diffusion policies across 51 RLBench tasks.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning suffers covariate shift in closed-loop execution: small prediction or execution errors push the robot into states poorly covered by demonstrations, degrading performance.
- Interactive corrective methods like DAgger, DART, and HG-DAgger require additional environment interaction and continued expert or reference-policy access, making them costly for real robots.
- Conventional input-noise augmentation keeps the original target over the full horizon, encouraging invariance to perturbations rather than specifying how the trajectory should return to demonstrated behavior.

## Method

- ==Suffix-anchoring loss==: perturb proprioceptive observations with uniform noise and supervise only the predicted suffix (steps 10-20 of a 20-step chunk) toward the demonstrated suffix, alongside standard BC on clean data.
- Theoretical analysis shows the suffix loss acts as a selective ==Jacobian-sensitivity regularizer== on the suffix only, and derives sufficient conditions keeping the free prefix near demonstrations and within joint speed and acceleration limits.
- Policy-class instantiations: suffix-restricted reconstruction for transformer and ==ACT==, velocity regression after integrating to the prefix end for ==Streaming Flow Policy==, and suffix-refined surrogate targets for ==flow-matching and diffusion policies==.

## Results

- Across **51** RLBench tasks, PARS lifts mean success for all six policies, e.g. flow policy **58.0%** to **66.9%**, ACT **58.8%** to **68.2%**, diffusion policy **47.6%** to **56.0%**.
- Conventional full-target noise augmentation gives modest gains or hurts (SFP **49.8%** to **46.7%**, DP **47.6%** to **46.2%**), while PARS matches or beats baselines on **42-48** of **51** tasks.
- PARS complements existing robustness methods (SFP **49.8%** to **61.6%**, KDPE **53.5%** to **60.2%**) and pulls predicted trajectories back toward demonstrated configurations, with prefix kinematics closer to demonstrations than noise augmentation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - What a perturbed sample is supervised toward matters more than the perturbation itself: specifying where to return beats demanding invariance to the perturbation.
> - Recovery supervision can be synthesized offline from existing demonstrations by anchoring only the future, removing the need for DAgger-style expert queries or extra rollouts.
> - Leaving part of an action chunk unsupervised need not cause erratic motion; free prefixes mostly stay within kinematic limits, though diffusion-based policies still sometimes exceed acceleration limits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot can follow a demonstrated sequence of joint-position commands, yet a small execution error can put it somewhere the demonstrations did not cover. Behavior cloning (BC), which trains a policy to imitate demonstrations, can fail in closed-loop robot use for this reason. Small errors move the robot into states poorly represented in its training data, a mismatch called *covariate shift*. Obtaining corrective demonstrations at those states can require more environment interaction and access to an expert.

PARS (Perturbation-Augmented Recovery Supervision) changes the training target instead. It perturbs the robot's sensed state, leaves the beginning of its next planned sequence room to correct, and requires the later part to meet the demonstrated continuation. This return point is learned from demonstrations already on hand, so an expert does not have to correct the robot at each new state.

In standard BC (top), the predicted sequence is supervised against the demonstrated sequence across the horizon. With a perturbed start (bottom), the prefix predictions have no direct augmented target, and the later predictions are linked to their corresponding demonstrated targets. The diagram explains the supervision scheme, not a measured recovery outcome.

### 2. A return point in the plan

The planner predicts a finite sequence of desired joint configurations, and a low-level controller tracks them. PARS perturbs proprioceptive observations from existing demonstrations. It then applies augmented supervision only to the later suffix of the predicted sequence, matching it to the corresponding demonstrated suffix.

The prefix is "free" only in a limited sense. It receives no direct augmented target from the perturbed sample, and ordinary BC still trains it on unperturbed demonstrations. The study uses a 20-step plan and leaves the first 10 steps free of direct augmented supervision.

Conventional noise augmentation also perturbs proprioceptive inputs, but it keeps the original target over the full horizon. Suffix anchoring instead tells the policy where the perturbed plan should rejoin the demonstrated continuation.

### 3. Does the suffix change matter?

The main test uses 51 simulated RLBench tasks. For each task, success rate is the percentage of 50 evaluation episodes that succeed, and the reported mean is averaged across tasks.

ACT is a transformer-based action-sequence policy. With ACT, mean success was:

- 58.8% without PARS
- 68.2% with PARS
- 60.0% with conventional full-target noise augmentation

PARS matched or exceeded the no-PARS baseline on 48 of 51 tasks. The architecture and policy hyperparameters were held fixed across these conditions. The PARS figure is a tuned result: it uses the best per-task augmentation setting selected by grid search.

Mean success improved with PARS for all six evaluated policies, including methods that already add robustness mechanisms. Conventional noise augmentation gave smaller gains or declines.

A separate test measured recovery directly. The authors selected off-nominal observations reached in rollouts of the unaugmented policy and queried all variants at the same recorded states. For each predicted step, they computed the nearest-neighbor distance from the predicted joint configuration to demonstrated configurations. They divided this by the step-zero distance and averaged across observations, then across the 51 tasks. A value below 1 indicates approach toward the demonstrations, and a value above 1 indicates further deviation. These are predicted sequences, not executed recovery rollouts.

Normalized nearest-neighbor distance to demonstrated configurations by predicted step, where 1 marks the starting distance. Gray dashed lines show results without PARS and blue solid lines show results with PARS. Bands show ±1 standard error over 51 tasks. PARS finishes below 1 for FP, SFP, TP, and ACT, while DP and KDPE remain above 1 but below their no-PARS curves.

PARS predictions end closer to demonstrations for FP, SFP, TP, and ACT. For DP and KDPE, they still end farther away than at the starting point, though they diverge less than without PARS.

### 4. Closing

PARS changes the target for a perturbed plan. Instead of repeating the whole demonstration, the plan leaves room to correct and then matches the demonstrated continuation. In this tuned simulated evaluation, success favored that change over full-target noise augmentation. The distance test shows that the return toward the demonstrations is partial and depends on the policy.

PARS avoids collecting additional corrective trajectories or querying an expert during training. It still relies on task-specific settings chosen by grid search. The evaluation is simulated, uses low-dimensional observations, and covers one variation per task; visual observations and physical robots remain future work.
