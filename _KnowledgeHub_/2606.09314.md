# KPGrasp: Scalable Keypoint Flow Matching for Dexterous Grasp Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> KPGrasp introduces a dexterous grasp generator that employs an all-Euclidean 3D hand-keypoint parameterization with a Transformer-based flow-matching model to learn implicit grasp priors from large datasets. It achieved a 76.3% grasp success rate on the Dexonomy benchmark with a 2.4 mm penetration depth and reduced inference time by 87-fold compared to prior methods employing test-time refinement.

## Problem

- Conventional grasp parameterization (SE(3) wrist pose and joint angles) presents learning challenges due to manifold discontinuities, kinematic error accumulation, and difficulty in balancing mixed loss components.
- Many learning-based grasp generation methods re-introduce complexity by relying on explicit physics-based priors (e.g., contact losses, test-time refinement), leading to brittle parameter tuning and slow inference.
- The mixed nature of input/output representations makes it difficult for neural networks to perform native spatial reasoning about hand-object interactions.

## Method

- An all-Euclidean ==3D hand-keypoint== parameterization directly predicts 21 keypoints in the object's coordinate frame, simplifying the learning problem and enabling native spatial reasoning within a Transformer architecture.
- A Transformer-based conditional flow-matching model is trained solely with a standard flow-matching objective, learning grasp priors implicitly from data without explicit contact losses or test-time physics-based heuristics.
- An efficient inference pipeline generates grasp candidates via 5 Euler integration steps and ranks them using the flow model's likelihood, followed by rigid registration and inverse kinematics for conversion to executable robot commands.

## Results

- Achieved a **76.3%** grasp success rate (GSR) and **2.4 mm** penetration depth on the Dexonomy benchmark, outperforming the strongest directly comparable baseline by 47.4 percentage points GSR.
- Demonstrated strong cross-benchmark generalization, achieving the best average success rate (**63.8%**) and lowest penetration depth (**12.9 mm**) on DexGrasp Anything when trained solely on Dexonomy.
- Reduced inference time to **0.032** seconds per returned grasp, an 87-fold speed improvement over methods with contact-based test-time refinement, and achieved 83 successful grasps in real-world robot experiments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An all-Euclidean keypoint representation simplifies dexterous grasp learning by removing manifold complexities and enabling direct spatial reasoning, proving more scalable and effective than hybrid pose-joint parameterizations.
> - Large-scale, high-quality grasp data implicitly conveys sufficient physical priors for generating stable grasps, eliminating the need for computationally expensive explicit contact-based objectives or refinement steps.
> - Flow likelihood estimation provides an effective intrinsic method for ranking generated grasp candidates, improving selection quality without requiring external discriminators or auxiliary networks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous grasping is a foundational capability for advanced robotic manipulation, yet reliably generating suitable hand configurations remains a complex challenge. This complexity arises from the intricate interplay between object geometry and the high-dimensional kinematics of robotic hands.

Historically, the field has approached this problem through two main paradigms:
- **Traditional Analytic Methods:** These methods, such as those optimizing for force closure, aim to derive physically stable grasps. While robust and effective for generating large-scale datasets offline, they are computationally intensive, often require precise object meshes, and are generally too slow for real-time online inference. They have, however, served as powerful tools for synthesizing extensive grasp datasets, such as the Dexonomy dataset which includes millions of diverse grasps.
- **Learning-Based Methods:** With the availability of large grasp datasets, a natural progression has been to train neural networks to learn and model these complex grasp distributions. Early methods often treated grasp generation as a regression problem, but the multi-modal nature of valid grasp distributions led to the adoption of generative models, including conditional variational autoencoders, diffusion models, and normalizing flow models.

A prevalent characteristic of many existing learning-based approaches is the reintroduction of explicit physics-based priors into the learning or inference pipeline. This often manifests as:
- **Contact-aware objectives:** Incorporating contact, penetration, or other physics-aware penalties during training.
- **Test-time refinement:** Utilizing contact-based optimization or adaptation steps during inference to improve grasp feasibility.
- **Auxiliary evaluators:** Employing discriminators or evaluators to filter or refine generated grasps.

These components, while aiming to improve grasp quality, often inherit drawbacks from analytic methods, such as the need for sensitive parameter tuning and increased computational cost, leading to slower inference times. Furthermore, even with these additions, some methods may still produce grasps with notable penetrations or suboptimal stability.

KPGrasp positions itself within this landscape by addressing the question of whether large-scale, high-quality data can implicitly provide sufficient grasp priors for learning, thereby obviating the need for explicit physics-based designs in the learning objective or test-time process. The work aims to demonstrate that a well-designed generative model, trained on comprehensive datasets, can learn robust grasp priors without relying on these computationally expensive or tuning-sensitive explicit physics-based heuristics. It seeks to develop a scalable and efficient learning-based method that leverages data effectively to overcome the limitations of prior approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a dexterous grasp generator, named KPGrasp, that can learn grasp priors directly from large-scale, offline-synthesized grasp data. This approach aims to eliminate the reliance on explicit contact losses or contact-based test-time refinement, which are common but often problematic features in many learning-based grasp generation methods.

The motivation behind KPGrasp stems from two core issues identified in the existing literature:

#### 2.1. Limitations of Conventional Grasp Parameterization

Most learning-based methods represent a grasp as a hybrid tuple comprising a non-Euclidean SE(3) wrist pose (rotation and translation) and Euclidean joint angles. This conventional "pose-joint" parameterization presents several challenges for scalable generative learning:
- **Manifold Discontinuities:** Predicting rotations in SE(3) often introduces discontinuities that can complicate the learning process for neural networks.
- **Kinematic Error Accumulation:** Small errors in the predicted wrist pose can propagate along the kinematic chain of the robotic hand, leading to larger positional inaccuracies for the fingers and fingertips.
- **Loss Balancing Issues:** The mixed nature of the output space (non-Euclidean pose and Euclidean joint angles) often necessitates careful balancing of different loss components, which can be a brittle and time-consuming process.
- **Lack of Native Spatial Reasoning:** The mixed parameterization does not naturally align the hand's representation with the object's input representation (typically a point cloud), making it less intuitive for the network to directly reason about spatial proximity and interaction between the hand and the object.

#### 2.2. Desire for Simplified and Scalable Generative Modeling

Many learning-based methods often re-introduce the complexity they aim to avoid by incorporating explicit physics priors into their training objectives or inference pipelines. This can include contact or penetration penalties, test-time optimization, or auxiliary discriminators. These additions can lead to:
- **Brittle Parameter Tuning:** The additional loss terms or refinement steps often require extensive and delicate tuning of hyperparameters.
- **Slow Inference:** Optimization-based refinement steps at test time can significantly increase inference latency, hindering real-time deployment.
- **Potential for Suboptimal Grasps:** Despite these efforts, some methods may still generate grasps with significant penetrations or inadequate stability, indicating that integrating these priors effectively can be challenging.

To address these motivations, KPGrasp proposes two key design choices:
1. **All-Euclidean 3D Hand-Keypoint Parameterization:** By predicting the 3D coordinates of hand keypoints directly, KPGrasp transforms grasp generation into a purely Euclidean learning problem. These keypoints are expressed in the same coordinate frame as the object point cloud input, enabling the network to perform native spatial reasoning about hand-object interactions. Furthermore, this parameterization is naturally compatible with Transformer architectures, where each keypoint can be treated as a distinct token, allowing attention mechanisms to model complex dependencies across the hand structure.
2. **Simple yet Scalable Transformer-based Flow-Matching Model:** This generative model is designed to operate effectively within the Euclidean keypoint space. It is trained solely with a standard flow-matching objective, aiming to avoid the need for complex, hand-crafted auxiliary losses or post-processing steps. The Transformer architecture is selected for its demonstrated scalability with increasing data, model capacity, and batch size, fostering the learning of generalizable grasp priors.

By implementing these designs, KPGrasp aims to generate high-quality, stable, and diverse dexterous grasps efficiently and without the typical drawbacks associated with explicit physics-based heuristics in learning pipelines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

KPGrasp employs a flow-matching framework that integrates an all-Euclidean 3D hand-keypoint parameterization with a Transformer-based conditional flow model.

#### 3.1. Keypoint Grasp Parameterization

Instead of the conventional [R, t, θ] representation (SE(3) wrist pose and joint angles), KPGrasp directly predicts a set of 3D hand keypoints, $K = [k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_J]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{J \times 3}$. These keypoints are defined in the same coordinate frame as the input object point cloud.

- **Keypoint Definition:** For the Shadow Hand, KPGrasp uses J=21 keypoints. These include points located at the mechanical joints, fingertips, and five additional keypoints on the rigid palm (one palm-center point and four finger-root points). This palm keypoint configuration provides stable correspondences for wrist pose registration.
- **Parameterization Conversion:**
 - **Training Labels:** During training, the ground truth keypoint labels $K$ are derived by applying forward kinematics to the pose-joint grasps [R, t, θ] available in the dataset.
 - **Deployment (Inference):** The predicted keypoints are converted back into executable robot commands. First, the palm keypoints are used to determine the rigid wrist pose (R, t) through SVD-based rigid registration. Subsequently, the joint angles θ are recovered using a standard inverse-kinematics (IK) solver. This IK step projects the generated keypoints onto the hand's kinematic manifold but does not incorporate object geometry to optimize for contact, penetration, or force closure. The IK process has been found to be both effective (average keypoint reconstruction error of 0.7 mm) and efficient (0.002 s per grasp in batched inference).

#### 3.2. Conditional Keypoint Flow Model

The core of KPGrasp is a conditional Transformer-based network designed to learn the keypoint flow field.

- **Object Encoder:**
 - Input: An object point cloud O, sampled to 1,024 points.
 - Processing: A MinkUNet, a 3D sparse convolutional backbone, processes the point cloud.
 - Output: Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) is applied to extract $P' = 128$ geometry tokens $T_{obj} \in \mathbb{R}^{P' \times C}$, where C=512 is the hidden feature dimension. These tokens are designed to capture object geometry relevant to grasp generation while maintaining computational efficiency for attention layers.

- **Keypoint Token Embedding:**
 - Input: A noisy keypoint set $K_\tau \in \mathbb{R}^{J \times 3}$ at a given flow time $\tau$.
 - Processing: Each keypoint $k_j \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is independently projected to C dimensions using a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). Learnable keypoint positional embeddings are then added to these projections.
 - Output: Hand keypoint tokens $T_{kp} \in \mathbb{R}^{J \times C}$.
 - **Time Injection:** The flow time $\tau$ is integrated into the model through adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) in each attention or Feed-Forward Network (FFN) sublayer, allowing the model to condition its predictions on the current point in the flow trajectory.

- **Action Head (DiT-based Network):**
 - Architecture: The action head consists of $M=8$ Diffusion Transformer (DiT) blocks.
 - DiT Block Functionality: Each block alternates between two types of attention:
 - **Keypoint Self-Attention:** Operates on $T_{kp}$ to model interdependencies and correlations among the hand keypoints.
 - **Object-Conditioned Cross-Attention:** Uses $T_{kp}$ as queries and $T_{obj}$ (from the object encoder) as keys and values. This mechanism enables the hand geometry to be aligned with the object surface, facilitating hand-object interaction reasoning.
 - Final Output: A final MLP maps the updated $T_{kp}$ tokens to the velocity field $v \in \mathbb{R}^{J \times 3}$, which is the target for the flow-matching objective.

- **Training:**
 - Loss Function: KPGrasp is trained exclusively using the standard flow-matching objective (Eq. 1), which minimizes the difference between the predicted velocity field and the target velocity field along linear interpolation paths between Gaussian noise and data samples. This design avoids the need for contact losses, penetration penalties, or other physics-aware objectives.
 - Optimization: The Muon optimizer is used with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-4}$.
 - Resources: Training spans 200,000 steps with a global batch size of 4,096, distributed across 8 NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. The training duration is approximately two days.

- **Inference:**
 - Candidate Generation: For a given object, a batch of $N=100$ Gaussian keypoint-noise samples are drawn. The learned ODE is then solved with 5 Euler integration steps to generate 100 grasp candidates.
 - Likelihood Ranking: For each generated candidate, its likelihood (log-density) under the learned flow model is estimated. This is performed using the Hutchinson trace estimator with one Rademacher probe vector per ODE trajectory. This likelihood serves as a model-confidence score.
 - Selection: The 10 grasps with the highest likelihood scores are selected. This scoring mechanism is intrinsic to the flow model and does not require external discriminators or auxiliary networks.
 - Execution: The selected keypoints are mapped to the standard [R, t, θ] format using the rigid-registration and IK procedure for simulation or real-robot execution.

This methodology emphasizes learning directly from data through a Euclidean representation and a scalable generative model, intentionally omitting explicit contact-based heuristics during both training and inference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

KPGrasp was evaluated across two simulation benchmarks, Dexonomy and DexGrasp Anything, and in real-world robot experiments.

#### 4.1. Benchmark on Dexonomy

The Dexonomy benchmark, featuring 10.7k objects and 9.5M grasps across 31 types, presents a challenging environment for generative models due to its multi-modal grasp distribution. KPGrasp and baselines were retrained on the official Dexonomy dataset.

- **Quantitative Results (Table 1):** KPGrasp demonstrated improvements across all reported metrics:
 - **Grasp Success Rate (GSR):** 76.3%, an improvement of 47.4 percentage points over the strongest directly comparable baseline.
 - **Object Success Rate (OSR):** 97.3%.
 - **Penetration Depth (PD):** Reduced to 2.4 mm, indicating cleaner hand-object interactions.
 - **Contact Distance Consistency (CDC):** Achieved 5.6 mm, suggesting more stable contacts.
 - KPGrasp also outperformed the classifier-based Dexonomy variant (63.9% GSR) by 12.4% in GSR, without requiring additional generated training data or a separate classifier.
- **Qualitative Results (Figure 4):** KPGrasp generated stable and diverse grasps on complex objects, while several baselines exhibited significant hand-object penetration. This suggests KPGrasp's ability to learn a general grasp prior from data, leading to accurate contact and low penetration without explicit refinement.

#### 4.2. Benchmark on DexGrasp Anything

To assess cross-benchmark generalization, KPGrasp, trained solely on Dexonomy, was tested on the DexGrasp Anything benchmark's official object sets without fine-tuning or contact-based test-time refinement.

- **Quantitative Results (Table 2):** KPGrasp achieved the best average success rate (63.8%) and the lowest average penetration depth (12.9 mm) across the five object sets (GRAB, RealD, MD, UDG, DGN). This performance, despite being trained on a different dataset, suggests that the keypoint flow model learns a transferable grasp prior. In comparison, baselines with task-specific heuristics, while sometimes excelling on individual datasets, did not demonstrate uniform transferability.
- **Additional Analysis (Supplementary Table 6):** When KPGrasp was trained directly on the individual DexGrasp Anything datasets, its average success rate (47.8%) was lower than the DexGrasp Anything model (58.5%). This was attributed to potential mismatches between the DGA testing metric and the dataset filtering protocol, resulting in lower inherent quality of the DGA training data, and KPGrasp's sensitivity to data quality compared to DGA's physics-aware heuristics. The model's best performance was consistently achieved when trained on the large-scale, high-quality Dexonomy dataset.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

Ablations on the Dexonomy benchmark explored the impact of key design choices.

- **Hand Parameterization (Table 3):** The keypoint parameterization consistently outperformed pose-joint variants (using either Euclidean 9D matrix flow or SE(3) flow) in terms of grasp quality. The SE(3) flow variant performed worse than the simpler Euclidean rotation, indicating potential tuning difficulties with manifold flow matching.
- **Tokenization (Table 3):** Performance degraded significantly when keypoints were collapsed into a single token (GSR dropped from 76.3% to 55.4%). Conversely, pose-joint outputs, even with multiple tokens, did not match the performance of keypoint parameterization, suggesting that the spatial reasoning enabled by multiple keypoint tokens is beneficial.
- **Likelihood Ranking (Table 3):** Using likelihood ranking to select the top 10 grasps from 100 candidates improved GSR from 67.0% (random selection) to 76.3%, confirming the effectiveness of the flow likelihood as a selection criterion.

#### 4.4. Scaling Behavior (Figure 5)

KPGrasp's performance (success rate) improved with increases in training data size, model capacity (number of DiT blocks), and batch size. The keypoint parameterization consistently outperformed the pose-joint variant across all scaling levels, with the performance gap widening as scale increased. This indicates the scalability of KPGrasp's architecture and the effectiveness of the keypoint parameterization.

#### 4.5. Inference Time Analysis

On an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, KPGrasp required 0.032 seconds per returned grasp (selecting 10 top-likelihood grasps from 100 candidates for 4 objects in a batch). This speed represents an 87-fold improvement compared to DexGrasp Anything, which relies on contact-based test-time refinement (approx. 2.8 s per grasp). KPGrasp also demonstrated a valid-grasp throughput of approximately 24 grasps per second, which is over three times faster than typical analytic data-generation pipelines like Dexonomy (less than 7 valid grasps per second per GPU). Analysis of sampling steps (Supplementary Table 5) showed that 5 Euler steps provided the most effective balance between quality and speed.

#### 4.6. Real-World Experiments

KPGrasp was evaluated on a real-world setup with a Galbot platform, Sharpa hand, and RealSense D405 camera, using 20 diverse objects. The model was retrained on simulated single-view point clouds (3.3M grasps from 5k DGN objects).

- **Results:** Out of 100 trials (5 trials per object), KPGrasp achieved 83 successful grasps. A trial was considered successful if the object remained held after lifting.
- **Failure Modes:** Observed failure modes included sensitivity to small object-pose perturbations, which could arise from calibration errors and sensor noise, as well as potential collisions with the table during trajectory interpolation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of KPGrasp represents an advancement in dexterous grasp generation for robotics, with several potential implications:

- **Reduced Reliance on Explicit Physics Priors:** KPGrasp demonstrates that high-quality dexterous grasps can be generated by learning implicit grasp priors directly from large-scale data, without the need for auxiliary contact losses, penetration penalties, or computationally intensive contact-based test-time refinement. This simplification streamlines the learning process and reduces the reliance on domain-specific engineering and hyperparameter tuning.
- **Enhanced Efficiency and Scalability:** By achieving a significantly faster inference time (0.032 s per grasp) compared to methods employing test-time refinement, KPGrasp becomes more viable for real-time online deployment in robotic systems. Its demonstrated scalability with data, model capacity, and batch size suggests that performance could further improve with larger datasets and computational resources, supporting the trend towards large-scale generative models in robotics.
- **Novel Grasp Parameterization:** The introduction and validation of an all-Euclidean 3D hand-keypoint parameterization addresses several limitations of conventional mixed SE(3) pose and joint-angle representations. By offering a clean Euclidean learning problem that aligns naturally with object point cloud inputs and Transformer architectures, this parameterization provides a foundation for improved spatial reasoning and more robust grasp generation.
- **Transferability of Grasp Priors:** The observation that KPGrasp, trained on the Dexonomy dataset, achieved strong performance on the distinct DexGrasp Anything benchmark without fine-tuning indicates that the model learns generalizable and transferable grasp priors. This capability is valuable for deploying robotic systems across varied tasks and environments without extensive retraining.
- **Practical Real-World Deployment:** The successful deployment of KPGrasp in real-world experiments with partial object observations underscores its practical utility. While specific failure modes related to calibration and sensor noise were identified, the high success rate (83%) on diverse physical objects demonstrates the method's potential for integration into functional robotic manipulation platforms.

In summary, KPGrasp contributes to the field by proposing a more data-centric and scalable approach to dexterous grasp generation. Its findings support the hypothesis that rich, high-quality data can implicitly convey complex physical priors, paving the way for more robust, efficient, and broadly applicable robotic grasping solutions.
