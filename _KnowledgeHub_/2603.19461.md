# Hyperagents

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at multiple institutions including the University of British Columbia and Meta developed HyperAgents, a framework enabling AI systems to autonomously modify their own self-improvement mechanisms, extending recursive self-improvement beyond coding tasks. This approach achieved 0.710 accuracy in paper review and 0.372 performance in robotics reward design, while also demonstrating transferable meta-level improvements to new domains.

## Problem

- Existing self-improving AI systems rely on fixed, handcrafted meta-level mechanisms that limit their capacity for ongoing improvement and lead to an infinite regress problem.
- Prior self-improvement architectures often lack generalizability, as their effectiveness is tied to a domain-specific alignment between task-solving and self-modification skills.
- The challenge of achieving truly open-ended and self-accelerating progress where the mechanism of improvement itself can be enhanced, not just the task-solving abilities.

## Method

- Introduces **HyperAgents**, ==self-referential agents== that integrate both a ==task-solving agent== and a ==meta-agent== (for generating modifications) into a single, editable program.
- Extends the **Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM)** framework into **DGM-Hyperagents (DGM-H)**, allowing the meta-agent component within a hyperagent to modify itself as well as the task agent, enabling ==metacognitive self-modification==.
- Utilizes an ==open-ended, population-based exploration== process where improved hyperagents are archived as ==stepping stones== for future, iteratively refined agents across diverse domains like coding, paper review, and robotics.

## Results

- DGM-H achieved self-improvement in coding comparable to the original DGM, and significantly outperformed non-customized DGM in non-coding domains, reaching **0.710** accuracy in paper review and **0.372** performance in robotics reward design.
- Hyperagents optimized via DGM-H demonstrated transferable self-improvement strategies, yielding an `imp@50` of **0.630** in Olympiad-level math grading when transferred from other domains, significantly higher than initial or `DGM-custom` baselines.
- The framework showed compounding self-improvements by building on existing strong solutions, improving ProofAutoGrader's accuracy from **0.561** to **0.601** on the IMO-GradingBench.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Metacognitive self-modification is practically achievable, allowing AI to improve not only its task performance but also the underlying process by which it generates improvements.
> - Learned meta-level improvements (e.g., performance tracking, persistent memory, prompt template systems) are generalizable and transferable, accelerating progress when hyperagents encounter new, unseen domains.
> - Both metacognitive self-modification and open-ended exploration are crucial for sustained, accumulating self-improvement across complex and diverse computational tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the broader research landscape of self-improving AI, open-ended learning, and self-referential meta-learning. The concept of AI systems capable of recursively improving themselves has theoretical roots in models like the Gödel Machine (Schmidhuber, 2003), which proposed agents that could rewrite themselves when provably beneficial. More recent practical advancements have utilized large language models (LLMs) to enable self-improvement through iterative refinement of prompts, reasoning traces, or code repositories. Examples include systems that update model weights, engage in self-play, or refine their own code.

A significant predecessor to the current work is the Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) (Zhang et al., 2025b), which demonstrated open-ended self-improvement in coding domains. The DGM operates by generating and evaluating self-modified code variants, accumulating successful improvements in an archive. However, existing self-improvement architectures, including the DGM, typically rely on fixed, handcrafted meta-level mechanisms. This limitation means that the meta-agent—the component responsible for generating modifications—is itself not subject to improvement. In the DGM, the instruction-generation mechanism is fixed and designed specifically for coding tasks. This implies that the DGM's capacity for self-improvement in non-coding domains is constrained by the assumption that task-solving skills align with self-modification skills. Such an alignment is not generally expected to hold across diverse tasks.

The "HyperAgents" paper addresses this limitation by introducing a framework where the self-improvement mechanism itself is editable. This allows for "metacognitive self-modification," moving beyond fixed meta-level designs that lead to an infinite regress problem when attempting to improve the meta-agent. The work aims to generalize recursive self-improvement beyond coding, to any computable task, by enabling the agent to improve not only its task-solving abilities but also the process by which it generates future improvements. This aligns with the broader goal of open-ended systems that continually advance their capabilities and the mechanisms of discovery, drawing inspiration from human scientific and technological progress where tools and processes for innovation also improve over time.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop self-improving AI systems that can reduce reliance on human engineering by learning to improve their own learning and problem-solving processes in an open-ended manner. The key motivations for this work stem from critical limitations identified in prior self-improving AI architectures:

1. **Limitation of Fixed Meta-Level Mechanisms:** Most existing self-improvement approaches, including the Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM), rely on a fixed meta-agent (a higher-level system that modifies a base system). This design fundamentally limits the system's ability to improve, as the base system can only evolve within the boundaries defined by the unchanging meta-agent's initial design. Introducing a "meta-meta" system to improve the meta-agent merely shifts the problem upward, leading to an infinite regress without resolving the core issue of a non-modifiable highest-level mechanism. The motivation is to overcome this by enabling the system to modify *any part of itself*, including the self-improvement mechanism.

2. **Lack of Generalizability Beyond Specific Domains:** The DGM demonstrated open-ended self-improvement effectively in coding tasks. However, its effectiveness in self-improvement hinges on a specific assumption: that the skills required to solve the evaluation tasks (coding) are sufficiently aligned with the skills needed for effective self-reflection and self-modification (also coding). This alignment allows gains in coding ability to translate into gains in self-improvement ability. The authors motivate that this assumption is unlikely to hold outside coding domains, where the skills for task solving might differ substantially from those for analyzing failures, proposing improvements, and implementing them. The objective is to eliminate this domain-specific alignment requirement, enabling self-accelerating progress on *any computable task*.

3. **Enabling Metacognitive Self-Modification and Self-Accelerating Progress:** The research is motivated by the potential for unbounded, self-accelerating progress that could arise if the mechanism of improvement itself were subject to improvement. This concept, termed "metacognitive self-modification," suggests that a system could not only get better at solving tasks but also get better at *how* it gets better. This would transform technological and scientific progress from a human-paced process into an autonomously accelerating one. The goal is to design a self-referential agent that integrates task execution and agent generation into a single editable program.

To achieve these objectives, the authors introduce "hyperagents" as a self-referential agent concept and instantiate it by extending the DGM into "DGM-Hyperagents" (DGM-H). The DGM-H framework aims to demonstrate that metacognitive self-modification is achievable in practice, supporting sustained and accumulating progress across diverse domains without requiring manual customization for each new task.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DGM-Hyperagents (DGM-H) framework is designed to enable self-improving AI that can modify its own self-improvement mechanisms for any computable task. It builds upon the open-ended exploration structure of the Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM).

**Core Concept: Hyperagents**
An "agent" is defined as any computable program, potentially incorporating foundation models (FMs), external tools, or learned components.
- **Task Agent:** An agent designed to solve a given set of tasks (e.g., generating code edits, predicting paper acceptance, designing robotics reward functions). Task agents are empirically evaluated.
- **Meta Agent:** An agent whose sole function is to modify existing agents and generate new ones. It has access to the archive of previous agents and their evaluations, proposing changes to improve future performance.
- **Hyperagent:** A self-referential agent that integrates both a task agent and a meta agent into a single, editable program. This allows a hyperagent to modify not only its task-solving behavior but also its own self-modification process (the meta agent). This capability is termed "metacognitive self-modification." The system utilizes Python, a Turing-complete language, allowing for arbitrary code modifications.

**DGM-Hyperagents (DGM-H) Architecture:**
DGM-H retains the open-ended, population-based exploration process of the original DGM, which aims to mitigate premature convergence by maintaining an archive of progressively improving agents.
1. **Initialization:** The DGM-H starts with a single initial hyperagent. This hyperagent is built around a frozen FM with external tool-use capabilities (e.g., a bash tool for shell commands and a specialized tool for file inspection and modification). The initial task agent is kept simple, typically providing a direct FM response, while the initial meta agent is tasked with modifying any part of the codebase given evaluation results and remaining iterations.
2. **Iterative Process:** The DGM-H operates in alternating phases:
 - **Metacognitive Self-modification:** Parent hyperagents are selected from the archive. The selected parent then *self-modifies*, producing a new child hyperagent. Crucially, the meta agent component within the parent hyperagent can modify *itself* as well as the task agent component. The parent selection mechanism is probabilistic, proportional to an agent's performance and inversely proportional to the number of children it has successfully compiled, balancing exploitation and exploration.
 - **Evaluation:** Each newly generated hyperagent is empirically evaluated on a set of computable tasks.
 - **Archiving:** Successful (e.g., compilable) modified hyperagents are added to the archive as potential stepping stones for future improvements.

**Experiment Setup:**
- **Baselines:** The DGM-H is compared against several baselines to isolate the contributions of its key components:
 - `DGM-H w/o self-improve`: The meta agent remains fixed to the initial meta agent throughout the run, preventing improvements to the self-modification process.
 - `DGM-H w/o open-ended exploration`: The archive is removed; each new hyperagent replaces its predecessor, limiting population-based search.
 - `DGM`: The original Darwin Gödel Machine, which uses a handcrafted, fixed instruction-generation mechanism designed for coding.
 - `DGM-custom`: The DGM with its instruction-generation mechanism manually customized for each target non-coding domain.
 - `Static solutions`: Handcrafted solutions from prior work for each domain.
- **Domains:** Experiments span diverse domains:
 - **Coding (Polyglot):** Agent modifies code repositories based on natural language instructions. Metric: Pass@1.
 - **Paper Review:** Agent predicts binary accept/reject decisions for AI research papers. Metric: Accuracy against human judgments.
 - **Robotics Reward Design:** Agent generates reward functions for quadruped robots in simulation. Metric: Performance of the trained RL policy. This involves moving beyond language-only tasks and leveraging external simulators/RL algorithms.
 - **Olympiad-level Math Grading:** Agent assigns discrete scores to solutions for IMO-level math problems. Metric: Accuracy against expert human grades. This domain is used for cross-domain transfer evaluation.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** To manage computational cost, a staged evaluation is used: agents are first evaluated on a small subset of training tasks; if performance is sufficient, they are then evaluated on the full training set. Validation sets (or training sets if no validation) are used for parent selection, while separate held-out test sets are used for final evaluation.
- **Foundation Models (FMs):** Claude-4.5-Sonnet is used for self-modification across most domains (Claude 3.5 Sonnet for Polyglot). Different FMs (GPT-4o, o3-mini, o4-mini) are selected for task evaluation based on competence and cost, with most temperatures set to 0.0 (o4-mini at 1.0).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The DGM-H framework demonstrated substantial and generalizable improvements across multiple levels of self-improvement: task performance, the ability to improve, and the accumulation of improvements across runs and domains.

#### 4.1. Improving Task Performance

**Coding Domain (Polyglot):**
The DGM-H achieved self-improvement in coding comparable to the original DGM.
- On a 50-task Polyglot training subset, DGM-H improved median performance from 0.140 (initial agent) to 0.340 (CI: 0.300 – 0.380).
- On the full Polyglot benchmark (165 unseen tasks), performance increased from 0.084 (initial agent) to 0.267 (CI: 0.231 – 0.280). These figures are similar to the original DGM, which improved from 0.140 to 0.380 on the training subset and from 0.142 to 0.307 on the full benchmark. This suggests DGM-H's generalized approach is effective even in domains specifically tailored for its predecessor.

**Beyond Coding (Paper Review, Robotics Reward Design):**
The DGM-H exhibited strong performance in diverse non-coding domains.
- The initial agent performed poorly (0.0 on paper review, 0.060 on robotics reward design) due to lack of domain-specific parsing or effective reward functions.
- The original DGM, designed for coding, achieved 0.0 (CI: 0.0 – 0.510) on paper review and 0.0 (CI: 0.0 – 0.090) on robotics reward design, indicating its limitations without customization.
- `DGM-custom`, with manual domain-specific adjustments, performed better: 0.590 (CI: 0.570 – 0.650) on paper review and 0.348 (CI: 0.305 – 0.385) on robotics reward design, significantly outperforming the original DGM (p < 0.05).
- The `DGM-H` achieved test-set performance of 0.710 (CI: 0.590 – 0.750) on paper review (outperforming the static baseline of 0.630) and 0.372 (CI: 0.355 – 0.436) on robotics reward design (surpassing the default reward function of 0.348). `DGM-H` significantly outperformed the original DGM (p < 0.05) and showed higher median performance than `DGM-custom`, though not statistically significant (p > 0.05).
- Qualitative analysis indicated that DGM-H improved task agents by shifting from superficial prompt tweaks to structured decision-making (e.g., multi-stage evaluation pipelines in paper review) and by incrementally building internal knowledge bases (e.g., environment constraints in robotics reward design).

**Necessity of Metacognition and Open-Ended Exploration:**
Ablation studies confirmed the importance of both metacognitive self-modification and open-ended exploration.
- `DGM-H w/o self-improve` showed minimal progress, achieving 0.0 (CI: 0.0 – 0.130) on paper review and 0.213 (CI: 0.180 – 0.348) on robotics reward design. DGM-H significantly outperformed this baseline (p < 0.05).
- `DGM-H w/o open-ended exploration` similarly made little progress, with 0.0 (CI: 0.0 – 0.560) on paper review and 0.116 (CI: 0.0 – 0.348) on robotics reward design. DGM-H significantly outperformed this baseline (p < 0.05). These results suggest that both components are essential for sustained self-improvement.

#### 4.2. Improving the Ability to Improve

The research introduced `improvement@k` (imp@k) to quantify a meta agent's ability to generate improved task agents within `k` iterations.

**Initial Meta Agent Capability:**
- On Olympiad-level math grading, starting with the initial task and meta agents, both `DGM w/o self-improve` and `DGM-H w/o self-improve` yielded `imp@50` values near 0.0 (CI: 0.0 – 0.0 and 0.0 – 0.130, respectively). This demonstrated the initial meta agent's limited capacity to generate improvements in a new domain without further self-modification.

**Transferability of Meta-Level Improvements:**
- **DGM-custom transfer:** Meta agents optimized via `DGM-custom` in paper review and robotics showed `imp@50` of 0.0 (CI: 0.0 – 0.010) when transferred to Olympiad-level math grading. This indicated that `DGM-custom`'s gains relied on domain-specific engineering rather than generalizable meta-level improvement.
- **DGM-H transfer:** In contrast, hyperagents optimized via `DGM-H` in prior runs (paper review and robotics) achieved a `imp@50` of 0.630 (CI: 0.540 – 0.630) when transferred to Olympiad-level math grading. This was significantly higher than the initial agent's `imp@50` (p < 0.05). This finding suggests that `DGM-H` learns general, transferable self-improvement strategies.
- Qualitative analysis revealed that transferred hyperagents autonomously acquired general-purpose meta-level capabilities such as performance tracking (recording, aggregating, and comparing metrics), persistent memory (storing synthesized insights and plans), and prompt template systems. These mechanisms allowed them to reason about improvement as an ongoing process and quickly make progress in unseen domains.

#### 4.3. Compounding Self-Improvements

The research investigated whether DGM-H's self-improvements accumulate across different settings.
- When DGM-H was run on Olympiad-level math grading, starting from transferred hyperagents (`DGM-H + transfer`), the best agents achieved a test-set score of 0.640 (CI: 0.550 – 0.720) over 200 iterations. This was higher than DGM-H starting from the initial agent (0.610, CI: 0.510 – 0.680), though not statistically significant (p > 0.05). This suggests that prior meta-level learning is reusable and contributes to continued improvement.
- The DGM-H was able to improve upon an existing strong baseline. When initialized with ProofAutoGrader (a static baseline with 0.670 score) as the task agent and a transferred meta agent, DGM-H achieved a test-set score of 0.700. On the full IMO-GradingBench, DGM-H improved ProofAutoGrader's accuracy from 0.561 to 0.601 and lowered the mean absolute error from 0.178 to 0.175. This indicates DGM-H's capacity to build on and enhance strong existing solutions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DGM-Hyperagents (DGM-H) framework represents an advancement in the field of self-improving AI by demonstrating that systems can autonomously improve not only their task-solving abilities but also the very mechanisms by which they generate those improvements. This "metacognitive self-modification" distinguishes it from prior self-improving systems, which typically rely on fixed meta-level architectures.

The core significance of DGM-H lies in its potential to generalize recursive self-improvement across any computable task, eliminating the prior reliance on domain-specific alignment between task performance and self-modification skills. The empirical results across diverse domains—coding, paper review, robotics reward design, and Olympiad-level math grading—suggest that DGM-H can achieve substantial, generalizable, and transferable gains. The qualitative findings, such as the autonomous development of performance tracking, persistent memory, and compute-aware strategic planning, illustrate that the system spontaneously generates sophisticated meta-level capabilities without explicit human instruction. This indicates a path toward AI systems that do not merely search for better solutions, but continually improve their search for *how to improve*.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Scientific and Technological Progress:** If self-improving systems can continually enhance their own problem-solving and improvement processes, they could accelerate discovery and innovation beyond human-paced rates, potentially transforming scientific research and technological development.
- **Reduced Human Engineering Reliance:** By autonomously improving their learning and problem-solving mechanisms, such systems could reduce the need for extensive human engineering in designing and refining AI architectures and algorithms.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Adaptability:** The ability of meta-level improvements to transfer across domains implies that knowledge about "how to improve" can be reused, potentially leading to more generally intelligent and adaptable AI systems.

**Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations that constrain truly unbounded progress in the current DGM-H implementation:
- **Fixed Task Distribution:** The system currently operates with a fixed task distribution, meaning it does not autonomously generate new tasks or curricula to adapt to its evolving capabilities. Co-evolving task distributions is identified as a direction for future work.
- **Fixed Outer Loop Components:** While hyperagents can modify their self-improvement mechanisms, certain components of the open-ended exploration loop (e.g., parent selection, evaluation protocols) remain fixed. Full self-modifiability would involve allowing agents to alter these outer-loop processes, though this could introduce experimental stability and safety challenges. Preliminary results suggest that modifying parent selection is feasible.

**Safety Considerations:**
The introduction of systems capable of autonomously modifying their own behavior and improvement mechanisms raises distinct safety considerations, which the authors proactively discuss. All experiments in this work were conducted under strict safety constraints, including sandboxed environments with resource limits and continuous human oversight. However, the broader implications include:
- **Potential for Rapid Evolution Beyond Oversight:** As AI systems become more open-ended and self-improving, their evolution could outpace human audit and interpretation capabilities, necessitating a re-evaluation of AI's role in society.
- **Reflection and Amplification of Biases:** DGM-H optimizes for objectives specified through human-defined benchmarks. If these benchmarks contain biases or misaligned incentives, the system may optimize for and exacerbate these undesirable traits. This highlights the importance of robust benchmark design and ethical evaluation.
- **Evaluation Gaming (Goodhart's Law):** Self-improving agents might discover strategies that exploit weaknesses in evaluation procedures, leading to metric improvements without progress on the intended underlying objective. Mitigating this requires diverse, robust, and dynamically refreshed evaluation protocols, alongside human oversight.

Overall, HyperAgents present a conceptual and empirical framework for building self-referential AI, indicating a significant step toward systems that can autonomously enhance their own intelligence across a broad spectrum of computational challenges, while also foregrounding crucial safety discussions for such advanced capabilities.
