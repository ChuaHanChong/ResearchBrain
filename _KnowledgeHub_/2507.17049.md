# Evaluating Uncertainty and Quality of Visual Language Action-enabled Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Mondragon University and Simula Research Laboratory developed a comprehensive suite of uncertainty and quality metrics specifically for Visual Language Action (VLA) models in robotic manipulation. Their empirical study demonstrates that these new metrics align with human expert assessments of task execution quality and offer a more nuanced understanding of robot performance beyond traditional binary success rates.

## Problem

- Existing evaluation methods for Visual Language Action (VLA) robots primarily rely on binary task success rates (pass/fail), failing to capture the nuance of performance.
- Current evaluations do not assess the physical 'quality' of a robot's execution, such as smoothness, efficiency, or avoidance of collisions.
- There is a lack of metrics to quantify a VLA model's 'confidence' or 'uncertainty' in its decisions during task execution.

## Method

- The authors proposed eight ==uncertainty metrics== (e.g., Action Position Instability, Entropy) and five ==quality metrics== (e.g., Trajectory Velocity Instability, Optimal Trajectory Difference) specifically tailored for **VLA** models.
- A large-scale empirical study was conducted, evaluating three state-of-the-art **VLA** models (**OpenVLA**, **π0**, **SpatialVLA**) across four robotic manipulation tasks in a simulator.
- Human domain experts manually labeled 908 successful task executions into 'high-quality,' 'medium-quality,' or 'low-quality' categories to provide a ground truth for assessing metric performance.

## Results

- SpatialVLA consistently demonstrated higher execution quality, with **43.5%** to **69.1%** of its successful tasks categorized as 'high-quality,' often outperforming other models even when raw success rates were similar.
- TCP-VI emerged as the most consistent quality metric, showing statistically significant positive correlations with human-assigned quality across various models and tasks.
- OT was identified as a robust and model-agnostic discriminator, consistently distinguishing successful tasks (regardless of quality) from outright failures, while most other metrics (except EV) incurred low computational overhead suitable for real-time use.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Binary success rates are insufficient for evaluating VLA models, as models with similar success rates can exhibit vastly different execution qualities as judged by humans.
> - Metrics like TCP-VI (Trajectory Velocity Instability), A-VI (Action Velocity Instability), A-AI (Action Acceleration Instability), and OT (Optimal Trajectory Difference) consistently correlate with human judgment of task quality and are computationally efficient.
> - Low-quality successful task executions often resemble complete failures in terms of measured characteristics, indicating a precarious state that existing metrics struggle to clearly differentiate from outright failure.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the crucial intersection of advanced artificial intelligence, robotics, and robust system evaluation, specifically focusing on Visual Language Action (VLA) models. VLA models represent a cutting-edge class of multimodal AI systems designed to enable embodied agents, such as robots, to perceive their environment visually, understand natural language instructions, and execute complex physical tasks autonomously. Propelled by recent advances in generative AI, these models are envisioned to revolutionize various sectors, from manufacturing to domestic applications, exemplified by robots like Tesla's Optimus and Boston Dynamics' Spot.

The current landscape of VLA model evaluation, however, suffers from significant limitations. Existing approaches predominantly rely on binary task success rates, often determined by symbolic oracles that check the final state of target objects (e.g., "is the object in the correct final position?"). While frameworks like VLATest have emerged to standardize benchmarking through fuzzing, they still primarily focus on this binary outcome. This paper critically identifies a major gap: these evaluations fail to capture the *quality* of task execution (e.g., smoothness, efficiency, avoidance of collisions) and the model's *confidence* or *uncertainty* in its decisions. A robot might successfully complete a task but do so inefficiently, with excessive jerky movements, multiple drops, or collisions – behaviors that are undesirable and indicative of low quality in real-world deployments. Moreover, without understanding model confidence, it is unclear whether a success was due to robust competence or mere chance.

This work directly addresses these shortcomings by proposing a novel suite of uncertainty and quality metrics specifically tailored for VLA models in robotic manipulation. By moving beyond simple success/failure to quantify "how well" and "how confidently" a task is performed, the paper aims to provide a more nuanced, reliable, and holistic evaluation framework. This shift is crucial for the safe and effective deployment of VLA-enabled robots in real-world, potentially high-stakes cyber-physical systems, where issues of quality, robustness, and trustworthiness are paramount. The research also contributes to the broader field of uncertainty quantification in deep learning and foundation models, adapting and extending existing UQ techniques to the unique multimodal and sequential decision-making context of VLA robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the recognized deficiencies in current evaluation practices for Visual Language Action (VLA) models. While VLA models show immense promise for enabling autonomous robotic task execution, their widespread adoption, especially in safety-critical or high-revenue environments, is hindered by an inability to comprehensively assess their performance beyond simple task completion.

The **key objectives** of this paper are precisely articulated through its contributions:

1. **To propose a comprehensive set of evaluation metrics:** The primary objective is to introduce eight novel uncertainty metrics and five novel quality metrics specifically designed for VLA models engaged in robotic manipulation tasks. These metrics aim to capture aspects of performance that binary success rates overlook, such as model confidence and the quality of physical execution.
2. **To empirically validate the proposed metrics:** A crucial objective is to assess the effectiveness of these new metrics through a large-scale empirical study. This involves manually labeling the quality of 908 successful task executions from state-of-the-art VLA models, leveraging human domain experts to establish a ground truth for task quality.
3. **To analyze the correlation between metrics and human judgment:** The paper aims to evaluate how well the proposed metrics align with human experts' qualitative assessments of task execution. This objective is vital for confirming the practical utility and interpretability of the new metrics.
4. **To assess the discriminatory power of the metrics:** An additional objective is to investigate the extent to which the proposed metrics can differentiate between tasks of varying quality levels (high, medium, low) and, crucially, between successful and entirely failing tasks. This aims to identify metrics that can serve as reliable indicators of performance degradation or outright failure.
5. **To evaluate the computational overhead of the metrics:** For practical real-time deployment, the computational cost of integrating these metrics is critical. This objective seeks to quantify the inference time overhead incurred by each proposed metric, guiding practitioners in their adoption choices.
6. **To provide a robust and reproducible research foundation:** By releasing a complete replication package, including code and results, the authors aim to foster reproducibility and facilitate further research in this nascent evaluation area.

In essence, the overarching **motivation** is to challenge the adequacy of existing evaluation practices that rely solely on binary success rates and to pave the way for more rigorous, comprehensive, and actionable evaluation frameworks. This shift is expected to enable improved real-time monitoring, adaptive enhancement, and ultimately, safer and more reliable deployment of VLA-enabled robotic systems in diverse, dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research is multifaceted, combining novel metric development with a rigorous empirical study and statistical analysis.

#### 3.1. Proposed Metrics

The paper introduces a total of thirteen metrics, categorized into uncertainty and quality measures:

- **Uncertainty Metrics (8):** These metrics quantify a VLA model's confidence in its decisions.
 - **Action-based Instability Metrics:**
 - **Action Position Instability (A-PI):** Measures the first-order difference of the robot's predicted actions, indicating sudden shifts or indecision.
 - **Action Velocity Instability (A-VI):** Measures the second-order difference of actions (change in velocity), revealing erratic control signals.
 - **Action Acceleration Instability (A-AI):** Measures the third-order difference of actions (change in acceleration), capturing oscillatory motions or jitter.
 - **Token-based Probability Distribution Metrics:** (Applicable to VLA models utilizing Visual Language Models (VLMs) as backbones or for action generation, outputting discrete tokens with probabilities).
 - **Token Probability (TB-TP):** Based on `1 - MaxP(token)`, where `MaxP` is the highest predicted probability for a token. Higher values indicate lower confidence.
 - **Prediction Confidence Score (TB-PCS):** Based on `1 - PCS(token)`, where `PCS` is the difference between the highest and second-highest token probabilities. Lower `PCS` suggests uncertainty.
 - **DeepGini (TB-D):** Quantifies the concentration of the token probability distribution. Higher values mean greater uncertainty.
 - **Entropy (TB-E):** Measures the dispersion of the token probability distribution. Higher entropy indicates greater uncertainty.
 - **Execution Variability (EV):** Quantifies the internal consistency of the model by measuring the standard deviation of outputs from multiple inferences on the same input at each step.

- **Quality Metrics (5):** These metrics assess the quality of the robot's physical movements and task execution.
 - **Tool Center Point (TCP) Trajectory Instability Metrics:**
 - **Trajectory Position Instability (TCP-PI):** Measures the first-order difference of the end-effector's 3D position, assessing trajectory smoothness.
 - **Trajectory Velocity Instability (TCP-VI):** Measures the second-order difference of TCP position, evaluating acceleration changes.
 - **Trajectory Acceleration Instability (TCP-AI):** Measures the third-order difference of TCP position, capturing abrupt changes in acceleration (shaking/jittering).
 - **Trajectory Instability (TI):** Quantifies overall motion quality using Root Mean Square (RMS) jerk (third derivative of position) of the end-effector trajectory, aligning with human movement principles.
 - **Optimal Trajectory Difference (OT):** Evaluates goal-oriented behavior by measuring the spatial proximity of the TCP to task-relevant reference points. It dynamically adapts, measuring distance to the object before grasping and to the final target pose afterward.

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup

- **VLA Models:** Three state-of-the-art VLA models were selected: OpenVLA (7B parameters, Prismatic-7b VLM, Llama 2 backbone), π0 (3.3B parameters, SigLIP/Gemma-2B VLM, conditional flow matching with action expert transformer), and SpatialVLA (3B parameters, PaLIGemma-2 backbone, SigLIP/ZoeDepth for visual/3D features). These models were chosen for their recent development, known performance, and availability for an open-source simulator.
- **Environments and Tasks:** The SimplerEnv benchmark was used, encompassing two robotic platforms and four manipulation tasks:
 - Google robot (Everyday Robot): "Pick Up an Object" and "Move Object A near Object B".
 - WidowX robot: "Stack Object A on Object B" and "Place Object A Inside Object B".
 For each task, 500 diverse scenes (from VLATest) were generated, featuring random object selection, positions, and confounding objects.
- **Configurations:** A fixed random seed ensured consistent initial scene states. The EV metric used 4 inference samples due to hardware constraints. Temporal difference metrics (A-PI, A-VI, A-AI, TCP-PI, TCP-VI, TCP-AI) employed an 8-step time window for reliable estimations.
- **Execution Platform:** Experiments were distributed across two high-performance servers, equipped with AMD EPYC CPUs and NVIDIA RTX A6000/A100 GPUs, accumulating approximately 397 hours of GPU time.

#### 3.3. Human Evaluation Procedure

- **Labeling Process:** Two experienced domain engineers (one with a robotics master's, another with extensive industrial automation experience) manually reviewed 908 successful task executions. They categorized each successful task as "high-quality," "medium-quality," or "low-quality." A third expert resolved disagreements.
- **Quality Criteria:** Detailed, objective definitions for each quality level were established for every task (Table I), accompanied by illustrative examples.
- **Tooling:** A custom web-based application facilitated the labeling process, providing video playback and instruction prompts.
- **Inter-rater Agreement:** Cohen’s Kappa coefficient revealed an 85% agreement, indicating "almost perfect" consensus.

#### 3.4. Statistical Analysis

- **Data Aggregation:** For statistical tests, raw metric values (per time step) were aggregated by computing their average over each entire task execution, yielding a single value per metric per run.
- **Correlation (RQ2):** Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) was used to measure monotonic relationships between metric values and human-assigned quality levels. A p-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance, with correlation strength interpreted using Cohen's guidelines.
- **Discrimination (RQ3):** Given non-normally distributed data (Shapiro-Wilk test), the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (p-value < 0.05) was employed. Effect sizes were quantified using Vargha and Delaney's Â12 value, interpreted based on Romano et al.'s guidelines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical study yielded several critical findings, challenging conventional evaluation paradigms and highlighting the utility of the proposed metrics.

#### 4.1. RQ1 – Replication: Task Success vs. Human Judgment of Quality

The study replicated previous evaluations but introduced a manual quality assessment by human experts.
- **Success Rate Discrepancy:** While raw success rates positioned π0 and SpatialVLA as generally outperforming OpenVLA, this metric alone was found to be misleading. For instance, in "Pick up," π0 achieved 161 successes compared to SpatialVLA's 191. However, human experts labeled only 51 of π0's successes as "high-quality," versus SpatialVLA's 132.
- **Dominance of SpatialVLA in Quality:** SpatialVLA consistently demonstrated higher task execution quality, with 43.5% to 69.1% of its successful tasks categorized as "high-quality." In contrast, π0's successful executions often skewed towards "low-quality," especially in "Pick up" and "Move near" tasks.
- **Finding 1:** VLA model performance in terms of raw success rate varied by robot type (Google robot tasks favored SpatialVLA, WidowX robot tasks favored π0).
- **Finding 2:** Success rate is an insufficient metric for VLA model assessment; significant quality differences exist even with similar success rates. SpatialVLA exhibited the highest overall execution quality. False negatives in initial "success" labeling were also identified, underscoring the need for more robust assessment.

#### 4.2. RQ2 – Correlation: Metrics Reflecting Robot Performance

This research question investigated the correlation between the proposed uncertainty and quality metrics and human expert judgments of task quality.
- **Uncertainty Metrics Performance:** The performance varied by task and model.
 - For SpatialVLA, nearly all uncertainty metrics showed statistically significant positive correlations (moderate to strong).
 - A-PI, A-VI, and A-AI (Action-based Instability) showed strong statistically significant correlations for π0 in "Pick up" and "Put on."
 - For OpenVLA, all uncertainty metrics showed moderate correlation for "Pick up" but no significant correlation for "Move near."
 - **EV (Execution Variability)** proved unreliable, showing statistical significance in only one out of twelve task-model combinations and often providing identical inference values, making it unsuitable.
- **Quality Metrics Performance:**
 - For "Pick up," all available quality metrics showed statistically significant correlation, with TCP-VI, TCP-AI, and TI exhibiting strong correlations for all three models.
 - **TCP-VI (Trajectory Velocity Instability)** emerged as the most consistent metric across models and tasks, demonstrating statistically significant positive correlations in many scenarios. Smooth velocity changes indicate precise control, while spikes suggest hesitation or errors.
- **Finding 3:** Uncertainty metrics (especially A-VI and A-AI) can be reliable indicators, but practitioners should contextually evaluate them. EV is not recommended.
- **Finding 4:** TCP-VI is the most consistent and recommended quality metric, reflecting robot end-effector velocity stability.
- **Object Orientation Limitation:** For the "Move near" task, a significant portion of low-quality labels stemmed from incorrect final object orientation, which the proposed metrics did not adequately capture. After removing these cases, correlations for π0 and SpatialVLA in "Move near" significantly improved, suggesting a gap in orientation-aware quality assessment.
- **Finding 5:** The current metrics do not adequately capture final object orientation, a critical factor in some tasks. Future work should integrate orientation into metrics.

#### 4.3. RQ3 – Discrimination: Distinguishing Successful and Failing Tasks

This section assessed the metrics' ability to distinguish between different quality levels of successful tasks and outright failures.
- **Optimal Trajectory (OT) as a Universal Discriminator:** OT consistently emerged as the most powerful discriminator, exhibiting statistically significant and large Â12 effect sizes across almost all models and tasks when comparing successful tasks (regardless of quality) to failing ones.
- **High-Quality vs. Failing Tasks:** Most uncertainty and quality metrics showed promising results in distinguishing high-quality successful tasks from failing ones, particularly in "Pick up" and "Move near" for π0 and SpatialVLA. The token-based metrics (TB-TP, TB-PCS, TB-D, TB-E) also showed strong discrimination for OpenVLA in "Put in."
- **Medium/Low-Quality vs. Failing Tasks:** The discrimination capabilities of most metrics (except OT) substantially decreased as task quality declined. Many metrics failed to clearly distinguish between medium- or low-quality successful tasks and outright failures. This suggests that tasks executed with low quality increasingly resemble failures in terms of measured characteristics.
- **Finding 6:** OT is a consistent, model-agnostic indicator for distinguishing successful from unsuccessful tasks.
- **Finding 7:** Many metrics (beyond OT, and with exceptions for OpenVLA) can reliably distinguish high-quality task executions from failures.
- **Finding 8:** Most metrics (except OT) struggle to differentiate between medium/low-quality successful tasks and outright failures, reinforcing that low-quality success is a precarious state close to failure.
- **Static Robot Anomaly:** Some failing tasks exhibited low uncertainty or high "quality" metric values due to the robot remaining static (e.g., unable to detect an object). This misleading outcome can be addressed by setting a minimal motion threshold.
- **Finding 9:** Unsuccessful tasks with static robots can produce spuriously low uncertainty/high quality scores; a minimal robot motion threshold is necessary for accurate interpretation.

#### 4.4. RQ4 – Overhead: Inference Time Impact

The computational cost of integrating the metrics was evaluated.
- **EV's High Overhead:** The EV metric incurred the highest overhead due to requiring multiple VLA model inferences, making it impractical for real-time applications.
- **Low Overhead for Other Metrics:** All other proposed metrics (TB-TP, TB-PCS, TB-D, TB-E, A-PI, A-VI, A-AI, TCP-PI, TCP-VI, TCP-AI, TI, OT) exhibited low computational overhead, deeming them suitable for practical integration into VLA-enabled robotic systems.
- **Finding 10:** EV's overhead is prohibitive; remaining metrics are computationally efficient enough for practical use.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the robust evaluation and deployment of Visual Language Action (VLA) models for robotic systems. By systematically challenging the prevalent reliance on binary success rates, the paper highlights a critical blind spot in current AI and robotics research: the nuanced assessment of *how well* a task is performed and *how confident* the AI agent is in its actions.

The **significance** of this work lies in several key areas:

1. **Elevating Evaluation Standards:** The proposed suite of uncertainty and quality metrics provides a much-needed, comprehensive framework for evaluating VLA models. This moves beyond a simple pass/fail, enabling developers and researchers to gain deeper insights into model performance, identify specific weaknesses (e.g., erratic movements, hesitation, poor trajectory planning), and ultimately build more reliable and trustworthy systems.
2. **Bridging the Gap to Real-World Deployment:** For VLA models to transition from controlled laboratory settings to real-world, potentially high-stakes applications (like manufacturing, logistics, or domestic assistance), granular insights into execution quality and model confidence are indispensable. The findings, particularly the strong correlation of metrics like TCP-VI, A-VI, and A-AI with human judgment, indicate their potential to serve as objective performance indicators that align with human perception of quality.
3. **Enabling Real-time Monitoring and Adaptive Control:** The low computational overhead of most proposed metrics makes them highly suitable for real-time applications. This opens avenues for:
 - **Run-time Monitoring:** Continuously assessing the quality and confidence of robot actions during operation, allowing for early detection of deviations or potential failures.
 - **Adaptive Enhancement:** Triggering re-planning, human intervention, or activating alternative strategies when metrics indicate low confidence or poor execution quality.
 - **Falsification-based Testing:** Guiding automated test generation to explore scenarios that expose weaknesses in VLA models, moving beyond simple success/failure conditions.
4. **Informing Model Development:** By providing richer feedback than success rates, these metrics can guide the development of future VLA models, encouraging optimization for smoother, more efficient, and more confident task execution rather than just task completion. The identification of OT as a robust success/failure discriminator is also valuable for simplifying initial task outcome assessment without complex symbolic oracles.
5. **Catalyst for Standardization:** The demonstrated utility of these metrics can serve as a foundation for establishing standardized evaluation and benchmarking frameworks for VLA models, fostering more equitable comparisons and accelerating progress across the field.

The **potential impact** is profound. By fostering the development and adoption of more sophisticated evaluation paradigms, this research contributes directly to making VLA-enabled robots more robust, predictable, and safe. This is crucial for their integration into cyber-physical systems where reliability, safety, and operational quality are paramount. The identification of specific metric limitations (e.g., object orientation, static robot failures) also clearly outlines actionable future research directions, ensuring continuous refinement of VLA evaluation methodologies.
