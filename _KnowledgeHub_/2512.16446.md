# E-SDS: Environment-aware See it, Do it, Sorted - Automated Environment-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> UCL researchers developed E-SDS, an automated framework that leverages a multi-agent VLM system and real-time terrain analysis to generate environment-aware reward functions for humanoid locomotion. This approach enabled a simulated Unitree G1 robot to perform complex behaviors like stair descent, a task previously challenging for both manual and perception-blind automated methods, while reducing reward design time from days to under two hours.

## Problem

- Manual engineering of reward functions for deep reinforcement learning in humanoid locomotion is time-consuming, error-prone, and limits the scalability of acquired skills.
- Existing automated reward generation methods often lack environmental perception, limiting their applicability to simple terrains.
- Perceptive locomotion systems, while capable of navigating complex environments, traditionally rely on labor-intensive, manually tuned reward functions.

## Method

- **E-SDS** introduces a multi-agent VLM system, augmented with a novel ==Environment Analysis Agent==, to synthesize reward functions that explicitly account for terrain characteristics.
- The Environment Analysis Agent deploys simulated robots to collect sensor data and compute quantitative terrain statistics, which are then integrated into the VLM prompt for reward code generation.
- A closed-loop pipeline iteratively refines reward functions by training perceptive policies, evaluating their performance via a VLM-powered Feedback Agent, and using natural language feedback for subsequent reward generations.

## Results

- E-SDS uniquely enabled a simulated Unitree G1 humanoid robot to successfully descend stairs, achieving an exploration score of **10.93** with zero torso contacts, a task where manual and perception-blind baselines failed completely.
- The framework demonstrated superior command following on simple terrain, achieving an **82.6%** improvement in velocity tracking error compared to a manual baseline, and significantly better navigation in terrains with gaps and obstacles (e.g., **2.07x** more exploration on gaps).
- The automated process reduced the time required for reward design from days to approximately 99 minutes per terrain, while generating policies that were more robust and capable than manually engineered or perception-blind alternatives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating real-time environmental statistics directly into the automated reward generation process is critical for producing robust, perceptive locomotion policies in complex terrains.
> - A multi-agent VLM system, when conditioned on both desired behaviors and environmental summaries, can effectively bridge the gap between perception-blind automated reward synthesis and perceptive control.
> - Iterative, VLM-driven refinement of reward functions significantly reduces human engineering effort while yielding more capable and adaptive locomotion policies for humanoid robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust and agile locomotion controllers for humanoid robots using deep reinforcement learning (RL) has gained significant traction. However, a major bottleneck remains: the **manual engineering of reward functions**. This process is notoriously time-consuming, prone to errors, and limits the scalability and diversity of skills robots can acquire. E-SDS addresses this fundamental challenge by bridging a critical gap in the existing research landscape.

The paper identifies two distinct, yet previously disconnected, research directions:

- **Automated Reward Generation:** Recent advancements leverage vision-language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs) to automatically synthesize reward functions from high-level instructions or video demonstrations. Examples include methods like Eureka and Text2Reward, which generate reward code, and SDS, which specifically synthesizes reward functions for quadrupedal gait imitation from video. While these methods successfully reduce human effort, their primary limitation is a lack of "environmental perception." The resulting policies are often "blind" to the robot's immediate physical surroundings, relying only on proprioceptive state or motion priors. Consequently, their applicability has been largely confined to simple, uniform terrains.
- **Perceptive Locomotion:** This direction integrates exteroceptive sensors (e.g., height scanners, LiDAR, depth cameras) with proprioception to enable robots to navigate complex, unstructured environments. Techniques like Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA) and other sensorimotor feedback systems have demonstrated the crucial role of environmental awareness for robust performance and generalization. However, these advanced perceptive systems still heavily rely on manually designed and meticulously tuned reward functions, which is a labor-intensive and platform-specific endeavor.

**E-SDS lies at the intersection of these two fields.** It is the first framework to unify automated reward generation with environment-aware control. While previous automated methods like SDS were perception-blind, E-SDS conditions VLM-based reward synthesis directly on real-time terrain statistics. Conversely, while perceptive locomotion systems (e.g.,) enable robust navigation, E-SDS removes the manual reward tuning by closing the loop between environment analysis, code generation, and training feedback. By doing so, E-SDS directly tackles the critical limitation of automated reward generation (lack of environment awareness) and the scalability issue of perceptive locomotion (reliance on manual reward design), paving the way for more autonomous and capable robots in real-world, unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for E-SDS stems from the significant bottleneck of manual reward engineering in reinforcement learning for humanoid locomotion. This process is highly demanding, requiring expert knowledge and extensive iterative tuning, which limits the practical deployment and generalization of RL-based controllers.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Automate Reward Function Design:** To eliminate or drastically reduce the human effort involved in crafting reward functions for humanoid locomotion.
2. **Enable Environment-Aware Reward Generation:** To create a system that can automatically synthesize reward functions that explicitly account for and leverage environmental sensor data, allowing policies to perceive and interact intelligently with complex terrains.
3. **Train Robust Perceptive Locomotion Policies:** To develop a framework capable of generating policies that are not only effective but also robust and adaptable to various challenging environments, going beyond simple, flat terrains.
4. **Bridge the Perception Gap:** To unify automated reward generation (typically "blind" to the environment) with perceptive control (which traditionally relies on manual reward design).
5. **Demonstrate Complex Behaviors:** To enable policies to acquire complex skills, such as stair descent, which have proven difficult for both manual and automated perception-blind methods.

In essence, E-SDS aims to make the reward engineering process for perceptive humanoid locomotion both automated and intelligent, leading to more capable and generalized robotic behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

E-SDS (Environment-aware See it, Do it, Sorted) introduces a novel closed-loop pipeline for automated, environment-aware reward generation and policy refinement. The framework is built upon a multi-agent VLM system integrated with real-time terrain sensor analysis.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), where the objective is to learn a recurrent policy $\pi(a_t|s_t, h_t)$ that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward. The observation space ($s_t$) is high-dimensional (792 dimensions) and critically includes both proprioceptive state (joint positions, velocities, base orientation) and **exteroceptive environmental data** from an onboard height scanner (27x21 grid) and a LiDAR sensor (144 measurements). The system aims to automate the synthesis of the reward function R, conditioning its generation on both the desired skill and the specific environment.

#### 3.2. Environment-Aware Reward Generation Agent

The core innovation lies in this multi-agent system, which extends the SDS framework by adding an **Environment Analysis Agent**. The process unfolds as follows:

1. **Video Demonstration Encoding:** A video demonstration of the desired locomotion skill is encoded for VLM (GPT-5) comprehension using **Grid-Frame Prompting**. This adaptively samples video frames into a composite image grid, preserving spatio-temporal information efficiently.
2. **Behavioral Analysis (SUS Prompting):** The image grid is analyzed using the **SUS (See it, Understand it, Sorted) prompting strategy**. Specialized agents within this multi-stage, chain-of-thought approach deconstruct the desired behavior:
 - **Contact Sequence Analyzer:** Extracts footfall patterns.
 - **Gait Analyzer:** Identifies gait characteristics.
 - **Task Requirement Analyzer:** Determines high-level objectives (e.g., target velocity, posture).
3. **Environment Analysis Agent (Novel Contribution):** This is the key differentiating component.
 - **Data Collection:** A fleet of 1000 simulated robots is deployed in the target environment for a brief period (10 seconds) to collect comprehensive sensor data.
 - **Statistical Summary:** These data are processed to compute quantitative terrain statistics, such as obstacle density, gap ratios, and terrain roughness.
4. **Combined Prompt for Code Synthesis:** The statistical summary from the Environment Analysis Agent is integrated with the behavioral description from the SUS analysis. This combined, environment-sensitive prompt is then fed to the VLM (GPT-5) to synthesize a Python reward function. This generated function not only includes terms for the desired gait but also **environment-specific terms that explicitly leverage the robot’s onboard exteroceptive sensors.**

#### 3.3. The E-SDS Training and Refinement Pipeline

This fully automated, closed-loop pipeline iteratively refines the generated reward functions and trains the perceptive policies:

1. **Reward Synthesis:** At each iteration, the reward generation agent produces two candidate reward functions.
2. **Perceptive Policy Training:** For each candidate reward function, a corresponding perceptive policy is trained using **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. Training occurs in a massively parallel simulation environment, utilizing 3000 robot instances for 500 iterations. The policy utilizes the high-dimensional observation space including both proprioceptive and exteroceptive data.
3. **Automated Evaluation:** Post-training, each policy is evaluated. Quantitative metrics (velocity tracking error, torso contact rate) are collected, and rollout footage is captured. A **Feedback Agent**, powered by the VLM, analyzes these data and assigns a performance score.
4. **Iterative Reward Refinement:** The best-performing reward function from the current iteration is selected. Its Python code, along with natural-language feedback describing policy performance and identified failure modes (e.g., "freezing"), is used to update the prompt for the next iteration. This entire process is repeated for three iterations, progressively refining the reward function.

The complete pipeline for each terrain takes approximately 99 minutes, a significant reduction in development time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of E-SDS on a simulated Unitree G1 humanoid robot across four distinct terrains (simple, gaps, obstacles, stairs) revealed significant improvements compared to baselines.

#### 4.1. Comparative Performance Analysis

The E-SDS framework consistently generated policies that outperformed both a manually-engineered perceptive baseline and a perception-blind automated baseline (Foundation-Only).

- **Stair Descent (Most Challenging):**
 - **E-SDS:** Uniquely successful in learning to descend stairs, achieving a high exploration score (10.93) and zero torso contacts (no falls). Its velocity tracking error was 0.663 m/s.
 - **Manual Baseline:** Despite having sensor access, remained stationary at the top of the stairs, resulting in a very low exploration score (3.50) and high velocity tracking error (2.278 m/s), indicating failure to perform the task.
 - **Foundation-Only (Perception-Blind):** Attempted to walk forward but frequently fell, leading to an extremely high torso contact rate of 333.5. This clearly demonstrated the critical need for environment awareness.

- **Navigation of Discontinuous (Gaps) and Cluttered (Obstacles) Terrains:**
 - **E-SDS:** Learned an active navigation strategy, exploring significantly more area (2.07x on gaps, 2.36x on obstacles) than the baseline, while maintaining lower velocity tracking errors (0.660 m/s on gaps, 0.492 m/s on obstacles). It exhibited controlled navigation, although with some falls (5.136 torso contacts on gaps, 37.92 on obstacles), demonstrating its active engagement with the environment.
 - **Manual Baseline:** Adopted a conservative avoidance strategy, often remaining stationary or moving cautiously to avoid falling, resulting in high velocity tracking errors (1.373 m/s on gaps, 2.058 m/s on obstacles) and limited exploration.
 - **Foundation-Only:** Suffered from frequent falls due to its inability to perceive gaps and obstacles (140.476 torso contacts on gaps, 316.98 on obstacles), indicating severe performance degradation.

- **Locomotion on Simple Terrain:**
 - **E-SDS:** Demonstrated superior command following, achieving a velocity tracking error of 0.387 m/s. This represents an **82.6% improvement** over the Manual Baseline's error of 2.225 m/s. It maintained excellent stability with zero torso contacts.
 - **Manual Baseline:** Showed poor tracking performance, often remaining stationary (6.2% of the time).
 - **Foundation-Only:** Had a slightly higher velocity tracking error (0.549 m/s) and some falls (1.584 torso contacts), indicating it was less robust even on simple terrain without explicit environmental conditioning.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study: The Necessity of Environment Awareness

The comparison between E-SDS (Environment-Aware) and Foundation-Only policies directly validated the importance of integrating environmental analysis into the automated reward generation. Without this, the Foundation-Only policy consistently failed or performed poorly in complex terrains. Its torso contact rates were drastically higher across gaps (27.4x higher), obstacles (8.4x higher), and especially stairs (complete failure) compared to E-SDS. This unequivocally demonstrates that conditioning the automated reward generation process on environmental statistics is a critical factor for synthesizing rewards that lead to robust, perceptive behaviors.

#### 4.3. Efficiency Gains

E-SDS successfully reduced the human effort of reward design from "days to less than two hours" (specifically, approximately 99 minutes per terrain) while simultaneously producing "more robust and capable locomotion policies."

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The E-SDS framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic locomotion, particularly for humanoid robots operating in complex, unstructured environments. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

1. **Breaking the Reward Engineering Bottleneck:** By automating the generation of environment-aware reward functions, E-SDS addresses one of the most persistent and labor-intensive challenges in reinforcement learning for robotics. This drastically reduces the time and expert knowledge required to develop new robotic skills, moving from "days" of manual tuning to "less than two hours" of automated processing.
2. **Enabling Robust Perceptive Locomotion:** E-SDS successfully bridges the gap between automated reward generation (previously perception-blind) and perceptive control (previously relying on manual rewards). The ability to automatically synthesize rewards that leverage exteroceptive sensor data is crucial for developing policies that can truly navigate and adapt to varied and challenging terrains, as dramatically demonstrated by the unique success in stair descent.
3. **Advancing Humanoid Capabilities:** The ability to achieve complex behaviors like stair descent, which were unattainable by both manual and perception-blind automated methods, underscores the framework's power. This paves the way for humanoids to operate effectively in environments that closely mimic human living and working spaces, which are often multi-level and cluttered.
4. **Accelerating Robotic Development and Deployment:** The efficiency and robustness offered by E-SDS have broad implications for accelerating the development cycle of robotic systems. Researchers and engineers can rapidly iterate on new skills and adapt existing ones to novel environments without extensive manual intervention, potentially leading to faster deployment of robots in real-world applications.
5. **Foundation for More Autonomous Robots:** By making the reward generation process intelligent and environment-aware, E-SDS moves closer to the vision of truly autonomous robots that can learn and adapt complex behaviors with minimal human oversight. This sets a strong precedent for future research into generalizable, multi-task locomotion policies.

While the current evaluation is in simulation and generates specialized policies per terrain, the work clearly establishes the essential role of environment conditioning in automated reward generation for complex terrains. Future work, including sim-to-real transfer and scaling to unified multi-task policies, will build upon this foundational contribution to realize even more profound impacts on the capabilities of autonomous robots.
