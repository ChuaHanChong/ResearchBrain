# Think or Not? Selective Reasoning via Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework from researchers at The Chinese University of Hong Kong and the National University of Singapore enables Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to selectively engage in step-by-step reasoning, deciding when to generate explicit thoughts. This approach reduces output completion length by up to 90% while consistently matching or improving accuracy across diverse multimodal tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) mandate comprehensive, step-by-step reasoning for all queries, leading to inefficient token generation and higher computational costs for simple tasks.
- VLMs currently lack adaptive cognitive control, unable to selectively engage in deep reasoning based on perceived task difficulty, unlike human intelligence.
- Existing methods primarily focus on compressing or truncating reasoning processes rather than enabling models to autonomously decide if explicit reasoning is required at all.

## Method

- Introduces ==TON==, a ==two-stage training framework== for selective reasoning in VLMs.
- Employs Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) with a =="thought dropout" mechanism==, where reasoning traces are randomly replaced with empty delimiters to teach the model to skip thoughts, combined with a "reverse thinking" strategy for thought data generation.
- Utilizes ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== in the second stage, leveraging the SFT-induced output diversity to learn an adaptive policy for deciding whether to generate explicit reasoning, guided by task-specific outcome rewards.

## Results

- Demonstrated significant reductions in completion length, decreasing token usage by up to **90%** (e.g., GeoQA) and **70%** (AITZ), leading to lower inference costs and faster response times.
- Achieved comparable or superior accuracy across benchmarks, notably gaining up to **17%** over GRPO on GeoQA and improving CLEVR accuracy from **93.5%** to **98.5%**, proving efficiency gains without performance compromise.
- Showed strong generalizability across diverse tasks (counting, mathematical reasoning, agentic navigation) and modalities, with ablation studies confirming progressive learning of selective reasoning and robust out-of-distribution performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Models can achieve comparable or superior performance with significantly reduced reasoning steps, suggesting that "overthinking" might sometimes lead to less accurate outcomes.
> - Explicitly teaching the model to skip reasoning through Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) with "thought dropout" is crucial, as mere prompt engineering does not effectively enable selective thinking.
> - Reinforcement Learning, particularly GRPO, leverages the diversity introduced by "thought dropout" to enable models to progressively learn and adaptively decide when to generate or omit explicit reasoning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper addresses a crucial and emerging challenge in the field of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs): the efficiency and appropriateness of reasoning processes.

**Reinforcement Learning for VLMs:** The paper builds upon the recent success of Reinforcement Learning (RL) as a post-training strategy for enhancing reasoning in VLMs. Methods like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have demonstrated significant improvements in model capabilities by aligning outputs with desired behaviors or outcomes. GRPO, specifically, is a prominent method that incentivizes models to generate complete reasoning traces.

**The "Thinking" Paradigm:** The broader landscape has been heavily influenced by "Chain-of-Thought" (CoT) prompting and its variants, which encourage LLMs and VLMs to break down complex problems into intermediate steps, thereby improving reasoning accuracy. This paradigm often implicitly or explicitly promotes longer, more explicit reasoning paths.

**Emerging Concern: Reasoning Inefficiency:** While CoT and RL-based reasoning have boosted performance, a growing concern is the computational cost and redundancy associated with these lengthy reasoning traces. Several recent works have highlighted this "overthinking" problem, noting that models often generate verbose explanations even for simple questions. Prior attempts to address this include rule-based reward penalties, external control mechanisms (e.g., in Qwen3), or strategies like "long2short" compression or "token-budget-aware reasoning." However, these often focus on *how to shorten* a reasoning process once it has begun, or how to terminate unproductive reasoning.

**The Novelty of "When to Think":** "Think or Not?" distinguishes itself by pioneering the question of *when* a VLM should engage in explicit reasoning. This aligns with human cognitive processes, where individuals adapt their thinking effort to task difficulty, skipping elaborate steps for easy problems. Existing methods compel models to "think" for every query, irrespective of its complexity. This paper is the first to directly tackle this fundamental aspect of selective reasoning in VLMs, positioning itself as a foundational step towards more human-like, adaptive intelligence. It shifts the focus from optimizing the *length* of a given thought to optimizing the *decision* to think at all.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for "Think or Not? Selective Reasoning via Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Language Models" (TON) stems from two critical observations regarding the current state of VLM reasoning:

1. **Inefficiency of Universal Reasoning:** Contemporary RL-based VLM training strategies, particularly those like GRPO, encourage models to produce comprehensive, step-by-step reasoning traces for *every* question. While effective for complex tasks, this "full thinking" approach leads to significant redundancy, increased token usage, and higher computational costs when applied to simpler queries that could be answered directly. The authors empirically demonstrate this: in the AITZ dataset, 51% of questions could be answered correctly even with the entire reasoning trace omitted, signifying substantial potential for token savings.

2. **Lack of Adaptive Cognitive Control:** Unlike humans, who intuitively decide whether to engage in deep thought based on perceived task difficulty, VLMs tend to default to a conservative, verbose reasoning approach. Even when prompted to skip reasoning for easy questions, models often struggle to adaptively omit thought generation, consistently producing full traces. This suggests that the ability to selectively reason is not an inherent outcome of improved reasoning capacity but rather a distinct skill that needs to be explicitly taught and activated.

Based on these motivations, the paper sets forth the following key objectives:

- **Enable Selective Reasoning:** To equip VLMs with the capacity to determine, on a question-by-question basis, whether explicit, step-by-step reasoning is necessary or if a direct answer suffices.
- **Enhance Efficiency:** To substantially reduce the completion length and associated computational costs (inference time and token generation) by bypassing unnecessary reasoning processes.
- **Maintain or Improve Performance:** To achieve these efficiency gains without compromising task accuracy; ideally, to demonstrate that selective reasoning can even lead to performance improvements by avoiding "overthinking."
- **Develop a Generalizable Framework:** To propose a training strategy that is effective across diverse vision-language tasks, varying in reasoning difficulty, modality (text-only, VLM), and application domain (counting, math, agentic navigation).

By addressing these objectives, TON aims to move VLMs towards more efficient, intelligent, and human-like cognitive patterns.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose **TON (Think-or-Not)**, a novel two-stage training framework designed to teach Vision-Language Models (VLMs) the ability to selectively reason.

#### 3.1. First Stage: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) with "Thought Dropout"

The initial stage of TON focuses on explicitly introducing the concept of "thinking or not thinking" to the model.

- **Format Introduction:** Typically, SFT for reasoning-enhanced models involves fine-tuning on "think-answer" formatted data, where `<think>` tags encapsulate detailed reasoning processes before the `<answer>` tag. TON extends this by treating "think vs. non-think" as part of the output format itself.
- **Thought Dropout Mechanism:** To teach the model that reasoning can be skipped, a simple yet effective "thought dropout" operation is introduced. During SFT, with a specified `dropout_prob` (e.g., 50%), the high-quality reasoning traces (`thought`) for randomly selected samples are replaced with an empty thought delimiter (`\n\n`). This process injects both the standard "think-answer" format and the "skip-thought" format into the model's prior knowledge. The authors argue that random dropout is more effective than difficulty-aware dropout, as human-crafted heuristics might introduce noise.
- **Sourcing High-Quality Thoughts:** A key challenge is obtaining a large corpus of high-quality "thought" data for SFT. Instead of relying on external, potentially closed-source models, TON employs a "reverse thinking" strategy. Given the visual context (V), textual query (Q), and ground-truth answer (S*), the base policy itself (`π_θ`) is prompted to deduce and generate the intermediate thought (T). This self-generation approach ensures a sufficient and relevant cold-start training corpus.

#### 3.2. Second Stage: Reinforcement Learning (RL) via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

Once the SFT stage has established the "skip-thought" format, the second stage uses reinforcement learning to refine the model's policy, enabling it to *explore and decide* when to apply this selective reasoning.

- **GRPO as the Core RL Algorithm:** TON adopts Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a method known for enhancing reasoning capabilities using rule-driven rewards. GRPO operates by sampling a group of `N` candidate responses from the current policy (`π_θ`) for a given input (V, Q). Each response (`o_i`) is evaluated using a reward function `r(·)`.
- **Advantage Calculation:** The relative quality of each response is determined by its advantage (`A_i`), calculated by normalizing its reward against the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the sampled group. This relative advantage guides policy updates.
- **Policy Update:** GRPO updates the policy by maximizing an objective function that encourages responses with higher advantages within the group, while also incorporating a KL-divergence penalty against a reference policy (`π_ref`) to prevent excessive policy shifts.
- **TON's Impact on GRPO:** The "thought dropout" from SFT empowers TON to generate responses with "empty-think" options (e.g., `<think>\n\n</think><answer>...</answer>`). This dramatically increases the diversity in the latent distribution space of generated responses (specifically, the `α_i` and `β_i` terms in GRPO's objective function). This enhanced diversity allows GRPO to explore a wider range of reasoning strategies, including the decision to skip thinking, which is crucial for learning selective reasoning.
- **Reward Function Design:** The paper meticulously designs outcome rewards `r_o` based on the task:
 - **Discrete Matching:** For tasks like classification, counting, or math, a binary reward `r_d = 1` is assigned if the predicted answer perfectly matches the ground truth, else `0`.
 - **Continuous Matching:** For tasks involving continuous outputs like spatial coordinates (e.g., UI navigation), a reward `r_c = 1` is given if the predicted point falls within a ground-truth bounding box or within a specified distance threshold (`θ`) of a ground-truth point, else `0`.
 - The total outcome reward `r_o` is a sum of applicable components (`r_d + r_c`). Crucially, the reward function *does not include an explicit penalty for longer reasoning paths*. The efficiency gains are learned implicitly through exploration and the positive reward for correct, potentially shorter, answers.

This two-stage approach ensures that the model first understands the *format* of selective reasoning through SFT and then *learns when to apply it* through the exploration capabilities of GRPO, guided by task-specific rewards.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TON across various benchmarks consistently demonstrates its effectiveness in achieving selective reasoning while enhancing efficiency and maintaining or improving performance.

- **Significant Reduction in Completion Length:**
 - TON achieves remarkable reductions in average completion length compared to vanilla GRPO. For the 7B model on GeoQA, it reduces length by up to 90%.
 - On CLEVR, token usage is cut by 87%, and on GeoQA (3B model), by 65%.
 - For the multi-step navigation task AITZ, the average task-level output length is dramatically reduced from 3.6K to 0.9K tokens (a 70% saving).
 - This reduction directly translates to lower inference costs and faster response times.

- **Comparable or Superior Performance:**
 - Despite drastically reducing output length, TON either matches or outperforms vanilla GRPO in accuracy.
 - Notably, on GeoQA, TON achieves up to a 17% accuracy gain over GRPO, demonstrating a "free-lunch" effect where shorter, more focused reasoning can lead to better outcomes by potentially avoiding "overthinking" or hallucinated reasoning steps.
 - On CLEVR, TON improved accuracy from 93.5% (GRPO) to 98.5%, and on AITZ, it maintained comparable exact match accuracy (0.59) to GRPO (0.59) for 3B models, while significantly improving type accuracy (0.75 vs 0.74).

- **Progressive Learning of Selective Reasoning:**
 - Ablation studies reveal that the skip-thought ratio (percentage of outputs with empty reasoning traces) progressively increases as training advances and rewards improve. This indicates that the model learns to internalize the reasoning process and adaptively bypass explicit thoughts when it is confident in generating a correct answer.
 - Crucially, when TON *does* decide to think, the length of these "thoughtful" outputs remains comparable to GRPO's, confirming that the model still engages in detailed reasoning when necessary.

- **Generalizability Across Modalities and Difficulties:**
 - TON's efficacy extends beyond Vision-Language Models to LLM benchmarks like GSM8K, where it reduces response length while maintaining high accuracy.
 - Evaluations across CLEVR (counting), Super-CLEVR (OOD counting), GeoQA (mathematical reasoning), and AITZ (agentic navigation) confirm its broad applicability across varying reasoning difficulties and task types.
 - The framework demonstrates robust Out-of-Distribution (OOD) generalization. On AITZ, TON performs comparably to GRPO on OOD domains while significantly reducing output length. On Super-CLEVR, TON shows superior OOD performance, suggesting it helps avoid overfitting to specific training patterns.

- **Significance of SFT with Thought Dropout:**
 - A critical finding from ablation studies is that simply modifying the prompt (e.g., a "hybrid-thought prompt") to encourage skipping reasoning is insufficient. Models tend to default to full reasoning, producing minimal or no "skip" cases.
 - This highlights the necessity of the Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) stage with explicit "thought dropout" to establish the desired format-following behavior and "activate" the ability to bypass thoughts.

- **Random vs. Difficulty-Aware Dropout:**
 - The authors compared random thought dropout with a difficulty-aware dropout strategy (skipping thoughts only for samples correctly answered by the base VLM).
 - Surprisingly, random dropout slightly outperformed difficulty-aware dropout (51.0% vs. 50.5% accuracy on GeoQA), suggesting that hand-crafted heuristics can introduce noise, and random dropout, combined with RL, allows the model to learn the optimal skipping strategy adaptively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Think or Not? Selective Reasoning via Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Language Models" (TON) paper introduces a highly significant contribution to the fields of multimodal AI and reinforcement learning, with a substantial potential impact across several dimensions:

- **Pioneering Selective Reasoning in VLMs:** TON is the first work to explicitly address "when to think" in vision-language models. This shift from solely optimizing *how to reason* or *how long to reason* to optimizing the *decision to reason at all* is a fundamental advancement. It fills a critical gap in enabling more intelligent, human-like cognitive control in AI systems.

- **Dramatic Efficiency Gains:** The most immediate and tangible impact is the significant reduction in completion length (up to 90% savings in token generation). This directly translates to:
 - **Reduced Computational Costs:** Lower API calls, less energy consumption, and faster inference for large-scale VLM deployments.
 - **Improved User Experience:** Quicker responses, especially for interactive applications.
 - **Enhanced Scalability:** Makes deploying powerful VLMs more feasible in resource-constrained environments or for applications requiring high throughput.

- **Challenging the "More Reasoning is Better" Paradigm:** By demonstrating that shorter, selective reasoning can not only maintain but even *improve* performance (the "free-lunch" effect), TON challenges the widely held assumption that more verbose reasoning always leads to better answers. This suggests that "overthinking" can be detrimental, and brevity, when appropriate, is a virtue. This insight could influence future prompt engineering and model design strategies.

- **Advancing Human-like AI Cognition:** The ability of TON to adapt its reasoning depth to task complexity mirrors human cognitive processes. This moves VLMs closer to exhibiting more sophisticated, resource-aware intelligence, laying groundwork for AI systems that can allocate cognitive effort adaptively, much like humans. This aspect has profound implications for building more autonomous and intuitively intelligent agents.

- **Generalizability and Robustness:** The effectiveness of TON across diverse benchmarks (LLM, VLM, agentic tasks) and its robust performance on out-of-distribution data highlight its broad applicability. This suggests that the principles of selective reasoning introduced by TON could be generalized to a wide array of AI tasks and model architectures.

- **Inspiration for RL in Multimodal AI:** The paper's two-stage approach, particularly the innovative "thought dropout" during SFT followed by GRPO, offers a new paradigm for injecting specific behavioral priors into large models before refining them with RL. This could inspire novel methods for incorporating various forms of prior knowledge or format-following behaviors into LLM/VLM training, moving beyond manual rule-based rewards.

In conclusion, TON presents a timely and impactful solution to the problem of reasoning inefficiency in VLMs. By enabling models to intelligently decide when to "think or not," it not only offers substantial practical benefits in terms of cost and speed but also pushes the boundaries of AI towards more adaptive, efficient, and human-like cognitive abilities.
