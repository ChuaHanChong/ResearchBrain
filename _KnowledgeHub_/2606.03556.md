# Partially Observable Adversarial Patch Attacks on Vision-Language-Action Models in Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Science and Technology of China and Guangzhou University introduced a framework for partially observable adversarial patch attacks on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotics. Their method generates a static physical patch using limited initial observations, causing sustained disruptions in robotic task execution by simultaneously misguiding semantic grounding and amplifying trajectory curvature.

## Problem

- Prior adversarial attack research on VLA models assumes an unrealistic full access to the robot's entire execution trajectory for patch optimization.
- Real-world adversaries typically have limited observation capabilities, accessing only a short initial segment of a robot's execution.
- A formal definition and empirical demonstration of adversarial patch attacks on VLA models under practical, partially observable constraints were lacking.

## Method

- A two-phase framework was proposed for adversarial patch attacks under a partially observable threat model, where a static patch is designed using a limited initial trajectory prefix.
- Attention-based patch localization identifies visually critical regions for patch placement by analyzing the VLA model's cross-modal attention maps.
- Content-aware patch optimization uses a unified objective function to disrupt semantic grounding of objects and amplify trajectory curvature, leading to misperception and inefficient robot motion.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved higher Attack Success Rates (ASR) and Normalized Attack Success Rates (nASR) across LIBERO benchmark suites, with up to **90.7%** ASR (Object suite, K=30), outperforming existing baselines.
- Patch generation time was significantly reduced by **6.9x** to **16.7x** compared to full-rollout optimization, demonstrating efficiency for realistic scenarios (e.g., 47-53 seconds per patch).
- Physical adversarial patches caused substantial task failure rate reductions in real-world robot experiments, from **72%** to **12%** and **58%** to **8%** on two manipulation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversaries with limited initial observation (e.g., K=40 frames out of a full trajectory) can design static physical patches that induce long-horizon, persistent disruptions in VLA-based robotic tasks.
> - Combining semantic grounding disruption and trajectory smoothness disruption is a powerful strategy, exploiting vulnerabilities in both the VLA model's perception and control mechanisms.
> - Strategic patch placement in semantically salient regions, identified by the VLA model's attention, significantly enhances attack effectiveness under partial observability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm for robotic intelligence, integrating visual perception, natural language understanding, and action generation to facilitate generalist robotic capabilities across various tasks and environments. As these models are increasingly considered for deployment in applications such as service robotics and manufacturing, their safety and robustness become areas of investigation.

The broader field of adversarial machine learning has demonstrated that deep learning models, particularly in computer vision, can be vulnerable to imperceptible perturbations in input data, leading to misclassifications or erroneous outputs. In robotics, prior research has explored adversarial attacks across multiple layers, including network communication (e.g., data injection, denial-of-service affecting localization and planning), software frameworks (e.g., vulnerabilities in ROS), motion planners (misleading trajectory generation), grasping systems (reducing grasp quality), and visual SLAM (compromising localization and mapping). These studies indicate that modern robotic systems may be susceptible to a range of adversarial manipulations.

More specifically concerning VLA models, security threats are an emerging area of study. Prior work has identified vulnerabilities such as text-based jailbreaks (prompt injection inducing targeted actions), physical-world jailbreaks on embodied LLMs (instruction-following vulnerabilities), decision-level robustness issues, and backdoor attacks during training. These attacks often target language processing or training mechanisms, rather than direct visual input manipulation.

Within visual adversarial attacks on VLA models, a relevant prior study by Wang et al. empirically demonstrated that adversarial patches applied to visual input could mislead VLA-based robots. However, this earlier work did not formally define the threat model and assumed the adversary had full access to the entire execution trajectory for patch optimization. This assumption is considered unrealistic for practical robotic deployments, as adversaries typically have limited observation capabilities.

This paper addresses this specific limitation by formalizing a partially observable threat model. It investigates whether a single, static adversarial patch, optimized with limited initial observations, can still induce long-term disruptive effects on VLA-based robots. By doing so, the work aims to bridge the gap between theoretical adversarial attack formulations and practical deployment constraints, providing insights into realistic vulnerabilities of VLA-based robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotic applications and the corresponding need to understand their robustness against adversarial attacks. While VLA models offer capabilities for scalable generalist robotics, their security against malicious inputs has remained largely unexplored under realistic operational constraints.

The paper identifies a critical limitation in existing adversarial attack research against VLA models: prior work, such as that by Wang et al., assumes an adversary possesses full access to the robot's entire execution trajectory to design and optimize an adversarial patch. This "full-rollout optimization" is considered an idealized setting that is rarely attainable in practical robotic deployments. Real-world adversaries are unlikely to observe or control the entire future trajectory; at most, they may access a short initial segment of the execution. This discrepancy between theoretical attack models and practical scenarios motivates the need for a more realistic threat model.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Formulate a Partially Observable Threat Model:** The central objective is to establish a formal definition of adversarial patch attacks on VLA models under a partially observable setting. This model aims to reflect practical constraints where an adversary can only observe a limited prefix of the robot's execution trajectory (e.g., the first K frames) to design a static physical patch. Once optimized, this patch is then applied to all subsequent frames for the entire task duration. This formulation seeks to capture scenarios where an attacker places a physical artifact in the robot's workspace without continuous observation or re-optimization capabilities.

2. **Develop a Two-Phase Adversarial Patch Attack Framework:** To operate within the constraints of the partially observable model, the paper aims to propose a novel attack framework. This framework is designed to exploit specific vulnerabilities in VLA models by disrupting both their perceptual and control mechanisms. The two phases are:
 - **Attention-based Patch Localization:** To effectively place a patch with limited information, the framework aims to identify visually critical regions within the robot's field of view that are most relevant to the natural language instruction. This localization strategy seeks to maximize the impact of the patch by targeting areas that the VLA model's cross-modal attention mechanisms deem important for understanding the task.
 - **Content-aware Patch Optimization:** Following localization, the framework aims to optimize the patch's visual content. This optimization is designed to achieve two distinct but complementary adversarial effects:
 - **Disrupt Semantic Grounding:** To mislead the VLA model's perception by shifting the visual grounding of task-relevant objects mentioned in the instruction. This aims to cause the model to misinterpret or mislocate critical entities.
 - **Amplify Trajectory Curvature:** To induce irregular and inefficient robot motion. This objective targets the control aspect, aiming to disrupt the smoothness of the robot's actions and steer it towards high-curvature, non-optimal paths, thereby impeding task completion.

3. **Validate the Approach in Simulation and Real-World Robotic Environments:** A final objective is to empirically demonstrate the effectiveness and sustained adversarial effects of the proposed method. This involves conducting extensive experiments both in simulation using established benchmarks (e.g., LIBERO) and on physical robots under practical conditions. The validation aims to show that the proposed partially observable attack can induce long-horizon disruptions, significantly reduce task success rates, and outperform existing baselines that assume full observability or employ less targeted strategies.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need to understand and address the practical security implications of deploying VLA-based robots, moving beyond idealized attack scenarios to consider more realistic adversary capabilities and observational limits.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a two-phase framework for adversarial patch attacks on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models under a partially observable threat model. This approach is designed to generate a static physical patch using limited initial observations, which then causes long-term disruptions in robotic task execution.

**A. Problem Formulation**

The VLA model, denoted as *f*, maps a visual observation *x_t* and a natural language instruction *l* to an action *a_t* at time *t*: *a_t = f(x_t, l)*. The environment state *s_t* evolves via a transition function *T*: *s_t+1 = T(s_t, a_t)*. The adversary operates in a partial-access gray-box setting, able to query the VLA model and use model-side information (e.g., attention maps) for optimization, but cannot modify model parameters, directly control robot actions, or alter the robot's controller. The adversary places a single static physical patch *p* within the robot's visible workspace, constrained by a feasible set *P* for physical realizability.

Two threat models are considered:
- **Fully Observable Patch Attack:** An idealized scenario where the adversary has access to the entire observation sequence *{x_t}* from *t=1* to *T*. The patch *p_full* is optimized using all frames to maximize an adversarial objective *L_adv* that measures the discrepancy between clean and adversarial states: *p_full = arg max_{p∈P} L_adv({s_t}, {ˆs_t})*. This serves as a theoretical upper bound.
- **Partially Observable Patch Attack:** The primary focus of this paper. The adversary observes only a limited prefix of the trajectory, specifically the first *K ≪ T* frames and their state-action tuples *{(x_t, a_t, s_t)}* from *t=1* to *K*. Based on this limited information, a single static patch *p** is designed. This patch is then fixed and applied to all subsequent frames from *t=K+1* to *T*. The patch *p*** is learned by maximizing *L_adv* on the observable prefix: *p* = arg max_{p∈P} L_adv({s_t}_{1:K}, {ˆs_t}_{1:K})*. Its effectiveness is evaluated on the unseen suffix of the trajectory.

**B. Two-Phase Attack Framework**

The proposed framework involves two main stages: attention-based patch localization and content-aware patch optimization.

**1. Attention-Based Patch Localization:**
This phase aims to identify a compact and influential region in the visual input for patch placement using the VLA model's cross-modal attention.
- **Attention Map Generation:** The VLA model, typically with a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone, encodes visual frames into patch tokens. For the last available prefix frame *x_K*, the model generates a head-summed attention matrix *A^(K) ∈ R^(Z×Nv)*, where *Z* is the number of text tokens for the instruction and *Nv* is the number of visual patch tokens.
- **Saliency Map Construction:** A saliency map *S ∈ R^(h×w)* (where *h×w* is the 2D grid of visual tokens) is created by reshaping and aggregating attention scores across all text tokens. Specifically, *S[i, j] = max_{1≤z≤Z} Reshape_{h×w}(A^(K)z,:) [i, j]*. This aggregates the maximum attention for each visual patch from any instruction token.
- **Patch Region Selection:** To ensure a compact, contiguous patch, an *s × s* window is slid over the saliency map *S*. The top-left index *(*i*, *j*)* that maximizes the sum of saliency scores within the window is selected. This window defines the region for the adversarial patch.
- **Binary Mask Generation:** The selected *h × w* token window is then upsampled to the original image resolution to create a binary mask *M_patch ∈ {0, 1}^(H×W)*. This mask restricts all subsequent pixel modifications to the chosen region.

**2. Content-Aware Patch Optimization:**
Once the patch location is fixed by *M_patch*, its content is optimized to maximize adversarial impact through a unified objective *L_adv*, which combines a semantic term and a trajectory term.

- **Semantic Grounding Disruption (L_sem):**
 - **Motivation:** VLA policies rely on accurate grounding of task-relevant nouns to visual regions. Shifting this grounding is hypothesized to cause downstream errors. Attention displacement serves as a proxy for grounding changes.
 - **Mechanism:** Task-relevant nouns *n ∈ N_align* are extracted from the instruction. For each noun and prefix frame *t*, clean *M_n^(t)* and adversarial *ˆM_n^(t)* grounding maps are constructed by summing reshaped attention scores for the noun's subword tokens.
 - **Displacement Calculation:** Soft-argmax is used on these maps to obtain clean *(*x^(t)n, y^(t)n)* and adversarial *(*ˆx^(t)n, ˆy^(t)n)* focus coordinates. The squared Euclidean distance *d^(2)n,t* between these foci measures displacement.
 - **Loss Function:** *L_sem(p) = - Σ_{t=1 to K} Σ_{n∈N_align} d^(2)n,t*. This term encourages large shifts in the attention-derived grounding focus of target objects across the observed prefix frames.

- **Trajectory Smoothness Disruption (L_traj):**
 - **Motivation:** Robotic motion often prioritizes smoothness (low curvature, low jerk). Increasing curvature is used as a surrogate to induce irregular and potentially failure-inducing motion.
 - **Mechanism:** End-effector positions *{P_t}* from the prefix *{s_t}* are used. *U* candidate future directions *{d_u}* are uniformly sampled on a unit sphere, normalized to a typical step scale.
 - **Curvature Calculation:** For a horizon *T_f*, each *d_u* is used to stitch a full sequence *P_full^(u)* from the prefix, and a cubic spline *r^(u)(t)* is fitted. The curvature *κ^(u)(t)* is calculated using the standard formula involving the first and second derivatives of the spline.
 - **Target Direction Selection:** The adversarial target direction *u* is identified as the one that maximizes the increase in maximum curvature compared to the prefix spline: *u* = arg max_{1≤u≤U} (*¯κ_full^(u) - ¯κ_hist)*.
 - **Loss Function:** *L_traj(p) = - Σ_{t=1 to K} cos(ˆDir^(t), d_u*)*, where *ˆDir^(t)* is the VLA's predicted action direction under attack. This loss encourages the adversarial action directions to align with the selected high-curvature direction.

- **Unified Adversarial Objective:**
 The final objective integrates both components: *L_adv(p) = λ_sem L_sem(p) + λ_traj L_traj(p)*. The patch *p* is optimized using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) on the prefix data, with gradient updates restricted by the *M_patch* mask.

**C. Experimental Setup**
- **Implementation:** PyTorch, SGD with exponential learning rate, 50 iterations. Prefix length *K*=40 (default). Patch window size *s*=3 (42x42 pixels). Loss weights *λ_sem*=1.0, *λ_traj*=12.0. *U*=100 candidate directions.
- **Benchmarks:** LIBERO benchmark (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long suites), 10 tasks/category, 100 trials each.
- **Victim Models:** OpenVLA variants (trained per suite), and Hume with fine-tuned checkpoints for architectural diversity.
- **Baselines:** UADA, UPA, TMA from Wang et al., using same prefix length and iterations for comparison.
- **Metrics:** Attack Success Rate (ASR, % task failures) and Normalized Attack Success Rate (nASR, unified score for failure or delay).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated that the proposed partially observable adversarial patch attack framework can induce significant and sustained disruptions in VLA-based robotic systems, outperforming existing baselines under realistic constraints.

**A. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods:**
- **Attack Success Rate (ASR):** The proposed method achieved the highest ASR in nearly all evaluated settings across the four LIBERO suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long) and various prefix lengths (K=10, 20, 30, 40). Its advantage was particularly noticeable at moderate prefix lengths (e.g., K=30). Baselines like UADA, UPA, and TMA showed reduced effectiveness with shorter prefixes, indicating their difficulty in generating generalizable attacks from limited observations. For instance, at K=30, the proposed method achieved ASRs of 73.8% (Spatial), 90.7% (Object), 72.8% (Goal), and 86.6% (Long), consistently higher than the baselines (e.g., UADA at K=30: 57.4% Spatial, 63.8% Object, 59.5% Goal, 54.7% Long).
- **Normalized Attack Success Rate (nASR):** When considering nASR, which accounts for both task failure and rollout slowdowns, the proposed method also demonstrated superior performance across almost all configurations. This suggests that the attack not only causes failures but also induces less efficient, longer trajectories even when tasks are eventually completed.

**B. Visualization of Task Executions:**
Visualizations of robot executions (Figure 3) showed that, when the adversarial patch generated by the proposed method was applied, the VLA policy rapidly deviated from its nominal path. The robot exhibited behaviors such as oscillations, overshoots, and detours, often getting trapped in high-curvature motion patterns. These observed behaviors were consistent with the objectives of the two loss terms: the semantic loss altering object grounding, and the trajectory loss biasing actions towards curvature-amplifying directions. In contrast, patches generated by the UPA baseline typically caused only transient deviations, from which the robot often recovered.

**C. Ablation Studies and Additional Analyses:**
- **Importance of Attention-Based Patch Localization:**
 - Comparing attention-based localization with random placement on LIBERO-Goal tasks demonstrated that the attention-based strategy consistently led to higher ASR (Figure 4), confirming the benefit of placing patches in semantically salient regions.
 - Among attention aggregation strategies, using the attention map from the *last prefix frame (x_K)* was more effective (higher average ASR/nASR) than averaging attention over all prefix frames, particularly at K=30 (Table III).
- **Effect of Semantic and Trajectory Losses:**
 - Ablation studies (Table IV) showed that removing either the semantic loss (L_sem) or the trajectory loss (L_traj) individually degraded attack performance (ASR and nASR). The full model, integrating both components, achieved the best results in most settings, indicating their complementary contributions to the attack's effectiveness.
 - Analysis of loss weights (Figure 5) indicated that a balanced contribution, with moderate weights for both terms (e.g., λ_sem=1.0 and λ_traj=12.0), optimized nASR while maintaining strong ASR.
- **Computational Cost:** The proposed partially observable attack significantly reduced patch generation time compared to full-rollout optimization. At K=30, the method achieved a 6.9x to 16.7x speedup, with generation times of 47-53 seconds across different suites, compared to 351-783 seconds for full-rollout optimization (Table V).
- **Analysis of Semantic Grounding Disruption:** Visualizations of grounding maps (Figure 6) confirmed that the attack successfully altered the VLA model's visual grounding for instruction nouns. Post-attack, the peaks of attention for target objects shifted away from their intended referents towards background regions or areas adjacent to the patch, consistent with the objective of L_sem.
- **Analysis of Trajectory Smoothness Disruption:** An analysis of angular deviations in action directions at the first frame after patch application (K+1) showed substantial changes. Most deviations concentrated in the 30°–60° range (Figure 7), indicating that the patch effectively steered the end-effector into high-curvature motions, supporting the role of L_traj.
- **Trajectory Sampling and Curvature Estimation:** Increasing the number of candidate future directions *U* for curvature estimation from 50 to 100 improved performance, with diminishing returns beyond U=100 (Table VI). Spline-based curvature estimation demonstrated stability and reliability even with short prefix lengths (Table VII), maintaining high agreement rates and positive curvature increase rates.
- **Effectiveness on Other VLA Architectures:** The attack's effectiveness was further validated on Hume VLA models. Similar to OpenVLA, the full method performed best, and removing either loss term reduced performance (Table VIII), confirming that the attack is not specific to a single VLA architecture.
- **Robustness against Simple Defenses:** The attack's performance remained largely unaffected by common input-level defenses like JPEG compression (q=60) and Gaussian blur (r=2). The average ASR remained close to the undefended case (e.g., 79.2% and 80.2% vs. 81.0% undefended, Table IX), suggesting these simple defenses are insufficient.
- **Failure-Case Analysis:** The attack's impact was reduced by very short prefixes (e.g., K=10), on tasks with short and tightly constrained motions (e.g., Spatial suite), or when a large prefix length left a minimal suffix for observation, leading to less pronounced long-term effects. The attack was most effective with a moderate prefix on tasks requiring longer horizons.

**D. Physical Attack Evaluation:**
- Experiments with a ROKAE xMate ER7 Pro arm and a physically printed 18 cm × 18 cm adversarial patch demonstrated real-world effectiveness. The physical patch, mounted in the workspace, caused incorrect object interactions and task failures (Figure 8).
- Quantitative results on two real-world manipulation tasks showed significant reductions in success rates: from 72% to 12% on Task A and from 58% to 8% on Task B (Table X), confirming substantial performance degradation in physical robot execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of adversarial robustness in robotic systems driven by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, particularly by addressing the practical constraints of real-world attack scenarios.

The primary significance lies in the formulation and empirical validation of a **partially observable threat model** for adversarial patch attacks on VLA-based robots. Unlike prior work that assumed full access to robot trajectories, this paper demonstrates that adversaries with limited observation capabilities—only a short initial prefix of execution—can still design static physical patches capable of inducing long-horizon, persistent disruptions. This finding is significant because it shifts the understanding of VLA model vulnerabilities from idealized, high-privilege attack scenarios to more realistic and attainable adversary conditions.

The proposed two-phase framework, which combines **attention-based patch localization** and **content-aware patch optimization**, represents a practical methodology for generating effective adversarial patches under partial observability. By targeting both the semantic grounding of objects and the trajectory smoothness of actions, the method systematically exploits dual vulnerabilities in VLA models, leading to failures in both perception and control. The empirical results, showing consistent outperformance against baselines and substantial reductions in task success rates in both simulation and real-world robotic environments, underscore the efficacy of this approach.

The potential impact of this work is multi-faceted:

- **Highlighting Practical Vulnerabilities:** The research directly demonstrates that VLA-based robots, despite their advanced capabilities, are susceptible to physically realizable adversarial patches under realistic observation constraints. This highlights a critical security concern for their deployment in sensitive domains such as service robotics, manufacturing, and healthcare, where safety and reliability are paramount.
- **Guiding Robustness Research and Development:** By formalizing a more realistic threat model and providing an effective attack methodology, this work offers a concrete benchmark for evaluating the robustness of future VLA models. It motivates developers and researchers to systematically assess and enhance the resilience of these systems against such practical adversarial threats. Robustness enhancements might include developing adversarial training techniques, certified robustness methods, or robust perception and control architectures specifically designed to counter localized and persistent visual perturbations.
- **Informing Defensive Strategies:** The insights into how the attack manipulates semantic grounding and trajectory planning can inform the design of proactive and reactive defensive mechanisms. For example, defenses could focus on robust attention mechanisms, anomaly detection for unusual visual patterns or control signals, or redundant perception systems that are less susceptible to localized visual changes. The observed limited effectiveness of simple input-level defenses (JPEG compression, Gaussian blur) suggests that more sophisticated countermeasures may be required.
- **Advancing Adversarial ML in Robotics:** This research contributes to the broader field of adversarial machine learning in robotics by providing a practical attack framework. It expands the understanding of how adversarial examples can translate from digital perturbations to physical artifacts with sustained effects in dynamic, real-world robotic tasks.

While the work acknowledges limitations, such as reliance on gray-box access to model attention and prefix trajectory information, it establishes a foundational understanding for evaluating VLA robustness under practical constraints. Future research can build upon this to explore even weaker-access settings and broader VLA architectures, further solidifying the security posture of intelligent robotic systems.
