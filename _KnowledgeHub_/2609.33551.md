# FoLD: Force-Informed Learning for Dexterous Articulated Object Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> FoLD transfers bimanual human demonstrations of articulated-object manipulation to dexterous robot hands by computing a virtual compensatory force field: a PD-style corrective wrench from object-motion deviation, allocated over contact-reachable surface anchors via a friction-constrained QP. A PointNet-style encoder compresses the field into a latent that conditions a PPO residual policy over a frozen imitator, raising ARCTIC success from 0.606 to 0.772 across four hands.

## Problem

- Kinematic retargeting matches joint configurations or keypoints but ignores object dynamics, so small hand-pose discrepancies shift contacts and fail to produce the demonstrated articulation.
- Virtual object controllers like DexMachina's push the object directly, never specifying how hand contact forces should sustain the motion once the assistance decays.
- Wrench-support rewards such as CHORD's evaluate contact quality after the fact, leaving the needed force adjustment implicit rather than available as state-conditioned guidance when choosing actions.

## Method

- ==Compensatory force-field estimation==: a clipped PD corrective wrench toward the next reference object pose is allocated over contact-map-masked surface anchors by an ==OSQP quadratic program== with normal-force and ==friction-cone constraints==.
- Force-field representation learning: a part-aware ==PointNet-style encoder== maps anchor positions, normals, forces, and part labels to a 128-d latent, pretrained with force, generalized-force, and direction reconstruction losses.
- Force-informed residual policy: a ==PPO residual==, scaled by 0.1 and zero-initialized, corrects a frozen ==ManipTrans-style hand imitator==, trained under DexMachina's decaying virtual-object-controller curriculum with the field only as observation.

## Results

- Across **27** paired ARCTIC hand-task cells on Inspire, Allegro, XHand, and Schunk, success rises from **0.606** to **0.772** over DexMachina and articulation error falls **52.3%** (**31.06** to **14.83** degrees).
- Appending the force-field latent to CHORD on the Sharpa Wave hand cuts root position and rotation errors by **38.6%** and **22.9%**, while success stays flat (**0.392** to **0.397**).
- Inspire ablation with curriculum fixed: adding force-field input keeps success at **1.000**, raises Contact Mean **0.275** to **0.351**, and lowers position error **37.3%**, though rotation error rises **5.55** to **7.69** degrees.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Translating object-trajectory error into a physically admissible force distribution gives policies actionable guidance; correction signals as observations can complement reward shaping rather than replace it.
> - Describing the desired mechanical effect on the object surface travels across embodiments, letting each hand-specific policy discover how its own fingers should realize that effect.
> - Caution: evidence comes from single-seed, per-task policies with one numerically divergent cell excluded, and real-robot results are open-loop replay, so closed-loop sim-to-real benefit remains unproven.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Human demonstrations are a natural source of dexterous skills, but transferring them to robots exposes a practical gap. Retargeted hand poses can look right yet make the wrong contact, so the articulated object fails to move as demonstrated. Consider a person closing a laptop by stabilizing its base with one hand and moving its screen with the other. A robot must produce the same object motion with a differently shaped hand.

FoLD turns the object's motion error into a *virtual* guide for corrective hand actions. A policy learns how to adjust its contacts rather than receiving forces applied directly to the object. The authors show laptop closing on physical hardware, as open-loop playback of a simulated trajectory rather than a closed-loop policy running on the robot.

Closing a laptop: one hand stabilizes the base while the other moves the screen. The physical playback is open-loop.

### 2. From motion error to hand correction

FoLD starts with a hand imitator that supplies retargeted reference actions. During rollout, it compares the current object state with the next demonstrated state to compute a corrective wrench. This wrench is a combined force and torque indicating the motion correction needed.

FoLD then estimates a spatial force field over surface anchors, restricted to regions indicated by the robot's current contact map. The solver seeks forces that match the corrective wrench while minimizing force magnitude and respecting normal-force and friction constraints. FoLD encodes this high-dimensional field as a 128-dimensional latent input. A residual policy combines it with the current state and demonstration reference to adjust the imitator's actions.

The field is a mechanically constrained *prior*. It is not a measured robot force, and it is not a force applied to the object. The policy learns through its own hand–object contacts. VOC assistance is used during training and decays to zero, and evaluation also disables VOC. The field computation takes about 60–120 ms per frame, with a reported update rate of about 10–11 Hz.

Reference and current object states produce an object-motion deviation. The current contact map constrains a compensatory force field, which is encoded as a latent input to the residual policy. The schematic shows the proposed information flow, not measured forces.

### 3. What the comparisons establish, and where they stop

The nearest contrast is [DexMachina](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2505.24853). It uses a virtual object controller (VOC) that guides the object along its reference trajectory during training and gradually hands control to the policy. FoLD instead estimates a state-conditioned force field to tell the policy how available contacts could correct object-motion error.

The benchmark covers box, ketchup bottle, mixer, notebook, and waffle iron tasks, evaluated with Inspire, Allegro, XHand, and Schunk hands. Success rate (SR) is the fraction of the trajectory completed before root-position error exceeds 15 cm or root-rotation error exceeds 40°. Tracking errors are averaged over the *full* trajectory, including frames after a threshold violation.

The comparison with DexMachina covers 27 paired hand–task evaluations under shared demonstrations, retargeted references, initial states, and physical parameters. Across those pairs, overall SR rises from 0.606 to 0.772. Mean root-position error falls from 36.67 to 19.30 cm, root-rotation error from 46.54° to 29.33°, and articulation error from 31.06° to 14.83°.

These results come with several conditions. One cell, Schunk–Ketchup-300, was excluded from both methods because the DexMachina rollout numerically diverged. The main comparison uses one trained policy per configuration, not independent training seeds. The per-hand pattern also has exceptions: XHand SR falls from 0.724 to 0.697, and Allegro position error rises from 20.07 to 23.62 cm.

The Inspire ablation is the most direct mechanism test. It averages three tasks and holds the VOC curriculum fixed between the relevant rows. In that setup, adding the field changes contact mean from 0.275 to 0.351 and position error from 1.34 to 0.84 cm, with SR remaining 1.000. Rotation error, however, increases from 5.55° to 7.69°, and joint error from 13.30° to 13.76°. The ablation supports a specific benefit of conditioning, not uniform improvement on every metric.

Inspire ablation. All rows use the frozen imitator, the second and third rows share VOC, and the final row adds the force field. Contact mean and position error improve, and SR stays saturated. Rotation and joint errors worsen.

### 4. Closing

FoLD links a desired object motion to a contact-dependent correction that the robot policy can learn to enact. The method has clear limits. It is state-based, depends on accurate interaction reconstructions, and trains each policy for one object–task pair.
