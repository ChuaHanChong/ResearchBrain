# Is One Layer Enough? Training A Single Transformer Layer Can Match Full-Parameter RL Training

> [!summary] Summary
> A study of large language model adaptation via reinforcement learning finds that performance gains are primarily concentrated in a stable subset of middle transformer layers, with single-layer training sometimes recovering over 100% of full-parameter training improvements and enabling more efficient training strategies.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) post-training for large language models (LLMs) typically updates all model parameters uniformly, assuming similar contributions from each transformer layer.
- The internal mechanisms of how RL adaptation distributes across transformer layers of an LLM are largely unexamined, leading to potentially suboptimal or inefficient training methods.
- A lack of understanding regarding layer-wise contributions prevents the development of targeted and more efficient RL training strategies for LLMs.

## Method

- Researchers developed a ==single-layer training framework== to isolate and quantify the capacity of individual transformer layers to absorb RL-induced performance improvements (using ==GRPO==).
- A ==layer contribution== metric was introduced, comparing single-layer training gains against ==full-parameter training== for various LLMs, RL algorithms, and task domains.
- New RL training strategies were explored, including ==layer-adaptive learning rates==, ==layer-selective training==, and ==heuristic middle-layer selection==, based on observed layer contribution patterns.

## Results

- The best individual layers recovered up to **114%** of the performance gains achieved by full-parameter RL training, while the weakest layers recovered less than **30%**.
- Layer-selective training, focusing on a subset of high-contribution layers, improved performance, such as on Qwen3-8B-Base where training only **10 layers** surpassed full-parameter RL training by **2.68 percentage points**.
- A profiling-free heuristic of training only middle layers consistently outperformed full-parameter training, providing a practical method to improve RL adaptation efficiency without prior layer analysis.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RL adaptation exhibits dramatic non-uniformity across transformer layers, with certain individual layers capable of recovering or exceeding full-parameter training gains.
> - A consistent structural pattern emerges where high-contribution layers are concentrated in the middle of the transformer stack, robust across different models, RL algorithms, and task domains.
> - Ensembles of models trained on different high-contribution layers solve complementary sets of problems, suggesting diverse specialized capabilities within distinct layers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has become a crucial methodology for the post-training adaptation of large language models (LLMs), leading to substantial performance enhancements in areas such as mathematical reasoning, code generation, and agentic decision-making. Despite extensive efforts to develop more effective RL objectives, reward models, and optimization algorithms, the internal mechanisms of how RL adaptation distributes across the transformer layers of an LLM remain largely unexamined. Current RL post-training methods typically update all model parameters uniformly, implicitly assuming that each layer contributes similarly to the observed performance gains.

This work integrates with a growing body of research that highlights the non-uniform characteristics of LLM layers. Prior studies have indicated that different transformer layers play distinct roles, with some layers being critical for performance while others have less impact when removed. Similar layer-wise heterogeneity has been observed during supervised finetuning (SFT), where parameter updates and adaptation behaviors vary significantly across layers. These findings have led to the development of layer-aware optimization strategies for SFT, such as layer-wise sampling and adaptive layer selection. However, these previous investigations primarily focused on inference-time behavior or supervised finetuning contexts. This paper addresses a gap by systematically investigating whether RL post-training exhibits a similar structured, layer-wise pattern. It seeks to understand where RL-induced gains emerge within the network, challenging the assumption of uniform contribution and exploring the potential for exploiting any discovered structural properties to improve RL post-training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are as follows:

1. **Challenge the Uniform Contribution Assumption:** The work directly challenges the prevailing assumption in RL post-training that all transformer layers contribute uniformly to performance improvements. Current methodologies often update all model parameters without differentiation, which may not be optimal if adaptation is non-uniform.
2. **Systematic Layer-wise Investigation:** The authors aim to conduct a systematic and controlled study to understand how RL adaptation is distributed across individual transformer layers within an LLM. This involves isolating the training process to single layers to observe their independent capacity for learning.
3. **Quantify Layer Contribution:** To precisely measure the impact of individual layers, the study introduces a metric called "layer contribution." This metric quantifies the fraction of total RL improvement achieved by training a single layer in isolation compared to full-parameter training. This allows for a granular assessment of each layer's importance.
4. **Identify Structural Patterns in RL Adaptation:** A key motivation is to uncover whether RL post-training exhibits an underlying structural pattern in how it modifies LLMs. Specifically, the researchers investigate if certain layers consistently show higher contributions across different models, tasks, and algorithms, and if these patterns are stable or idiosyncratic.
5. **Inform and Improve RL Training Strategies:** The findings are intended to have practical implications for optimizing RL post-training. If specific layers are found to be disproportionately important, this knowledge could guide the development of more efficient and effective layer-aware training strategies that prioritize these high-contribution layers, potentially outperforming standard full-parameter training.
6. **Enhance Fundamental Understanding of LLM Adaptation:** Beyond practical improvements, the research seeks to provide new insights into the fundamental question of *how* RL reshapes a pretrained language model. Understanding the layer-wise dynamics of RL adaptation can deepen the comprehension of LLM internal mechanisms and learning processes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic layer-wise study approach, coupled with controlled experiments and comparative analyses across various models, RL algorithms, and task domains.

**A. Single-Layer Training Framework:**
For an LLM with *L* transformer layers, embedding parameters (θ_emb), and a language model head (θ_head), the standard full-parameter RL training updates all parameters jointly. In contrast, this study implements a single-layer training framework where only the parameters of a specific transformer layer (θ_k) are updated, while all other parameters in the network are frozen. The gradient ∇θ_k L_GRPO(θ) is computed via backpropagation through the entire network, but the update is restricted solely to layer *k*. This process is repeated independently for each layer *k* ∈ {0..., L-1}.

**B. Layer Contribution Metric:**
To quantify the capacity of each layer to absorb RL-induced improvement, the researchers define "layer contribution" *C(k)*. Let *S_k* be the performance of the model trained on layer *k*, *S_base* the performance of the original pretrained model without RL training, and *S_full* the performance after standard full-parameter RL training.
The layer contribution is calculated as:
*C(k) = (S_k - S_base) / (S_full - S_base)*
A *C(k)* of 1.0 indicates that single-layer training matches the gain of full-parameter training, values >1.0 signify surpassing it, and values near 0 indicate minimal improvement.

**C. Experimental Setup and Models:**
- **Primary Models:** Qwen3-1.7B-Base (28 layers), Qwen3-4B-Base (36 layers), and Qwen3-8B-Base (36 layers). These models were selected as they had not undergone post-training, providing a "clean" baseline.
- **Primary RL Algorithm:** Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was used for the Qwen3 models. GRPO estimates advantages without a learned value network, sampling multiple responses to compute a group-normalized advantage.
- **Primary Task Domain/Dataset:** Mathematical reasoning, using the NuminaMath-CoT dataset, a collection of competition-level math problems.
- **Generalization Models:**
 - Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (28 layers) with Dr. GRPO on the MATH training set.
 - Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (28 layers) and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (36 layers) with GiGPO on the agentic task ALFWorld.
 - DeepSeek-Distilled-Qwen-7B (28 layers) with GRPO on the Skywork mathematics dataset.
- **Cross-Dataset/Cross-Task Consistency:** Qwen3-1.7B-Base was also trained with DeepScaleR (mathematics) and DeepCoder (coding) datasets to assess consistency.

**D. Evaluation and Fair Comparison Protocol:**
- **Primary Evaluation:** For Qwen3 models, evaluation was conducted on 12 benchmarks across four categories: Math (in-domain: MATH500, GSM8K, OlympiadBench, AMC), Code (HumanEval+, MBPP, LiveCodeBench), Reasoning (GPQA-Diamond, MMLU-Pro), and Language (C-Eval, IFEval, MGSM). The overall score was the unweighted average.
- **Other Evaluations:** Specific math benchmarks for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and DeepSeek-Distilled-Qwen-7B, and ALFWorld tasks for agentic models.
- **Fair Comparison:**
 1. The learning rate for the full-parameter baseline was optimally tuned.
 2. This same optimal learning rate was applied to all single-layer training runs.
 3. All other hyperparameters (batch size, KL coefficient, clip range, epochs) were kept identical across full-parameter and single-layer configurations.
 4. All models were trained to convergence under the same training steps.
 5. Publicly available results were referenced where applicable.
 6. An ablation study was conducted to confirm that adjusting the learning rate did not alter the layer contribution rankings, indicating robustness.

**E. Layer-Aware Training Strategies:**
Based on the observed layer contribution patterns, three strategies were explored for improving RLVR:
1. **Layer-Adaptive Learning Rate:** Boosting the learning rate for high-contribution layers while keeping others at a default rate.
2. **Layer-Selective Training:** Freezing low-contribution layers and training only a subset of highest-contribution layers.
3. **Heuristic Layer Selection:** Training only the middle *k* layers by their positional index, without prior profiling, as a practical, profiling-free approach.

**F. Analysis of Diversity and Weight Change:**
- **Diversity:** Jaccard similarity was used to measure the overlap in newly solved problems by different layer-trained models. Majority voting was performed on ensembles of top-performing layer-trained models.
- **Weight Change:** The L2 norm of parameter differences (∥∆θ_k∥_2) was calculated for both full-parameter and single-layer training to assess if contribution correlated with parameter movement magnitude.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the distribution of RL adaptation across transformer layers:

**A. Dramatic Variation in Layer Contribution:**
- **Non-uniformity:** The contribution of individual layers to RL-induced improvement varies significantly. The best individual layers were observed to recover up to 114% of the gains achieved by full-parameter RL training, while the weakest layers recovered less than 30%.
- **Outperformance:** In multiple instances across different models, single-layer training not only matched but also surpassed the performance of full-parameter RL training, suggesting that joint training of all layers can sometimes dilute or hinder optimal adaptation.
- **Degradation:** For some models, particularly Qwen3-8B-Base, training specific layers (e.g., Layer 0) in isolation resulted in negative contribution, degrading performance below the base model.

**B. Consistent Structural Pattern of High Contribution:**
- **Middle-Layer Concentration:** Across all seven models studied, encompassing two model families (Qwen3, Qwen2.5), three RL algorithms (GRPO, GiGPO, Dr. GRPO), and three task domains (mathematical reasoning, code generation, agentic decision-making), a remarkably stable pattern emerged: high-contribution layers consistently concentrated in the middle of the transformer stack. Layers near the input and output ends consistently contributed substantially less.
- **Cross-Dataset Consistency:** For a fixed model (Qwen3-1.7B-Base), the per-layer contribution rankings remained strongly correlated across different mathematics datasets (NuminaMath-CoT vs. DeepScaleR, Spearman ρ = 0.76).
- **Cross-Task Consistency:** This consistency extended even to fundamentally different tasks, with mathematical reasoning (NuminaMath-CoT) and code generation (DeepCoder) layer rankings showing a significant correlation (Spearman ρ = 0.59). This suggests layer contribution is an intrinsic property of the pretrained model, less dependent on specific data or tasks.
- **Generalizability:** The qualitative structure of middle-layer concentration and high-contribution was robust across varying model scales (1.5B to 8B parameters), different model architectures (including distilled models like DeepSeek-Distilled-Qwen-7B), and diverse task domains like multi-step agentic decision-making (ALFWorld).

**C. Broad Capability Improvement:**
- High-contribution layers did not merely improve in-domain task performance. The overall layer contribution (considering an average across Math, Code, Reasoning, and Language capabilities) closely tracked the in-domain math contribution (Pearson r > 0.6 across Qwen3 models). This indicated that single-layer training captured genuine, broad capability improvements rather than overfitting to the training objective.

**D. Improved RL Training Strategies Guided by Layer Contribution:**
- **Layer-Adaptive Learning Rate:** Boosting the learning rate of high-contribution layers consistently improved math performance over the uniform-learning-rate baseline. For example, on Qwen3-1.7B-Base, boosting the 10 best layers (Boost B10) achieved 53.70% average math accuracy, compared to 50.82% for the baseline, representing 43% of the total RL training gain. Boosting low-contribution layers, conversely, led to performance decline.
- **Layer-Selective Training:** Training only the highest-contribution layers further enhanced performance. On Qwen3-8B-Base, training only the 10 highest-contribution layers (Only B10) yielded 69.11% accuracy, surpassing the full-parameter RL training at 66.43% (a +2.68 percentage point gain, representing 32% of the total RL gain). This suggested that freezing low-contribution layers can lead to cleaner optimization.
- **Heuristic Layer Selection:** A profiling-free heuristic of training only the middle layers by position (e.g., layers 15-19 for Qwen3-8B-Base) also consistently outperformed the full-parameter baseline (68.19% vs. 66.43% on Qwen3-8B-Base, representing 21% of the total RL gain). This indicates a practical alternative when layer-wise profiling is not feasible.

**E. Diversity and Complementarity of Layer-Trained Models:**
- Analysis on OlympiadBench for Qwen3-1.7B-Base revealed that models trained on different high-contribution layers, despite achieving similar individual performance, solved largely non-overlapping sets of problems (average pairwise Jaccard similarity of newly solved problems was 34.1%).
- Aggregating predictions from the top-7 layer-trained models via majority voting achieved 33.6% accuracy on OlympiadBench, outperforming both the best single layer-trained model (28.3%) and the full-parameter baseline (26.9%). This ensemble also surpassed self-consistency-style voting from a single full-parameter model (31.3%), highlighting the value of diversity from distinct layer adaptations.

**F. Dissociation of Weight Change Magnitude and Layer Contribution:**
- Measurements of L2 norm of parameter changes showed that during full-parameter training, weight changes were relatively uniform across layers, contrasting with the highly non-uniform layer contribution profile.
- In single-layer training, while trained layers underwent substantially larger weight changes than during full training, the magnitude of this change was comparable across layers with vastly different contribution values. This indicated that layer contribution reflects the effectiveness of a layer's parameter subspace rather than simply the extent of its parameter modification.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research carry several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Fundamental Insight into LLM Adaptation:** The work reveals a previously unrecognized structural property of RL post-training: the gains are not uniformly distributed but are highly concentrated in a small, stable subset of transformer layers, specifically in the middle of the network. This challenges the intuition that RL improvements arise from coordinated adaptation across the entire network and provides a more granular understanding of how LLMs learn and adapt under RL.
2. **Guidance for Future RL Algorithm Design:** The consistent identification of high-contribution layers across diverse models, tasks, and algorithms suggests that RL optimization might be made more efficient by selectively targeting or prioritizing these layers. This could lead to the development of novel RL algorithms that intrinsically leverage this layer-wise importance, potentially reducing computational costs and improving sample efficiency.
3. **Improved Practical Training Strategies:** The demonstrated success of layer-adaptive learning rates and layer-selective training strategies, which consistently outperform standard full-parameter RL training, offers direct avenues for practical improvements in LLM post-training. Furthermore, the effectiveness of the heuristic middle-layer selection strategy provides a readily implementable, profiling-free approach for practitioners.
4. **Enhanced Model Interpretability:** By pinpointing which layers are most critical for RL adaptation, the research contributes to a deeper understanding of the functional roles of different transformer layers. This could facilitate further investigations into *why* middle layers exhibit this disproportionate importance and what specific capabilities or representations they are responsible for consolidating during RL.
5. **New Perspectives on Expert Specialization:** The observation that different high-contribution layers solve complementary sets of problems, rather than redundant ones, supports the notion of diverse task-specific experts being distributed within a pretrained model's weight space. Single-layer training offers a structured method for exploring and identifying these specialized experts along the dimension of transformer depth, which could inform future ensemble methods or modular model architectures.
6. **Potential for Resource Optimization:** By focusing computational resources on the most impactful layers, layer-aware training strategies could lead to more resource-efficient RL post-training, which is particularly relevant given the increasing size and training costs of modern LLMs.

While the guided training strategies were validated primarily on mathematical reasoning tasks, and a deeper theoretical understanding of the middle-layer phenomenon is an open question, this research provides a new perspective for comprehending and enhancing RL post-training for LLMs.
