# Human POSEitioning System (HPS): 3D Human Pose Estimation and Self-localization in Large Scenes from Body-Mounted Sensors

> [!summary] Summary
> HPS (Human POSEitioning System) recovers full 3D body pose and location inside a pre-scanned large scene using only wearable sensors: 17 body IMUs plus an outward-looking head camera. A joint optimization fuses drift-free but noisy camera self-localization, short-term-accurate IMU pose, foot-contact scene constraints, and smoothness terms, yielding drift-free motion over 300-1000 m2 scenes, plus a 3-hour, 7-subject dataset.

## Problem

- Third-person camera capture suffers occlusion, cannot span multiple rooms or exceed a fixed recording volume, so recorded sequences stay short and scene-limited.
- Pure IMU motion capture accumulates severe drift in global orientation and translation, so estimated bodies penetrate or float above the real scene geometry.
- Egocentric methods point cameras at the body and recover only upper body or inaccurate poses, and none register the person inside a scanned 3D environment.

## Method

- Hierarchical structure-based self-localization: ==NetVLAD== retrieval over a scene image database, ==SuperPoint== 2D-3D matches from a dense 3D model, ==P3P== inside ==RANSAC== gives head camera pose.
- Joint optimization over **SMPL** pose and translation combining a geodesic self-localization term, foot contact and anti-sliding scene terms, translation/orientation smoothness, and an IMU pose regularizer.
- Initialization and frame alignment: outlier filtering of camera translations by a 3 m/s velocity threshold, plus rotating IMU root orientation to align its trajectory tangent with the localization trajectory.

## Results

- Chamfer error stays flat with distance: **4.60**, **4.26**, **4.53** cm after 70, 200, 380 m travelled, versus IMU-only drifting from **54.49** to **108.44** cm.
- Foot-contact frames show **0.056** cm mean foot-to-surface distance for HPS versus **0.32** cm without scene terms, **0.95** cm filtered camera baseline, **188.38** cm IMU-only.
- Dynamic-motion evaluation gives **4.76-15.96** cm error, degrading only where camera localization fails for long stretches, and recovering once nearby frames localize again.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fusing a drift-free but outlier-prone signal with a smooth but drifting one at optimization time, rather than filtering either alone, cancels both failure modes.
> - Contact constraints against a known 3D scene act as a cheap physical prior, converting merely accurate trajectories into physically plausible ones without dynamics simulation.
> - The approach inherits its floor from localization: featureless regions or scene changes since scanning cause persistent failure, so map freshness bounds achievable accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of 3D human pose estimation and localization has primarily focused on methods using external, third-person cameras. These approaches typically involve analyzing human motion without extensive consideration of the surrounding scene context. While significant progress has been made in capturing 3D scenes and human motion using external cameras, these methods are subject to limitations such as occlusions, restricted recording volumes, and limited capture durations, often failing to record interactions across multiple rooms or beyond a fixed viewing area.

A separate line of research involves IMU-based human pose estimation, which utilizes body-mounted inertial measurement units. Commercial IMU systems offer reasonable short-term articulation accuracy but are known to suffer from significant global orientation and position drift over extended periods. Attempts to mitigate this drift have included combining IMUs with external cameras (e.g., multiple cameras, depth cameras, or single handheld RGB cameras), but these still necessitate an external perspective and thus share some limitations of third-person camera systems regarding capture volume and the need for operator presence.

Egocentric capture has also garnered attention, particularly for activity recognition. However, most egocentric body capture methods place cameras facing the body, providing limited scene context. Methods that place cameras looking outwards for 3D pose estimation from an egocentric view often yield inaccurate and uncertain pose estimates. Visual-inertial odometry and localization systems, which fuse camera data with IMU data, have demonstrated robust and drift-free localization, but typically do not integrate full 3D human pose estimation with scene interaction.

This research introduces a novel approach, Human POSEitioning System (HPS), which addresses the aforementioned limitations by uniquely combining wearable sensors from an egocentric perspective—body-mounted IMUs and a head-mounted camera looking outwards—with a pre-scanned 3D environment. This configuration allows for continuous, drift-free 3D human pose estimation and self-localization in large, complex scenes over long durations, a capability not comprehensively achieved by prior methods. HPS directly tackles the challenges of IMU drift and camera localization outliers by integrating these modalities within a unified optimization framework that also incorporates 3D scene constraints. This positions HPS as a complementary method to existing third-person systems, expanding the scope of human motion capture into environments and durations previously inaccessible or problematic for other technologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the Human POSEitioning System (HPS) is to achieve the recovery of the full 3D pose of a human subject and their precise location, registered with a 3D scan of the surrounding environment, using exclusively wearable sensors. This involves leveraging body-mounted Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) and a head-mounted camera directed outwards.

More specifically, the work aims to achieve several interconnected objectives:

- **Drift-Free Pose and Localization:** To overcome the inherent drift observed in standalone IMU-based tracking systems, especially concerning global orientation and position, by integrating supplementary information from an external source.
- **Robustness to Camera Outliers:** To mitigate the noise and frequent outliers that can occur in camera-based self-localization estimates, ensuring a stable and continuous localization signal.
- **Physically Plausible Motion:** To incorporate 3D scene constraints, such as foot contact with the ground, directly into the pose estimation process, thereby generating human motion that adheres to physical reality and scene geometry.
- **Large-Scale and Long-Duration Capture:** To enable the capture of human motion in extensive environments (e.g., multiple rooms, large indoor spaces, or outdoors) and over prolonged periods, transcending the spatial and temporal limitations of traditional external camera-based systems.

The motivation for pursuing these objectives stems from several application-driven and research-oriented needs:

- **Augmented, Mixed, and Virtual Reality (AR/MR/VR):** The ability to track a user's full 3D pose and their exact location within a virtual or augmented environment, without requiring external cameras, would allow for unrestricted movement and interaction with virtual objects. This eliminates line-of-sight issues and expands the user experience in large-scale interactive applications.
- **Training Embodied AI Agents:** Capturing human motion and corresponding first-person visual input in natural, complex environments can provide valuable data for training autonomous agents. These agents could learn to navigate, interact with, and understand environments in a manner analogous to human perception and action.
- **Expanded Human Motion Datasets:** Current 3D pose datasets are predominantly captured from a third-person perspective, often in restricted volumes. An egocentric capture system that tracks humans in diverse, large-scale environments opens possibilities for collecting a new genre of datasets. These datasets would provide insights into human-environment interaction, long-term behavior, and the relationship between visual input and body movement from a first-person viewpoint.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Systems:** The research directly addresses fundamental limitations of current 3D pose estimation techniques, such as the spatial constraints of external camera systems and the temporal drift of pure IMU systems. By providing a method that fuses the complementary strengths of these sensors (drift-free but noisy camera localization with short-term accurate but drifting IMU data), it seeks to achieve a more robust and versatile solution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Human POSEitioning System (HPS) integrates data from wearable sensors—specifically, body-mounted IMUs and a head-mounted RGB camera—with a pre-scanned 3D model of the environment to estimate the full 3D human pose and self-localization. The methodology centers around a joint optimization framework that fuses these heterogeneous data sources and incorporates scene constraints.

**Input Data:**
The system requires three primary inputs:
1. **Head-mounted RGB camera video:** Captures the egocentric visual perspective of the subject.
2. **Body-mounted IMU data:** Collected from 17 IMUs attached to various body limbs.
3. **Pre-built 3D scene scan:** A dense 3D model of the environment, accompanied by a database of RGB images with known camera parameters used for scene reconstruction and localization.

**SMPL Body Model:**
The human subject's geometry and articulation are represented using the Skinned Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) body model. SMPL defines a differentiable function that maps pose parameters ($\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{72}$ representing relative joint orientations), translation ($t \in \mathbb{R}^3$), and shape parameters ($\beta \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$) to a 3D human mesh. For HPS, the shape parameters ($\beta$) are estimated once for a subject and assumed constant, while pose ($\theta$) and translation ($t$) are optimized over time.

**Camera Self-localization:**
This module estimates the 6-Degrees of Freedom (6-DoF) pose (position $t_C$ and orientation $R_C$) of the head-mounted camera within the pre-scanned 3D scene. A hierarchical structure-based localization algorithm is employed for scalability to large scenes. This process involves:
1. **Image Retrieval:** Identifying relevant database images (used to build the 3D scene map) corresponding to the query image from the head camera, typically using global descriptors like NetVLAD.
2. **Feature Matching:** Establishing 2D-3D correspondences by matching local features (e.g., SuperPoint) extracted from the query image with 3D points visible in the retrieved database images. The 3D point positions are directly obtained by rendering the dense 3D scene model from the known poses of the database images.
3. **Pose Estimation:** Solving the P3P problem within a RANSAC loop, combined with local optimization, to estimate the camera's pose.

**IMU-based Pose Estimation:**
A commercial inertial motion capture system (XSens) provides initial 3D pose estimates ($\theta_I$) and relative location estimates ($t_I$). While IMUs excel at capturing body articulation accurately in the short term, their global orientation and position estimates are prone to significant drift over time. The IMU system also detects foot contacts with the ground, which are crucial for scene constraint integration.

**Joint Optimization:**
The core of HPS is a non-convex optimization problem that simultaneously refines the SMPL pose ($\theta_{1:T}$) and translation ($t_{1:T}$) parameters over a batch of $T$ frames. The objective function, $E(\theta_{1:T}, t_{1:T})$, is a weighted sum of four terms:

1. **Self-localization Term ($E_{self}$):** This term minimizes the geodesic distance between the head camera's orientation as derived from the SMPL model ($R_C(\theta_j)$) and the camera self-localization estimate ($R_{C_j}$). The SMPL head orientation is calculated via the kinematic chain, and a pre-calibrated camera-to-head offset transforms it to the camera's orientation.
2. **Scene Contact Term ($E_{scene}$):** This term is activated when IMUs detect foot contact with the ground. It consists of two sub-terms:
 - `E_contact`: Snaps specific foot vertices (toe and heel regions) of the SMPL mesh to the closest points on the 3D scene surface.
 - `E_slide`: Minimizes the movement of contacting foot vertices between successive frames to prevent unnatural sliding along the surface.
3. **Smoothness Term ($E_{sm}$):** This term regularizes the motion to ensure temporal smoothness. It includes sub-terms for global translation ($E_T$), global orientation ($E_G$), and head orientation ($E_H$), penalizing abrupt changes in these parameters over time.
4. **Pose Term ($E_{IMU}$):** This term constrains the non-root pose parameters of the SMPL model to remain close to the IMU's articulated pose estimates ($\theta_{I_j}$). This leverages the IMUs' strength in local articulation while allowing the optimization to correct for global drift.

**Initialization:**
Given the non-convexity of the objective function, proper initialization is critical.
- **Translation:** Initialized using camera self-localization estimates ($t_{C_j}$). Outliers in camera localization are detected based on velocity thresholds and replaced via interpolation to provide a smoother initial trajectory.
- **Pose:** IMU pose estimates ($\theta_{I_j}$) are used, but their global orientation is corrected. This correction aligns the tangent direction of the IMU's trajectory with the (filtered) camera's trajectory, ensuring the body's global orientation is consistent with its direction of movement.

**Coordinate Frame Alignment:**
Prior to optimization, the IMU's local coordinate frame is aligned with the global 3D scene frame. This is achieved by finding a planar rotation that aligns the SMPL head's orientation (derived from IMUs at frame zero) with the initial camera self-localization orientation. This rotation is then applied to all subsequent IMU pose and position estimates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HPS focused on demonstrating its ability to provide drift-free, robust, and physically plausible 3D human pose and localization in large scenes. Since no direct baselines existed for this specific problem, the authors developed several comparative baselines by isolating components of HPS.

**Quantitative Evaluation:**
To quantify accuracy, a protocol was established where subjects moved freely in large scenes but periodically returned to a small sub-area equipped with three synchronized and calibrated Azure Kinect RGB-D cameras. These cameras captured dynamic ground truth point clouds of the human subject, which were registered with the global 3D scene. Accuracy was measured as the bidirectional Chamfer distance between the estimated SMPL mesh and the ground truth point cloud, without Procrustes alignment.

The following baselines were used for comparison:
1. **IMU:** Pure IMU tracker (XSens).
2. **IMU+Cam:** IMU pose with raw camera self-localization for translation.
3. **IMU+Cam (filtered):** IMU pose with filtered camera self-localization for translation (outliers removed, similar to HPS initialization).
4. **HPS w/o scene:** The full HPS optimization pipeline, but without the 3D scene contact constraints.
5. **HPS:** The complete Human POSEitioning System.

**Key Findings on Drift and Outliers (Tables 1 & 2):**

- **IMU-only drift:** The pure IMU method exhibited significant drift in global body translation and orientation, with errors accumulating substantially over time and distance traveled (e.g., 108.44 cm error after 380m of travel).
- **Camera localization noise:** Directly combining IMU poses with raw camera self-localization (IMU+Cam) introduced high noise and severe jitter, as camera localization is prone to outliers.
- **Filtered camera improvement:** Filtering camera outliers (IMU+Cam (filtered)) mitigated much of the jitter but still lacked precision and suffered from global orientation errors, where the body orientation did not align with the direction of movement.
- **HPS without scene constraints:** This version showed improvement over filtered IMU+Cam, indicating the benefit of the joint optimization and smoothness terms. However, without explicit scene contact constraints, the estimated human mesh could unrealistically penetrate or float above the ground.
- **Full HPS Performance:** HPS consistently achieved the best accuracy among all baselines. Its error did not accumulate with increased distance traveled or time, demonstrating drift-free performance. It demonstrated robustness to non-persistent camera localization outliers. The reported errors ranged from approximately 4 cm to 15 cm. The system's performance was noted to degrade during prolonged periods of camera localization failure, but it could recover once good localization data became available again.

**Key Findings on Foot Contacts (Table 3):**

- HPS demonstrated superior performance in maintaining realistic foot-to-scene contact compared to baselines. The average distance between foot vertices and the scene surface was significantly reduced (0.056 cm for HPS vs. 0.95 cm for IMU+Cam (filtered) and 188.38 cm for IMU).
- Foot sliding was also reduced (0.90 cm for HPS vs. 1.75 cm for IMU+Cam (filtered)), contributing to more stable and physically plausible motion. This indicates the effectiveness of the $E_{scene}$ term in ensuring physical interaction with the environment.

**Qualitative Evaluation (Figures 5, 6, 7):**

- Qualitative results corroborated the quantitative findings. HPS outputs showed natural human motions, accurately registered with the 3D scene.
- Figure 5 illustrated how scene contact constraints in HPS prevented the human mesh from floating or penetrating the ground, leading to more stable results.
- Figure 6 highlighted the improvement in global body orientation, with HPS correctly aligning the body's facing direction with its movement, a common issue in IMU+Cam (filtered) approaches.
- The system was shown to successfully estimate 3D pose and localization for diverse activities (e.g., exercising, dancing, reading, sitting, eating) in a variety of indoor and outdoor large scenes, all without external cameras.

**HPS Dataset:**
A significant outcome of this work is the creation and release of the HPS dataset. This dataset comprises over three hours of diverse human motion from 7 subjects across 8 large scenes (ranging from 300 to over 1000 m$^2$). It provides synchronized RGB images from a head-mounted camera, IMU data, and the corresponding 3D reconstructed human poses and locations registered within the 3D scene. This dataset serves as a valuable resource for future research in egocentric tracking, human-scene interaction, and visual processing from a first-person perspective.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Human POSEitioning System (HPS) represents a notable advancement in the field of 3D human pose estimation and localization, primarily due to its ability to achieve drift-free, robust, and physically plausible tracking in large 3D environments using solely wearable sensors.

**Novel Technical Contribution:**
HPS is presented as the first method to estimate full 3D human body pose while simultaneously localizing the person within a pre-scanned, large 3D scene, relying entirely on body-mounted IMUs and an outward-facing head camera. The core innovation lies in its joint optimization framework, which effectively fuses disparate sensor inputs (IMU pose, camera self-localization) and integrates scene-specific geometric constraints (e.g., foot contact) to overcome the individual limitations of each sensor modality. This integration results in a system that mitigates IMU drift and is robust to non-persistent camera localization outliers, yielding smooth and accurate motion estimates over extended periods and large areas.

**Enabling New Applications and Research Directions:**
The capabilities of HPS have the potential to significantly impact several domains:

- **Virtual and Augmented Reality (VR/AR):** By eliminating the need for external cameras, HPS allows VR/AR users to move freely and naturally within large physical spaces, interacting with virtual content without line-of-sight restrictions or constrained recording volumes. This could lead to more immersive and expansive VR/AR experiences.
- **Embodied AI and Robotics:** The ability to capture human movement synchronized with egocentric visual input in complex, real-world environments provides an unprecedented dataset for training AI agents. These agents could learn human-like navigation strategies, interaction behaviors, and environmental understanding from a first-person perspective, leading to more capable and human-aware robots.
- **Human-Environment Interaction Studies:** HPS facilitates the capture of natural human behavior in diverse settings and over long durations. This data can be instrumental in understanding how humans perceive, navigate, and interact with their physical surroundings, potentially informing architectural design, ergonomics, and human-computer interaction principles.
- **Digital Human Modeling:** The system can be used to generate realistic data for the development and evaluation of digital human models, particularly those that integrate appearance, pose, and interaction with 3D scenes.

**Addressing Limitations of Current Methods:**
HPS directly addresses fundamental limitations of existing 3D pose estimation techniques:
- It overcomes the spatial and temporal restrictions of external camera-based systems, which are prone to occlusions and limited capture volumes.
- It resolves the global drift issue inherent in pure IMU-based systems, enhancing their utility for long-term tracking.
- By focusing on an outward-facing egocentric camera, it moves beyond methods that require cameras to view the body itself, enabling a broader context of human-scene interaction.

**Contribution of the HPS Dataset:**
The creation and public release of the HPS dataset is a significant contribution. This dataset offers a unique resource of synchronized egocentric video, IMU data, and corresponding 3D human poses within large-scale 3D environments. This directly addresses a gap in existing datasets, which are predominantly captured from a third-person perspective, and provides a valuable testbed for developing new algorithms in areas such as egocentric tracking, human-environment modeling, and first-person vision.

**Future Directions:**
While HPS achieves a notable level of accuracy, the authors acknowledge that many applications will require sub-centimeter precision. Future research avenues include:
- Integrating on-the-fly local map building to dynamically update the static 3D scene model with moving objects and new structures, improving localization and enabling interaction with dynamic elements.
- Incorporating semantic information into camera localization to enhance robustness, especially in challenging environments.
- Extending scene constraints beyond foot contact to other body parts and learning models to anticipate human intent for more predictive and robust tracking.
- Combining HPS with virtual human appearance models to generate synthetic, yet realistic, training data for human analysis methods.

Overall, HPS represents a foundational step in egocentric human motion capture, opening new possibilities for research and applications in VR/AR, AI, and human behavior analysis.
