# GAT-Grasp: Gesture-Driven Affordance Transfer for Task-Aware Robotic Grasping

> [!summary] Summary
> GAT-Grasp introduces a system for task-aware robotic grasping that utilizes human pointing and grasp gestures to guide robots. It achieves zero-shot generalization to novel objects by retrieving and transferring affordance knowledge from a memory bank of human-object interactions, outperforming prior methods in success rate across seen and unseen objects.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with ambiguous affordance reasoning, making it difficult to determine task-specific grasp points and orientations.
- Existing robotic grasping systems have limited adaptability and often require extensive training data or pre-given object models to generalize to novel objects or cluttered environments.
- Traditional communication methods like language-based instructions can be too imprecise for fine-grained robotic manipulation tasks.

## Method

- A two-stage gesture interface allows humans to first point to a target object and then demonstrate the intended grasp.
- A retrieval and transfer system matches input gestures and object contexts with a memory bank of human-object interaction videos, enabling the transfer of affordance knowledge.
- A hand-gripper mapping component translates human hand configurations from the gesture into precise 7-DOF robot gripper orientations.

## Results

- GAT-Grasp achieved higher success rates than existing language-based systems (GPT-4o) and other retrieval-based methods (RAM) on both seen and unseen objects.
- On seen objects, GAT-Grasp demonstrated a **70%** average success rate, surpassing GPT-4o (**48%**) and RAM (**40%**).
- The system maintained a **40%** success rate on unseen objects, outperforming GPT-4o (**33%**) and RAM (**20%**), demonstrating its generalization capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human gestures provide a more intuitive and precise spatial and kinematic guide for robotic grasping than language commands.
> - Leveraging a retrieval-based approach on a diverse memory bank of human demonstrations enables zero-shot generalization of task-aware grasping to novel objects.
> - By transferring dense feature maps from retrieved human-object interactions, robots can adapt grasping affordances based on visual and functional similarities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Robotic grasping has long been a fundamental challenge in robotics, particularly when robots need to perform task-specific grasps in complex environments. Traditional approaches often struggle with generalizing to novel objects or understanding the appropriate way to grasp objects for specific tasks. GAT-Grasp (Gesture-driven Affordance Transfer for Task-Aware Robotic Grasping) introduces an innovative solution that leverages human gestures to guide robots in determining both where and how to grasp objects.

As shown in the figure above, GAT-Grasp uses a two-stage approach where humans provide intuitive gestural cues: first a pointing gesture to indicate the target object, and then a grasp gesture to demonstrate how the object should be manipulated. This information is processed through a retrieval and transfer system that connects to a memory bank of human-object interactions (HOI), ultimately enabling the robot to execute appropriate grasps for specific tasks.

### 2. Challenges in Robotic Grasping

Task-aware robotic grasping faces several key challenges that GAT-Grasp aims to address:

1. **Ambiguous Affordance Reasoning**: Determining the appropriate grasp point and orientation for a specific task is difficult, especially when objects have multiple potential grasp locations.

2. **Limited Adaptability**: Most existing systems struggle to generalize to novel objects or cluttered environments without extensive training data.

3. **Imprecise Communication**: Language-based instructions (like "grasp the handle") can be too coarse-grained for precise manipulation tasks.

4. **Dependency on Object Priors**: Many systems rely heavily on pre-given object models or extensive training data, limiting their application in real-world scenarios.

The core insight of GAT-Grasp is that human gestures can provide more intuitive and precise spatial guidance than language, while a retrieval-based approach leveraging human demonstrations can enable zero-shot generalization to novel objects.

### 3. The GAT-Grasp Framework

GAT-Grasp consists of several key components working together to enable gesture-driven, task-aware grasping:

1. **Two-Stage Gesture Interface**:
 - A pointing gesture identifies the target object or region
 - A grasp gesture demonstrates how the object should be grasped

2. **Affordance Memory Bank**:
 - A database of human-object interaction (HOI) videos capturing diverse grasping behaviors
 - Encoded with gesture-conditioned affordance priors

3. **Retrieval and Transfer System**:
 - Matches input gestures with similar instances in the memory bank
 - Transfers affordance knowledge from retrieved examples to novel objects

4. **Hand-Gripper Mapping**:
 - Translates human hand configurations into appropriate robot gripper orientations

5. **Grasp Pose Generation**:
 - Creates executable 7-DOF grasp poses for the robot

This modular design allows GAT-Grasp to leverage human intuition while maintaining the flexibility to adapt to new objects and tasks.

### 4. Pointing Gesture for Target Localization

The first stage of GAT-Grasp uses a pointing gesture to identify the target object region. This process involves:

1. **Hand Keypoint Detection**: Using WiLoR to generate 3D hand keypoints from the pointing gesture.

2. **Gesture Refinement**: Applying stereo matching to improve the accuracy of the detected points.

3. **3D Line Projection**: Projecting a line from the pointing gesture to intersect with the depth map of the scene.

4. **Outlier Removal**: Using RANSAC to remove outliers and improve robustness of the estimation.

The pointing gesture provides an initial spatial prior that narrows down the search space for the subsequent affordance retrieval process. This is particularly valuable in cluttered environments where multiple objects may be present.

### 5. Grasp Gesture Retrieval and Affordance Transfer

Once the target region is identified, GAT-Grasp uses a grasp gesture to retrieve relevant affordance information from the memory bank:

The retrieval process follows a two-stage hierarchical strategy:

1. **Gesture Alignment**: Aligns the query (input) gesture with stored gestures in the memory bank using anatomical landmarks (wrist, index finger, pinky finger).

2. **Object Retrieval**: Identifies the most contextually relevant object affordance by comparing the target image with source images in the memory bank using CLIP embeddings.

After retrieving the most similar instance, GAT-Grasp transfers the affordance information from the source to the target object using dense feature maps extracted by visual foundation models (Diffusion Features - DIFT). This transfer mechanism allows the system to generalize to novel objects by leveraging similarities in how humans interact with objects of similar function or shape.

The figure shows examples of successful retrieval (green checkmarks) and unsuccessful attempts (red warning signs), demonstrating how the system selects appropriate grasping affordances based on gesture similarity and object context.

### 6. Hand-Gripper Mapping

A crucial component of GAT-Grasp is the ability to translate human hand configurations into appropriate robot gripper orientations:

This mapping is accomplished by:

1. Defining a gripper-aligned coordinate system using three key points on the human hand:
 - Thumb
 - Index finger proximal phalanx
 - Index fingertip

2. Computing a rotation matrix based on this coordinate system that orients the robot gripper in a way that mimics the human grasp intention.

This transformation ensures that the robot not only grasps at the correct location but also with the appropriate orientation for the specific task. For example, grasping a cup by the handle requires a different gripper orientation than grasping it from the top.

The mathematical formulation for this mapping can be expressed as:

$$R = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{x} & \vec{y} & \vec{z} \end{bmatrix}$$

where $\vec{x}$, $\vec{y}$, and $\vec{z}$ are orthogonal unit vectors derived from the hand keypoints that define the desired gripper orientation.

### 7. Experimental Results

GAT-Grasp was evaluated on a range of objects in both seen and unseen scenarios, demonstrating its effectiveness in real-world settings:

The experiments showed that GAT-Grasp:

1. **Outperforms existing approaches**: Achieves higher success rates compared to language-based systems (GPT-4o) and other retrieval-based methods (RAM).

2. **Generalizes to novel objects**: Maintains reasonable performance on previously unseen objects, highlighting the effectiveness of the affordance transfer mechanism.

3. **Handles cluttered environments**: Successfully identifies and grasps target objects even in complex scenes with multiple potential targets.

4. **Adapts to task-specific requirements**: Produces grasp configurations that are appropriate for the intended task, not just stable grasps.

The quantitative results, as shown below, demonstrate the superiority of GAT-Grasp over existing methods across both seen and unseen object categories:

On seen objects, GAT-Grasp achieved an average success rate of 70%, significantly outperforming GPT-4o (48%) and RAM (40%). For unseen objects, while performance decreased as expected, GAT-Grasp still maintained a 40% success rate, compared to 33% for GPT-4o and 20% for RAM.

### 8. Advantages Over Existing Approaches

GAT-Grasp offers several key advantages over existing robotic grasping systems:

1. **Intuitive Interface**: Gestures provide a more natural and precise way to communicate grasping intentions compared to language instructions.

2. **Zero-Shot Generalization**: The retrieval-based approach enables adaptation to novel objects without requiring extensive training data.

3. **Task Specificity**: By capturing both "where" and "how" to grasp through the two-stage gesture interface, GAT-Grasp produces grasps that are appropriate for specific tasks.

4. **No Dependency on Object Priors**: Unlike many existing approaches, GAT-Grasp does not require pre-given object models or extensive object-specific training.

5. **Robustness to Clutter**: The pointing gesture helps disambiguate target objects in cluttered environments, improving performance in real-world scenarios.

### 9. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its advantages, GAT-Grasp has several limitations that suggest directions for future research:

1. **Memory Bank Coverage**: The system's performance depends on the diversity and comprehensiveness of the affordance memory bank. Expanding this database could further improve generalization capabilities.

2. **Hand Tracking Accuracy**: The approach relies on accurate hand tracking, which can be challenging in certain lighting conditions or with occlusions.

3. **Single-Hand Gestures**: The current implementation only supports single-hand gestures, limiting the complexity of the grasping strategies that can be communicated.

4. **Bimanual Manipulation**: Extending the approach to bimanual tasks would require significant modifications to both the gesture interface and affordance transfer mechanism.

Future work could explore integrating language and gesture for more comprehensive communication, incorporating tactile feedback for adaptive grasping, and expanding to more complex manipulation tasks beyond simple grasping.

### 10. Conclusion

GAT-Grasp represents a significant advancement in task-aware robotic grasping by leveraging the intuitive nature of human gestures and the power of affordance transfer from human demonstrations. By using a two-stage gesture interface and a retrieval-based learning approach, the system enables robots to perform precise, task-specific grasps on both familiar and novel objects.

The system demonstrates how human-robot interaction can be made more natural and efficient through gesture-based communication, while the affordance transfer mechanism provides a solution to the challenge of generalizing grasping skills to new objects and environments.

As robotic systems continue to move from structured industrial environments to human-centric settings like homes and healthcare facilities, approaches like GAT-Grasp that enable intuitive interaction and robust task execution will be increasingly valuable for creating helpful and adaptable robotic assistants.
