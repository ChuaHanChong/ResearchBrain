# Non-conflicting Energy Minimization in Reinforcement Learning based Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> PEGrad is a hyperparameter-free method for robot control that enables energy minimization without compromising task performance. It achieves this by prioritizing task success and applying energy reduction gradients only when they do not conflict with the primary objective, resulting in up to a 64% average energy reduction in simulation and tangible real-world efficiency gains on a Unitree Go2 robot.

## Problem

- Untethered robots face limitations due to battery capacity, necessitating control policies that maximize operational duration through energy efficiency.
- Conventional Reinforcement Learning approaches incorporate energy expenditure as a weighted penalty in the reward function, requiring careful and often task-specific tuning of a 'λ' hyperparameter.
- Incorrect tuning of the 'λ' parameter can lead to suboptimal policies, either by sacrificing task performance for energy savings or by failing to achieve meaningful energy reduction.

## Method

- The problem is framed as a ==Multi-Objective Markov Decision Process (MOMDP)==, with a vector-valued reward for both task success and energy consumption.
- The **Projecting Energy Gradients (PEGrad)** method is introduced, which computes separate ==policy gradients== for task reward and energy minimization objectives.
- **PEGrad** ensures non-conflicting optimization by projecting the energy minimization gradient onto the ==orthogonal subspace== of the task reward gradient, adaptively scaling it to avoid degrading task performance.

## Results

- Policies trained with PEGrad achieved an average **64%** reduction in energy consumption across simulation benchmarks (DM-Control, HumanoidBench) compared to baseline policies, without compromising task performance.
- On a real-world Unitree Go2 quadruped, PEGrad produced walking controllers that were approximately **19.74%** more efficient in current draw and **15.8%** more efficient in applied torque than a manually tuned baseline, while successfully completing tasks.
- PEGrad often yielded policies that either consumed less energy for the same task performance or achieved higher performance for the same energy level, surpassing the Pareto front established by fixed 'λ' reward weighting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Energy minimization can be effectively pursued as a subordinate objective to task performance by manipulating policy gradients to ensure no conflict.
> - Orthogonally projecting the energy minimization gradient onto the task reward gradient's null space allows for energy reduction without detrimentally affecting task achievement.
> - An adaptive scaling mechanism for the projected energy gradient is crucial to prevent energy optimization from dominating and compromising the primary task.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Untethered robotic systems are inherently constrained by their battery capacity, which dictates their operational duration. Maximizing the operational time of such robots necessitates the development of control policies that minimize energy consumption while concurrently ensuring the successful completion of assigned tasks. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a prominent methodology for developing complex and dynamic robot control policies, particularly for legged robots.

A common strategy in RL to promote energy efficiency involves incorporating energy expenditure as a penalty within the reward function. This typically takes the form `r = r_task + λ r_energy`, where `r_task` represents the task-specific reward, `r_energy` is an energy penalty, and `λ` is a weighting coefficient. This approach often introduces a critical challenge: the `λ` hyperparameter requires careful tuning. Its optimal value can vary significantly across different robot embodiments and tasks, leading to a complex trade-off where an improperly tuned `λ` might cause energy minimization to detrimentally affect task success, or, conversely, fail to enforce meaningful energy savings.

This tuning problem is situated within the broader field of multi-objective optimization (MOO), where several objectives, often conflicting, need to be optimized simultaneously. Existing MOO methods in deep learning and RL have explored various approaches to manage conflicting gradients and balance objective magnitudes. Techniques like GradNorm aim to balance gradient magnitudes, while PCGrad modifies gradients by projecting them onto orthogonal subspaces, typically without an inherent priority ordering among objectives. Other methods like CAGrad and GradDrop also exist, often involving hyperparameters or specific assumptions about objective relationships.

Previous research in energy optimization for legged robots has included methods such as training surrogate models for energy prediction in the real world to fine-tune policies, or explicitly minimizing mechanical work alongside task rewards, often relying on manual tuning of reward weights. Approaches like Mahankali et al. (2024) formulate the energy-performance trade-off as a constrained optimization problem, maintaining two separate policies to keep their task rewards close, and introducing a learnable parameter for dynamic adjustment.

This paper distinguishes itself by proposing a hyperparameter-free method that explicitly establishes a priority ordering: task performance is paramount, and energy minimization is pursued only to the extent that it does not conflict with or degrade task success. This approach offers an alternative to the laborious manual tuning of `λ` and other adaptive mechanisms that often require additional learnable parameters or iterative refinement loops. By drawing inspiration from multitask learning, the work seeks to provide a principled, integrated solution for balancing these objectives within standard RL pipelines.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for learning robot control policies that can achieve high task performance while simultaneously minimizing energy expenditure, without requiring manual tuning of a trade-off parameter. The motivation for this objective stems from several practical and theoretical considerations:

1. **Addressing Battery Life Constraints:** Untethered robots, such as quadrupedal systems, are limited by their on-board battery capacity. Factory controllers often provide only limited operational time (e.g., 1-4 hours for a Unitree Go2). Extending deployment duration is a critical practical goal for real-world robotic applications. This requires control policies that are intrinsically energy-efficient.

2. **Overcoming Hyperparameter Tuning Challenges:** The standard approach in RL for energy minimization involves adding a weighted energy penalty to the reward function (`r = r_task + λ r_energy`). The `λ` coefficient is difficult to tune because the optimal balance between task performance and energy minimization varies significantly across different tasks, environments, and robot embodiments. An incorrect `λ` can lead to policies that either consume excessive energy or, conversely, achieve energy efficiency at the cost of task failure or suboptimal performance (e.g., a robot crawling instead of running). The goal is to eliminate this `λ` tuning burden.

3. **Prioritizing Task Success:** The ideal scenario is not to trade off energy for performance, but to expend the *least amount of energy necessary* to successfully complete a task. This implies a hierarchical ordering of objectives where task performance takes precedence, and energy optimization operates within the bounds set by acceptable task performance. Traditional weighted sum reward functions do not inherently enforce such an ordering, often leading to conflicting objectives during optimization.

4. **Enhancing Robot Safety and Longevity:** High-energy behaviors can lead to increased actuator wear, which shortens the robot's lifespan and increases maintenance costs. Excessive or sudden force applications can also pose safety risks to the robot and its operating environment. Policies that are energy-efficient tend to produce smoother, less aggressive motions, potentially enhancing both safety and longevity.

5. **Simplicity and Generalizability:** The researchers aim for a method that is easily implementable within existing RL frameworks, requiring minimal code changes, and is broadly applicable to any policy gradient method. This promotes the practical adoption and widespread use of energy-efficient control. Furthermore, demonstrating effective Sim2Real transfer is a key objective, showing that policies learned in simulation can yield real-world energy savings.

In summary, the key objective is to provide a principled, hyperparameter-free gradient optimization method, named PEGrad, that allows robot control policies to automatically adapt their energy usage to task demands, ensuring task completion while minimizing energy expenditure without detrimental trade-offs.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The authors formulate the problem of learning energy-efficient robot control as a Multi-Objective Markov Decision Process (MOMDP). In this framework, the reward function is vector-valued, specifically 2-dimensional, returning both a task reward `r(s)` and an energy consumption value `e(s)` at a given state `s`. The overarching goal is to minimize energy consumption while strictly avoiding any degradation in task reward.

The proposed method, Projecting Energy Gradients (PEGrad), is designed to be compatible with policy-gradient algorithms. The paper primarily details its application to Soft Actor Critic (SAC) and briefly describes its extension to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in the appendix.

**Preliminaries with Soft Actor Critic (SAC):**
In a standard single-objective SAC setup, a critic network `Q_φ(s, a)` estimates action-values, and a stochastic policy `π_θ(a|s)` is learned. The critic minimizes a loss `L_Q` based on the Bellman error, and the actor minimizes a loss `L_π` to maximize expected returns and policy entropy.
For the multi-objective setting, the authors employ two independent critic networks: `Q_r_φ(s, a)` for task reward and `Q_e_φ_e(s, a)` for energy consumption. These critics are trained independently, each following the standard `L_Q` formulation.

**The PEGrad Approach:**
The core idea behind PEGrad is to treat energy minimization as a *subordinate* objective to reward maximization. Energy should only be minimized if doing so does not interfere with achieving the primary task reward. This is achieved by manipulating the gradients during policy optimization.

1. **Loss Separation:** The actor's objective is separated into two distinct loss components:
 - `L_R(θ)`: The loss related to maximizing the task reward.
 - `L_E(θ)`: The loss related to minimizing energy consumption (defined such that lower energy means lower `Q_e` values, hence `+ Q_e` in the loss to minimize it).

2. **Gradient Calculation:** The gradients for each loss component with respect to the policy parameters `θ` are computed:
 - `g_R = ∇_θ L_R(θ)`
 - `g_E = ∇_θ L_E(θ)`

3. **Orthogonal Projection:** A first-order Taylor approximation suggests that moving the policy parameters `θ` in a direction orthogonal to `g_R` would result in no change to the task reward loss, effectively moving along a level-set of the reward function. PEGrad exploits this by projecting the energy loss gradient `g_E` onto the orthogonal subspace of the task reward gradient `g_R`. This results in `g_E_⊥R`, the component of `g_E` that is orthogonal to `g_R`.
 - The formula for `g_E_⊥R` is given by: `g_E_⊥R = g_E - ((g_R^T g_E) / (g_R^T g_R)) g_R`.

4. **Adaptive Scaling of Energy Gradient:** The total policy update direction `d` is then a combination of the task reward gradient and the projected energy gradient: `d = -αg_R - β g_E_⊥R`.
 Crucially, an adaptive scaling factor `β` is introduced for `g_E_⊥R`:
 - `β = α * min(1, ||g_R||^2 / ||g_E_⊥R||^2)`
 This ensures that the norm of the energy minimization component (`β g_E_⊥R`) does not exceed the norm of the task reward component (`αg_R`). This adaptive scaling prevents energy minimization from overpowering task optimization, even when the projected energy gradient is substantial.

5. **Policy Update:** The final update direction for the policy parameters `∆θ` is `g_R + g_E_⊥R` (after applying the adaptive scaling to `g_E_⊥R`). This combined gradient is then passed to the optimizer.

**Energy Formulation:**
The authors define energy consumption `e(s)` as the sum of absolute torques applied to all actuated motors: `e(s) = ∑_m |τ_m|`. This choice is justified as a proxy for battery current draw, arguing that `|τ|` better reflects current draw regardless of direction, compared to mechanical power (`τ.ω`) which can underestimate current during high-torque, low-motion phases.

**Implementation Details:**
PEGrad requires two backward passes to compute `g_R` and `g_E` separately, slightly increasing computational cost compared to a single-objective setup. However, it represents a relatively small change to existing RL frameworks.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research paper presents empirical evaluations across a range of simulation benchmarks and a real-world Sim2Real transfer experiment.

**Q1: Simulation Experiments (DM-Control and HumanoidBench)**

- **Environments and Baselines:** Experiments were conducted on six tasks from the DM-Control suite (Quadruped-{Walk, Run}, Dog-{Stand, Walk, Run, Trot}) and four locomotion tasks from HumanoidBench (h1-{Stand, Sit, Walk, Run}). Baselines included a standard RL policy without energy penalty (`Base` or `λ=0`), multi-objective SAC with various fixed `λ` values (from 0.0001 to 0.5) for the energy penalty, and a modified PCGrad+ (which conditionally projects conflicting gradients).
- **Performance Metrics:** Results were analyzed using Pareto fronts (total torque vs. average return), average return over environment steps (sample efficiency), and average absolute torque per step over environment steps (energy efficiency).
- **Key Findings - DM-Control Suite:**
 - PEGrad consistently produced policies that were both highly energy-efficient and maintained task performance comparable to the unconstrained `Base` policy (`λ=0`).
 - For 4 out of 6 tasks, PEGrad achieved results that went beyond the Pareto front established by tuning the `λ` hyperparameter in the multi-objective baseline. This indicates that PEGrad could achieve lower energy consumption for a given task performance level, or higher performance for a given energy level, compared to the best `λ`-tuned policies.
 - PCGrad+ generally led to suboptimal policies on these tasks, sacrificing significant task returns for marginal or no gains in energy efficiency.
- **Key Findings - HumanoidBench:**
 - PEGrad demonstrated a similar ability to achieve low-energy and high-performing policies.
 - Notably, for tasks like `h1-run-v0` and `h1-walk-v0`, PEGrad policies also exhibited improvements in sample efficiency, converging to high returns faster than the `Base` policy. This suggests that energy minimization, when applied non-conflictually, can sometimes aid learning.
 - PCGrad+ performed poorly on HumanoidBench tasks, often leading to policies that barely achieved any return, essentially collapsing to minimal actuation. The authors attribute this to PCGrad+'s lack of adaptive gradient scaling, which allows energy minimization to dominate.
- **Overall Simulation Efficiency:** PEGrad achieved an average reduction of 64% in energy usage across the benchmarks compared to a vanilla RL policy (`λ=0`), while maintaining comparable task performance.

**Q2: Sim2Real on Unitree Go2 Quadruped**

- **Experimental Setup:** A Standing and Walking (SaW) controller was trained for the Unitree Go2 using PPO in IsaacLab, incorporating the Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) framework for natural gaits and standard domain randomization. Policies were deployed on a real Unitree Go2 robot.
- **Baselines:** The policies were compared against the factory-issued Go2 controller (`Factory`), an AMP+PPO policy without energy penalty (`AMP+PPO λ=0`), and a manually tuned AMP+PPO policy with an energy penalty (`AMP+PPO λ=0.0002`).
- **Metrics:** Real-world current drawn (mA) and net absolute torque applied (Nm) were measured for standing (0 m/s velocity for 20s) and walking (0.5 m/s velocity over 12 feet).
- **Key Findings - Real-World Performance:**
 - For the standing task, PEGrad achieved comparable or slightly better energy efficiency (current draw and torque) compared to the tuned `AMP+PPO λ=0.0002` baseline, both outperforming the `Factory` controller.
 - For the walking task, the `AMP+PPO λ=0` policy failed to complete the distance safely, demonstrating unsafe behaviors and thus was excluded from evaluation.
 - PEGrad showed significant energy efficiency gains over the tuned `AMP+PPO λ=0.0002` baseline for the walking task, being approximately 19.74% more efficient in current usage and 15.8% more efficient in net torques applied, while maintaining task performance.
 - Further experiments on outdoor concrete and grass terrains (Appendix 8.6) showed PEGrad maintaining its superior energy efficiency, outperforming the tuned `AMP+PPO λ=0.0002` baseline by ~24.5% on concrete and ~7.68% on grass for the walking task.
- **Computational Cost:** PEGrad incurred a minimal overhead during training, with execution times comparable to baselines (e.g., ~4.5 hours for 10k iterations on an RTX 4090 GPU, ~62k steps per second).

**Energy Formulation Robustness (Appendix 8.5):**
The efficacy of PEGrad was also demonstrated using mechanical power (τ.ω) as the energy formulation instead of absolute torques, showing consistent energy-efficient and task-performant policies.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research contributes a significant advancement to the field of robotic control and reinforcement learning, particularly in addressing the practical challenges of deploying untethered robots. The significance and potential impact of PEGrad can be summarized as follows:

1. **Principled Energy Minimization without Trade-offs:** The most substantial impact is the provision of a principled method to minimize energy expenditure without requiring an explicit trade-off with task performance. By prioritizing task success and projecting energy gradients orthogonally to task gradients, PEGrad offers a solution where energy savings are achieved only when they do not compromise the primary objective. This moves beyond the limitations of traditional reward shaping with sensitive weighting hyperparameters (`λ`).

2. **Hyperparameter-Free Operation:** The elimination of the critical `λ` hyperparameter tuning is a major practical benefit. This simplifies the development process for RL-based robot control policies, reduces development time, and makes policies more robust across different tasks and robot embodiments, as developers no longer need to manually search for optimal `λ` values for each specific scenario.

3. **Enhanced Robot Endurance and Longevity:** The demonstrated significant reductions in energy usage (up to 64% in simulation, and substantial real-world current draw reductions) directly translate to increased operational time for untethered robots. This extends the utility of robots in applications such as exploration, surveillance, delivery, and industrial inspection. Furthermore, lower energy expenditure often correlates with reduced mechanical stress and wear on actuators, potentially prolonging the robot's lifespan and reducing maintenance costs.

4. **Improved Sample Efficiency:** The observation that energy minimization, when applied non-conflictually, can sometimes improve sample efficiency (as seen in HumanoidBench tasks) suggests a positive interaction where the structured optimization guidance provided by PEGrad helps the agent learn faster or more effectively.

5. **Robust Sim2Real Transfer:** The successful Sim2Real transfer of energy-efficient policies to a real Unitree Go2 quadruped robot, showing tangible reductions in current draw and torque on various terrains, highlights the practical applicability and robustness of PEGrad. This is a critical validation step for any robotic control method developed in simulation.

6. **Generalizability Across RL Algorithms:** The method's applicability to common policy gradient algorithms like SAC and PPO indicates its broad potential for integration into existing and future RL frameworks, making it a versatile tool for the RL community.

7. **Contribution to Multi-Objective Optimization in RL:** PEGrad offers a novel approach to multi-objective optimization in RL, particularly for scenarios with prioritized objectives. It provides a blueprint for managing conflicting gradients where one objective is strictly subordinate to another, a common scenario in many real-world control problems beyond just energy efficiency.

While the paper acknowledges limitations, such as challenges in combining PEGrad with complex "style rewards" without careful consideration of their interplay, the core contribution of a robust, hyperparameter-free, and non-conflicting energy minimization strategy marks a substantial step forward for practical and efficient robot control.
