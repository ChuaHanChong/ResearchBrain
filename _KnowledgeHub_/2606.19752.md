# Temporal Self-Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Duke University developed Temporal Self-Imitation Learning (TSIL), a reinforcement learning framework that boosts robot manipulation policy performance by incorporating temporal efficiency as a self-supervisory signal. TSIL achieved a final success rate of 0.986 ± 0.022 and reduced successful episode completion time to 0.674 ± 0.148 seconds across 15 Meta-World tasks, outperforming existing baselines in both learning speed and behavioral efficiency.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning policies for long-horizon robot manipulation often achieve high cumulative reward through inefficient interactions, such as prolonged recovery or lingering, leading to brittle solutions.
- Efficient behaviors, once discovered, are often forgotten during subsequent on-policy updates due to distribution shift and optimization instabilities.
- Existing temporal guidance methods (e.g., fixed time limits, per-step penalties) rely on hand-specified preferences and do not leverage emergent temporal efficiency as a dynamic self-supervisory signal.

## Method

- **TSIL** introduces ==configuration-conditioned adaptive temporal targets==, which dynamically refine expectations for task completion time based on the fastest successes observed for each specific configuration.
- The policy's observation space is augmented with elapsed time and the current adaptive temporal target, and terminal success rewards are modified to bonus trajectories completing at or before the target.
- An efficiency-weighted self-imitation mechanism stores only the fastest successful trajectories in a replay buffer and applies a positive-advantage, efficiency-weighted loss to reinforce these behaviors during off-policy updates.

## Results

- TSIL achieved the highest overall performance across 15 Meta-World tasks, with a final success rate of **0.986** ± **0.022** and the fastest successful episode completion time of **0.674** ± **0.148** seconds.
- The framework demonstrated superior learning efficiency, evidenced by the highest Area Under Success Curve (AUC) of **0.692** ± **0.149** and the largest count of successful episodes.
- TSIL significantly improved robustness under unstable training conditions (e.g., policy gradient noise, aggressive PPO clip ratios), acting as an optimization anchor by replaying efficient behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Temporal efficiency itself, specifically the speed of task completion, provides a powerful and scalable self-supervisory signal for refining robot manipulation policies beyond traditional reward shaping.
> - Dynamically updated, configuration-conditioned temporal targets allow policies to progressively improve their efficiency and achieve more direct task completion, surpassing static, human-engineered objectives.
> - Prioritizing and replaying an agent's own *temporally efficient* successes helps stabilize learning, prevents the forgetting of valuable behaviors, and guides optimization towards more robust and compact interaction strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses challenges within the field of reinforcement learning (RL), specifically as applied to long-horizon robot manipulation tasks. A common approach in this domain involves training policies with reward shaping, which combines dense rewards for intermediate progress with sparse rewards for task completion. While this strategy can facilitate exploration and lead to high cumulative reward, the authors identify a limitation: policies may achieve high returns through inefficient interactions. This inefficiency can manifest as prolonged recovery sequences, exploitation of reward-dense intermediate states, or excessive lingering near partially solved configurations, resulting in brittle or behaviorally suboptimal solutions. Crucially, even when efficient behaviors are discovered, they may be forgotten during subsequent on-policy updates due to distribution shift or optimization instabilities.

The broader research landscape includes work on temporal reinforcement learning, which explores how time is incorporated into the decision-making process. Existing methods in this area often involve fixed time limits, per-step penalties, or manually defined reward schedules to encourage shorter, more efficient behaviors. However, the authors argue that these approaches typically rely on hand-specified temporal preferences and do not leverage the temporal structure of successful behavior *emerging during learning* as a reusable source of supervision. Similarly, self-imitation learning (SIL) leverages an agent's own successful experiences for training. Traditional SIL primarily prioritizes high-return trajectories. This work distinguishes itself by arguing that high return alone does not guarantee temporal efficiency, thus advocating for a refined approach that specifically targets *temporally efficient* successes.

This paper positions itself by proposing that temporal efficiency itself provides an underutilized self-supervisory signal. It aims to bridge the gap between achieving task success and achieving *efficient* task success by integrating this temporal information directly into the learning process, moving beyond manually engineered temporal objectives alone.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from an observed discrepancy between biological motor learning and current reinforcement learning paradigms for robot manipulation. In biological systems, motor learning refines movements towards greater efficiency and reliability, suppressing unnecessary motion and discovering direct strategies for goal achievement. In contrast, current RL methods for robot manipulation primarily evaluate successful trajectories based on cumulative reward.

The authors highlight several problems arising from this reward-centric evaluation in long-horizon manipulation:
- **Mismatch between reward and efficiency:** A high cumulative reward does not necessarily equate to efficient task-solving behavior. Policies might exploit dense intermediate rewards, leading to behaviors such as lingering, or lengthy, unstable recovery sequences.
- **Practical utility:** In practical robot deployment, a robot that completes a task quickly and decisively is often more useful than one that succeeds after prolonged interaction. This underscores that temporal efficiency is not merely a deployment preference but a critical aspect of behavioral quality.
- **Information loss:** Fast successful trajectories contain valuable information regarding direct and coherent interaction sequences that reward signals alone may fail to isolate. These rare, efficient behaviors, if discovered, are susceptible to being forgotten as the policy undergoes further on-policy updates and distribution shifts.
- **Limitations of existing temporal guidance:** Current methods to encourage efficiency, such as increasing sparse success rewards or applying per-step penalties, often prove brittle. Large success rewards can weaken dense guidance and increase advantage-estimation variance, while strong per-step penalties can suppress exploration. Manually specified time limits or dense-to-sparse reward schedules also require human engineering and may not adapt to emergent efficiencies.

Based on these observations, the authors formulated their central hypothesis: **temporally efficient successful behavior discovered during learning contains reusable self-supervisory signals for future policy improvement.** They posit that once a policy learns an efficient solution for a specific task configuration, subsequent learning should be biased towards matching or exceeding this discovered efficiency, thereby allowing the policy itself to progressively refine its temporal targets.

The key objectives of this work were to:
1. Identify and formalize temporal efficiency as a self-supervisory signal for long-horizon robot reinforcement learning, demonstrating how reward alone can be insufficient to distinguish efficient from inefficient successful behavior.
2. Introduce Temporal Self-Imitation Learning (TSIL), a novel reinforcement learning framework designed to leverage this temporal efficiency.
3. Demonstrate through extensive experimentation that TSIL consistently improves learning efficiency, behavioral efficiency, revisitation of fast successful behaviors, and robustness under various unstable training conditions across a range of manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Temporal Self-Imitation Learning (TSIL) is a reinforcement learning framework that integrates temporal efficiency as a self-supervisory signal to guide policy improvement. It operates on two complementary mechanisms: configuration-conditioned adaptive temporal targets and efficiency-weighted self-imitation.

#### 3.1. Temporal Target Conditioning

The foundation of TSIL is the explicit treatment of task completion time as a critical learning signal. Unlike traditional infinite-horizon discounted objectives, which only weakly favor shorter trajectories, TSIL introduces dynamic temporal targets.
- **Configuration-conditioned Targets:** Recognizing that optimal completion times can vary significantly across different initial task configurations (e.g., object initial pose, robot start state), TSIL maintains a distinct adaptive temporal target, `D(ϕ)`, for each task configuration `ϕ`. This target represents the fastest successful completion time discovered for that specific configuration so far. Initially, `D(ϕ)` is set to the maximum allowed rollout duration (`T_max`).
- **Observation Augmentation:** The policy's observation space is augmented to include temporal information. At each time step `t`, the policy receives `˜o_t = (o_t, T_used_t, D(ϕ))`, where `o_t` is the original observation, `T_used_t` is the elapsed interaction time, and `D(ϕ)` is the current adaptive temporal target for the given configuration.
- **Adaptive Target Update:** After each training iteration, `D(ϕ)` is updated by comparing its current value with the completion times of all successful trajectories collected during that iteration for configuration `ϕ`. The target is then tightened to the minimum of its current value and the fastest successful completion time observed: `D_i+1(ϕ) = min(D_i(ϕ), min_{τ ∈ T_i(ϕ) | succ(τ)=1} T_succ(τ))`. This ensures that targets progressively become stricter as more efficient solutions are discovered.
- **Target-Conditioned Reward Shaping:** The terminal success reward is modified to incorporate the temporal target. For a successful trajectory completing at step `K` with elapsed time `T_used`, the success reward `r_succ_K` is scaled by a bonus term: `r_succ * 1_succ * (1 + min(D_i(ϕ) / T_used, 1))`. This mechanism preserves the task-completion incentive while providing an additional bonus for trajectories that finish at or before the current temporal target, and a reduced bonus for slower ones. The temporal target, however, does not impose a hard cutoff; rollouts can continue beyond `D(ϕ)`.

#### 3.2. Efficiency-Weighted Self-Imitation

To counter the problem of efficient behaviors being forgotten due to on-policy distribution shifts, TSIL employs an auxiliary replay mechanism that explicitly preserves and reuses these trajectories.
- **Fast-Success Replay Buffer:** For each configuration `ϕ`, TSIL maintains a small replay buffer `B_i(ϕ)` that stores the `k` (typically `k=5`) fastest successful trajectories discovered so far. This prioritizes temporal efficiency over generic high-return, recognizing that high return does not guarantee fast completion.
- **Relabeling for Replay:** Before replay, the stored trajectories in `B_i(ϕ)` are "relabeled" based on the current adaptive temporal target `D_i(ϕ)`. This involves updating the observation (`ˆo_τ_t`) to include `D_i(ϕ)` and recomputing the terminal success reward (`ˆr_succ_K`) and consequently the returns (`ˆG_τ_t`) using the current target.
- **Self-Imitation Loss:** The core self-imitation objective (L_SI) encourages the policy to increase the likelihood of actions taken in these efficient, stored trajectories. This loss is computed as:
 `L_SI = E_{(τ,t)∼ˆB_i} [-w_i(τ)A^+_t(τ) log π_θ(a_τ_t | ˆo_τ_t) + (λ_V,SI/2) A^+_t(τ)^2]`.
 - `A^+_t(τ) = [ˆG_τ_t - V_ψ(ˆo_τ_t)]^+`: This is a positive advantage filter, meaning only transitions where the relabeled return `ˆG_τ_t` is greater than the current critic's value estimate `V_ψ(ˆo_τ_t)` contribute to the policy update. This acts as a soft gate, focusing replay on behaviors that outperform the current policy's expectation.
 - `w_i(τ) = 1 + 1_succ(τ) min(D_i(ϕ_τ) / T_succ(τ), 1)`: This weighting function further prioritizes trajectories based on their temporal efficiency relative to the current target, reinforcing faster successes more strongly.
- **Overall Objective:** TSIL integrates this self-imitation loss as an auxiliary term to the standard PPO objective: `L = L_PPO + λ_SI L_SI`. This means the policy is updated using both on-policy PPO gradients and the off-policy gradients from the efficiency-weighted self-imitation.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

TSIL is implemented on top of the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. Training occurs in parallel simulation environments, with each environment maintaining its own task configuration, adaptive temporal target, and replay buffer. To stabilize early training phases when successful trajectories are rare, the replay buffer can be supplemented with high-return trajectories (a fallback mechanism). Replay sampling is performed at the trajectory level for efficient behaviors, ensuring full sequences are revisited. The approach uses standard actor-critic neural network architectures and a consistent normalization pipeline for observations, rewards, and advantages across all methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of TSIL was evaluated across 15 distinct long-horizon manipulation tasks from the Meta-World benchmark, implemented in Isaac Gym via MTBench. The evaluation compared TSIL against several baselines: standard infinite-horizon PPO (IH), IH with per-step penalties (Step-cost IH), dense-to-sparse reward scheduling (D2S IH), fixed temporal target learning (FTTL), adaptive temporal target learning without replay (ATTL), and ATTL with standard self-imitation learning (ATTL + SIL). Metrics included success rate, learning efficiency (Area Under Success Curve (AUC), steps-to-80% success, successful episode count), and behavioral efficiency (successful episode completion time). Robustness was also assessed under disturbed training conditions, including policy gradient noise, dense reward dropout, and sweeps of PPO clip ratio and learning rate.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness, Learning Efficiency, and Behavioral Efficiency

- **Overall Performance:** TSIL achieved the highest overall performance in terms of final success rate (0.986 ± 0.022), learning efficiency (AUC of 0.692 ± 0.149, highest successful episode count), and behavioral efficiency (fastest successful episode completion time of 0.674 ± 0.148 seconds). These results suggest that temporal self-supervision contributes to improved policy performance across these dimensions.
- **Component Contributions:**
 - Basic reward engineering baselines (IH, Step-cost IH, D2S IH) generally underperformed compared to methods incorporating temporal targets.
 - FTTL, which introduced temporal conditioning with a fixed maximum episode length as a target, achieved high final success but resulted in slower successful behaviors, indicating that fixed temporal constraints are less effective at encouraging efficiency.
 - ATTL, by introducing adaptive temporal targets, substantially improved behavioral efficiency, with successful completion times notably faster than FTTL. This indicates the benefit of dynamically refining temporal expectations.
 - ATTL + SIL, which added generic self-imitation replay to ATTL, further accelerated early learning. However, replaying only high-return trajectories could still preserve slower successful behaviors.
 - TSIL, with its efficiency-weighted replay of *fast successful trajectories*, consistently demonstrated the strongest balance between success rate, learning speed, and behavioral efficiency, suggesting that prioritizing and replaying temporally efficient experiences is beneficial.

#### 4.2. Robustness Under Unstable Training

- **Improved Robustness:** Replay-based methods (ATTL + SIL and TSIL) showed enhanced robustness compared to ATTL alone, particularly under conditions of policy gradient noise and aggressive PPO clip ratios, where policies are prone to drift. TSIL achieved the strongest overall performance under these unstable conditions.
- **Efficiency-Weighted Replay Advantage:** While ATTL + SIL sometimes yielded slightly higher success rates in scenarios like dense reward dropout (possibly due to replaying a broader range of high-return experiences when fast successes were scarce), TSIL consistently produced *faster successful behaviors* and stronger *behavioral efficiency*. This suggests that TSIL's focus on efficient trajectories guides optimization towards more compact and reliable interaction strategies, rather than merely stabilizing generic successful behavior. The findings imply that preserving and replaying temporally efficient experiences acts as a stabilizing anchor against policy drift.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Mechanisms

- **Adaptive Temporal Targets:** Analysis of positive-advantage mass (which indicates where PPO's reinforcement pressure concentrates) showed that adaptive temporal targets (ATTL) rapidly directed PPO updates towards fast successful trajectories. This mechanism effectively suppressed optimization pressure from reward-distracted or slow-failure behaviors. In contrast, fixed temporal targets (FTTL) did not effectively distinguish between truly efficient behavior and generic timing constraints, allowing slow high-return trajectories to continue receiving positive updates.
- **Efficiency-Weighted Replay:** Replay-based methods reduced the "replay revisitation NLL" (negative log-likelihood), indicating that stored behaviors became easier for the current policy to revisit. TSIL specifically maintained the lowest "buffer time" (mean completion time of stored successful trajectories), confirming that it successfully preserved the fastest observed successes. Comparison of optimization landscapes showed that both generic SIL and TSIL rotated PPO updates towards stored successful behaviors. However, TSIL demonstrated a stronger alignment of updates with regions of high *fast-success memory likelihood*, whereas generic replay (driven by return) exhibited weaker alignment with temporal efficiency. This suggests that efficiency-weighted replay not only helps in preserving successful behaviors but also guides policy optimization toward more efficient actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Temporal Self-Imitation Learning" introduces a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for robot manipulation by formalizing and practically leveraging temporal efficiency as a self-supervisory signal. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating that the intrinsic temporal structure of successful actions, specifically the speed of task completion, provides a powerful and scalable learning cue that extends beyond traditional reward shaping.

The potential impact of TSIL is multifaceted:
- **Improved Robot Performance:** By explicitly optimizing for temporal efficiency, TSIL leads to robot policies that are not only capable of completing tasks but do so more quickly and decisively. This translates to more practical and "useful" robots in real-world applications where speed and directness of action are valuable attributes.
- **Enhanced Learning Efficiency:** The framework consistently improves the speed at which policies learn complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks (evidenced by metrics like AUC and successful episode count). This can reduce the substantial computational resources and data requirements typically associated with training advanced robot skills.
- **Increased Robustness:** TSIL's ability to preserve and replay efficient behaviors acts as an optimization anchor, improving policy robustness against common training instabilities such as noisy gradients or aggressive hyperparameters. This makes the learning process more reliable and less susceptible to performance degradation.
- **New Self-Supervision Paradigm:** The core idea of mining self-generated efficient experiences for progressive refinement offers a generalizable paradigm for RL. It suggests that agents can extract high-quality learning signals directly from their own interactions by evaluating not just "what" was achieved, but "how efficiently" it was achieved. This could inspire similar approaches leveraging other intrinsic behavioral qualities (e.g., smoothness, energy consumption) as self-supervisory signals.
- **Addressing Reward Shaping Limitations:** The work highlights and offers a solution to the limitations of dense reward shaping, which, while beneficial for exploration, can inadvertently encourage inefficient behaviors. TSIL provides a mechanism to refine these behaviors towards efficiency without requiring complex manual reward engineering.

**Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**
The authors identify several limitations of the current TSIL framework:
- **Initial Exploration:** TSIL primarily focuses on improving exploitation and consolidation of behaviors *after* successful manipulation trajectories have been discovered. It does not directly address the initial exploration challenge of finding rare successes in complex tasks.
- **Scope of Behavioral Signal:** Temporal efficiency is the sole behavioral signal leveraged. For diverse deployment scenarios requiring other qualities like safety, smoothness, or stability, TSIL might need to be combined with additional metrics or objectives.
- **Memory Footprint:** The method requires storing successful trajectories in a replay buffer. While a small fixed-size buffer was used, more memory-efficient replay strategies or data compression techniques could be beneficial for scaling to even more complex or numerous tasks.
