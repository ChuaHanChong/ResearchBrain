# Sample More to Think Less: Group Filtered Policy Optimization for Concise Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Microsoft Research developed Group Filtered Policy Optimization (GFPO), an RL fine-tuning method that addresses response length inflation in large language models by integrating rejection sampling into the training process. This approach enables models to generate substantially shorter and more concise reasoning chains while preserving or improving accuracy, leading to significant inference-time compute savings across various reasoning and coding benchmarks.

## Problem

- Large language models fine-tuned with Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) often suffer from "length inflation," producing excessively verbose responses that do not correlate with improved accuracy.
- This verbosity leads to increased inference-time compute costs, slower response times, and a higher carbon footprint, hindering practical deployment.
- Existing solutions like token-level loss normalization can inadvertently reinforce verbosity for long correct answers or have limited effectiveness in addressing the core issue.

## Method

- ==Group Filtered Policy Optimization (GFPO)== modifies GRPO by sampling a larger group of candidate responses during training and then filtering them based on desirable attributes like brevity or =="token efficiency"== (reward per token ratio).
- Only the top-k most desirable responses from the filtered group are used to compute policy gradients, effectively zeroing out gradients for rejected samples and implicitly guiding the policy towards conciseness.
- An ==adaptive variant of GFPO== dynamically adjusts the number of retained responses (k) based on real-time estimates of question difficulty, allowing for more exploration on harder problems and more aggressive shortening on easier ones.

## Results

- GFPO achieved significant Excess Length Reductions (ELR) across benchmarks, including **70.9%** on AIME 25 and **84.6%** on AIME 24 with Token Efficiency GFPO, while maintaining or improving pass@1 accuracy compared to the GRPO baseline.
- Adaptive Difficulty GFPO delivered superior ELR on **4 out of 5** benchmarks and improved accuracy on the hardest AIME 25 questions (**27%**), demonstrating effective resource allocation for diverse problem difficulties.
- GFPO variants generalized robustly to out-of-distribution tasks like LiveCodeBench, mitigating length inflation (up to **79.7%** ELR) and sometimes even improving accuracy (e.g., Shortest 8/24 GFPO at **59.2%** vs. **56.7%** GRPO).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Strategically increasing compute during the training phase by sampling more responses directly translates to continuous and substantial reductions in inference-time compute, creating more efficient LLMs for deployment.
> - The ratio of retained responses (k/G) acts as a critical control knob to precisely balance the trade-off between response length reduction and reasoning accuracy.
> - GFPO primarily prunes verbosity in the "Solution" and "Verification" phases of reasoning, indicating it targets redundant steps and filler content without compromising core problem-solving logic.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a significant and pervasive challenge in the field of large language models (LLMs), particularly those fine-tuned with Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). While RLVR has been instrumental in enhancing LLM performance on complex reasoning tasks (e.g., AIME, IMO), leading to models like OpenAI's O3 and DeepSeek-R1 achieving unprecedented capabilities, it often comes at the cost of "length inflation." This phenomenon describes LLMs generating excessively verbose responses, frequently replete with "filler" or repetitive text, that do not correlate with improved accuracy. In some cases, longer responses are even found to be less accurate, especially on problems where both correct and incorrect solutions are generated.

Previous research (e.g., Balachandran et al., 2025; Hassid et al., 2025) has empirically documented this length explosion, showing that models can generate responses several times longer than necessary for similar accuracy levels. This issue is not merely aesthetic; it leads to increased inference-time compute costs, slower response times, and higher carbon footprints, hindering the practical deployment and scalability of advanced reasoning models.

Existing approaches to mitigate length inflation include token-level loss normalization (e.g., DAPO, Dr. GRPO), which aims to penalize long incorrect responses more heavily. However, as the authors highlight, these methods can inadvertently amplify rewards for *long correct* chains, thereby reinforcing the inherent verbosity of strong base models that are often extensively Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) for step-by-step reasoning. Other methods involve explicit length-aware penalties in the reward function, dynamic token limits, or inference-time interventions such as early stopping or voting over shortest samples.

GFPO distinguishes itself by integrating rejection sampling directly into the RL training loop. Unlike previous methods that primarily modify loss normalization or add explicit penalties, GFPO implicitly shapes the reward by *selectively training* on a subset of sampled responses based on desired attributes (like brevity or token efficiency). This approach offers a more fundamental way to steer the policy towards concise reasoning without relying solely on complex reward engineering or being limited by the inherent trade-offs of loss normalization. By demonstrating that increased *training-time* compute can lead to reduced *inference-time* compute, GFPO positions itself as a strategic solution that optimizes for long-term operational efficiency. This work thus contributes a novel and effective mechanism to the ongoing efforts to make LLMs not only powerful but also practically efficient and sustainable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the persistent and problematic "length inflation" observed in large language models fine-tuned with Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). While RLVR methods like GRPO have significantly advanced LLM reasoning capabilities, they inadvertently encourage models to generate verbose responses, often filled with unnecessary "filler" text, without a corresponding increase in accuracy. The authors empirically observe this rapid response length growth (e.g., 4k to 14k tokens in 100 GRPO training steps on Phi-4-reasoning-plus) and cite prior work demonstrating that longer responses can even be *less* accurate for the same problems. They also highlight the limitations of existing solutions, such as token-level loss normalization, which, while penalizing long incorrect answers, can unintentionally reinforce verbosity by rewarding long correct chains.

Against this backdrop, the **key objective** of this paper is to develop "efficient reasoning models" – models that can maintain the high reasoning accuracy afforded by GRPO while producing substantially shorter and more concise reasoning chains.

To achieve this overarching goal, the authors set forth several specific contributions:

- **Introduce Group Filtered Policy Optimization (GFPO):** The primary objective is to propose GFPO, a novel variant of GRPO that explicitly counteracts response length inflation by integrating rejection sampling. This involves sampling a larger group of candidate responses during training, filtering them based on desirable attributes (primarily length), and only learning from the policy gradients of the top-k retained chains.
- **Leverage "Token Efficiency":** Beyond simple length reduction, an objective is to define and optimize for "token efficiency" (reward per token ratio) using GFPO. This metric aims to encourage responses that justify their length with proportionally higher rewards, leading to more succinct and effective reasoning.
- **Develop Adaptive Difficulty GFPO:** A further objective is to introduce an adaptive variant of GFPO that dynamically adjusts the number of retained responses based on real-time estimates of question difficulty. This aims to optimize computational efficiency and accuracy by allocating more training resources (exploration) to harder problems and aggressively shortening easier ones.
- **Demonstrate Generalization:** The work also aims to show that GFPO's benefits (accuracy preservation and length reduction) extend to out-of-distribution tasks, ensuring broader applicability.
- **Provide Detailed Analysis:** A crucial objective is to thoroughly analyze GFPO's impact on accuracy and length reductions across different difficulty levels and to investigate precisely which parts of the reasoning traces GFPO is effectively trimming.
- **Exploit Training-time vs. Inference-time Trade-off:** Underlying these objectives is the motivation to demonstrate a fundamental and advantageous trade-off: strategically increased compute during training (by sampling more responses) directly translates to continuous and significant savings in inference-time compute (due to shorter, more efficient responses). This makes GFPO a compelling solution for practical LLM deployment.

In essence, the motivation is to resolve the efficiency paradox in RLVR-trained LLMs, making them not just capable, but also economically and environmentally sustainable for real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is **Group Filtered Policy Optimization (GFPO)**, a modification of the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm. GRPO, like PPO, optimizes a clipped surrogate objective, but it simplifies advantage estimation by using the average reward of multiple sampled responses per question as a baseline, eliminating the need for a separate value model.

GFPO introduces a selective learning mechanism by modifying how policy gradients are computed:

1. **Expanded Sampling:** For each question `q`, GFPO samples a larger group `G` of candidate responses `{o_1..., o_G}` from the current policy. This increases the model's exposure to a wider range of potential outputs, including those with desirable traits like brevity.
2. **Filtering based on Metric:** Instead of using all `G` responses for training, GFPO applies a selection step. A user-specified `metric` (e.g., response length, token efficiency) is computed for each response. Responses are then sorted, and only the `k` most desirable responses (the "retained subset" `S ⊆ G`) are selected for learning. Responses not in `S` are effectively rejected.
3. **Modified Advantage Calculation:** For the retained subset `S`, advantages are normalized using the mean (`μ_S`) and standard deviation (`σ_S`) of the *response-level rewards within `S`*. This ensures that within the filtered group, the policy prioritizes responses that yield higher rewards. Crucially, responses *not* in `S` receive zero advantage, meaning they do not influence policy updates. This selective learning acts as an implicit form of reward shaping, guiding the policy towards the desired attributes without complex explicit reward engineering.

The mathematical formulation for GFPO's objective largely mirrors GRPO but incorporates a binary mask `m_i` (where `m_i = 1` if response `o_i` is in `S`, `0` otherwise) into the advantage term (`A^(m)_(i,t)`), effectively zeroing out gradients for rejected samples. The paper notes that it uses DAPO's token-level loss aggregation for GRPO, which is also applied to GFPO for consistency.

GFPO can employ various filtering metrics; this work focuses on two:
- **Response Length:** Selecting the `k` shortest responses to directly encourage brevity.
- **Token Efficiency:** Selecting the `k` responses with the highest reward-to-length ratio, promoting succinctness while ensuring rewards justify length.

A notable extension is **Adaptive Difficulty GFPO**:
- **Dynamic `k` Adjustment:** This variant estimates question difficulty at each training step by computing the average reward of sampled responses for that question (lower average reward implies higher difficulty).
- **Streaming Difficulty Quartiles:** A lightweight t-digest data structure is used to maintain a streaming summary of prompt difficulties and categorize new questions into difficulty buckets (e.g., very hard, hard, medium, easy).
- **Resource Allocation:** Based on the difficulty bucket, the number of retained responses `k` is dynamically adjusted (e.g., 8 for very hard/hard, 6 for medium, 4 for easy questions, out of a total sampled `G=16`). This strategy allows for more exploration (larger `k`) on challenging problems and more aggressive shortening (smaller `k`) on simpler ones.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Base Model:** Phi-4-reasoning (14 billion parameters), fine-tuned with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on `o3-mini` reasoning traces. The GRPO baseline is Phi-4-reasoning-plus, which is Phi-4-reasoning further trained with GRPO.
- **Training Details:** RL training for 100 steps on 72k math problems (6.4k problems seen), global batch size of 64 on 32 H100s, Adam optimizer, KL regularization, and entropy coefficient.
- **Reward Function:** A weighted sum of a "length-aware" 0/1 accuracy reward (penalizing long correct answers) and an n-gram repetition penalty. The authors emphasize that GRPO still inflated length despite this length-aware reward, motivating GFPO.
- **Group Sizes:** GRPO baseline uses `G=8`. GFPO experiments with `G` in {8, 16, 24}, with `k` always `≤8` to ensure a fair comparison regarding the number of responses from which policy gradients are derived.
- **Evaluation:** Benchmarks include AIME 25/24, GPQA, Omni-MATH (mathematical reasoning), and LiveCodeBench (coding, serving as an out-of-distribution test). Metrics reported are pass@1 accuracy, raw response lengths, and Excess Length Reduction (ELR). Statistical significance is assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. An extensive analysis involves segmenting reasoning traces using GPT-4o to understand what parts of responses GFPO trims.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research effectively demonstrates that Group Filtered Policy Optimization (GFPO) can significantly curb response length inflation in RLVR-trained LLMs while maintaining or even improving reasoning accuracy.

1. **Importance of Increased Sampling (G):** Initial experiments with Shortest 6/8 GFPO (subsampling from a small group, G=8) yielded only modest length reductions (1.8-11.5% ELR), and even a slight increase on Omni-MATH. This highlights that simply rejecting responses from a small pool is insufficient; increasing the *total number of sampled responses (G)* during training is crucial to expose the model to enough desirable short chains.

2. **GFPO Enables Efficient Reasoning:** By increasing the sampled group size (e.g., Shortest 8/16 GFPO), significant reductions in excess length were achieved across various benchmarks: 23.8% on AIME 25, 33% on AIME 24, 23.7% on GPQA, and 31.5% on Omni-MATH, all without statistically significant drops in accuracy compared to GRPO. This confirms GFPO's core premise that sampling more during training can lead to thinking less at inference time.

3. **The k/G Ratio as a Control Knob:** Varying the retained group size `k` and sampled group size `G` revealed that the *proportion of retained responses (k/G)* is the critical lever for controlling response length. Lowering `k` or raising `G` further reduced length. For instance, moving from Shortest 8/16 to Shortest 4/16 (decreasing k) yielded additional ELR of 14.2% on AIME 25. Similarly, increasing `G` from 16 to 24 (e.g., Shortest 8/16 to 8/24) provided substantial additional reductions (up to 30.6% more on AIME 25). The study found that retaining 25-33% of responses often yielded optimal results, with aggressive filtering beyond this point showing diminishing returns. Shortest 4/24 emerged as the best length-optimized GFPO variant.

4. **Token Efficiency for Maximal Cuts:** The "Token Efficiency" GFPO, which ranks responses by reward-per-token ratio, proved to be the most effective strategy for length reduction. It delivered the largest ELR across all tasks (70.9% on AIME 25, 84.6% on AIME 24, 79.7% on GPQA, 82.6% on Omni-MATH, 79.7% on LiveCodeBench). While this variant showed slightly higher variance during training and minor, non-statistically significant accuracy degradations, it confirmed that optimizing for truly concise and high-value reasoning is possible.

5. **Adaptive Difficulty GFPO for Strategic Efficiency:** Adaptive Difficulty GFPO dynamically adjusts `k` based on estimated question difficulty. Compared to fixed `k` variants with equivalent compute, it achieved superior ELR on 4 out of 5 benchmarks. Notably, it attained the highest accuracy on GPQA (70.8%) and on the hardest AIME 25 questions (27%), demonstrating its ability to strategically allocate exploration resources to challenging problems while still reducing excess length.

6. **Out-of-Distribution Generalization:** On LiveCodeBench (a coding benchmark not included in RL training), GRPO exhibited significant length inflation without accuracy gains. GFPO variants, however, successfully mitigated this unintended verbosity, with Token Efficiency GFPO achieving 79.7% ELR. Some GFPO variants even yielded modest accuracy improvements on LiveCodeBench (e.g., Shortest 8/24 GFPO at 59.2% vs. 57.7% SFT and 56.7% GRPO), demonstrating robust generalization.

7. **Accuracy-Length Pareto Frontier:** Across most benchmarks, at least one GFPO variant was found to be strictly Pareto-superior to GRPO, achieving both higher accuracy and shorter responses. Even on AIME 25, where GRPO had slightly higher peak accuracy, GFPO variants remained on the Pareto front by offering significant length reductions without statistically significant accuracy losses. This indicates a robust trade-off.

8. **Analysis of Trimming and Difficulty Interaction:**
 - **Length Reduction Across Difficulties:** GFPO consistently shortened responses across all difficulty levels. Token Efficiency GFPO was particularly effective on easier problems, generating responses even shorter than the SFT baseline while maintaining accuracy. Shortest 8/24 GFPO achieved the greatest reductions on very hard questions.
 - **Accuracy Across Difficulties:** Adaptive Difficulty GFPO matched or surpassed GRPO's accuracy on easy, medium, and very hard questions, notably improving on medium problems (90.2% vs. 88.4%) while reducing ELR by 47%.
 - **Accuracy of Long Responses:** Analysis revealed that longer responses were less accurate even when question difficulty was held constant. GFPO variants outperformed GRPO in the lengthiest response quartiles, as their long responses were less verbose and more accurate.
 - **What GFPO Trims:** Qualitative and quantitative analysis using GPT-4o annotations showed that GFPO primarily slashes verbosity in the "Solution" and "Verification" phases of reasoning. For instance, on AIME 25, Shortest 8/24 GFPO cut Solution length by 94.4% and Verification length by 66.7% compared to GRPO's excess length, while leaving "Problem" and "Final" segments largely unchanged. This indicates GFPO effectively targets redundant reasoning steps and "filler" content.
 - **Distribution Shift:** GFPO compressed the long tail of response length distributions, shifting mass towards shorter outputs. For example, the fraction of responses ≥ 20k tokens dropped from 32% (GRPO) to 22-23% (GFPO). GFPO also demonstrated solving harder problems with shorter responses (prompts answered in ≤5k tokens were 9x harder in GFPO than GRPO).

In summary, GFPO provides a powerful, versatile, and empirically validated method to achieve concise yet accurate reasoning in LLMs, directly addressing critical efficiency concerns.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Group Filtered Policy Optimization (GFPO) represents a significant advance in the field of large language model (LLM) fine-tuning, with profound implications for both research and practical deployment.

**Research Significance:**

- **Novel Approach to Length Control:** GFPO offers a simple yet highly effective mechanism to combat the pervasive problem of "length inflation" in RLVR-trained LLMs. Unlike prior methods that rely on complex reward engineering or loss normalization, GFPO's use of rejection sampling directly within the policy optimization loop provides an implicit and flexible form of reward shaping. This demonstrates that modifying the *data used for learning* can be a more powerful lever than solely modifying the loss function or reward signal.
- **Fundamental Trade-off Exploitation:** The work rigorously establishes and exploits a crucial trade-off: increased compute during the *training phase* (by sampling a larger group of responses) directly translates into sustained and significant reductions in *inference-time compute* (due to substantially shorter, high-quality responses). This is a vital insight for the economic and environmental sustainability of large AI models, as training is a one-time cost, while inference savings are continuous.
- **Multi-objective Optimization:** GFPO's flexibility allows for optimization of multiple desirable response attributes beyond just brevity. By introducing and demonstrating the effectiveness of "token efficiency" (reward/length), the paper opens avenues for future work to integrate other criteria like factuality, safety, or diversity into the filtering mechanism, fostering more controllable and aligned LLMs.
- **Adaptive Learning:** The Adaptive Difficulty GFPO variant is a significant methodological contribution, showing how dynamic resource allocation based on real-time problem difficulty can further refine the efficiency-accuracy trade-off. This intelligent curriculum learning approach is highly relevant for training powerful yet resource-aware models.

**Practical Impact:**

- **Enhanced LLM Efficiency:** The most immediate and tangible impact of GFPO is the creation of LLMs that are substantially more efficient at inference time. Shorter responses mean:
 - **Reduced Computational Cost:** Fewer tokens to generate translates directly to lower GPU hours and energy consumption per query, making LLM deployment more affordable and scalable.
 - **Faster Response Times:** Concise outputs improve user experience by delivering answers more quickly.
 - **Lower Carbon Footprint:** By reducing compute, GFPO contributes to more environmentally friendly AI systems.
- **Improved User Experience:** Concise, direct, and high-quality responses are inherently more user-friendly. By trimming "filler" and redundant reasoning, GFPO-trained models produce outputs that are easier to parse and more satisfying for users.
- **Broader Applicability:** The demonstration of GFPO's effectiveness across challenging STEM and coding benchmarks, including out-of-distribution tasks, suggests its wide applicability. This makes it a valuable tool for developers and researchers working on diverse LLM applications where reasoning efficiency is paramount.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** GFPO's modular nature means it can be combined with existing techniques (e.g., advanced length-aware penalties, different GRPO variants, or inference-time stopping criteria) to potentially achieve even greater gains in efficiency and accuracy. It provides a robust framework for further exploration into controlled and efficient LLM generation.

In conclusion, GFPO addresses a critical bottleneck in the real-world deployment of advanced reasoning LLMs. By providing a principled and effective method to generate "more thought, less talk," it paves the way for a new generation of LLMs that are not only intelligent but also lean, fast, and sustainable.
