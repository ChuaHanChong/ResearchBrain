# Olaf: Bringing an Animated Character to Life in the Physical World

> [!summary] Summary
> Disney Research Imagineering developed Olaf, a physical robot embodying the animated character, by integrating a novel asymmetric mechanical design with reinforcement learning. The robot achieves realistic stylized motion, effectively manages actuator temperatures to prevent overheating, and significantly reduces foot impact noise by 13.5 dB.

## Problem

- Physically realizing an animated character like Olaf with non-physical proportions and stylized motion without compromising visual fidelity or stability.
- Concealing all mechanical components within a tight, character-specific form factor while enabling dynamic movements.
- Managing operational challenges such as actuator overheating due to compact design and costume insulation, and reducing distracting mechanical noise from foot impacts.

## Method

- Developed an asymmetric 6-DoF leg design and extensive remote actuation via spherical and planar linkages to fit mechanisms within **Olaf**'s constrained body while achieving character-accurate movements.
- Implemented a two-layered control strategy, primarily using reinforcement learning (PPO) for the robot's backbone (legs, neck) guided by artist-created animation references.
- Integrated novel thermal-aware policies using a ==Control Barrier Function (CBF)== reward to prevent actuator overheating and an impact reduction reward to minimize footstep noise.

## Results

- The robot successfully replicated Olaf's stylized motion, achieving mean absolute joint tracking errors of **3.87**° for standing and **4.02**° for walking over 5-minute trajectories.
- The thermal-aware policy prevented actuator overheating, maintaining the neck-pitch actuator's mean temperature at **77.3**°C during one-hour continuous operation, compared to rapid overheating in baseline policies.
- The foot impact reduction reward decreased footstep noise by **13.5** dB over a 5-minute run on hardware, enhancing character believability without significant impact on gait performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Non-traditional, asymmetric mechanical designs and remote actuation are crucial for embodying animated characters with extreme proportions and tight aesthetic requirements.
> - Reinforcement learning can effectively integrate complex physical constraints like thermal management (via CBF-based rewards) and operational characteristics like noise reduction into control policies.
> - Accurate imitation of stylized animation references, including specific gait characteristics like heel-toe motion, is essential for achieving believability in character robotics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traditional legged robotics has largely focused on functional performance, robustness, and efficiency, exemplified by robots capable of navigating challenging terrains or demonstrating dynamic capabilities. This includes anthropomorphic and zoomorphic designs, with advancements in areas such as energy-efficient gait and multimodal skeletal structures. However, as robots increasingly interact with humans in domains like entertainment and companionship, the engineering objectives expand to include aspects of believability and character fidelity, which impose specific aesthetic and functional constraints.

This work contributes to the evolving field of character robotics, which aims to translate animated characters into physical forms. Previous efforts in this area include the creation of new robotic characters with associated animation and control pipelines, or the robotic representation of existing movie characters that inherently possess robotic attributes. This paper differentiates itself by focusing on the physical realization of an *existing, non-robotic, costumed animated character*, specifically Olaf, whose proportions and motion style are inherently non-physical. This objective necessitates addressing unique challenges in mechanical design and motion control that are not typically encountered in general-purpose legged robots.

In terms of control methodology, the paper builds upon recent advancements in reinforcement learning (RL). RL has demonstrated utility in achieving robust locomotion, facilitating imitation learning from human or animated references, and enabling navigation in complex environments. More recent applications of RL have extended to addressing real-world phenomena such as energy consumption and impact reduction for quieter locomotion. The present work extends these RL applications by introducing novel thermal-aware policies and strategies for significant impact noise reduction, specifically tailored to the constraints of a character robot. The mechanical design also advances existing practices by implementing remote actuation through various linkages and adopting non-traditional, asymmetric leg designs to meet tight spatial and aesthetic requirements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to physically embody Olaf, an animated character, in the real world. This goal is motivated by a series of specific challenges and requirements stemming from the character's unique design and desired behavior:

- **Addressing Non-Physical Proportions:** Olaf's animated design features a large, heavy head, small snowball-shaped feet, and a motion style that deviates from typical biomechanical principles. Translating these characteristics to a physical robot without compromising its visual fidelity or stability presents a fundamental challenge for both mechanical design and motion control.
- **Concealing Mechanical Components:** A critical aesthetic constraint is the requirement to hide all mechanical and actuated components within the robot's tight form factor, particularly to maintain the illusion of Olaf's feet moving freely beneath his body without visible legs. This necessitates compact designs and unconventional actuation strategies.
- **Achieving Believable and Stylized Motion:** The character's lifelike appearance is sensitive to inconsistencies such as rough foot impacts, mechanical noise, or jitter. The control system must produce movements that are not only dynamically stable but also accurately imitate Olaf's stylized, often non-physical, animation references. This includes features like a characteristic heel-toe walk.
- **Managing Thermal Constraints:** The design requires small actuators to support a disproportionately large head within a slim, costume-covered neck. This configuration creates a significant risk of actuator overheating, a problem amplified by the insulating properties of the costume. A key motivation is to develop control strategies that prevent overheating while maintaining performance.
- **Reducing Operational Noise:** Harsh footstep sounds generated by robotic impacts can break the illusion of a living character. Therefore, reducing impact noise is a specific objective to enhance the character's believability and immersive quality.
- **Enforcing Physical Limits:** Due to the compact mechanical design, joint limits and potential self-collisions between parts, particularly the feet, must be actively managed by the control system to prevent damage and maintain character integrity.
- **Enabling Expressive Show Functions:** Beyond basic locomotion, the robot needs to exhibit expressive behaviors through its arms, mouth, and eyes to fully convey the character. This requires designing and controlling these "show functions" within the character's aesthetic envelope.

In summary, the motivation is to create a physically convincing and robust robotic rendition of an animated character by integrating innovative mechanical solutions with advanced, character-specific reinforcement learning control techniques that address aesthetic, dynamic, and operational challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed an iterative workflow encompassing mechanical design and a two-layered control strategy primarily based on reinforcement learning.

**Workflow Overview:**
The development began with the mechanical design of the robot's articulated backbone, including the legs and neck. Concurrently, an animation rig and animation references with corresponding degrees of freedom were maintained. Policies for standing and walking were iteratively trained and evaluated in simulation to explore optimal actuator placement and character expressiveness within the robot's physical envelope. In a subsequent phase, mechanical "show functions" for expressive behaviors—such as arms, mouth, eyes, and eyebrows—were integrated. These show functions were designed not to significantly affect the system's core dynamics.

**Mechatronic Design (Section IV):**
The robotic representation of Olaf stands 88.7 cm tall and weighs 14.9 kg, featuring 25 degrees of freedom (6 per leg, 2 per shoulder, 3 in the neck, 1 in the jaw, 1 in the eyebrow, and 4 in the mechanical eyes). Unitree and Dynamixel actuators are employed, with computation managed by three on-board computers.

- **Compact Design Envelope:** To emulate Olaf's free-floating feet, the leg mechanisms are largely concealed within the lower body. This was achieved through a novel **asymmetric 6-DoF leg design**. One leg has a rear-facing hip roll actuator and a forward knee, while the other has a forward hip roll and a rear-facing knee. This configuration maximizes workspace within the limited volume, mitigates collisions between hip roll actuators and knees, and reduces part count by using identical, unmirrored legs.
- **Remote Actuation:** Due to space constraints, actuators for the shoulders are placed within the torso and drive the shoulder joints via a **spherical 5-bar linkage**. For the mouth, a single actuator drives both jaws, with the upper jaw coupled through a 4-bar linkage. The mechanical eyes have direct-drive yaw, while pitch and eyelid movements are remotely actuated via 4-bar linkages. Other joints use direct drive.
- **Soft Shells:** The lower body, designed as a "snowball," is a flexible skirt made from polyurethane (PU) foam. This material provides structural shape while allowing deflection for larger leg movements, such as during recovery steps. The foot snowballs are similarly constructed. This flexible foam also helps absorb impacts, reducing fall severity.
- **Costuming and Appendages:** The costume is made of 4-way stretch fabric. A semi-rigid "boning" structure maintains costume shape around the mouth cavity. Snap fasteners and magnets secure the costume around the eyes and mouth. Appendages like arms, nose, buttons, eyebrows, and hair are held by magnets, allowing them to break away during falls or impacts to prevent damage.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL) Control (Section V):**
Control is divided into two layers: the articulation backbone (legs, neck) and the show functions. The backbone is controlled by RL policies, while show functions use classical methods.

- **Policy Formulation:** Separate policies are trained for walking and standing using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). At each time step, an action \(a_t\) (position targets for PD controllers) is produced by a policy \(\pi(a_t | s_t, g_t)\). The observable state \(s_t\) includes proprioceptive information (torso pose and velocities, joint positions and velocities, past actions, actuator temperatures \(T_t\), and gait phase \(\phi_t\) for walking). The control input \(g_t\) represents high-level commands, such as target neck orientation, torso height, or path velocity.
- **Path Frame:** The concept of a path frame, introduced in prior work, is utilized to achieve invariance to the robot's global pose and enable smooth policy transitions. This frame advances with commanded path velocity during walking or converges to the midpoint between the feet during standing.
- **Animation Reference:** Artist-created animation references for walking and standing, conditioned on \(g_t\), guide the RL process. A gait generation tool was used to design stylized walk cycles with heel-toe motion, crucial for Olaf's characteristic gait. A generator function maps the path-frame state and control input to the kinematic target state.
- **Reward Formulation:** The reward function \(r_t\) combines four components:
 - **Imitation (\(r_{imitation}\)):** Encourages accurate tracking of reference torso position, orientation, linear/angular velocities, joint positions/velocities, and foot contact states. Joint weights differ for neck and legs. Early termination is applied if the head, torso, upper legs, or arms contact the ground.
 - **Regularization (\(r_{regularization}\)):** Penalizes excessive joint torques, accelerations, and action rates to promote smooth and efficient motions.
 - **Limits (\(r_{limits}\)):**
 - **Thermal Constraint:** A critical component is a reward based on a **Control Barrier Function (CBF)** to prevent actuator overheating. This involves a first-order thermal model (\(\dot{T} = -\alpha(T - T_{ambient}) + \beta\tau^2\)) where temperature \(T\) is driven by squared torque \(\tau^2\). The CBF condition ensures that as \(T\) approaches a maximum \(T_{max}\), the system maintains \(\dot{T} \le 0\). Actuator temperatures are included as policy inputs.
 - **Joint Limits:** CBF conditions also enforce a margin from physical joint limits to prevent violations.
 - **Foot-Foot Collision:** Penalizes self-collisions between Olaf's feet.
 - **Impact Reduction (\(r_{impact\_reduction}\)):** Penalizes changes in vertical foot velocity between simulation steps to produce smoother foot motions and reduce impact noise. This term is saturated to stabilize learning.

**Show Functions Control (Section VI):**
For show functions with low inertia (eyes, eyebrows, jaw, arms), classical control methods are used.

- **Mapping:** A forward kinematics solver is used to map the functional space (how motions are animated) to the actuator space. This mapping is derived by uniformly sampling the functional region and fitting polynomials.
- **Specific Implementations:** For the eyes, a first-order polynomial per actuator suffices. For the spherical 5-bar linkage arms, a cubic polynomial fit determines the second actuator position. All eye and arm actuators are controlled using PD loops.
- **Jaw Control:** To compensate for costume fabric tension and wrinkling, a feedforward torque term \(\tau_{jaw}^{ff}(q_{jaw})\) (a first-order polynomial with an additional cosine term) is added to the PD controller, fitted from measured torque data.

**Implementation Details (Section VII):**
The thermal model parameters (\(\alpha, \beta, T_{ambient}\)) are fitted using least-squares regression on 20 minutes of recorded actuator data. The maximum actuator temperature \(T_{max}\) is set to 80°C. During training, temperatures used in policy observations and rewards are clipped to [70°C, 85°C]. Policies are trained in Isaac Sim using 8192 parallel environments on an RTX 4090 GPU for 100,000 iterations. At runtime, policies are deployed to the robot's on-board computer. A state estimator fuses IMU and actuator measurements. The 50 Hz policy output is upsampled to 600 Hz and low-pass filtered. An Animation Engine processes puppeteering commands, blending background animations, triggered clips, and joystick control to generate policy inputs and show function signals.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results supporting the efficacy of both the mechatronic design and the control methodologies.

- **Mechatronic Design Validation:**
 - The **asymmetric leg design** successfully accommodated Olaf's characteristic animations within the stringent space constraints, confirming its functional validity in the physical robot. The concealment of legs beneath the foam skirt effectively creates the illusion of freely moving feet.
 - The use of **magnetic attachments** for arms and nose enabled dynamic, in-character interactions, as demonstrated in supplementary material, while also mitigating potential damage during falls.

- **Tracking Performance:**
 - The control policies exhibited accurate imitation of the animation references. The **standing policy** achieved a mean absolute joint-tracking error of 3.87° ± 2.40° over a 5-minute trajectory. The **walking policy** demonstrated comparable performance with a mean absolute joint-tracking error of 4.02° ± 2.01° over 5 minutes.
 - The incorporation of **stylized heel-toe walking** reference was validated as crucial for character fidelity; policies trained without this specific gait characteristic resulted in motion that appeared "more robotic."

- **Thermal Model and Thermal-Aware Policy Evaluation:**
 - The fitted **thermal model** demonstrated good predictive accuracy, with a mean absolute error of 1.87°C over a 10-minute unseen trajectory when compared against measured actuator temperatures.
 - The **thermal reward** mechanism proved effective in preventing actuator overheating. A baseline policy without the thermal reward caused the neck-pitch actuator temperature to rise rapidly, reaching 100°C within 40 seconds, necessitating termination of the experiment. In contrast, the policy trained with the thermal reward maintained a significantly slower temperature rise. While preserving tracking accuracy at lower temperatures, the policy with the thermal reward demonstrated a capacity to reduce torque usage as temperatures approached the 80°C threshold, maintaining safety with only a slightly larger tracking error.
 - During a 1-hour continuous operation, the system reached thermal equilibrium, with the neck-pitch actuator maintaining a mean temperature of 77.3°C and a mean absolute joint tracking error of 0.14 rad over the final minute, demonstrating robust long-duration performance.

- **Foot Impact Reduction:**
 - The **foot impact reduction reward** significantly reduced stepping noise. Over a 5-minute run on hardware, the mean sound level decreased by 13.5 dB, a difference noticeable in the physical demonstration.
 - Despite this noise reduction, the policy largely preserved the overall gait trajectory and tracking performance. The reward acted as a regularizer, smoothing out minor nuances in the reference motion (such as a mid-swing foot dip) and reducing peak foot velocities at impact compared to a policy without the reward, thus decreasing impact forces.

- **System Deployment:**
 - The robot has undergone extended deployments and real-world testing. The thermal reward successfully prevented actuator overheating throughout these operations, and no detrimental effects on performance were observed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by demonstrating a comprehensive approach to realizing complex, non-physical animated characters, pushing the boundaries beyond purely functional robot design.

- **Advancement in Robotic Character Realization:** The work provides a validated methodology for bringing animated characters with challenging proportions and motion styles into the physical world. This includes not only faithful imitation of animation references but also addressing practical operational challenges inherent in costumed robotics. This can inspire further research and applications in entertainment robotics, theme park attractions, and human-robot interaction where character fidelity is paramount.

- **Novel Mechatronic Design Solutions:** The paper introduces specific mechanical innovations tailored for constrained aesthetic requirements. The asymmetric 6-DoF leg mechanism offers a solution for compact integration and expanded workspace within limited volumes. The extensive use of remotely actuated spherical, planar, and spatial linkages allows for expressive motion in areas like arms, mouth, and eyes where direct actuation is not feasible. The use of soft foam for the skirt and feet provides both structural integrity and functional flexibility, enhancing believability and impact absorption.

- **Enhanced Reinforcement Learning for Real-World Robotics:**
 - **Thermal-Aware Control:** The integration of actuator temperature as a policy input and the formulation of a Control Barrier Function (CBF)-based thermal reward represent a notable advancement in robust robot control. This approach enables robots to self-manage thermal constraints, preventing overheating and potential damage, particularly crucial for long-duration operations in constrained environments or with challenging load profiles. The methodology has potential generalizability beyond this specific character.
 - **Impact Reduction:** The development and validation of an impact-reduction reward demonstrate a practical method for reducing operational noise. This not only enhances the aesthetic believability of character robots but also has potential implications for reducing wear-and-tear on robotic components, potentially extending robot operational lifetimes across various applications.
 - **Constraint Handling:** The effective use of CBF constraints within an RL framework for managing joint limits and thermal conditions provides a valuable tool for ensuring safe and reliable robot operation, addressing a critical aspect of real-world robot deployment.

The findings suggest that the proposed impact-reducing rewards could decrease component wear and extend a robot’s operational lifetime, while the CBF reward formulation offers an effective means of handling diverse constraints in RL, particularly for slowly varying, long-horizon effects like thermal management. This work opens avenues for future research, including the development of higher-fidelity thermal models to account for mechanical heat generation and explicit modeling of costume-leg interaction forces to reduce reliance on domain randomization.
