# Scalable Pre-training of Large Autoregressive Image Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Apple developed Autoregressive Image Models (AIM), a scalable pre-training approach for large vision models that predicts image patches sequentially. AIM demonstrates strong scaling properties with increased model size and training data, achieving competitive performance on 15 image recognition benchmarks, including 84.0% ImageNet-1k accuracy with a frozen backbone.

## Problem

- Traditional self-supervised learning methods in vision (contrastive, masked autoencoding) have limitations like computational intensity, reliance on complex techniques to avoid trivial solutions, or hyperparameter sensitivity.
- Existing vision models had not fully explored the effectiveness of autoregressive objectives, which are highly successful in NLP, for large-scale pre-training.
- There was a need to investigate if similar scaling properties observed in large language models could be replicated in the visual domain without manual annotations.

## Method

- Introduces ==Autoregressive Image Models (AIM)== that treat images as sequences of patches and predict each patch based on preceding ones, mirroring language model training.
- Employs a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone with a novel ==prefix attention mechanism== that allows bidirectional attention for an initial set of patches while maintaining causal attention for the rest.
- Utilizes heavily parameterized ==token-level prediction heads== (MLP blocks) for predicting patches and prioritizes increasing model width over depth when scaling up.

## Results

- AIM models consistently improved performance with increased model size (from **0.6B** to **7B** parameters) and training data (up to **2 billion** images), showing no saturation in performance.
- The largest model, AIM-7B, achieved **84.0%** accuracy on ImageNet-1k with a frozen backbone and attained competitive performance across **15** diverse image recognition benchmarks.
- AIM's approach proved competitive with or outperformed state-of-the-art self-supervised methods like DINO and MAE on various benchmarks, particularly with larger model sizes, while being simpler and more stable.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Autoregressive objectives successfully apply to vision tasks and exhibit strong scaling properties similar to language models across model size, training data, and compute budget.
> - The choice of patch ordering significantly impacts performance, with raster (row-major) order outperforming other patterns due to maintaining consistent prediction difficulty throughout the sequence.
> - Prefix attention and heavily parameterized MLP prediction heads contribute to the effectiveness and scalability of the autoregressive vision model.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive scaling properties, where performance consistently improves with increased model size and training data. This paper by El-Nouby et al. from Apple investigates whether similar scaling properties can be achieved in the visual domain using autoregressive training objectives similar to those used in LLMs.

The researchers introduce Autoregressive Image Models (AIM), a scalable approach for pre-training large vision models without relying on manual annotations. AIM divides images into patches and predicts each patch based on preceding patches in a sequence, similar to how language models predict the next token in text.

### 2. Background and Context

Self-supervised learning (SSL) in computer vision has traditionally followed different approaches compared to NLP. While language models have successfully employed autoregressive objectives, vision models have relied more on contrastive learning (like DINO, BYOL) or masked autoencoding (like MAE).

These approaches have limitations: contrastive learning is computationally intensive and requires careful implementation to avoid trivial solutions, while masked autoencoding can be sensitive to hyperparameters and architecture choices.

The central question this paper addresses is whether autoregressive objectives, which have proven effective for language, can be successfully applied to vision tasks at scale, potentially offering a more straightforward and stable training approach.

### 3. The AIM Approach

The core of the AIM approach involves treating images as sequences of patches and predicting each patch based on previous patches in the sequence. This process is visualized below:

The key components of AIM include:

1. **Image Tokenization**: Images are divided into a grid of non-overlapping patches (e.g., 16×16 pixels).
2. **Sequence Creation**: These patches are arranged in a specific order (primarily raster order, from left to right, top to bottom).
3. **Autoregressive Objective**: The model is trained to predict each patch given all previous patches in the sequence.

This approach is conceptually simple and mirrors the training objectives used in language models, potentially enabling similar scaling properties.

### 4. Model Architecture

AIM employs a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone with several architectural innovations:

1. **Prefix Attention**: A novel mechanism that allows bidirectional attention for a prefix of patches during pre-training, while maintaining causal attention for the remainder:

2. **MLP Prediction Heads**: Instead of directly using the transformer output features for prediction, AIM employs heavily parameterized token-level prediction heads consisting of multiple MLP blocks.

3. **Model Scaling**: The researchers prioritize increasing model width rather than depth when scaling up. Through ablation studies, they determine optimal depth for different model sizes.

### 5. Training Methodology

AIM models are trained using:

1. **Dataset**: The DFN dataset, derived from a large collection of image-text pairs filtered from Common Crawl, with a small percentage (5%) of ImageNet-1k images included.

2. **Optimization**: AdamW optimizer with linear warmup and cosine decay learning rate schedule, using bfloat16 precision.

3. **Positional Encoding**: Sinusoidal positional embeddings to encode position information.

The training progress shows a clear correlation between pre-training validation loss and downstream performance.

### 6. Scaling Properties

One of the key findings is that AIM exhibits strong scaling properties in three dimensions:

1. **Model Size**: Larger models (from 0.6B to 7B parameters) consistently perform better on downstream tasks.

2. **Training Data**: Performance improves as models are trained on more unique images, with no sign of saturation even at 2 billion images.

3. **Compute Budget**: The paper explores the relationship between computational resources and model performance:

The researchers also investigate data efficiency, finding that a mix of diverse web data (DFN) with a small percentage of ImageNet images (DFN-2B+) provides better performance than training solely on either dataset.

### 7. Sequence Ordering Patterns

A crucial finding in the paper relates to the impact of different patch ordering sequences on model performance. The researchers compare several patterns:

Among these patterns, the raster ordering consistently outperforms others:

The researchers attribute this to the raster pattern's ability to maintain a challenging prediction task throughout the sequence, forcing the model to retain more information about the image. Additionally, they find that the standard horizontal raster pattern (left-to-right, top-to-bottom) performs better than a vertically flipped version.

### 8. Downstream Evaluation

The quality of AIM's learned features is assessed through downstream evaluation on 15 image recognition benchmarks. The evaluation protocol involves:

1. Freezing the pre-trained model backbone
2. Training only a classification head on top
3. Using an attention pooling operation ("Attentive Probe") to build a global feature from patch features

The results demonstrate that AIM features are highly transferable:

The largest model, AIM-7B, achieves 84.0% accuracy on ImageNet-1k with a frozen backbone when fine-tuned with the DFN-2B+ dataset, demonstrating the high quality of the learned features.

### 9. Comparison with Other Methods

AIM's performance is competitive with other state-of-the-art self-supervised learning methods:

1. **Compared to Contrastive Learning**: AIM achieves comparable performance to methods like DINO and iBOT, especially at larger scales.

2. **Compared to Masked Autoencoding**: AIM outperforms MAE in several benchmarks, particularly with larger model sizes.

3. **Advantages of the Approach**: Unlike contrastive methods, AIM doesn't require complex techniques to avoid collapsed solutions. And unlike masked autoencoding, AIM's training objective is more closely aligned with the final task of understanding complete images.

The key advantage of AIM lies in its simplicity and scalability - it provides a straightforward approach that scales efficiently with model size and training data, without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning or complex implementation details.

### 10. Conclusion

The paper demonstrates that autoregressive objectives, which have been highly successful in language modeling, can be effectively applied to vision tasks. The AIM approach exhibits strong scaling properties, with larger models and more training data consistently leading to better performance.

Key takeaways include:

1. **Scaling Benefits**: Autoregressive image models show similar scaling behaviors to language models, with performance improving as model size and training data increase.

2. **Architectural Choices**: Prefix attention and heavily parameterized prediction heads contribute to the effectiveness of the approach.

3. **Sequence Ordering**: The choice of patch ordering pattern significantly impacts model performance, with raster ordering providing the best results.

4. **Feature Quality**: AIM learns high-quality visual features that transfer well to a variety of downstream tasks, achieving competitive performance with state-of-the-art self-supervised methods.

This work provides a compelling case for the use of autoregressive objectives in vision model pre-training, offering a simple yet effective approach that benefits from increased scale in the same way that language models do. The lack of saturation in performance suggests that further improvements may be possible with even larger models and more training data.
