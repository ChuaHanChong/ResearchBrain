# Verifying Chain-of-Thought Reasoning via Its Computational Graph

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from FAIR at Meta and the University of Edinburgh developed Circuit-based Reasoning Verification (CRV), a white-box method that analyzes the computational graph of a Large Language Model to verify its Chain-of-Thought reasoning. This approach demonstrated superior performance in identifying reasoning errors and provided insights into the causal mechanisms of these failures through structural 'fingerprints' and targeted interventions.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) employing Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning can produce complex, multi-step solutions that are sometimes plausible but fundamentally flawed.
- Existing black-box and gray-box verification methods can detect correlations with errors but lack the ability to explain *why* an LLM's reasoning fails or to pinpoint the exact computational malfunction.
- A deeper, mechanistic approach was needed to move beyond error detection to a causal understanding and potential intervention for LLM reasoning failures.

## Method

- Introduced ==Circuit-based Reasoning Verification (CRV)==, a four-stage white-box pipeline that modifies an LLM (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct) by replacing its MLP modules with interpretable ==transcoders==.
- Constructed sparse, weighted, directed ==attribution graphs== for each CoT reasoning step, capturing causal information flow between model components and active transcoder features.
- Extracted a fixed-size feature vector (==structural fingerprint==) from each attribution graph, comprising global, node influence, and topological statistics, which was then used to train a Gradient Boosting Classifier to predict step correctness.

## Results

- CRV consistently outperformed all black-box and gray-box baselines across synthetic (Boolean, Arithmetic) and real-world (GSM8K) datasets, achieving an AUROC of **92.47** on the Arithmetic task compared to the strongest baseline's **76.45**.
- Leave-one-out ablation studies revealed that features related to Node Influence & Activation were most critical for verification performance, contributing the largest share to predictive accuracy.
- Proof-of-concept causal interventions, such as manually suppressing or amplifying specific transcoder features, successfully corrected faulty reasoning paths, providing strong evidence that CRV's identified structural signatures are causally implicated in reasoning errors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reasoning failures in LLMs manifest as distinct structural "fingerprints" within the model's computational graph, making direct white-box verification feasible and effective.
> - The identified error signatures are highly domain-specific, meaning that distinct computational patterns characterize failures in different reasoning tasks (e.g., arithmetic vs. general problem-solving).
> - Analysis of feature importance and distributions confirmed that the structural properties captured by CRV distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning, delineating a "zone of computational integrity."

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting has enabled Large Language Models (LLMs) to exhibit complex reasoning abilities. Despite these advancements, a persistent challenge in LLM development is the occurrence of flaws within their reasoning processes. This issue has led to the emergence of research focused on automating the verification of LLM reasoning.

Current approaches to verifying CoT reasoning can be categorized into two main paradigms:
- **Black-box methods:** These methods assess reasoning correctness based solely on the model's generated text or the final logit distribution. While effective at detecting errors in outputs, they offer limited insight into the internal computational processes that lead to these errors.
- **Gray-box methods:** These approaches analyze the model's internal states, such as raw activations or hidden state trajectories. They can identify correlations between internal states and reasoning errors, but generally do not provide mechanistic explanations for *why* a particular computation failed.

This research addresses a recognized gap in the existing landscape by proposing a "white-box" approach. It is motivated by the hypothesis that LLMs implement latent algorithms through specialized subgraphs, or "circuits," and that reasoning failures represent flaws in the execution of these circuits. By directly inspecting the underlying computational process, akin to examining an execution trace in traditional software, the authors aim to move beyond mere error detection to a more causal understanding of LLM reasoning failures.

The paper draws inspiration from the field of mechanistic interpretability, which seeks to reverse-engineer the algorithms learned by neural networks. Specifically, it builds upon concepts such as "circuits" in transformers (Olah et al., 2020) and the use of "transcoders" (Dunefsky etal., 2025) and "attribution graphs" (Ameisen et al., 2025) for analyzing information flow. While previous work has utilized these tools for qualitative analysis of CoT faithfulness, this paper distinguishes itself by operationalizing these mechanistic interpretability techniques for *automated verification* of reasoning steps. The authors state that they are not aware of prior attempts to verify reasoning by analyzing the structural properties of its underlying computational graph, positioning their work as a novel contribution in this specific area.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of existing Chain-of-Thought (CoT) verification methods. Current black-box and gray-box approaches can identify instances of incorrect reasoning or states correlated with errors, but they provide limited insight into the underlying computational mechanisms that lead to these failures. This diagnostic gap hinders the ability to debug and improve LLMs effectively.

The central hypothesis guiding this work is that reasoning failures in LLMs are not merely erroneous outputs or states, but rather manifestations of flaws in the execution of latent algorithms implemented by the model's specialized computational "circuits." The authors postulate that these flaws leave distinct structural "fingerprints" on the model's computational execution traces, which can be represented as attribution graphs.

Based on this motivation and hypothesis, the key objectives of this research are detailed as follows:

1. **Introduce and Validate Circuit-based Reasoning Verification (CRV):** The primary objective is to develop and present CRV as a novel white-box methodology. The authors aim to empirically demonstrate that verifying reasoning directly by analyzing the structural properties of its underlying computational graph is feasible and yields predictive power.
2. **Investigate the Nature of Error Signatures:** The research seeks to determine whether the structural signatures of reasoning errors are consistently predictive of incorrect steps. Furthermore, it aims to explore if these signatures are highly domain-specific, meaning that failures in different reasoning tasks manifest as distinct computational patterns.
3. **Establish the Causal Role of Error Signatures:** A crucial objective is to move beyond correlational observations and provide evidence that the identified structural signatures are causally implicated in reasoning errors. This involves conducting experiments to determine if insights derived from CRV's analysis can guide targeted interventions on specific model components to correct faulty reasoning.
4. **Contribute to Research Infrastructure:** The authors aim to support future research in mechanistic interpretability and reasoning verification by releasing datasets with step-level correctness labels for CoT reasoning on both synthetic and real-world tasks, as well as the trained transcoders used in their methodology.

The authors characterize CRV as a "scientific instrument" rather than a practical, drop-in verifier. This distinction underscores that the primary goal is not immediate, scalable deployment, but rather to facilitate a deeper, causal understanding of LLM reasoning failures, which is considered unattainable by other methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Circuit-based Reasoning Verification (CRV) methodology is a four-stage pipeline designed to classify the correctness of a CoT step by analyzing the computational graph of a modified, interpretable LLM.

#### 3.1. Dataset Curation and Step-Level Annotation

A prerequisite for CRV is a dataset providing reliable step-level correctness labels, along with the full internal computational state of the specific model during generation. To meet this requirement, the authors curated new benchmarks:

- **Synthetic Datasets (Boolean and Arithmetic):** These datasets were procedurally generated to study reasoning failures in a controlled environment. They involve evaluating complex boolean expressions and multi-step arithmetic problems, respectively. The ground truth for individual steps (e.g., "15 + 7 = 22") can be verified automatically. The complexity was controlled by the number of operators (n ∈ {3, 5, 7, 10}).
- **Step-Level Annotation for GSM8K:** For a real-world benchmark, the GSM8K dataset (Cobbe et al., 2021) was used. A semi-automated process was employed, utilizing a larger LLM (Llama 3.3 70B Instruct) as an expert judge to evaluate step correctness given the full problem context. Labels were validated through manual review of a subset.

**Labeling Protocol:** For synthetic datasets, a two-pronged strategy was used: an LLM-as-a-Judge (Llama 3.3 70B Instruct) and programmatic state verification. A step was labeled correct only if both methods agreed. For GSM8K, only the LLM-as-a-Judge was used due to the natural language nature of the problems.

**Handling Error Propagation:** To avoid ambiguity when a model makes a technically correct calculation based on a previous error, a strict truncation policy was adopted. Only steps up to and including the *first incorrect step* in a reasoning chain were retained in the dataset, with all subsequent steps discarded.

The final datasets consisted of: Synthetic (Boolean) with 126,624 steps (0.2% incorrect), Synthetic (Arithmetic) with 155,434 steps (1.2% incorrect), and GSM8K with 8,737 steps (6.6% incorrect).

#### 3.2. Circuit-based Reasoning Verification (CRV) Pipeline

CRV operates through the following stages.

#### 3.3. Step 1: Replacing MLPs with Interpretable Transcoders

To make the target LLM interpretable, its standard MLP modules are replaced with trained transcoders. These transcoders are a variant of sparse autoencoders, specifically designed to approximate the input-output function of a target component (e.g., `f(x) ≈ MLP(x)`). They are trained on a large dataset of activations from the original LLM, combining an L2 reconstruction loss with a TopK activation function to enforce sparsity (preserving only the k-largest feature activations). This modification forces the model's intermediate computations to be represented by a sparse combination of interpretable features. The Llama 3.1 8B Instruct model was modified with such transcoders, trained on a 10B token subset of the RedPajama-V2 dataset, with a latent feature dimension of 131,072 and k=128.

#### 3.4. Step 2: Constructing Step-Level Attribution Graphs

For each reasoning step, an attribution graph `G_i = (V, E)` is constructed to trace information flow. This is achieved by adapting a greedy path-finding algorithm that traces high-attribution connections backward from the final logits. The nodes `V` in the graph represent input tokens, active transcoder features, and output logits, while the directed edges `E` represent the causal influence between these components, weighted by their strength. The graph for a reasoning step `s_i` is computed at the position of the final token of that step, chosen after an ablation study showed superior performance for this "post-computation" trace.

#### 3.5. Step 3: Extracting Interpretable Graph Features

A fixed-size feature vector `x_i` (the "structural fingerprint") is extracted from each attribution graph. The graph is first pruned to its most influential components, retaining nodes and edges that account for a threshold (e.g., 80%) of total influence to the final logits. The extracted features are organized into three categories:

- **Global Graph Statistics:** High-level summaries such as the count of active feature nodes and residual stream nodes, and final logit probability and entropy.
- **Node Influence and Activation Statistics:** Quantifies properties of interpretable feature nodes, including mean/max/std of activation values and influence scores, and a histogram of active features by layer.
- **Topological and Path-Based Features:** Structural properties of information flow, including graph density, centrality measures (degree, betweenness), connectivity metrics (e.g., number of weakly connected components), and average shortest path length from input tokens to logits.

#### 3.6. Step 4: Diagnostic Classifier

A Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC) is trained on the extracted feature vectors (`f_θ(x_i) = ˆy_i`) to predict the correctness of the reasoning step. GBC was chosen for its suitability with heterogeneous tabular features and its ability to provide feature importance measures for interpretability.

**Experimental Setup:** Experiments were conducted on the Llama 3.1 8B Instruct model, modified with transcoders. Performance was evaluated on the three curated datasets. Baselines included black-box methods (MaxProb, PPL, Entropy, Temp. Scaling, Energy) and gray-box methods (CoE-R, CoE-C, CoT-Kinetics, LR Probe, MLP Probe). Evaluation metrics were AUROC, AUPR, and FPR@95, with "incorrect" designated as the positive class.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the efficacy and properties of Circuit-based Reasoning Verification (CRV), as well as insights into the nature of LLM reasoning failures.

#### 4.1. Verification Performance and Robustness (RQ1)

- **Main Verification Performance:** CRV consistently outperformed all black-box and gray-box baselines across all evaluated datasets and metrics. For instance, on the Synthetic (Arithmetic) task, CRV achieved an AUROC of 92.47, a notable improvement over the strongest baseline score of 76.45. Its FPR@95 on this task was 37.09%, significantly lower than the baseline's 63.33%. This performance gap was particularly pronounced on the structured synthetic datasets, suggesting that algorithmic reasoning produces consistent execution traces for valid solutions, making deviations (errors) highly detectable.
- **Cross-Domain Generalization:** CRV demonstrated limited zero-shot transferability across different domains. A classifier trained on one domain performed suboptimally when applied to an unseen task (e.g., CRV trained on Arithmetic achieved an AUROC of 57.04 on GSM8K, lower than the Energy baseline's 62.55). This indicates that the structural signatures of error are highly task-specific. However, a CRV model trained on a combination of all datasets ("CRV (Combined)") showed robust performance, nearly matching or slightly exceeding in-domain specialist models. This suggests that while error patterns are distinct, a single classifier can learn to identify a union of these complex failure geometries.
- **Performance Under Increasing Difficulty:** As the complexity of synthetic arithmetic problems (measured by the number of operators) increased, CRV maintained its performance advantage over baselines. This indicates the robustness of its structural signals across varying task difficulties and shifts in class balance.

#### 4.2. Mechanistic Analysis of Error Computations (RQ2)

- **Feature Ablation:** A leave-one-out ablation study revealed that "Node Influence & Activation features" were the most critical for verification, as their removal caused the most significant performance degradation (e.g., increasing FPR@95 by over 12 points on the Arithmetic dataset). "Global Graph Statistics" also contributed substantially, while "Topological & Path-Based features" were found to be less critical for this specific task.
- **Visualizing Structural Signatures of Error:** Qualitative analysis of feature distributions for correct versus incorrect steps showed clear distinctions. For instance, on GSM8K, features such as Graph Density, Total Active Features, Mean Node Influence, Pruned Feature Node Count, and Mean Edge Weights exhibited separable distributions between correct and incorrect computations, with statistical significance (p < 0.001) and medium-to-large effect sizes (Cohen's d). This provides quantitative evidence that attribution graphs capture distinct structural differences in valid and flawed computations.
- **Feature Space Visualization:** Principal Component Analysis (PCA) projection of the high-dimensional feature vectors indicated that incorrect steps often form a dense subset within the broader distribution of correct steps, suggesting "computational near misses." However, a distinct region of "computational integrity" was also identified, defined by structural properties exclusive to correct reasoning.

#### 4.3. From Diagnosis to Causal Intervention (RQ3)

The research included an exploratory study to determine if CRV's diagnostic insights could guide targeted interventions, demonstrating a causal link between structural signatures and errors.

- **Case Study 1 (Order-of-Operations Error):** In an arithmetic expression `(7*((5+9)+7))`, the model incorrectly computed `7*14=98` in its second step, rather than `(5+9)+7=21`. CRV flagged this error and identified an unusually high activation of a late-layer transcoder feature associated with multiplication. By clamping this specific "multiplication" feature's activation to zero using a forward hook, the model was successfully steered to generate the correct step (`14+7=21`) and subsequently arrived at the correct final answer (`147`).
- **Case Study 2 (Unary Minus Misinterpretation):** For the expression `(-(5+(4*9)))`, the model incorrectly interpreted the leading unary minus, leading to an incorrect result of -42. CRV's analysis suggested an insufficient "multiplication" signal. By amplifying a transcoder feature semantically corresponding to multiplication, the model correctly applied the unary minus as a multiplication by -1, yielding the correct answer of -41.

These interventions provided evidence that CRV's structural signatures are causally implicated in reasoning errors, indicating a potential pathway for interpretable debugging and targeted model repair.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Circuit-based Reasoning Verification (CRV) as a novel white-box methodology, marking a departure from existing black-box and gray-box approaches to LLM reasoning verification. Its significance lies in several key contributions:

- **Advancing Mechanistic Interpretability:** The work demonstrates the practical application of mechanistic interpretability concepts, such as learned circuits and interpretable features derived from transcoders, for an operational task: automated verification of reasoning steps. This bridges the gap between theoretical interpretability research and practical model reliability.
- **Causal Understanding of LLM Failures:** By scrutinizing the computational graph, CRV moves beyond merely detecting errors or identifying correlations with internal states. It provides a framework for gaining a deeper, potentially causal understanding of *how* and *why* LLMs fail to reason correctly. The successful targeted interventions on specific transcoder features offer direct evidence that the identified structural signatures are causally implicated in errors, opening avenues for prescriptive model debugging.
- **New Diagnostic Capabilities:** The findings establish that the computational graph of reasoning steps contains rich, detectable structural signals indicative of correctness. This suggests a new dimension for LLM diagnostics, allowing developers to inspect the "execution trace" of an LLM's internal computations.
- **Revealing Domain-Specific Failure Modes:** The observation that error signatures are highly domain-specific reveals that different reasoning tasks manifest distinct computational patterns of failure. This insight is critical for developing more robust and task-aware LLMs, as it implies that a universal "fix" for reasoning errors might be less effective than domain-specific interventions.
- **Foundation for Future Research and Development:** While CRV is currently computationally intensive and not presented as a production-ready verifier, its role as a "scientific instrument" is significant. It lays the groundwork for future research into:
 - **Interpretable Debugging Tools:** Developing tools that diagnose reasoning failures by mapping structural anomalies in the computational graph to semantically meaningful transcoder features.
 - **Targeted Model Repair:** Leveraging causal insights to perform precise interventions or training modifications to correct specific faulty reasoning behaviors.
 - **Neuro-Symbolic Approaches:** Future work could explore more sophisticated classifiers or rule-based systems that directly leverage the semantic content of individual transcoder features, potentially leading to a new class of neuro-symbolic verifiers.
- **Resource Contribution:** The release of datasets with step-level correctness labels and trained transcoders supports further open research in LLM reasoning and mechanistic interpretability.

In conclusion, this work contributes to the scientific understanding of LLM reasoning by providing a methodology to mechanistically analyze computational failures. It highlights the potential for white-box approaches to enhance LLM reliability through deeper, causal insights into their internal processes.
