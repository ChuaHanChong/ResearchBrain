# Survey on Evaluation of LLM-based Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey provides the first comprehensive mapping of evaluation methodologies for LLM-based agents, categorizing benchmarks across fundamental capabilities and application-specific domains. It highlights the field's shift towards more realistic and live evaluation, while identifying critical needs for granular metrics, cost efficiency, scalable automation, and robust safety assessments.

## Problem

- Traditional Large Language Models (LLMs) are limited to static, single-turn text-to-text interactions, lacking the dynamic capabilities of agents.
- The emergence of LLM-based agents, capable of multi-step reasoning, tool use, and memory in dynamic environments, necessitates new and robust evaluation methodologies.
- The existing evaluation landscape for these agents was fragmented, lacking a comprehensive and systematic overview.

## Method

- Provides the first comprehensive survey of ==evaluation methodologies for LLM-based agents==.
- Systematically analyzes existing benchmarks and frameworks, categorizing them by ==fundamental agent capabilities== and ==application-specific domains==.
- Synthesizes findings to identify current trends and highlight critical gaps in agent evaluation practices.

## Results

- Identified a clear trend towards more realistic, challenging, and live benchmarks, moving beyond static environments to dynamic, real-world scenarios.
- Revealed significant gaps in current evaluation practices, including a lack of granular, fine-grained metrics for diagnosing specific agent failures.
- Highlighted the insufficient focus on cost, efficiency, scalability, automation, and critical safety and compliance aspects in existing evaluation paradigms.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evaluating LLM-based agents requires new methodologies focusing on multi-step behavior, dynamic interaction, and decision-making processes, distinct from traditional LLM evaluation.
> - The lack of a comprehensive evaluation framework prior to this work hindered standardization and identification of research directions in a rapidly evolving field.
> - Current evaluation trends show a shift towards more realistic and continuously updated benchmarks for agent assessment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to significant advancements in artificial intelligence, enabling models to address a wide range of complex tasks primarily through text-to-text interactions. However, traditional LLMs possess fixed knowledge and operate as static entities, limiting their capacity for dynamic engagement with real-world environments.

LLM-based agents represent an evolution beyond static LLMs by integrating them into multi-step workflows and equipping them with external tools. This architecture allows agents to perform computations, retrieve up-to-date information, and interact autonomously with dynamic environments. Such agents can plan, execute, and adapt complex strategies, thereby addressing problems previously inaccessible to static AI systems and enabling applications across diverse domains. This transition from static models to adaptive, interactive agents constitutes a paradigm shift in AI capabilities.

This survey paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by providing the first comprehensive review dedicated to the evaluation methodologies for LLM-based agents. It acknowledges that evaluating these agents necessitates a departure from traditional LLM assessment, which primarily focuses on textual outputs. Instead, agent evaluation must assess capacities for sequential decision-making, operation within dynamic environments, and the ability to achieve user-defined tasks through a series of actions and interactions. The work underscores the importance of benchmarks that can co-evolve with rapidly advancing agent capabilities, accommodating new types of tasks and domains. By mapping the current evaluation landscape and identifying existing gaps, this survey aims to inform and guide future research and development in the field of agent evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to present the first comprehensive overview of evaluation methods specifically designed for LLM-based agents. The authors state a clear intention to benefit various stakeholders, including agent developers, benchmark creators, AI practitioners, and researchers, by systematically mapping the current evaluation landscape and identifying critical areas requiring further research.

The motivation for this survey stems from several factors:
1. **Rapid Advancement of LLMs and Emergence of Agents**: LLMs have achieved remarkable progress, but their inherent limitations (fixed knowledge, text-to-text confinement) are addressed by LLM-based agents. These agents leverage LLMs as a backbone but integrate them with multi-step workflows, external tools, and the ability to interact with dynamic environments. This integration allows agents to autonomously plan, execute, and adapt complex strategies in real-world settings, unlocking innovative applications.
2. **Need for a New Evaluation Paradigm**: The shift from static LLMs to adaptive, interactive agents necessitates a fundamentally new approach to evaluation. Traditional metrics for LLM textual outputs are insufficient for assessing an agent's capacity for sequential decision-making, its ability to operate within dynamic environments, or its proficiency in accomplishing tasks through a series of actions and interactions.
3. **Dynamic Nature of Agent Capabilities**: Agent capabilities are continuously evolving. Consequently, evaluation benchmarks must also co-evolve, accommodating new classes of tasks and domains that emerge with advancements in agent design. A systematic review is required to capture this dynamic landscape.
4. **Identification of Gaps and Future Directions**: By analyzing existing evaluation methods, the authors aim to reveal current trends, such as the move towards more realistic and challenging evaluations, including the use of continuously updated "live" benchmarks. Crucially, they seek to identify critical gaps that impede the robust and scalable development of agents, particularly in areas like cost-efficiency, safety, robustness, and the need for fine-grained, scalable evaluation methods. This identification serves as a roadmap for future research efforts to address these shortcomings.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the necessity to establish a structured understanding of how LLM-based agents are currently assessed, to highlight the deficiencies in current practices, and to provide strategic guidance for the development of more effective and comprehensive evaluation paradigms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The survey adopts a multi-perspective analytical approach to cover the evaluation landscape of LLM-based agents. The methodology involves organizing existing research into five distinct but interrelated perspectives, supported by a structured literature review process.

The analytical framework for the survey is structured as follows:
1. **Core LLM Capabilities Evaluation**: This section analyzes evaluation methods for fundamental LLM abilities that are essential for agentic workflows. These capabilities include:
 - **Planning and Multi-Step Reasoning**: Assessing an agent's ability to decompose problems and create strategic execution paths. Benchmarks discussed include PlanBench, FlowBench, and Natural Plan, with a focus on long-horizon planning.
 - **Function Calling & Tool Use**: Evaluating the agent's ability to recognize intent, select appropriate functions, map parameters, and execute tools to interact with external systems. Examples include BFCL, ToolBench, and ComplexFuncBench.
 - **Self-Reflection**: Examining methods to assess an agent's capacity for self-correction by dynamically adjusting reasoning or actions based on feedback, utilizing benchmarks like LLM-Evolve and LLF-Bench.
 - **Memory**: Reviewing mechanisms that enable agents to manage information and reason across extended interactions, covering episodic, semantic, and procedural memory types, with benchmarks such as StreamBench, MemBench, and MemoryAgentBench.
2. **Application-Specific Agent Evaluation**: This part reviews benchmarks and evaluation strategies tailored for prominent types of agentic applications:
 - **Web Agents**: Evaluations for tasks involving web interaction, ranging from simplified simulations (WebShop) to realistic dynamic environments (Mind2Web, WebArena).
 - **Software Engineering (SWE) Agents**: Benchmarks that moved from fundamental coding tasks (HumanEval) to real-world GitHub issues (SWE-bench, SWE-bench Verified, SWE-Lancer).
 - **Scientific Agents**: Evaluation spanning the full scientific research pipeline, from ideation to code generation and peer review (e.g., ScienceAgentBench, CORE-Bench, PaperBench).
 - **Conversational Agents**: Assessment of goal-directed, multi-turn dialogue agents that use tools and interact with environments (τ-Bench, τ²-Bench).
3. **Generalist Agents Evaluation**: This section describes benchmarks and leaderboards designed for evaluating agents with broad, integrated capabilities across diverse tasks. It discusses approaches like Gaia2, OSWorld, AppWorld, and AgentBench.
4. **Analysis of Agent Benchmarks' Core Dimensions**: This provides a cross-cutting analysis of benchmarks based on shared orthogonal dimensions:
 - **Data Curation**: Examining how evaluation data is collected (human-curated, hybrid, synthetic).
 - **Environment**: Categorizing environments as static or dynamic based on agent interaction.
 - **Interface**: Describing communication protocols (Code/Terminal, Tools, Graphical User Interfaces).
 - **Metric**: Analyzing the various success metrics used (task completion, state matching, answer matching).
 - **Safety**: Assessing whether benchmarks explicitly incorporate safety constraints and robustness metrics.
5. **Evaluation Frameworks and Tools**: This part reviews current frameworks that integrate with development environments to support continuous monitoring, evaluation, error analysis, and performance optimization (e.g., LangSmith, Langfuse, Google Vertex AI evaluation service). It also covers Gym-like environments for controlled simulations.

To ensure the comprehensiveness and validity of the survey, the authors implemented a multi-stage literature review methodology detailed in Appendix A:
- **Systematic Search and Citation Chasing**: Initial data collection involved a systematic search across major academic repositories (Google Scholar, ACL Anthology, HuggingFace Papers, arXiv) using targeted keywords. This was followed by an iterative "snowballing" technique, performing both forward and backward citation chasing on seminal papers to identify foundational and emerging works.
- **Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria**: Papers were included if they introduced a novel benchmark, an evaluation framework, or a significant methodological contribution to LLM-based agent assessment. Works focusing solely on new agent architectures without distinct evaluation contributions or traditional LLM evaluation without dynamic/interactive components were excluded. High-impact preprints were included to capture recent advancements.
- **Expert Consultation**: To mitigate coverage gaps and ensure accuracy, domain experts corresponding to the survey’s taxonomic categories (e.g., SWE and web agent researchers) were consulted. Engagement with creators of leading benchmarks and dominant agent architectures was undertaken to validate selected manuscripts and capture nuances of state-of-the-art evaluation protocols.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey identifies several current trends in LLM-based agent evaluation and highlights critical gaps that require future research attention.

#### 4.1. Current Trends

1. **Realistic and Challenging Evaluation**: There is a clear progression from simplified, static evaluation environments to more realistic and complex benchmarks.
 - **Web Agents**: Early web agent evaluations used basic simulations; current efforts leverage dynamic, real-world settings such as WebArena and Mind2Web, requiring complex interactions and multimodal reasoning.
 - **Software Engineering (SWE) Agents**: Benchmarks have evolved from fundamental coding tasks to addressing real-world GitHub issues with full repository contexts and executable environments, exemplified by SWE-bench and its rigorous variants like SWE-bench Verified and SWE-bench Pro, which involve long-horizon, multi-file code changes.
 - **Generalist Agents**: Evaluation is increasingly focused on integrated capabilities required for complex tasks, often performed by human experts, pushing benchmarks closer to real-world professional workflows (e.g., Gaia2, OSWorld, AppWorld).
2. **Live Benchmarks**: The rapid pace of LLM and agent development necessitates adaptive and continuously updated evaluation methods.
 - **Dynamic Adaptation**: Static benchmarks quickly become saturated and obsolete. In response, "live" benchmarks are emerging, characterized by continuous updates and refinements. Examples include multiple versions of the Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard (BFCL) and the iterative development of the SWE-bench family (Verified, Pro).
 - **Purpose**: These adaptations aim to match the increased capabilities of agents, address shortcomings of previous versions (e.g., through human verification and refined success metrics), and adapt to evolving research ecosystems like Model Context Protocol (MCP)-based tool calling. This dynamic approach maintains relevance in a fast-moving field.

#### 4.2. Critical Gaps and Future Directions

The survey identifies five critical gaps in current agent evaluation that future research must address:

1. **Advancing Granular Evaluation**:
 - **Problem**: Most current benchmarks rely on coarse-grained, end-to-end success metrics, which provide limited insight into specific agent failures or intermediate decision-making processes (e.g., tool selection, reasoning quality).
 - **Need**: Development of standardized, fine-grained evaluation metrics that target the trajectory of an agent’s task execution, providing richer feedback for targeted improvements.
2. **Cost and Efficiency Metrics**:
 - **Problem**: Current evaluations primarily prioritize performance, often overlooking cost and efficiency measurements. This can lead to the development of highly capable but resource-intensive agents, hindering practical deployment.
 - **Need**: Integration of cost-efficiency as a core metric, tracking factors such as token usage, API expenses, inference time, and overall resource consumption, to guide the development of performance-balanced agents.
3. **Scaling & Automating Evaluation**:
 - **Problem**: The reliance on static, human-annotated evaluation data is resource-intensive and quickly renders benchmarks outdated in a rapidly evolving field.
 - **Need**: Development of scalable, automated evaluation techniques, including synthetic data generation and the use of LLMs or agents as judges. This approach aims to reduce reliance on human annotation and capture nuanced aspects of agent performance.
4. **Safety and Compliance**:
 - **Problem**: Current benchmarks lack sufficient focus on safety, trustworthiness, and policy compliance. Existing efforts are nascent, with comprehensive tests for robustness against adversarial inputs, bias mitigation, and adherence to organizational/societal policies largely absent.
 - **Need**: Integration of "guardrail" metrics that penalize non-compliant actions, and the development of dedicated safety benchmarks that simulate real-world scenarios, especially in multi-agent contexts where emergent risks may arise.
5. **Decoupling LLM & Harness Evaluation**:
 - **Problem**: Most current agent benchmarks conflate the inherent capabilities of the backbone LLM with the design and effectiveness of the agent "harness" (or scaffold). This makes it difficult to systematically attribute performance gains to specific components.
 - **Need**: Development of controlled evaluation protocols that independently vary the backbone LLM and the agent harness design. This would enable systematic comparisons and isolate the contribution of individual components (LLM, planning, memory, tools) to overall agent performance.

#### 4.3. Specific Sectional Findings

- **Agent Capabilities**: Even state-of-the-art models struggle with long-horizon planning. Tool use evaluation has matured from simple one-step function calls to complex, multi-turn, stateful, and nested interactions. Self-reflection, while recognized as crucial, lacks standardized benchmarks and methodologies for robust assessment. Memory evaluation has progressed from using general long-context LLM benchmarks to dedicated benchmarks for episodic and semantic memory in agentic contexts.
- **Application-Specific Agents**: Web agents now face evaluation in realistic, dynamic, multimodal environments. SWE agents are tested on full-stack, real-world issues requiring long-horizon reasoning. Scientific agents evaluation spans the entire research workflow. Conversational agents are moving from textual tasks to goal-directed, tool-augmented dialogues in dynamic environments.
- **Generalist Agent Evaluation**: Two main strategies exist: benchmarks designed to inherently require a wide range of integrated capabilities (e.g., Gaia, OSWorld) and platforms that unify several task-specific benchmarks (e.g., AgentBench, HAL), although the latter often struggle with cross-environment consistency.
- **Core Benchmark Dimensions**: Hybrid data curation is prevalent to balance quality and scalability. There is a clear shift towards dynamic environments over static ones to capture cascading errors. Interaction interfaces vary from code/terminal to tool-specific API calls and graphical user interfaces. Metrics are diverse, often binary, and typically focus on task completion, highlighting the need for more granular progress indicators. Safety and robustness, despite their importance, are rarely explicitly incorporated as core evaluation metrics.
- **Evaluation Frameworks**: Frameworks like LangSmith and Google Vertex AI provide continuous monitoring and quality assessment at various granularities (final response, stepwise, trajectory-based). They often use LLM-based judges and support human-in-the-loop evaluation and synthetic data generation. However, challenges remain in deriving large-scale insights, considering the cost of evaluation, and integrating built-in safety/compliance checks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey contributes significantly to the field of LLM-based agent research by providing a detailed and structured synthesis of the current state of evaluation methodologies. Its impact can be observed in several key areas:

1. **Standardization and Clarity**: By mapping the diverse and rapidly evolving landscape of agent evaluation, the survey offers a foundational resource that organizes existing benchmarks, capabilities, applications, and frameworks. This structured overview can aid in standardizing discussions and fostering a common understanding among researchers and practitioners.
2. **Guidance for Researchers and Developers**: The comprehensive analysis of current methods and the identification of critical gaps provide actionable insights for future research directions. Researchers are presented with clear challenges, such as the need for granular evaluation, cost-efficiency metrics, automated scaling, and robust safety assessments. Developers gain a better understanding of the strengths and limitations of current evaluation tools, guiding them in selecting appropriate benchmarks and frameworks for their agent development.
3. **Informing Benchmark Design**: For creators of new benchmarks, the analysis of core benchmark dimensions (data curation, environment, interface, metric, safety) offers a blueprint for designing more rigorous, relevant, and future-proof evaluation tools. The discussion on "live" benchmarks emphasizes the need for adaptive and continuously updated evaluation to keep pace with rapid agent advancements.
4. **Fostering Responsible AI Development**: By explicitly highlighting the critical gaps in safety, trustworthiness, and policy compliance, the survey underscores the importance of integrating these considerations into future evaluation paradigms. This emphasis can encourage the development of agents that are not only capable but also reliable, secure, and ethically aligned, thereby promoting more responsible deployment of AI technologies in real-world settings.
5. **Advancing Agent Capabilities**: A clearer understanding of how to evaluate agents systematically can lead to more targeted improvements in agent architectures and capabilities. Decoupling the evaluation of the core LLM from the agent harness, for instance, can help pinpoint specific areas for enhancement, accelerating progress in planning, tool use, self-reflection, and memory mechanisms.
6. **Resource for Practitioners**: The "Benchmark Recommendations" section (Appendix E) provides practical guidance for practitioners navigating the extensive evaluation landscape, suggesting specific benchmarks based on environment dynamicity, agent modality, task difficulty, and interaction interface. This directly aids in effective benchmarking and development.

In summary, this survey serves as a vital reference point for the LLM-based agent community, facilitating a more informed, systematic, and forward-looking approach to the evaluation of these increasingly sophisticated AI systems.
