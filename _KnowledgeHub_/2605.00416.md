# Learning while Deploying: Fleet-Scale Reinforcement Learning for Generalist Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> The Learning While Deploying (LWD) framework enables generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) robot policies to continuously improve by leveraging fleet-scale reinforcement learning from real-world deployment experience. This system achieved an average success rate of 0.95 across eight manipulation tasks, including long-horizon scenarios, and reduced mean cycle time by 23.75 seconds compared to a supervised fine-tuning baseline.

## Problem

- Offline pretraining alone is insufficient for generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies to handle real-world distribution shifts, novel objects, and rare failure modes during deployment.
- Existing post-training methods often operate in slow collect-train-deploy cycles, rely on sample-inefficient on-policy data, or specialize generalist policies to narrow tasks.
- Leveraging heterogeneous data from a fleet of robots for continuous improvement of a single generalist policy presents challenges for robust value estimation and stable policy updates.

## Method

- A "Learning While Deploying (**LWD**)" framework that forms a closed-loop ==data flywheel==, allowing deployed robots to generate experience for continuous policy improvement at a fleet scale.
- ==Distributional Implicit Value Learning== (**DIVL**) for robust value estimation, which learns a distribution over replay action-values from heterogeneous, sparse-reward, and off-policy fleet data with an adaptive optimism strategy.
- ==Q-learning via Adjoint Matching== (**QAM**) for stable and efficient policy extraction within generative VLA architectures, translating reward-informed action gradients into local policy refinements.

## Results

- LWD (Online) achieved an average success rate of 0.95 across eight real-world manipulation tasks on a fleet of 16 dual-arm robots, which was the highest among all evaluated methods.
- Demonstrated substantial improvements on challenging long-horizon tasks, achieving an average step-wise score of 0.91, and reducing the mean cycle time by 23.75 seconds compared to Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).
- Ablation studies confirmed that Distributional Implicit Value Learning (DIVL) consistently outperformed scalar value learning (e.g., **16.7%** improvement in online long-horizon tasks), and the adaptive τ strategy further improved offline scores by **4%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuously learning from heterogeneous, fleet-scale real-world deployment experience is critical for generalist robot policies to achieve robust adaptation beyond offline pretraining.
> - Representing value as a distribution (DIVL) significantly enhances the robustness and informativeness of value estimates when dealing with complex, sparse-reward, and multi-modal fleet data, outperforming scalar value estimates.
> - Optimizing flow-based generative VLA policies can be effectively and stably guided by Q-learning via Adjoint Matching (QAM), which addresses the computational challenges of direct gradient backpropagation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robot policies, often leveraging Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, has been significantly advanced through large-scale pretraining on diverse offline datasets. These models, exemplified by architectures such as RT-1, RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA, π0, and π0.5, establish a foundational competence for robots to execute a wide array of tasks.

However, the authors identify a critical limitation: policies pretrained solely on offline data are often insufficient for robust real-world deployment. Real-world environments are dynamic, introducing distribution shifts, novel tasks, diverse object instances, varying user preferences, and rare failure modes that are not fully captured by static datasets. This necessitates policies that can continually improve from their deployment experience.

The concept of "deployment as a source of continual policy improvement" introduces the idea of a "data flywheel," where deployed robots generate experience on the target distribution, a shared policy improves from this aggregated data, and the enhanced policy is subsequently redeployed. For generalist robot policies, this experience is most effectively collected at "fleet scale," as individual robots sample only a limited portion of the overall deployment distribution, whereas a fleet can capture broad, heterogeneous experience including successes, failures, recoveries, partial progress, edge cases, and human interventions.

Existing approaches to policy improvement include:
- **Interactive Imitation Learning (e.g., HG-DAgger)**: These methods incorporate expert demonstrations or corrections during deployment but primarily treat this as a source of action labels for supervised learning. They may not fully leverage autonomous trials that involve successes, failures, or partial progress.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**: While RL can optimize policy behavior based on task outcomes, existing robotic RL approaches are often confined to small-scale, short-horizon, or task-specific scenarios. They frequently specialize a pretrained generalist policy to a narrow task rather than maintaining its generality.
- **Offline RL (e.g., π\*0.6, RLDG)**: These methods improve policies using previously collected rollouts. However, they follow a collect-train-deploy cycle, making adaptation to real-time distribution shifts slow.
- **Online RL (e.g., VLA-RL, RIPT)**: These approaches update pretrained policies directly from online rollouts. They can be sample-inefficient for real-world robots and are not designed to efficiently reuse large offline or off-policy deployment buffers. Furthermore, they may lack an explicit action-value critic to provide action-space gradients for policy improvement.
- **Offline-to-Online RL**: This hybrid approach combines pretraining on offline data with continuous online refinement. Prior works in this area often focus on task-specific policies, may employ inconsistent training objectives across offline and online stages, or operate at limited deployment scales. Methods like QAM (Q-learning with Adjoint Matching) have been shown to facilitate stable training for flow-based policies but have not been extensively validated for fleet-scale post-training of generalist VLA policies.

This work addresses the identified gap by proposing a scalable offline-to-online RL approach that can effectively reuse heterogeneous deployment data to stably improve a pretrained generative VLA policy across a fleet of robots, thereby preserving its generality. It aims to bridge the gap between large-scale pretraining and robust real-world deployment by enabling continuous, fleet-scale policy improvement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate Learning While Deploying (LWD), a fleet-scale offline-to-online reinforcement learning framework for the continual post-training of generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies.

The motivation for LWD stems from several detailed considerations:
- **Limitations of Static Offline Pretraining**: While large-scale offline pretraining provides a strong foundation for generalist VLA policies, real-world deployment inevitably introduces novel tasks, object instances, environmental configurations, user preferences, and rare failure modes that are not adequately represented in fixed demonstration datasets. Policies trained solely offline are thus not truly "deployment-ready" and require continuous adaptation.
- **Necessity for Continual Improvement from Deployment Experience**: To achieve high performance and robustness in dynamic real-world settings, robot policies must be capable of improving directly from their operational experience. This paradigm shifts deployment from being the final evaluation point of training to an ongoing source of data for policy enhancement.
- **Leveraging Fleet-Scale Data for Generalist Policies**: For generalist policies, experience collected from a single robot provides a narrow sample of the deployment distribution. A robot *fleet*, however, interacts across diverse tasks, environments, objects, and user instructions, generating a heterogeneous pool of experience that includes successes, failures, partial progress, critical edge cases, and human interventions. Aggregating this physical experience across a fleet offers a richer, more representative data source for policy improvement.
- **Establishing a Closed-Loop Data Flywheel**: The goal is to create a self-sustaining cycle where deployed robots generate experience, this aggregated data continually improves a shared policy, and the refined policy is then redeployed to gather even more informative experience, thus accelerating adaptation and robustness.
- **Addressing Shortcomings of Existing Learning Paradigms**:
 - **Interactive imitation learning** methods, while incorporating human feedback, primarily treat deployment as a source of action labels for supervised learning, underutilizing the valuable task outcomes (success/failure) and partial progress signals inherent in autonomous trials.
 - **Traditional Reinforcement Learning** approaches in robotics are often designed for small-scale, short-horizon, or task-specific problems, making them unsuitable for post-training large, end-to-end generalist VLA policies while preserving their broad capabilities.
 - **Offline-only post-training** methods, which operate in a collect-train-deploy cycle, are too slow to adapt to the rapid distribution shifts encountered in live deployment.
 - **On-policy online RL methods** are often sample-inefficient for real-world robots and are not optimized for leveraging the vast offline datasets or the off-policy nature of fleet deployment buffers. They also frequently lack an explicit action-value critic, which is crucial for deriving action-space gradients to guide policy improvement.
- **Algorithmic Challenges**: Successfully implementing this fleet-scale continual learning requires specific algorithmic advancements:
 - **Robust Value Learning**: The algorithm must produce reliable value estimates from heterogeneous, sparse-reward, and off-policy fleet data, which can exhibit multi-modal return distributions. A scalar value function might obscure critical high-return behaviors.
 - **Stable Policy Extraction**: It must translate learned values into improved actions within a large, generative VLA policy (which typically employs flow-based action generation) without destabilizing the model. Direct backpropagation of critic gradients through multi-step generative processes is computationally expensive and potentially unstable.
 - **Unified Training Objective**: Maintaining consistency in the RL objective across both offline pretraining and online finetuning stages is essential to mitigate issues like overly conservative offline critics and poor calibration for online value extrapolation.

LWD is motivated by the need for a practical, scalable, and robust framework that enables generalist robot policies to continuously learn and adapt in diverse, unstructured real-world environments, transforming deployment into an active component of the learning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LWD is structured as a two-stage offline-to-online training pipeline designed to continually improve generalist VLA policies using fleet-scale real-world experience.

**Framework Overview:**
The LWD framework initializes a VLA policy using previously collected human-demonstrated offline data. This policy is then deployed across a fleet of robots. Autonomous rollouts and human interventions gathered from this fleet form a shared physical experience buffer. This data is subsequently used to update the policy, which is then redeployed. This creates a closed-loop "data flywheel" for continuous improvement.

**Key Algorithmic Components:**

1. **Distributional Implicit Value Learning (DIVL) for Value Estimation:**
 - **Foundation**: DIVL builds upon Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) but replaces scalar expectile value regression with a distributional value model.
 - **Rationale**: Fleet deployment data is asynchronous and heterogeneous, leading to situations where the return for the same state-action pair can be multi-modal or heavy-tailed. A scalar critic might average these diverse outcomes, potentially obscuring rare but high-value behaviors. A distributional critic, in contrast, preserves the full distribution of returns.
 - **Mechanism**:
 - A distributional value model, Vψ(st), represents the state-conditioned distribution of dataset action-values (pψ(v | st)). This is implemented as a categorical discretization over a fixed support range.
 - This distribution is fitted by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of scalar critic targets derived from an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the critic network (Q¯ϕ).
 - For the TD target, the τ-quantile of Vψ(st) (Quantτ[Vψ(st)]) is used as the bootstrap statistic. This provides an in-distribution optimistic bootstrap signal without explicit action maximization.
 - The critic Qϕ is then trained to minimize the squared difference between its prediction and this TD target: yQ = rt + γH * Quantτ[Vψ(st+H)].
 - **Adaptive τ Strategy**: The optimism parameter, τ, is dynamically adjusted based on the normalized entropy of the predicted value distribution pψ(· | st+H). Higher entropy (indicating greater uncertainty) leads to a lower (more conservative) τ, while lower entropy leads to a higher (more optimistic) τ. This adaptive mechanism aims to calibrate the bootstrap target based on the confidence of the value estimate.

2. **Q-learning via Adjoint Matching (QAM) for Policy Extraction:**
 - **Challenge**: Directly backpropagating critic gradients through the multi-step denoising process of a flow-based VLA policy (like π0.5) is computationally expensive and prone to instability.
 - **Solution**: QAM reformulates the trajectory-level policy optimization into a local regression objective along the reference flow.
 - **Mechanism**:
 - The DIVL critic Qϕ provides a reward-informed action gradient (∇a Qϕ(s, a)) at the denoised action endpoint.
 - This gradient initializes the terminal adjoint state (˜g1).
 - The adjoint dynamics are then solved backward in time, which effectively translates the terminal gradient into step-wise supervision for the policy's vector field.
 - The flow policy (fθ) is updated by regressing towards these local targets, preserving the expressivity of generative action modeling while ensuring stable training.
 - A pretrained reference flow (fβ, derived from behavior cloning) is kept fixed, and fθ is optimized to improve upon it.

**Offline-to-Online Training Pipeline Stages:**

1. **Stage 1: Offline Pretraining**:
 - **Data Source**: A static offline buffer (Boff) comprising:
 - **Demonstrations**: Human-collected successful trajectories.
 - **Rollouts**: Data from historical policies, including both successes and failures.
 - **Play data**: Human-guided exploration of failure modes and edge cases.
 - **Objective**: The policy (πθ), critic (Qϕ), and distributional value model (Vψ) are initialized and fully fine-tuned using DIVL and QAM on Boff.
 - **N-step TD Target**: For long-horizon tasks, an n-step chunk-level TD target (n=10) is used to accelerate the propagation of sparse reward signals through the fixed offline buffer, especially when cold-starting the value functions. For short-horizon tasks, a 1-step chunk-level TD is used. This helps in initial learning of long-range dependencies.

2. **Stage 2: Continuous Online Post-training**:
 - **Initialization**: The offline-trained policy, critic, and value model parameters are used to initialize online training.
 - **Deployment & Data Collection**: The current policy checkpoint is deployed to a fleet of 16 dual-arm Agibot G1 robots. Robots execute the policy autonomously. If a rollout requires correction, human operators can intervene.
 - **Online Buffer (Bon)**: Both autonomous policy transitions and human intervention segments (treated as regular online replay with assigned rewards) are streamed asynchronously into Bon.
 - **Learning**: The learner continues to update Vψ, Qϕ, and fθ using DIVL and QAM, but now on a mixed replay buffer (Boff ∪ Bon) with an approximately balanced ratio of 1:1.
 - **1-step TD Target**: For online updates, a 1-step chunk-level TD target is used for all tasks. This is because online trajectories mix policy transitions with human interventions, and longer backups might cross these data sources, making longer TD paths less reliable for a single policy execution.
 - **Redeployment**: Updated policy checkpoints are periodically published back to the robot fleet, closing the data flywheel loop.
 - **Architecture during Online Update**: The VLM backbone of the policy is frozen, and only the action expert component is updated via QAM. The value and critic networks continue to be fully fine-tuned to adapt to the evolving replay distribution.

**Architectural Details:**
- **VLM Backbone**: Both the policy and value/critic networks utilize a shared Gemma3–SigLIP VLM backbone. The Gemma 3 language module and SigLIP vision encoder are initialized from publicly released checkpoints.
- **Value/Critic Networks**: They share a backbone, with separate prediction heads. A readout token's hidden state serves as the state representation. The value head predicts logits over a 201-atom categorical support. The critic conditions on the state representation and an encoded action chunk, employing a clipped double-Q design for robustness against overestimation.
- **Actor Policy**: Uses the π0.5 flow-based VLA architecture, consisting of a PaliGemma vision-language backbone and a Gemma-300M action expert for flow-based action generation.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is validated on a fleet of 16 dual-arm robots performing eight real-world manipulation tasks: four grocery restocking tasks (short-horizon, semantic generalization) and four long-horizon tasks (Gongfu Tea, Fruit Juice, Cocktail, Shoebox packing, requiring 3-5 minute executions with precise, contact-rich skills). Evaluation metrics include task-level success rates (binary for grocery, step-wise score for long-horizon) and cycle time for long-horizon tasks. The online experiments are conducted with a 4-hour wall-clock budget per method, corresponding to approximately 60 total hours of online data collection across the fleet.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluations demonstrate that Learning While Deploying (LWD) effectively improves generalist robot policies, particularly on complex, long-horizon tasks, by leveraging fleet-scale real-world experience.

**Overall Performance:**
- LWD (Online) achieved an average success rate of 0.95 across all eight evaluated real-world manipulation tasks, outperforming all baselines.

**Performance on Long-Horizon Tasks:**
- LWD (Online) showed its most significant gains on long-horizon tasks (Gongfu Tea, Fruit Juicing, Cocktail, Shoebox packing), achieving an average step-wise score of 0.91.
- This significantly surpassed the performance of:
 - SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning): 0.68
 - RECAP (Offline post-training): 0.77
 - HG-DAgger (Interactive imitation learning): 0.73
 - LWD (Offline): 0.79
- The improvements are attributed to LWD's consistent offline-to-online RL training pipeline and its comprehensive utilization of diverse data, including successful demonstrations, "play" data (exploring failure modes), and both successful and failed online trajectories. This enables the policy to continuously identify and mitigate deployment-encountered failure modes.
- HG-DAgger showed limited improvement or even performance degradation on some long-horizon tasks, which the authors suggest might be due to the variability inherent in human correction data and its limited exploration of the broader state space compared to RL. RL's ability to propagate sparse terminal success signals through multi-step TD backups was crucial for improving value estimation across different task stages, providing a stronger learning signal.

**Performance on Short-Horizon Tasks (Grocery Restocking):**
- On grocery restocking tasks, most methods (RECAP, HG-DAgger, LWD Offline, LWD Online) achieved high scores (e.g., LWD Online scored 1.00 on Restocking and Correction, and 0.97 on Freezer).
- Even in this high-performing regime, LWD (Online) maintained performance at or near the best results on every grocery task, indicating its benefits extend beyond long-horizon tasks while preserving the generalist capabilities of the shared policy.

**Execution Efficiency (Cycle Time):**
- LWD (Online) demonstrated a reduction in mean cycle time by 23.75 seconds on long-horizon tasks compared to the SFT reference policy. This improvement in efficiency is consistent with the critic-guided policy updates, which favor action chunks leading to reliable task progress, thereby reducing hesitations, retries, and unstable intermediate behaviors.

**Value Function Visualization:**
- Qualitative analysis of the learned value function during Gongfu Tea episodes showed that in successful trajectories, the value estimate generally increased as key sub-steps were completed, reflecting task progress despite sparse terminal rewards. In contrast, failing episodes exhibited lower and less progressive value estimates when the robot stopped making progress towards task milestones.

**Ablation Studies:**

1. **Value Learning Design (DIVL vs. Scalar Expectile Regression):**
 - DIVL outperformed a scalar expectile value regression baseline across all tasks.
 - The gains were more substantial on long-horizon tasks: a 9.7% increase in the offline stage and a 16.7% increase in the online stage.
 - This outcome supports the hypothesis that a distributional value function, by retaining the return distribution from heterogeneous fleet data, provides a more informative signal than a single averaged scalar value, particularly when rare but reproducible high-return behaviors exist.

2. **Adaptive τ Strategy:**
 - The adaptive τ strategy, which conditions the optimism parameter on the distributional entropy of the value function, improved the average offline score from 0.84 (with a constant τ) to 0.88.
 - While a constant τ baseline was competitive on some individual tasks, the adaptive schedule provided more consistent gains across the task suite, particularly on Restocking, Correction, and Cocktail tasks.
 - This suggests that dynamically adjusting τ based on uncertainty helps calibrate bootstrap optimism, leading to more robust and effective learning.

In summary, LWD effectively leverages fleet-scale real-world data and advanced RL techniques to significantly enhance generalist robot policies, particularly for challenging long-horizon tasks, while maintaining performance on shorter-horizon tasks and improving execution efficiency. The core algorithmic contributions of DIVL and QAM were found to be instrumental in achieving these results.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Learning While Deploying (LWD) framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic learning, offering a practical pathway towards the scalable deployment of continuously improving robot systems. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

- **Enabling Continual Learning in Real-World Deployment**: LWD fundamentally redefines the role of robot deployment. Instead of being merely an endpoint for evaluating a trained policy, deployment becomes an active and continuous mechanism for policy improvement. This paradigm shift addresses the inherent challenges of deploying robots in dynamic, unstructured real-world environments where fixed offline datasets are inherently incomplete.
- **Leveraging Fleet-Scale Experience for Generalization**: The framework's ability to aggregate heterogeneous experience from a distributed fleet of robots is critical. Individual robots encounter limited variations, but a fleet provides diverse data, including successes, failures, edge cases, and human interventions. This aggregated experience allows a *single generalist policy* to continually improve its robustness and generalization across a broad range of tasks and environments, which is essential for general-purpose robots.
- **Addressing Key Algorithmic Challenges**: LWD's algorithmic innovations, Distributional Implicit Value Learning (DIVL) and Q-learning via Adjoint Matching (QAM), are central to its success.
 - **DIVL** allows for robust value estimation from diverse, sparse-reward, and off-policy fleet data by capturing the multi-modal nature of returns, thereby providing a richer signal for policy improvement than scalar methods.
 - **QAM** provides a stable and efficient method for extracting improved actions from large, flow-based generative VLA policies, overcoming the computational and stability issues associated with direct backpropagation.
 This combination of robust value learning and stable policy extraction is a critical enabler for practical, large-scale RL in complex robotic systems.
- **Improved Performance and Efficiency on Complex Tasks**: The demonstrated significant performance gains on long-horizon manipulation tasks, coupled with reduced cycle times, indicates LWD's capability to learn and execute complex, multi-stage tasks more reliably and efficiently. This extends the practical applicability of generalist robot policies to domains requiring extended sequences of precise actions.
- **Unified Offline-to-Online Training**: Maintaining a consistent RL objective across both offline pretraining and online finetuning stages helps mitigate common issues like overly conservative offline critics and calibration mismatches, contributing to more stable and effective continuous learning.
- **A Concrete Step Towards General-Purpose Robots**: LWD offers a tangible approach to scaling robot learning systems for robust execution in unstructured environments. By demonstrating that deployment itself can fuel policy improvement, it provides a foundational step towards realizing truly general-purpose robots that can adapt and evolve autonomously in real-world scenarios.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The work also highlights limitations that indicate avenues for future research. These include optimizing online learning schedules for larger scales and long-term continual improvement, enhancing vision-language reasoning for complex task decomposition and error recovery, and explicitly incorporating safety-aware learning mechanisms. Addressing these aspects will further enhance the impact of such fleet-scale learning systems.

In conclusion, LWD significantly advances the state of the art in robot learning by presenting a verified framework for continuous, fleet-scale policy improvement. It has the potential to accelerate the development and widespread deployment of adaptable and intelligent robotic systems in diverse real-world applications.
