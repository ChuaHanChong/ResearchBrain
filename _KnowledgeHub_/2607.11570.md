# ERR@HRI 3.0 Challenge: Multimodal Detection of Errors and Anticipation in Human-Robot Interactions

> [!summary] Summary
> The ERR@HRI 3.0 Challenge established a benchmark for multimodal machine learning models to detect and anticipate robot errors from human behavioral signals. It provided raw, crowdsourced video datasets for both reactive and anticipatory tasks, demonstrating that participant models could outperform initial baselines on these challenging, naturalistic scenarios.

## Problem

- Robots need to detect and recover from errors to maintain user trust and ensure effective interaction in human-robot interaction (HRI).
- Existing HRI error detection systems often rely on task-specific knowledge, explicit user reports, or pre-extracted features, limiting their generalizability and ability for end-to-end learning.
- A lack of standardized benchmarking platforms and naturalistic, raw datasets hinders the development of robust, generalizable, and anticipatory error detection models for diverse real-world settings.

## Method

- The **ERR@HRI 3.0 Challenge** provided two crowdsourced datasets with raw, non-anonymized webcam videos: the **Bystander Affect Detection (BAD)** dataset for reactive error detection and the **Bad Idea** dataset for anticipatory error prediction.
- It defined three independent challenge tracks, including reactive detection, anticipatory prediction, and cross-dataset generalization, with ==subject-independent data splits== to evaluate model generalization to unseen individuals.
- Standardized evaluation metrics (e.g., ==Macro F1==, ==AUC-ROC==) and baseline models (==BadNet CNN==, fine-tuned ==ResNet-34==) were established to facilitate consistent comparison of multimodal machine learning approaches.

## Results

- The baseline model for reactive bystander reaction detection (Track 1) achieved a **Macro F1 of 0.502**, demonstrating difficulty in distinguishing control scenarios due to class imbalance, with an **F1 of 0.113** for the control class.
- For anticipatory error prediction (Track 2), the baseline model yielded an **AUC-ROC of 0.564**, indicating modest discriminative ability from subtle anticipatory facial behaviors in short video clips.
- All three participating teams successfully submitted models that surpassed the provided baselines in at least one challenge track, suggesting that more advanced or tailored approaches can achieve improved performance on these HRI tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Providing raw, non-anonymized video data enables end-to-end computer vision methods, fostering innovation beyond models reliant on pre-extracted, hand-crafted features.
> - Anticipatory error prediction, a key focus, allows for proactive robot intervention, shifting from error recovery to prevention, though baseline performance (AUC-ROC 0.564) suggests it is a challenging task.
> - The crowdsourced, naturalistic datasets expose models to real-world variability, which is crucial for developing robust HRI systems that can generalize beyond controlled laboratory settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of robots into human environments necessitates their ability to manage and recover from errors to maintain user trust and ensure effective interaction. The broader research landscape in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) has increasingly focused on developing methods for robots to detect their own mistakes. Early approaches often relied on task- or domain-specific knowledge, limiting their generalizability across different robot platforms, tasks, and contexts. Many systems also depended on explicit user reports of problems, which can introduce delays and hinder timely error recovery.

A significant area of investigation has involved leveraging human social signals—such as facial expressions, speech patterns, and body language—as implicit indicators of robot errors. This approach draws parallels with how humans infer errors from bystander reactions, recognizing that confusion, concern, or surprise can signal unexpected events. While prior research has demonstrated the feasibility of using these multimodal behavioral cues for error detection, several limitations have been identified. Most existing methods are reactive, focusing on recognizing that an error has already occurred and elicited an observable response. Furthermore, many studies have been conducted in controlled laboratory settings, which may not capture the variability encountered in real-world deployments. A common constraint has also been the reliance on pre-extracted features rather than raw sensor data, which limits the applicability of end-to-end machine learning methods.

Benchmarking efforts, characterized by shared datasets, tasks, and evaluation metrics, have proven instrumental in accelerating progress in various AI domains. In HRI, such sustained benchmarking has been less common, with recent efforts primarily targeting the perceptual and reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) rather than specific error detection. The ERR@HRI initiative, building on two prior editions, contributes to establishing a consistent benchmarking platform for multimodal machine learning models specifically for detecting robot errors from human behavioral signals. This third edition aims to address the aforementioned gaps by introducing anticipatory error prediction and providing raw, non-anonymized video data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of the ERR@HRI 3.0 Challenge were to:

1. **Facilitate end-to-end innovation in error detection and anticipation:** By providing raw, non-anonymized webcam video data, the challenge aimed to enable researchers to apply modern end-to-end computer vision methods, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), directly on pixel data. This contrasted with previous editions that provided pre-extracted features, which limited the scope of applicable machine learning techniques.
2. **Advance anticipatory error prediction:** A key shift in ERR@HRI 3.0 was the introduction of anticipatory error prediction alongside reactive error detection. The motivation was to move beyond merely recognizing errors *after* they occur, towards predicting potential errors *before* they fully manifest. This could enable proactive intervention by robots, leading to improvements in interaction quality and safety.
3. **Encourage robust and generalizable model development for naturalistic settings:** The datasets were collected via crowdsourcing, exposing models to real-world variability, including diverse lighting conditions, camera angles, participant positioning, and environmental backgrounds. This design choice aimed to create a more authentic testbed for developing robust error detection systems that can generalize to dynamic and unpredictable real-world HRI contexts, moving beyond the limitations of controlled laboratory settings.
4. **Provide a standardized benchmarking platform:** Continuing the ERR@HRI initiative, the challenge aimed to offer shared datasets, tasks, and metrics to allow the research community to compare different methods under consistent conditions, thereby accelerating progress in generalizable, multimodal error detection models for HRI.

In summary, the motivation stemmed from the recognized limitations of existing HRI error detection systems—namely, their reliance on pre-extracted features, focus on reactive detection, and performance primarily in controlled environments. ERR@HRI 3.0 sought to directly address these limitations by providing raw data for end-to-end learning and incorporating anticipatory behavior, all within a naturalistic data collection paradigm.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ERR@HRI 3.0 Challenge was structured around two complementary datasets, designed to promote the development of multimodal machine learning models for error detection and anticipation in HRI.

#### 3.1. Datasets

Both datasets were collected via crowdsourcing and released as raw, non-anonymized webcam video. This approach aimed to expose models to naturalistic variability.

- **Bystander Affect Detection (BAD) Dataset:**
 - **Participants:** 45 individuals.
 - **Stimulus:** 46 videos depicting robot and human failure scenarios (40 failure, 6 control).
 - **Data Content:** Webcam recordings of participants' spontaneous facial reactions while watching stimulus videos.
 - **Labels:** Binary (Failure: 1, Control: 0), derived from stimulus content.
 - **Temporal Focus:** Reactions *during* observed failures.
 - **Characteristics:** 1,645 video clips, total duration 25,527 seconds (average ∼15.5 seconds/clip), 30 fps.
 - **Imbalance:** Heavily skewed towards the Failure class (86.9%).

- **Bad Idea Dataset:**
 - **Participants:** 29 individuals.
 - **Stimulus:** 30 action scenario videos (robots or humans performing tasks), cut off before showing the outcome.
 - **Data Content:** Webcam recordings of participants' facial reactions as they predicted the outcome (well or poorly).
 - **Labels:** Binary (Well: 0, Poorly: 1), reflecting the participant's *predicted* outcome.
 - **Temporal Focus:** Anticipatory responses *before* failures occur.
 - **Characteristics:** 865 video clips, total duration 1,851 seconds (average ∼1.95 seconds/clip), 30 fps.
 - **Imbalance:** Close to balanced (Poorly: 47.4%, Well: 52.6%).

#### 3.2. Training and Test Sets

Datasets were split subject-independently, ensuring no participant appeared in both training/validation and test sets, to evaluate generalization to unseen individuals.
- **BAD Dataset:** 36 participants in Trainval (1,319 videos), 9 in Test (326 videos).
- **Bad Idea Dataset:** 23 participants in Trainval (685 videos), 6 in Test (180 videos).

#### 3.3. Challenge Tasks

Three independent tracks were offered:

- **Track 1: Bystander Reaction Detection (BAD dataset):** Binary classification of whether a participant is observing a failure scenario or a control scenario based on their webcam-recorded reaction.
- **Track 2: Anticipatory Response Prediction (Bad Idea dataset):** Binary classification of a participant's predicted outcome (well vs. poorly) from their anticipatory facial behavior recorded before the scenario's outcome was revealed.
- **Track 3: Cross-Dataset Generalization (Optional):** Encouraged exploration of transfer learning between the two datasets, evaluated separately.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics and Protocol

Predictions were submitted at the window level (fixed-length sliding windows).

- **Video-Level Aggregation:**
 - **Track 1:** Majority vote across windows (ties resolved to Failure).
 - **Track 2:** Maximum predicted probability for the positive class (Poorly) across windows, selected to reward brief, decisive anticipatory signals.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Track 1 (Primary):** Macro F1 (video level), with balanced accuracy as a tiebreaker, to account for class imbalance.
 - **Track 2 (Primary):** AUC-ROC (video level), chosen for its threshold-free nature.
 - **Secondary Metrics (both tracks, window and video level):** F1 for each class, precision, recall, accuracy, and the complementary primary metric of the other track.
 - **Temporal Metrics (positive-class clips only):** Earliest Detection Time (average percentage of clip elapsed before first correct positive window prediction) and False Negative Rate (FNR) per video (average fraction of positive-labeled windows missed).

#### 3.5. Challenge Baselines

Baselines were provided with reference code. Both baselines used weighted loss for class imbalance and were trained with inter-participant cross-validation.

- **Track 1 Baseline (BadNet):** A BadNet convolutional architecture trained on 5 fps frames extracted from raw videos.
 - Hyperparameters: sigmoid activation, kernel size 8, 64 base filters, dropout 0.7, learning rate 0.0001, batch size 32, 350 epochs, window size 5 frames, slide 2 frames (at 5 fps).
- **Track 2 Baseline (Fine-Tuned ResNet-34):** A ResNet-34 backbone (pretrained on ImageNet) fine-tuned on 30 fps Bad Idea frames.
 - Hyperparameters: dropout 0.7, learning rate 0.001, batch size 64, 100 epochs, window size 10 frames, slide 2 frames (at 30 fps).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The ERR@HRI 3.0 Challenge evaluated submitted models against established baselines, providing insights into the difficulty of the tasks and the effectiveness of current approaches.

#### 4.1. Baseline Performance

The provided baselines demonstrated varying levels of performance on the held-out test sets:

- **Track 1 (BAD Dataset - Bystander Reaction Detection):**
 - **Primary Metric (Macro F1):** 0.502
 - **Balanced Accuracy:** 0.502
 - **Earliest Detection Time:** 8.8%
 - **False Negative Rate (FNR):** 0.122
 - **Per-Class F1:** F1 for the Failure class (F1$_{\text{err}}$) was 0.892, while F1 for the Control class (F1$_{\text{cont}}$) was 0.113.
 - **Interpretation:** The baseline performed marginally above chance on Macro F1, indicative of the difficulty in distinguishing failure from control scenarios, particularly given the strong class imbalance in the BAD dataset. The highly asymmetric per-class F1 scores suggest the model frequently defaulted to predicting the majority Failure class, struggling to correctly identify control scenarios.

- **Track 2 (Bad Idea Dataset - Anticipatory Response Prediction):**
 - **Primary Metric (AUC-ROC):** 0.564
 - **F1-macro:** 0.561
 - **Balanced Accuracy:** 0.572
 - **Earliest Detection Time:** 35.6%
 - **False Negative Rate (FNR):** 0.384
 - **Per-Class F1:** F1 for the positive (Poorly) class (F1$_{\text{pos}}$) was 0.559, and for the negative (Well) class (F1$_{\text{neg}}$) was 0.563.
 - **Interpretation:** The baseline showed more balanced per-class performance, consistent with the near-balanced label distribution of the Bad Idea dataset. However, an AUC-ROC of 0.564 suggests only a modest discriminative ability when relying solely on anticipatory facial behavior. The higher Earliest Detection Time (35.6%) and FNR (0.384) compared to Track 1 indicate the challenge of extracting consistent and reliable anticipatory signals from very short video clips. These temporal metrics in Track 2 are better understood as measures of prediction consistency across windows rather than genuine detection latency.

#### 4.2. Challenge Participation and Outcomes

- A total of 9 teams from 6 countries registered to participate in the challenge.
- Three teams successfully submitted valid models across the challenge's tracks.
- **All three submitting teams surpassed the provided baselines in at least one track.** This indicates that while the baselines provided a reference point, more advanced or tailored approaches were able to achieve improved performance on the challenge tasks. Specific winning model details were not provided in this overview paper.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ERR@HRI 3.0 Challenge contributes to the field of human-robot interaction and machine learning in several ways, with implications for future research and development.

#### 5.1. Advancing Error Detection and Anticipation

By introducing anticipatory error prediction alongside reactive detection, the challenge expands the temporal scope of error management in HRI. Enabling robots to predict potential errors before they manifest fully could facilitate proactive interventions, leading to smoother interactions, improved safety, and higher user trust. This represents a shift from error *recovery* to error *prevention*.

#### 5.2. Promoting End-to-End Learning with Raw Data

The decision to provide raw, non-anonymized webcam video datasets marks a significant step towards enabling end-to-end machine learning approaches in HRI error detection. This allows researchers to bypass the limitations of pre-extracted, hand-crafted features, fostering innovation in models that can learn directly from pixel data. This approach is aligned with advancements in computer vision, where end-to-end learning has demonstrated superior performance and generalizability.

#### 5.3. Fostering Robustness in Naturalistic Settings

The crowdsourced nature of the datasets, capturing variability in lighting, camera angles, and participant positioning, creates a more realistic testbed for developing robust HRI systems. Models developed and evaluated under these conditions are more likely to generalize to the diverse and unpredictable environments of real-world robot deployment, addressing a common limitation of studies conducted in controlled laboratory settings.

#### 5.4. Establishing Benchmarks and Resources

The ERR@HRI 3.0 Challenge, as part of a recurring initiative, contributes to building a standardized benchmarking platform. By releasing datasets, baseline code, and results, the challenge provides durable resources for the community. This allows researchers to compare new methods against established benchmarks, accelerating progress and fostering a shared understanding of performance metrics and challenges in multimodal error detection for HRI. The subject-independent splits further ensure that models are evaluated on their ability to generalize to unseen individuals, a critical aspect for real-world applicability.

#### 5.5. Future Directions

The initiative plans to continue expanding the range of interaction contexts, robot embodiments, and temporal phases of error covered in future editions. This iterative approach aims to progressively develop more adaptive, context-aware, and anticipatory error detection systems that can operate reliably across the complex and diverse conditions of human-robot interaction, ultimately contributing to the successful and safe integration of robots into society.
