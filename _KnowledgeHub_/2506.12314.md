# Explosive Output to Enhance Jumping Ability: A Variable Reduction Ratio Design Paradigm for Humanoid Robots Knee Joint

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a variable reduction ratio knee joint (EVRR-K) for electrically driven humanoid robots, addressing the mismatch between motor characteristics and kinematic demands during explosive jumping. The system enabled a 1-DOF platform to achieve a 63 cm vertical jump, showing a 28.1% improvement over optimal fixed-ratio joints, and allowed a full-scale humanoid to perform 0.5 m vertical and 1.1 m forward jumps.

## Problem

- Electrically driven humanoid robots exhibit limited jumping capabilities (0.3-0.5 m) compared to hydraulic systems (>0.6 m) and humans.
- During a jump, the increasing knee-to-center-of-mass transmission ratio conflicts with electric motor characteristics, which perform optimally at mid-speeds but degrade significantly at high speeds due to power losses.
- Fixed-ratio knee joints struggle to provide both high initial torque and sustained high power output during the entire takeoff phase, leading to inefficient motor utilization.

## Method

- A novel ==Explosive Variable Reduction Ratio Knee (EVRR-K)== joint design paradigm was proposed, featuring a reduction ratio that dynamically decreases as the knee joint extends.
- A compact linear actuator-driven guide-rod mechanism was engineered to implement the variable reduction ratio with high parametric tunability.
- An optimization framework was developed to select mechanical parameters that maximize takeoff energy and jump height while keeping the motor operating within its high-power, low-loss region.

## Results

- A 1-DOF leg-like platform equipped with the EVRR-K joint achieved a vertical jump height of 63 cm, demonstrating a **28.1%** theoretical improvement over optimal fixed-ratio joints.
- A full-scale 45 kg humanoid robot (BHR8-J1) successfully performed a **0.5** m vertical jump, a **1.1** m forward jump, and a **0.5** m box jump.
- The EVRR-K design kept the knee motor speed under 3000 rpm, avoiding high power-loss regions, while enabling peak knee joint torque of 286 Nm and peak power of **1.5** kW during box jumping.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically decreasing the reduction ratio during joint extension effectively aligns the motor's operating characteristics with the kinematic demands of jumping, optimizing power delivery.
> - Maintaining the motor speed below 3000 rpm (outside the high power-loss region) is crucial for maximizing sustained power output and achieving higher jump performance in electrically driven systems.
> - The proposed guide-rod mechanism offers a compact and efficient method to achieve a variable reduction ratio, allowing for precise control over the knee joint's torque-speed profile.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Jumping is identified as a critical locomotion capability for humanoid robots, enabling navigation of complex terrains and overcoming obstacles. Historically, advanced jumping performance in humanoids, such as that demonstrated by Boston Dynamics' Atlas robot, relied on hydraulic actuation. While hydraulic systems offer high power density, they present challenges including leakage, noise, cost, and weight, leading to a shift towards electrically driven designs.

Electrically driven humanoid robots, primarily using Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSMs), offer advantages such as high efficiency, precise control, low noise, and lightweight construction. However, their jumping capabilities generally lag behind those of hydraulic systems and humans. Current electrically driven humanoids, as summarized in Table I of the paper, exhibit vertical jump heights ranging from 0.3 m to 0.5 m, which is lower than the 0.6 m-plus achieved by the hydraulic Atlas.

Existing electrically driven knee joint designs fall into rotary and linear actuator categories. Rotary actuators often use planetary gearboxes, harmonic drives, or cycloidal drives, with variations like hip-mounted motors with linkages (e.g., Unitree H1, electric Atlas) to reduce leg inertia or smaller robot designs (e.g., Unitree G1, MIT humanoid) to lower knee loading. Cable-driven systems (e.g., JAXON-3P) also exist. While these designs enhance dynamic performance, their explosive jumping capability remains constrained. Robots like Cassie utilize parallel RV reducers and elastic elements, achieving 0.5 m vertical jumps, but their leg volume can limit terrain adaptability, and adding an upper body significantly increases weight. Linear actuators (e.g., Optimus, Lola, RH5) prioritize torque output for stable walking but are generally not designed for explosive jumping.

A fundamental challenge identified is the mismatch between the knee-to-center-of-mass (CoM) transmission ratio, which increases with joint extension, and the motor's ideal operating characteristics during a jump (high torque initially, followed by high speed). Motors typically exhibit peak torque at low speeds and sufficient power in a mid-speed range, but their performance degrades significantly at high speeds due to rising power losses. Fixed or minimally varying reduction ratios in conventional joints cannot effectively address this mismatch, leading to underutilization of motor potential. Small-scale robots have explored parallel springs, parallel joint mechanisms, and momentum-boosting techniques, but scaling these to full-sized humanoids faces structural complexity, transmission chain length, energy density limits, and control difficulties.

This work directly addresses these limitations by proposing a novel knee joint design for electrically driven humanoids that aims to overcome the inherent trade-offs of fixed-ratio systems and enable higher explosive power output, thereby bridging the performance gap with hydraulic systems and human capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the explosive power output of humanoid robot knee joints to improve their agility and obstacle-crossing capabilities, specifically focusing on jumping performance. This objective is motivated by several identified challenges:

1. **Mismatch in Transmission Ratio and Jumping Demands:** During the takeoff phase of a jump, the transmission ratio from the knee joint to the robot's center of mass (CoM) increases as the knee joint extends. This kinematic characteristic requires high torque at the initiation of the jump (when the knee is flexed) and high speed towards the end of the jump (when the knee is extending rapidly).
2. **Motor Performance Limitations:** Electrically driven permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSMs), which are the mainstream actuators in humanoids, have distinct performance envelopes. They deliver peak torque at low speeds and high power in a mid-speed range, but their power output degrades significantly at high speeds due to increased power losses (copper, iron, and mechanical losses).
3. **Conflict between Kinematic Demands and Motor Capabilities:** The increasing knee-to-CoM transmission ratio during extension, coupled with the motor's performance degradation at high speeds, creates a conflict. Fixed-ratio knee joints cannot simultaneously provide high initial torque and sustain high power output at later, higher speeds, leading to inefficient exploitation of motor potential and limited jump performance. A low fixed ratio might provide speed but lack initial torque, while a high fixed ratio might offer initial torque but demand impractical motor speeds later, leading to high power losses.
4. **Desire for Compact and Lightweight Design:** High-power motors are often large and heavy, increasing the rotational inertia of the knee joint and reducing leg flexibility and responsiveness. The motivation is to achieve explosive power output without compromising compactness or adding excessive weight.
5. **Performance Gap with Hydraulic and Human Capabilities:** Current electrically driven humanoids exhibit significantly lower jumping capabilities compared to hydraulic robots (like Boston Dynamics' Atlas) and humans. The motivation is to develop a knee joint design that helps narrow this performance gap.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes a novel knee joint design paradigm called the Explosive Variable Reduction Ratio Knee (EVRR-K). The core objective is to dynamically decrease the reduction ratio as the knee joint extends during a jump. This strategy aims to:
- Utilize a high initial reduction ratio to rapidly increase torque at the jump's initiation, providing strong initial acceleration.
- Gradually reduce the ratio as the joint extends, minimizing the increment in motor speed and mitigating power losses, thereby maintaining sustained high-power output throughout the entire takeoff phase.
- Achieve this through a compact and efficient mechanical implementation (linear actuator-driven guide-rod mechanism) and an optimized design approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves a three-pronged approach: establishing a variable reduction ratio strategy based on motor and kinematic analysis, designing a mechanism to implement this strategy, and optimizing its parameters for maximal jumping performance.

**A. EVRR-K Strategy for Explosive Jumps (Section II)**
1. **Motor Output Characteristics Analysis:** The authors analyzed PMSM power loss (copper, iron, mechanical) and performance envelopes. They noted that peak output is thermally limited at low speeds (constant peak torque), power-limited at mid-speeds (constant peak power), and steeply declines at high speeds due to rapid power loss. The strategy aims to keep the motor operating near the mid-speed region to avoid thermal and excessive power-loss constraints.
2. **Kinematic Analysis of the Knee Joint During Jumping:** A simplified leg model (knee as main actuated joint, hip/ankle passive) was used to analyze the knee's contribution to CoM propulsion. The transmission ratio $\lambda(q_2)$ from the knee joint angular velocity to the CoM y-axis velocity was derived as inversely proportional to the Jacobian component $J_{CoM,y}(q_2)$. This analysis showed that $\lambda(q_2)$ increases with knee joint angle $q_2$ (joint extension), sharply rising after $q_2$ exceeds -1 rad.
3. **Variable Reduction Ratio Proposal:** Based on the kinematic analysis showing an increasing knee-to-CoM transmission ratio and motor limitations at high speeds, the paper proposes an EVRR-K strategy. This strategy involves a reduction ratio that progressively decreases as the knee joint extends. This allows for high initial torque at the beginning of the jump and keeps the motor operating in its high-power region by preventing excessive motor speeds during later, rapid extension phases.

**B. Mechanical Design (Section III)**
1. **Actuator Choice:** A linear actuator driven by a high-efficiency, dual-lead ball screw (10 mm lead) is selected for its ability to generate knee joint rotation.
2. **Guide-Rod Mechanism:** A compact guide-rod mechanism is designed to implement the variable reduction ratio. The mechanism's geometry dictates the relationship between the joint angle ($q_2$) and the reduction ratio ($k$). Key parameters include crank length ($r$), frame length ($S_0$), and the ball screw lead ($Q$).
3. **Parameter Tunability:** The mathematical relationship for the reduction ratio $k$ (Eq. 7) shows that varying $r$ changes the peak reduction ratio, while adjusting $S_0$ shifts the joint angle where the maximum reduction ratio occurs. An additional angular offset ($\Delta\theta$) can be introduced during assembly for further tuning, allowing the mechanism to achieve a monotonically decreasing reduction ratio in the desired working range.

**C. Joint Parameter Optimization Based on Explosive Jumping Control (Section IV)**
1. **Explosive Control Strategy:** An explosive control strategy is implemented, driving the joint to operate at its maximum available torque. This strategy considers motor torque limits (peak torque at low speeds, power-limited torque at higher speeds) and then calculates joint torque output.
2. **Jump Dynamics and Energy Calculation:** The CoM velocity and acceleration are derived from the knee joint motion. The robot enters free flight when CoM acceleration falls below $-g$. The total mechanical energy at takeoff ($W_{takeoff}$) is calculated as the sum of kinetic and potential energy.
3. **Optimization Objective:** The objective is to maximize the robot's jump height by maximizing the takeoff energy ($W_{takeoff}$). This is formulated as a minimization problem of $-W_{takeoff}$.
4. **Design Parameters and Constraints:** The optimization parameters are the mechanical design parameters $r$, $S_0$, and $\Delta\theta$, subject to realistic structural constraints (min/max ranges for each).
5. **Optimization Algorithm:** Algorithm 1 outlines the selection process: iterate through a set of design parameters, simulate a high-burst jump for each, record takeoff energy, and identify the parameter set yielding the maximum takeoff energy.
6. **Optimization Results:** For a full-scale humanoid robot model ($l_1=l_2=0.45m$, $m_1=2.5kg$, $m_2=5kg$, $m_3=20kg$) and a TQ-8526sp motor, optimization was performed for various initial knee joint angles. The EVRR-K consistently achieved greater theoretical jump heights than an optimal fixed-ratio knee (FRR-K), with an improvement of up to 28.9%. The optimal EVRR-K reduction ratio curve was found to decrease monotonically over the working range, maintaining a nearly constant transmission ratio from motor to CoM, and keeping motor speeds below 3000 rpm, thereby avoiding high power-loss regions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. 1-DOF Leg-like Platform Experiments**
1. **Platform Configuration:** A 1-DOF platform was constructed to isolate and validate the EVRR-K knee joint. It was actuated by a single EVRR-K joint (TQ-ILM8526sp motor, 10 mm lead ball screw) with optimized parameters ($S_0 = 0.259$ m, $r = 0.047$ m). The platform had a total weight of 24.93 kg (including a 20 kg load).
2. **Control System:** A NUC8 computer and Elmo driver enabled real-time control (1 kHz). Jump control involved a force-controlled PD algorithm to set the initial angle, followed by applying peak current ($I_q = 92$ A) until takeoff, then PD control for mid-air and landing.
3. **Jumping Performance:** The platform achieved a vertical jump height of **63 cm**. This represents a theoretical improvement of 28.1% over optimal fixed-ratio joints, supporting the numerical simulation prediction of 28.9%.
4. **Motor and Joint Characteristics:**
 - **Current Tracking:** The actual motor $q$-axis current closely followed the command current for most of the motion, but tracking degraded in the later phase of takeoff as increasing motor speed reduced torque output capability.
 - **Torque and Speed:** The knee joint exhibited a peak torque of **250 Nm** and a peak speed of **15 rad/s**.
 - **Motor Operation:** The motor torque-speed trajectory largely remained within its performance envelope, with maximum speed around **3000 rpm**, successfully avoiding the high power-loss region.
 - **Power Output:** Joint power rapidly increased to a peak output of **1.5 kW** during takeoff and was largely sustained until the takeoff angle. The motor temporarily exceeded its rated peak power and specified performance envelope in the late takeoff stage, indicating its overload capability.
 - **Efficiency:** The design allowed the motor to operate in a region of sustained high power output, demonstrating the method's effectiveness.

**B. Full-Scale Humanoid Robot Jumping Experiment**
1. **Robot Configuration:** The proposed EVRR-K design was integrated into a full-scale 14-DOF humanoid robot, BHR8-J1, with a total mass of 45 kg. The robot's knee and ankle joints used ball screws, while hip joints used harmonic drives or planetary gearboxes.
2. **Jumping Performance:** BHR8-J1 successfully performed:
 - A **0.5 m vertical jump**.
 - A **1.1 m forward jump**.
 - A **0.5 m box jump**.
3. **Joint Power Distribution:** Across all jumping tasks, the knee joint consistently demanded the highest torque, speed, and power compared to the hip and ankle joints.
 - **Hip and Ankle Joints:** Peak torque remained below 200 Nm, peak speed below 12 rad/s, and peak power under 1.2 kW.
 - **Knee Joint:** During a 0.5 m box jump, the knee reached **286 Nm** torque, **15.5 rad/s** speed, and **1.5 kW** power (motor peak at 72 V).
4. **Motor Speed Control:** The variable reduction ratio successfully kept the knee motor speed under **3000 rpm**, thereby avoiding the high power-loss region. For comparison, a fixed-ratio joint matching the same torque (e.g., ratio of 30) would require motor speeds exceeding 4500 rpm, which falls into the high power-loss region and near no-load speed.
5. **Further Potential:** The results indicated that the robot did not always operate at its maximum capacity, and the current control and trajectory optimization methods used in the full-scale robot did not fully exploit the advantages of the variable reduction ratio, suggesting potential for further performance improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant development in enhancing the explosive power output and jumping capabilities of electrically driven humanoid robots. The core significance lies in its proposed Variable Reduction Ratio Knee (EVRR-K) design paradigm, which directly addresses long-standing challenges in achieving high dynamic performance with electric actuators.

**Key areas of significance include:**

- **Overcoming Actuation Mismatch:** The primary impact is the effective resolution of the kinematic mismatch between the knee-to-CoM transmission ratio during a jump and the optimal operating characteristics of electric motors. By dynamically decreasing the reduction ratio as the joint extends, the design enables a high initial torque for rapid acceleration and maintains the motor within its high-power, lower-loss operating region throughout the jump, avoiding the high-speed, high-loss limitations of fixed-ratio systems.
- **Performance Improvement in Electric Humanoids:** The experimental results demonstrate a notable improvement in jumping performance. The 63 cm vertical jump on a 1-DOF platform (28.1% theoretical improvement over optimal fixed-ratio joints) and the successful 0.5 m vertical jump, 1.1 m long jump, and 0.5 m box jump on a full-scale humanoid robot (BHR8-J1) represent competitive achievements for electrically driven systems. This moves closer to the performance levels previously only attainable by hydraulic robots and contributes to narrowing the gap with human-level agility.
- **Compact and Efficient Design:** The proposed linear actuator-driven guide-rod mechanism offers a compact and efficient solution for implementing the variable reduction ratio. This is crucial for humanoid robots where weight and size are critical constraints for agility and responsiveness. The high parametric flexibility of the mechanism allows for precise tuning of the reduction ratio curve.
- **Enhanced Agility and Mobility:** Improved jumping capabilities directly translate to enhanced agility and mobility for humanoid robots. This enables them to traverse complex, uneven, or obstacle-laden terrains more effectively, which is critical for real-world applications in disaster response, logistics, and exploration.
- **Foundation for Future Robotics Research:** This work provides a validated design paradigm and an optimization framework that can serve as a foundation for future research in high-dynamic-performance robotic joints. It highlights the potential of variable ratio transmissions in optimizing actuator utilization.

**Potential impacts include:**

- **Advancements in Humanoid Locomotion:** The ability of humanoids to perform significant jumps opens new avenues for dynamic locomotion research, potentially leading to more versatile and robust robots.
- **Application in Diverse Environments:** Robots equipped with enhanced jumping capabilities could be deployed in environments challenging for traditional wheeled or walking robots, such as rubble-strewn disaster zones or construction sites.
- **Inspiration for Other Robotic Systems:** The principles of dynamic reduction ratio optimization could be extended to other robotic platforms requiring bursts of power or needing to manage motor performance across a wide range of speeds and torques.

The authors acknowledge that the crucial role of the ankle joint in humanoid jumping dynamics was not fully addressed, and future work will focus on integrating advanced ankle joint technologies. This suggests ongoing potential for further optimization and performance gains based on the variable reduction ratio paradigm.
