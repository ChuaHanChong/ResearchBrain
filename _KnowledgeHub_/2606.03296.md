# Bridging Predictive Uncertainty and Safe Action: Sample-Conditioned Differentiable Planning for Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and collaborators developed a sample-conditioned differentiable planning framework that integrates a conditional diffusion model for probabilistic trajectory prediction with an optimization-based motion planner for autonomous driving. The system achieved a 96.00% success rate and a 0.96% collision rate on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset, significantly outperforming baselines by incorporating multi-modal uncertainty into safety-critical decisions.

## Problem

- Traditional model-based methods struggle to represent complex, multi-modal uncertainties in surrounding agent behavior due to restrictive analytical assumptions.
- Existing learning-based integrated prediction-planning systems often discard rich uncertainty information by condensing predictions into single, deterministic future trajectories.
- End-to-end learning architectures for autonomous driving lack interpretability and explicit safety guarantees, making verification and constraint enforcement challenging.

## Method

- A framework integrating a conditional ==diffusion== model for probabilistic, multi-modal trajectory prediction of surrounding agents.
- A differentiable motion planner that explicitly incorporates multiple predicted future trajectories using an empirical Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) tail-risk constraint to manage safety uncertainty.
- A novel directed graph representation for scene context encoding, designed to improve computational efficiency and capture global topological relationships compared to vectorized maps.

## Results

- The framework achieved a **96.00%** success rate in closed-loop testing on WOMD (**43.28%** improvement over DIPP) and reduced collision rates by **74.12%** (**0.96%** collision rate) compared to DIPP in open-loop tests.
- Prediction accuracy improved substantially, with minADE of 0.64 m and minFDE of 0.50 m on WOMD, marking a **20.99%** and **74.09%** improvement over DIPP, respectively.
- The directed graph for scene context reduced average training time per epoch by **69.7%** and improved prediction accuracy (e.g., **8.3%** minADE reduction) compared to vectorized maps, while enabling larger batch sizes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly propagating multi-modal uncertainty from diffusion-based predictions into a differentiable planner via CVaR constraints effectively enhances safety by accounting for rare, high-risk scenarios.
> - Joint end-to-end training of prediction and planning modules, enabled by full differentiability, allows planning objectives to directly inform and refine the prediction model and learnable cost weights.
> - Utilizing a directed graph for scene context encoding significantly improves computational efficiency (e.g., 69.7% faster training per epoch) and predictive accuracy compared to traditional vectorized maps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous driving in complex, dynamic, and interactive traffic environments presents challenges due to the inherent uncertainty in the future behaviors of surrounding agents (SAs). Addressing this uncertainty effectively during motion planning is crucial for ensuring safety without resorting to overly conservative driving strategies.

Historically, approaches to managing this uncertainty have largely fallen into two categories:
- **Traditional Model-Based Methods**: These methods, such as probabilistic reachable sets or chance-constrained model predictive control, rely on explicit modeling of environmental dynamics and mathematical optimization. However, their reliance on restrictive analytical assumptions, such as Gaussian noise or unimodal distributions, limits their capacity to represent the complexities of real-world traffic.
- **Learning-Based Methods**: These methods excel at capturing complex distributions directly from large datasets. Recent advancements in diffusion-based generative models have demonstrated capabilities in modeling the joint distribution of multi-agent trajectories, generating diverse and realistic future scenarios.

Despite the progress in learning-based prediction, a critical gap persists: effectively propagating the rich uncertainty information from the prediction stage into the planning stage. Current learning-based approaches often exhibit limitations:
- **End-to-End Architectures**: While able to directly decode ego-vehicle (EV) plans from sensor inputs, these systems often operate as "black boxes," lacking physical interpretability. This opacity complicates the enforcement of strict safety constraints and post-hoc verification, which are critical for safety-critical applications.
- **Differentiable Optimization Frameworks**: Efforts to restore interpretability by embedding differentiable optimization modules into learning pipelines have been made (e.g., Differentiable Integrated Prediction and Planning, DIPP). However, these frameworks typically condense predicted futures into a single deterministic estimate before planning. This "deterministic bottleneck" discards valuable uncertainty information, potentially compromising safety in interactive scenarios where anticipating rare but risky evolutions is important.

This research aims to address the aforementioned limitations by proposing a framework that explicitly integrates the probabilistic predictions from diffusion models with interpretable, optimization-based planning. The work positions itself as a bridge between highly expressive uncertainty modeling and safe, differentiable motion planning, seeking to overcome the deterministic bottleneck of prior differentiable planning methods and the interpretability issues of black-box end-to-end systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a sample-conditioned differentiable planning framework that explicitly incorporates probabilistic future trajectories, generated by diffusion models, into the motion optimization process for autonomous driving. This aims to enable robust and safe navigation in complex and uncertain traffic environments.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Addressing Pervasive Uncertainty**: Autonomous vehicles operate in highly dynamic environments where the future actions of surrounding agents are inherently uncertain. Traditional planning methods struggle to account for the full spectrum of these uncertainties due to restrictive assumptions, leading to either overly conservative or potentially unsafe behaviors.
- **Leveraging Advanced Prediction Capabilities**: Recent advancements in learning-based models, particularly diffusion models, have enabled the generation of diverse and realistic future scenarios for multiple agents. However, effectively using this rich, multi-modal uncertainty information in downstream planning remains a challenge. Existing integrated prediction-planning systems often discard this diversity by collapsing predictions into a single, deterministic future.
- **Ensuring Interpretability and Safety Guarantees**: While end-to-end deep learning models can perform both prediction and planning, their black-box nature hinders the verification of safety and the enforcement of explicit physical constraints, which are critical for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving. The integration of differentiable optimization is motivated by the need for interpretable decision-making processes where safety constraints can be explicitly managed.
- **Mitigating Tail-Risk Scenarios**: In safety-critical applications, it is not sufficient to merely predict the most likely future. The planning system must also account for rare, yet highly hazardous, outcomes. The paper is motivated to develop a mechanism that specifically addresses these "tail-risk" scenarios during trajectory optimization.
- **Improving Computational Efficiency for Scene Context**: Traditional vectorized map representations can lead to high computational overhead and may not adequately capture global topological relationships between lane segments. The work is motivated to introduce a more efficient and effective scene context representation to enhance both predictive effectiveness and computational performance.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for an autonomous driving system that can robustly handle multi-modal uncertainty from surrounding agents, maintain interpretability and safety, specifically address high-risk scenarios, and achieve computational efficiency, ultimately bridging the current disconnect between expressive uncertainty modeling and safe, actionable motion planning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, termed "sample-conditioned differentiable planning," integrates a conditional diffusion model for probabilistic trajectory prediction with a differentiable motion planner.

**A. Diffusion-Based Prediction:**
1. **Input Representation**: The model takes two main input categories:
 - **Historical Agent States**: Dynamic features (2D coordinates, orientation, velocity) for the preceding 20 timesteps (H=20) of the ego vehicle and up to 10 surrounding agents. These are processed by an LSTM encoder to generate motion features.
 - **Scene Context**: Information regarding lane segments and pedestrian crossings. All positional data is transformed into an EV-centric local coordinate frame.
2. **Concatenated Observation Embedding**:
 - **Scene Context Encoding**: Instead of vectorized maps, a novel **directed graph representation** is used for lane context. Lane segments act as graph nodes, and edges are established based on driving directions and traffic regulations, capturing global topological relationships (Fig. 3). This graph is encoded using Graph Attention Networks (GATs). Crosswalk elements are encoded by a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). This approach aims to reduce computational overhead and enhance global context fusion.
 - **Agent and Agent–Map Feature Modeling**: Interdependencies are modeled using Transformers. A two-layer self-attention Transformer models agent-agent interactions. Two multi-head cross-attention encoders model agent-lane and agent-crosswalk interactions.
 - **Concatenation**: Motion features, agent interaction features, and agent-scene interaction features are concatenated to form the observation embedding (O_t), preserving temporal dynamics.
3. **Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) for Prediction**: A transformer-based DDPM is employed to approximate the conditional distribution p(A_t | O_t) for agent actions. The model iteratively denoises action sequences based on Langevin dynamics (Eq. 1), effectively learning to predict the gradient field of the action-score function (Eq. 2). During inference, this allows the generation of multiple (M) diverse and plausible future action sequences for SAs, which are then used to derive their predicted states. The model also outputs an initial control sequence for the EV by feeding its predicted action sequence into an MLP.

**B. Sample-Conditioned Differentiable Planning:**
1. **Planning Problem Formulation**: The motion planning is formulated as an open-loop, finite-horizon optimal control problem (Eq. 5). The objective is to determine an EV control sequence (u) that minimizes a composite cost function J, defined as a weighted sum of squared terms (Eq. 3). The EV's state is updated using a kinematic bicycle model (Eq. 4).
2. **Cost Function**: The cost function comprises several components:
 - **Travel Efficiency**: Penalizes the difference between the EV's speed and the legal speed limit (Eq. 6).
 - **Passenger Comfort**: Penalizes acceleration, jerk (rate of change of acceleration), steering angle, and steering rate (Eq. 7).
 - **Uncertainty-Aware Safety**: This is a core component. Instead of a single predicted future, safety is evaluated across the M diffusion-generated SA trajectories. To handle the lack of explicit probabilities for individual samples and the non-differentiability of traditional chance constraints, an **empirical Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) tail-risk constraint** is introduced.
 - A safety gap (d_gap) is defined as the extent to which minimum safety clearance (ϵ) is violated (Eq. 10).
 - The empirical CVaR (Eq. 13) approximates the conditional expectation of the worst ⌈Mδ⌉ violations, where δ is the risk tolerance. This penalizes the mean of the largest safety gap values across samples, focusing on severe safety violations. This provides a differentiable and conservative surrogate for chance constraints. Safety cost evaluation is restricted to a sparse set of critical time instants for efficiency.
3. **Optimization and Differentiability**: The planning problem is solved using the Gauss-Newton method. All operations, from prediction to planning, are designed to be fully differentiable, enabling end-to-end joint training. The initial control sequence from the predictor serves as the initial guess for the planner.

**C. Learning Process:**
The framework utilizes a joint training strategy (Fig. 4, Algorithm 1).
- **Training**: For computational efficiency and stable gradient propagation, the diffusion predictor generates a *single* conditioned future trajectory realization for SAs and an initial EV control sequence. The planner optimizes the EV trajectory, and gradients from the planning losses are backpropagated through the differentiable planner to update both the predictor's parameters and the learnable cost function weights.
- **Inference**: The diffusion model generates *multiple* (M=10 in experiments) trajectory samples to represent the predictive uncertainty, which are then used by the sample-conditioned planner for risk-aware optimization.
The overall loss function (Eq. 14) combines prediction loss (smooth L1 loss for SAs, Eq. 15), planning loss (smooth L1 loss for EV, Eq. 16), and the planning cost function value (L_cost).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The framework was evaluated through extensive open-loop and closed-loop experiments on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (WOMD) and the Argoverse 2 Motion Forecasting Dataset, comparing its performance against baselines including Vanilla IL, IL with Prediction Subtask, DIPP, and GameFormer.

**A. Open-Loop Testing (Table II):**
- **Prediction Accuracy**: The proposed approach demonstrated superior performance in motion prediction. On WOMD, it achieved a minADE of 0.64 m and a minFDE of 0.50 m, which represents a 20.99% and 74.09% improvement over DIPP, respectively. Similar improvements were observed on Argoverse 2, with minADE of 0.63 m (51.54% improvement) and minFDE of 0.38 m (87.74% improvement). The results indicated that FDE was often smaller than ADE, which the authors attributed to the terminal loss and denoising process stabilizing the trajectory tail.
- **Safety**: The framework recorded the minimum collision rate and off-route rate across both datasets. On WOMD, it achieved a collision rate of 0.96% (74.12% reduction compared to DIPP) and an off-route rate of 0.88% (71.15% reduction). On Argoverse 2, the collision rate was 4.63% (32.11% reduction compared to GameFormer) and the off-route rate was 5.43% (3.21% reduction compared to DIPP).
- **Vehicle Dynamics (Comfort)**: The framework achieved optimal or near-optimal values in metrics like acceleration, jerk, and lateral acceleration, indicating consistent driving comfort. For instance, on WOMD, the longitudinal jerk was 0.69 m/s³, which is lower than the reported human driving statistics (3.41 m/s³).
- **Planning Accuracy**: It exhibited lower planning errors compared to DIPP and GameFormer. For instance, on WOMD, the planning error at 5 seconds was 1.20 m, compared to DIPP's 3.03 m.

**B. Closed-Loop Testing (Table III):**
- **Safety and Progress**: In closed-loop scenarios, the framework achieved the highest success rate on WOMD (96.00%), representing a 43.28% improvement over the next best baseline (DIPP at 67.00%). This high success rate also translated to the longest progress (travel distance) of 80.48 m on WOMD, an improvement of 38.90% over DIPP. On Argoverse 2, it achieved a success rate of 92.00%, matching DIPP, and a progress of 26.43 m.
- **Vehicle Dynamics and Position Error**: The vehicle dynamics metrics remained favorable. The position error relative to ground truth was also minimized in closed-loop settings, with an error of 3.99 m at 10 seconds on WOMD, which was substantially lower than DIPP's 18.83 m.

**C. Ablation Studies:**
- **Effects of Individual Modules (Table IV)**:
 - Removing the neural network-generated initial control for the planner resulted in substantial performance degradation across all metrics (e.g., 380% increase in collision rate, 31% drop in success rate), highlighting the importance of a reasonable initial guess.
 - Removing the integrated prediction module (replacing with constant velocity model) or the learnable cost function also led to performance drops, though less severe than removing initialization, as the EV still received some initial control.
- **Effects of Sample Size M (Table V)**: The study indicated that both very small (M=5) and very large (M=20) sample sizes could marginally increase collision rates. M=10 was identified as an effective balance, providing sufficient coverage without introducing excessive noise from extreme scenarios.
- **Effects of Graph Structure (Table VI)**: The directed graph representation for scene context demonstrated improvements in both efficiency and effectiveness compared to a vectorized map. It reduced average training time per epoch by 69.7% (from 580s to 176s) and improved prediction accuracy (e.g., minADE reduction of 8.3%, minFDE reduction of 15.6%). The directed graph also accommodated larger batch sizes, indicating lower computational resource occupancy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a fundamental challenge in autonomous driving: the disconnect between advanced uncertainty modeling and safe, interpretable motion planning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Safety and Robustness**: By explicitly incorporating diverse, diffusion-generated future trajectories of surrounding agents and utilizing an empirical Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) tail-risk constraint, the framework enables the planner to account for rare yet safety-critical interactions. This approach systematically mitigates predictive uncertainty, leading to superior safety performance, as evidenced by significantly reduced collision and off-route rates in both open-loop and closed-loop evaluations on real-world datasets. This enhanced robustness in uncertain traffic environments is critical for real-world deployment of autonomous vehicles.
- **Improved Interpretability and Verifiability**: Unlike opaque end-to-end learning systems, the proposed differentiable optimization structure maintains physical interpretability. This allows for explicit enforcement of safety constraints and facilitates post-hoc verification, which are essential requirements for safety-critical applications. The ability to understand and audit planning decisions contributes to increased trust and regulatory acceptance.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance**: The framework consistently outperforms established baselines in key metrics, including safety, planning accuracy, and ride comfort. This demonstrates the efficacy of coupling diffusion-based uncertainty with differentiable planning.
- **Computational Efficiency in Scene Understanding**: The introduction of a directed graph representation for scene context provides a more effective and computationally efficient method for encoding global scene information. This reduces training time and resource consumption while improving predictive accuracy, making the system more scalable for complex urban scenarios.
- **Advancement in End-to-End Learning for Planning**: The fully differentiable design enables joint end-to-end training of the prediction and planning modules. This allows planning objectives to directly inform and refine the prediction model and cost function weights, fostering a more cohesive and optimized system behavior.
- **Contribution to Risk-Aware Planning**: The application of CVaR-based constraints offers a mathematically tractable method to incorporate tail-risk management into differentiable planning, pushing the boundaries of how safety is guaranteed in the presence of multi-modal uncertainty.

In summary, this work contributes to the development of more reliable, safe, and comfortable autonomous driving systems by providing a robust, interpretable, and computationally efficient method for managing complex predictive uncertainty during motion planning. The methodology has the potential to influence future integrated perception-prediction-planning architectures and accelerate the deployment of advanced autonomous driving technologies. Future efforts to address inference latency and enhance trajectory diversity could further strengthen its practical applicability.
