# Fine-tuning Behavioral Cloning Policies with Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Zurich and ETH AI Center developed BRIDGE, a theoretical framework and algorithm that combines offline expert demonstrations with online human preferences to fine-tune reinforcement learning policies. This work establishes the first theoretical regret bounds for this hybrid approach, quantifying how offline data enhances the sample efficiency and safety of online preference-based learning, outperforming standalone methods.

## Problem

- Reward mis-specification, where translating complex task goals into precise numerical reward functions for RL is difficult and can lead to unintended behaviors.
- Risky and data-hungry exploration, as standard online RL often requires extensive, potentially unsafe interactions with the environment.
- Lack of a rigorous theoretical foundation for the empirically successful paradigm of combining reward-free expert demonstrations with online preference-based feedback.

## Method

- The **BRIDGE** (==Bounded Regret== with Imitation Data and Guided Exploration) algorithm is proposed, a two-stage framework integrating offline behavioral cloning with online preference-based reinforcement learning.
- In the offline stage, an initial policy is learned from expert demonstrations, and a Hellinger-distance-based confidence set is constructed around it, which is theoretically proven to contain the optimal policy with high probability.
- During the online stage, policy refinement through preference queries is strictly constrained to this pre-defined confidence set, guiding exploration efficiently while also leveraging uncertainty in both the preference and transition models.

## Results

- The paper provides the first theoretical framework and regret bounds for offline imitation learning combined with online preference-based fine-tuning, demonstrating an optimal O(√(T)) dependence on the online horizon and an inverse dependence on the number of offline demonstrations 'n'.
- Empirically, BRIDGE consistently achieves lower cumulative regret and faster policy search space refinement compared to standalone Behavioral Cloning and purely online Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning across discrete and continuous control tasks.
- Ablation studies validate that the size of the offline confidence set and the quantity/quality of offline data are critical factors that directly impact BRIDGE's performance, aligning with theoretical predictions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Offline expert demonstrations can be theoretically proven to significantly reduce the sample complexity and online regret of subsequent preference-based reinforcement learning.
> - Constructing a Hellinger-distance-based confidence set from offline data provides an effective method for safely bounding the online policy search space, thereby preventing risky exploration.
> - The regret bounds derived establish a precise, quantifiable trade-off between the quantity of offline data and the online query efficiency, indicating that higher quality and quantity of offline data lead to more efficient online learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself at the intersection of several critical areas within reinforcement learning (RL): imitation learning, offline RL, preference-based RL, and the burgeoning field of hybrid offline-to-online learning.

Traditionally, deploying RL in real-world scenarios (robotics, industry, healthcare) faces two major hurdles:
1. **Reward Mis-specification:** It is notoriously difficult for human experts to translate complex task goals into precise numerical reward functions. Incorrectly specified rewards can lead to policies that achieve unintended or even unsafe behaviors (e.g., reward hacking).
2. **Risky and Data-Hungry Exploration:** Standard online RL algorithms often require extensive interaction with the environment to learn, during which they might explore unsafe or suboptimal actions, leading to damage to hardware, loss of trust, or high data collection costs.

Existing solutions address these challenges partially:
- **Imitation Learning (e.g., Behavioral Cloning - BC):** Learns a policy directly from expert demonstrations. It's inherently safe (as it imitates desired behavior) and reward-free, but struggles with covariate shift (when encountering states outside the training data distribution) and lacks mechanisms for refinement or adaptation to new circumstances.
- **Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL):** Addresses reward mis-specification by learning a reward model directly from human comparative feedback (e.g., "trajectory A is better than trajectory B"). It bypasses explicit reward engineering but can still be data-hungry and prone to risky exploration if starting from scratch.
- **Hybrid Offline-to-Online RL:** Uses a pre-collected offline dataset to warm-start an RL agent, which is then fine-tuned online. This mitigates risky exploration. However, the *majority* of prior work in this area (e.g., AWAC, IQL) assumes access to a *ground-truth reward function* for online fine-tuning. The paper explicitly states that this line of work is "fundamentally different" because it doesn't address the reward mis-specification problem.

**The critical gap this paper addresses is the lack of theoretical foundation for the empirically successful paradigm of combining reward-free expert demonstrations with online preference-based feedback for fine-tuning.** This hybrid approach has seen remarkable practical success, notably in large language models like ChatGPT (which uses RL from Human Feedback - RLHF), and in robotics for safe real-world manipulation. Despite its widespread adoption, the theoretical underpinnings of *how* offline demonstrations precisely improve online preference learning, or the quantifiable trade-offs between offline data quantity and online query efficiency, have remained largely unexplored.

This work fills this theoretical void by providing the first rigorous analysis of this "offline imitation + online preference fine-tuning" setting. It aims to formalize the benefits, derive precise regret bounds, and offer a principled understanding to guide practitioners in designing more sample-efficient interactive agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind this research are directly stated in the abstract and introduction:

1. **Provide a Theoretical Foundation for Offline-to-Online Preference Learning:** The most prominent objective is to address the significant theoretical gap in understanding how offline expert demonstrations can be effectively combined with online preference-based feedback. Despite empirical successes (like RLHF in LLMs), a principled analysis of this hybrid approach was missing.
2. **Quantify the Benefits of Offline Data:** The authors aim to precisely quantify how offline demonstrations improve online preference learning. This involves establishing theoretical guarantees (regret bounds) that explicitly link the quantity and quality of offline data to the efficiency of subsequent online learning (i.e., online sample efficiency).
3. **Develop a Unified Algorithm:** To validate their theoretical framework, the researchers sought to develop an algorithm that integrates both offline imitation learning signals and online preference feedback in a principled manner. This algorithm, named BRIDGE, should achieve the predicted theoretical benefits empirically.
4. **Enhance Safety and Sample Efficiency in RL Deployment:** The overarching motivation is to overcome the two major obstacles to RL deployment in real-world applications: reward mis-specification and risky, data-hungry exploration. By providing a method that starts with a safe, expert-derived policy and then refines it safely and efficiently through human preferences, the work aims to make RL more practical and trustworthy.
5. **Guide Practitioners:** By establishing formal regret bounds and understanding the trade-offs, the authors seek to provide formal guidance for practitioners in designing and deploying interactive learning systems that leverage diverse forms of human feedback.

In essence, the paper seeks to elevate an empirically successful, but theoretically ungrounded, hybrid RL paradigm by providing rigorous mathematical understanding and a concrete algorithmic implementation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, embodied in the **BRIDGE (Bounded Regret with Imitation Data and Guided Exploration)** algorithm, is a two-stage framework that integrates offline behavioral cloning with online preference-based reinforcement learning.

**Stage 1: Offline Estimation and Confidence Set Construction**

The initial phase leverages a reward-free dataset of expert demonstrations (D^H_n) to create a strong prior over the policy space. This involves three key steps:

1. **Policy and Model Estimation:**
 - **Behavioral Cloning (BC):** An initial policy, π_BC, is learned from the offline dataset via maximum likelihood estimation (log-loss BC). This provides a safe, but potentially brittle, starting point.
 - **Transition Model Estimation (MLE):** An estimated transition model, P̂, is also learned from the offline data, using maximum likelihood estimation. This is crucial for evaluating policy performance and constructing confidence sets in the continuous MDP setting (though simplified to count-based for tabular MDPs).
2. **Confidence Set Construction:** The core innovation of the offline phase is the construction of a high-probability confidence set, Π_offline, around the estimated BC policy in the trajectory distribution space.
 - **Hellinger Distance:** This confidence set is defined as a "Hellinger ball" in the space of trajectory distributions induced by policies and the estimated dynamics (P_{π,P̂}). The Hellinger distance is chosen for its tractability and direct relation to L2 norms of square-root densities, providing a geometric interpretation.
 - **Radius (O(1/√n)):** The authors derive a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.2) that the radius of this Hellinger ball shrinks at a rate of O(1/√n), where 'n' is the number of offline expert demonstrations. This establishes a direct, quantifiable link between the amount of offline data and the tightness of the initial search space for online learning.
 - **Concentrability Coefficient:** A key challenge is bounding the concentrability coefficient (which measures distribution shifts) without strong data coverage assumptions. The authors introduce a mild "expert policy concentration" assumption (Assumption 3: minimum visitation probability γ_min > 0 for states visited by the expert) to bound this coefficient, making the confidence set computable.

**Stage 2: Constrained Online Preference Learning**

The second stage performs online preference-based RL, but critically, its exploration is constrained by the Π_offline confidence set derived in the first stage. This prevents risky exploration in suboptimal or unsafe regions.

1. **Generalized Linear Model (GLM) for Preferences:** The online learning uses a GLM framework, where preferences are modeled using a Bradley-Terry model based on trajectory feature embeddings (ϕ(τ)) and a latent weight vector (w*). The algorithm iteratively updates a regularized maximum likelihood estimate (w_proj) of w* based on past preference queries.
2. **Offline-Online Transition Model Integration:** To improve sample efficiency, the online transition model estimator (P̂_t) is initialized using the offline MLE estimate (P̂) and continuously updated with new online data. This combined count-based approach leads to faster and more accurate transition model learning. Exploration bonuses (B̂_t) are adapted to leverage these combined counts, encouraging exploration in areas of high uncertainty within the transition model.
3. **Constrained and Uncertainty-Guided Policy Selection:** At each online step, the algorithm selects a pair of policies (π^1_t, π^2_t) to query for human preference.
 - **Constraint:** These policies are *only* sampled from within the Π_offline confidence set. This is the "BRIDGE" that connects offline safety to online learning.
 - **Uncertainty Guidance:** Within this constrained set, policy selection is guided by maximizing an objective function that combines two sources of uncertainty: uncertainty in the preference model (measured by the Mahalanobis norm with respect to the data matrix V_t) and uncertainty in the transition model (via the B̂_t bonus). This ensures targeted and efficient exploration.

**Theoretical Regret Analysis:** The methodology culminates in a novel theoretical regret bound (Theorem 4.1) that shows:
- An optimal O(√T) dependence on the online horizon T.
- A crucial inverse dependence on the number of offline demonstrations 'n', where regret vanishes as n → ∞.
- This explicitly formalizes how offline data reduces online learning complexity by shrinking the search space and improving initial model estimates.

**Empirical Validation:** The approach is validated in both discrete (StarMDP, Gridworld) and continuous (Reacher, Ant MuJoCo tasks) control environments, comparing BRIDGE against standalone BC and online PbRL baselines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents significant theoretical and empirical findings:

#### 4.1. Theoretical Contributions

- **First Theoretical Framework for Offline-to-Online Preference Learning:** The paper introduces the first rigorous theoretical framework for the widely used but previously unanalyzed "offline imitation + online preference fine-tuning" paradigm. This provides a principled understanding of how these two distinct learning signals can be effectively combined.
- **Hellinger-Distance-Based Confidence Set:** A novel technique is developed to construct a confidence set (Π_offline) around the initial Behavioral Cloning (BC) policy in trajectory distribution space, using the Hellinger distance. This set is proven to contain the true optimal policy with high probability.
- **Regret Bound Dependent on Offline Data Quantity (Theorem 4.1):** The central theoretical achievement is the derivation of regret bounds for BRIDGE that explicitly demonstrate the benefits of offline data. The regret bound for BRIDGE scales as:
 $R_T \leq \tilde{O} \left( \sqrt{T} \cdot \sqrt{\log \left( 1 + \frac{T}{n} \right) + \frac{\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{n} \cdot \gamma_{min}}} \right)$
 This bound shows:
 - An optimal dependence of $O(\sqrt{T})$ on the online horizon $T$, consistent with state-of-the-art online RL algorithms.
 - A critical inverse dependence on $n$ (the number of offline demonstrations). As $n \to \infty$, the online regret approaches zero, proving that high-quality offline data can dramatically reduce the complexity and sample-efficiency requirements of online preference-based learning. This is a direct theoretical validation of the empirical observation that pre-training with expert data is highly beneficial.
- **Explicit Connection between Offline Data and Online Efficiency:** The derived regret bounds quantify the precise trade-offs between the quantity of offline data and the number of online queries needed for effective learning. This provides formal guidance for practitioners.

#### 4.2. Empirical Contributions

- **Superior Performance over Baselines (Figure 2):** BRIDGE consistently achieves lower cumulative regret compared to both standalone Behavioral Cloning (BC) and purely online Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) algorithms across a range of discrete (StarMDP, Gridworld) and continuous control (MuJoCo Reacher, Ant) environments.
 - BC typically plateaus early due to its inability to refine its policy or correct for covariate shift.
 - Online PbRL, while capable of refinement, starts from a cold-start or less informed initial policy, leading to higher initial regret and slower convergence.
 - BRIDGE combines the safety and initial performance of BC with the refinement capabilities of PbRL, resulting in more efficient and lower-regret learning.
- **Faster Policy Search Space Refinement (Figure 3):** BRIDGE effectively prunes the policy search space (Π_t) much faster than the online PbRL baseline. This demonstrates that the confidence set construction from offline data effectively narrows down the candidate policies, allowing online learning to focus exploration more efficiently.
- **Ablation Studies Validate Theoretical Claims:**
 - **Impact of Confidence Set Radius (Figure 4):** Ablations show that the radius of the offline confidence set is crucial. An overly large radius diminishes the benefit of constraint, while an excessively small radius risks excluding the optimal policy, leading to unavoidable regret.
 - **Impact of Offline Data Quantity and Quality (Table 1, Figure 5):** Increasing the quantity of offline trajectories (n) and using higher-quality (less noisy) data leads to a tighter confidence set (smaller radius) and improved BRIDGE performance, directly validating the theoretical O(1/√n) scaling.
 - **Impact of Embedding Function (Figure 6):** The choice of trajectory embedding (ϕ) significantly impacts performance for both BRIDGE and PbRL, highlighting the importance of feature engineering that aligns well with the underlying preference signal.

In summary, the paper successfully provides a theoretical justification for a powerful hybrid RL paradigm and demonstrates its empirical effectiveness and efficiency across diverse control tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant theoretical and practical implications, addressing fundamental challenges in the deployment of reinforcement learning systems.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Significance

- **Closing a Theoretical Gap:** The most direct impact is the establishment of the "first theoretical framework" for offline-to-online preference learning. This moves an empirically successful paradigm (e.g., RLHF in LLMs, robotics applications) from a heuristic-driven approach to one grounded in rigorous mathematical understanding.
- **Principled Design:** By providing explicit regret bounds and quantifying the relationship between offline data quantity and online sample efficiency, the work offers a principled basis for designing future interactive learning systems. It guides practitioners on how to optimally leverage pre-collected data and human feedback.
- **Unified Framework for Diverse Signals:** BRIDGE provides a theoretically sound method for unifying two distinct forms of human input – demonstrations and preferences – to build more robust and adaptable agents. This integration is crucial for complex real-world tasks where a single feedback type might be insufficient.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact and Future Directions

- **Safer and More Sample-Efficient RL:** By starting with a safe initial policy derived from expert demonstrations and constraining online exploration, BRIDGE directly addresses the challenges of risky exploration in real-world settings. The demonstrated reduction in online regret means fewer risky interactions and faster learning, making RL more viable for sensitive applications like robotics and healthcare.
- **Enhanced Human-AI Collaboration:** The framework promotes more effective human-AI collaboration. Humans provide initial demonstrations and then subtle, comparative preferences, which are often easier and less cognitively demanding than specifying numerical reward functions. This lowers the barrier for human experts to guide complex AI agents.
- **Applicability to Large Models:** The principles laid out in BRIDGE are highly relevant to the fine-tuning of large foundation models (e.g., large language models, vision transformers) with human feedback. The theoretical understanding provided could help optimize current RLHF pipelines, potentially leading to more efficient training and better-aligned models.
- **Guidance for Data Collection:** The explicit dependence of regret on offline data quantity ('n') offers concrete guidance for data collection strategies. It helps in understanding the diminishing returns of additional offline data and the optimal balance between offline pre-training and online interaction.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens several new directions for research, including:
 - Extending the framework to handle non-stationary environments or non-expert offline data.
 - Developing more sophisticated embedding functions for complex real-world trajectories.
 - Investigating the impact of different preference models or feedback modalities.
 - Exploring adaptive strategies for dynamically adjusting the confidence set radius or the exploration-exploitation balance based on real-time learning progress.

In conclusion, "Fine-tuning Behavioral Cloning Policies with Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning" makes a crucial contribution by bridging the gap between the practical success and theoretical understanding of hybrid RL systems. It lays a foundational stone for the development of interactive agents that can safely and efficiently learn from human input without relying on difficult-to-specify reward signals, thereby accelerating the deployment of RL in complex real-world applications.
