# Sample, Simulate, Select: Physics-in-the-Loop Text-to-Motion for Humanoids Without Training

> [!summary] Summary
> Sample-simulate-select (S3) is a training-free text-to-humanoid pipeline: it draws N motions per prompt from a frozen text-to-motion model, retargets each to a Unitree G1 by direction-matching inverse kinematics, rolls all of them out under full rigid-body dynamics with a pretrained whole-body tracking policy, and keeps the one the policy executed best. Upright execution rises from 83.5% to 89.5%, and all 177 gate-selected clips completed standing on the real robot.

## Problem

- Text-to-motion generators model human kinematics only, with no notion of a robot's mass distribution, actuator limits or contact schedule, so their output is often dynamically infeasible.
- Whole-body tracking controllers can regularise a reference but cannot replan an infeasible one, so an unstable generated motion still ends in a fall.
- Recent language-to-humanoid systems all close this gap by training something, and none reports what a frozen composition of the same public components already achieves.

## Method

- **Sample-simulate-select** (S3): N candidates per prompt from frozen ==MoMask==, each rolled out with the pretrained ==SONIC== tracking policy in ==MuJoCo==, then picked lexicographically by deploy-gate level, ties broken by lowest mean joint-tracking error.
- ==Direction-matching inverse-kinematics== retargeter: limb directions transferred onto robot-scaled segment lengths, joint angles solved by ==damped least squares== regularised toward the G1's home stance and the previous frame, deterministic and untrained.
- Evaluation instrumentation: a ==reference-relative fall criterion== that does not penalise legitimate crouching, a three-level ==PASS/CAUTION/REJECT hardware deploy gate==, and a six-feature ==kinematic risk score== as the lower-bound baseline.

## Results

- Upright execution rose from **83.5%** to **89.5%** at N=8 on 200 stratified prompts and from **80.5%** to **89.5%** across all 4,184 test prompts, with gate passes up from **33** to **85**.
- The kinematic verifier predicted falls at **AUROC 0.90** yet recovered only **1.5 points** by ranking; all **177** gate-selected clips completed standing on the real G1, simulation tracking error matching hardware at **r=0.94**.
- Squat/bend plateaued at **57%** upright, whereas a generator trained on retargeted robot data hit **97%** first-sample execution and **93%** on that class; combining two retargeters lifted the ceiling to **95.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Population-level fall classification does not imply within-prompt ranking: candidates of one prompt share semantics, so discriminative features collapse and only a rollout observes the outcome.
> - Best-of-N selection recovers feasible samples already present but cannot create absent behaviour; training on retargeted robot data is what reshapes a systematically infeasible motion class.
> - Most semantic fidelity is lost at retargeting, before any physics, and real mocap degrades identically, so that floor belongs to the robot projection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Language-driven humanoids face a practical gap. Text-to-motion generators can describe a movement without accounting for a robot's balance, contacts, or actuators. A tracking controller can follow a reference, but it cannot rewrite one that fails. The authors' approach is to try several motions, run each through the very controller intended for the robot in a physics simulator, and send the controller's best-executed candidate. Nothing is trained. This approach, called S³, improves upright outcomes. It cannot, however, invent an executable motion that is absent from the candidate pool.

"This person waves forward with his right hand": generated joints, the retargeted G1 reference, and the G1 tracked by pretrained SONIC under physics. This clip was later executed on the real G1.

### 2. Let the robot's controller judge

For each prompt, S³ runs four steps:

1. A frozen MoMask model samples $N$ HumanML3D motions.
1. Deterministic direction-matching inverse kinematics retargets each motion to a Unitree G1 reference. Retargeting maps the human joint motion onto the robot's body and joint limits.
1. Pretrained SONIC rolls out each reference under MuJoCo rigid-body dynamics.
1. The selector ranks the candidates by gate level, then by lowest mean joint-tracking error.

SONIC is unchanged, and it is also the tracking policy used on hardware.

The method adds computation. S³ samples, retargets, and simulates every candidate. The study's practical claim is that this extra screening can recover feasible samples, not that it makes generation faster.

### 3. Selection finds a ceiling, not new motion

Success is measured as "upright," a paper-defined fall-avoidance proxy rather than a general measure of task completion. A rollout counts as fallen if any of the following holds:

- its pelvis drops below 0.25 m;
- its body tilts more than 60° from vertical;
- it sinks more than 0.30 m below the reference pelvis for over 0.5 s.

The reference-relative condition is intended to avoid labeling legitimate crouching as a fall.

On 200 stratified HumanML3D test prompts with $N=8$, upright execution rises from 83.5% on the first sample to 89.5% with S³. Hardware-gate PASS selections rise from 33 to 85. On the complete 4,184-prompt test split, the same upright measure reaches 89.5% from 80.5%.

For a given prompt, the "any-of-$N$ ceiling" is the best upright outcome available among the $N$ candidates the generator happened to produce. It does not guarantee that a good candidate exists. Because the simulator is deterministic, S³ selects that ceiling for simulated upright success by construction.

The decisive comparison is a kinematic risk score. It predicts falls across samples with AUROC 0.90, yet it reaches only 85.0% upright on the 200 prompts and yields 56 rather than 85 PASS clips. Population-level classification is a different task from ranking candidates that share one prompt's semantics. The authors suggest that such candidates share semantic features and may therefore be hard to rank from kinematics. The paper reports three-quarters of the gain by $N=4$ and a flat ceiling from $N=7$.

Left: prompts upright (%) against samples per prompt (1–8) for the oracle (any-of-$N$), the physics verifier (ours), and the kinematic verifier, with Wilson 95% intervals. Physics markers overlap the oracle line, while the kinematic series remains below. Right: gap to oracle (points), which is zero for physics at every $N$.

The ceiling also exposes a failure class. Squat/bend has only a 57% any-of-8 ceiling in the direction-IK category, and 21 prompts have no upright candidate among their eight samples. GMR, a different retargeter, recovers 11 of those 21 in some sample. Selecting across both retargeters reaches a 95.0% union ceiling on the 200 prompts. Ten prompts remain failures across both, including seven squat/bend prompts. The paper interprets this residual as motion the frozen generator does not produce executably, under these two tested retargeters and this setup.

Four example prompts, including "walking forward and then bending down," with the retargeted reference on top and the SONIC rollout below. These illustrate the all-eight-fell squat/bend failure class.

On hardware, all 177 executed clips completed standing with no fall or abort. For 134 logged clips, hardware versus simulated mean joint-tracking error was 0.114 versus 0.115 rad (Pearson $r=0.94$). This result covers these clips and this robot/controller setup.

### 4. Selection cannot create motion

Controller-in-the-loop sampling raises the chance of choosing a motion the robot executes upright. The ceiling it reaches is bounded by what the available generator and retargeter produce.
