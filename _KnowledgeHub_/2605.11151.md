# RankQ: Offline-to-Online Reinforcement Learning via Self-Supervised Action Ranking

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Horizon Robotics developed RankQ, an offline-to-online reinforcement learning method that uses self-supervised action ranking to guide policy improvement. This approach shapes Q-values by explicitly modeling relative action quality, enabling more efficient learning from sparse data and achieving a 41.6 percentage-point increase in real-world cube-stacking success over baseline VLA policies.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces limitations in practical application due to the need for extensive, often costly or hazardous, online data collection.
- Existing offline-to-online RL methods, such as those based on pessimism, can be overly restrictive by uniformly penalizing out-of-distribution actions, potentially hindering improvement if the offline dataset is suboptimal.
- Accurate estimation of value functions for out-of-distribution (OOD) actions in large state-action spaces with limited data coverage remains a significant challenge.

## Method

- RankQ augments the standard temporal-difference (TD) learning objective with a self-supervised multi-term ranking loss.
- It constructs categories of suboptimal actions by perturbing successful actions or sampling random/permuted actions, based on dataset partitioning into success and failure trajectories.
- A pairwise ranking loss enforces structured ordering constraints on action Q-values, ensuring higher-quality actions are valued above their suboptimal counterparts, and establishing relative preferences among different suboptimal action types.

## Results

- RankQ achieved competitive or superior performance across D4RL benchmarks, notably outperforming all baselines on antmaze-large and adroit-relocate tasks.
- In low-data vision-based robot manipulation tasks, RankQ consistently improved policies, yielding average performance gains of up to 64.3% over the next best non-RankQ baseline.
- A RankQ-trained policy demonstrated strong sim-to-real transfer, increasing real-world cube-stacking success rate from 43.1% to 84.7% (a 41.6 percentage-point gain) and reducing average task completion time by 17%.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling relative action quality through structured self-supervised ranking offers a more effective and sample-efficient alternative to uniformly pessimistic value estimation in offline-to-online RL.
> - Shaping the Q-value landscape to direct action gradients towards higher-quality behaviors, rather than just anchoring to dataset actions, facilitates greater policy improvement, especially when the offline data is suboptimal.
> - RankQ produces more stable Q-value gradient statistics and achieves higher critic accuracy in ranking actions, suggesting a more efficient learning of implicit action orderings compared to pessimism-based methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities across various domains; however, its practical application is often constrained by the substantial volume of online interaction required for policy learning. This limitation is particularly pronounced in fields such as robotics, where data collection can be resource-intensive, time-consuming, or involve safety risks.

Offline RL emerged as an approach to address this challenge by enabling policy learning solely from pre-collected, static datasets, thereby eliminating the need for further environment interaction during training. Building on this, offline-to-online RL integrates offline pretraining with subsequent online fine-tuning. This hybrid paradigm aims to leverage the benefits of pre-existing data for improved sample efficiency while retaining the capacity for policy enhancement beyond the scope of the initial offline dataset.

A central technical challenge in both offline and offline-to-online RL lies in the accurate estimation of value functions, especially when dealing with states and actions that are "out-of-distribution" (OOD) relative to the training dataset. Inaccurate value estimates for OOD actions can lead to detrimental policy updates during online fine-tuning. Prior methods have largely approached this by incorporating pessimism, which involves down-weighting the Q-values of OOD actions relative to those present in the dataset. This strategy, exemplified by methods like Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) and Calibrated Q-Learning (Cal-QL), aims to stabilize training by biasing the policy towards known, safe behaviors within the offline data distribution.

While effective for training stability, this pessimistic approach inherently relies on the assumption that unseen actions are uniformly less preferable than observed dataset actions. This assumption can be restrictive. If the offline dataset contains suboptimal or failure trajectories, an overly pessimistic bias toward these dataset actions can impede the policy's ability to discover and exploit genuinely optimal behaviors during online exploration, thus hindering the potential for significant policy improvement. This work positions itself as an alternative to these uniformly pessimistic methods, seeking to refine how value functions are shaped beyond the dataset to enable more effective and less restrictive online policy improvement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an offline-to-online Q-learning objective that enhances sample efficiency and promotes more effective policy improvement, particularly in environments characterized by large state-action spaces and limited dataset coverage. The motivation for this work stems from recognized limitations in existing offline-to-online RL methods, specifically those that rely on uniform pessimism.

Current pessimistic approaches, such as Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) and Calibrated Q-Learning (Cal-QL), augment the standard temporal-difference (TD) loss with regularizers that penalize out-of-distribution (OOD) actions. While this mitigates value overestimation and stabilizes training, it introduces two key issues:
1. **Unbounded Pessimism (CQL):** CQL's penalty on OOD actions can be unbounded, leading to excessively low Q-values and large gradients ($\partial Q / \partial a$). These gradients can cause policy instability during updates and necessitate extensive online training to "unlearn" the imposed pessimism as the policy explores OOD regions. Cal-QL addresses the unbounded nature with a calibration floor, but the fundamental preference structure remains.
2. **Uniform Preference for Dataset Actions:** Both CQL and Cal-QL primarily enforce that dataset actions are preferred over policy-sampled OOD actions. This uniform preference does not account for scenarios where policy actions might actually be superior to suboptimal dataset actions. If the offline dataset includes a substantial proportion of failure or suboptimal trajectories, this uniform bias can restrict policy improvement by anchoring it to potentially inferior behaviors.

Given these limitations, the motivation for RankQ is to move beyond uniform penalization of unseen actions. Instead, the authors aim to introduce a mechanism that explicitly models the relative quality of actions and enforces structured ordering among them. By doing so, the objective is to shape the Q-value landscape such that action gradients are consistently directed towards regions associated with higher-quality actions. This approach is intended to facilitate more effective policy improvement throughout both the offline pretraining and subsequent online fine-tuning phases, especially in challenging sparse-reward and low-data regimes.

The specific objectives outlined in the paper's contributions include:
- Achieving competitive or superior performance on established offline-to-online RL benchmarks (D4RL).
- Enabling effective fine-tuning of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in low-data regimes, demonstrating significant improvements over baselines.
- Demonstrating effective VLA fine-tuning in high-data regimes, achieving faster task completion and preparing policies for real-world deployment.
- Validating the sim-to-real transfer capabilities of policies trained with the proposed method for robotic manipulation tasks.
- Conducting extensive analysis to understand the impact of the proposed method on the Q-value landscape and critic accuracy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RankQ methodology augments the standard temporal-difference (TD) learning objective with a self-supervised multi-term ranking loss. This approach is designed to enforce structured action ordering by learning relative action preferences, rather than uniformly penalizing out-of-distribution (OOD) actions.

**Background: Temporal-Difference Learning and Challenges**
Reinforcement learning aims to find a policy $\pi(a|s)$ that maximizes expected discounted return. In actor-critic methods, a critic network estimates the action-value function $Q_\pi(s, a)$. The critic is typically trained using TD learning, which minimizes the squared difference between current Q-estimates and bootstrapped target values:
$L_{TD}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2} E_{s,a,s' \sim D_h} [Q_\theta(s, a) - (r(s, a) + \gamma E_{a' \sim \pi(\cdot|s')} [Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s', a')])^2]$.
In offline RL, applying naive TD learning on static datasets with limited coverage can lead to Q-value overestimation for OOD actions, resulting in unstable or detrimental policy updates.

**Conservative Q-Learning (CQL)**
CQL addresses value overestimation by augmenting the TD loss with a conservative regularizer that penalizes Q-values of policy-sampled actions, encouraging them to be lower than those observed in the dataset:
$L_{CQL_Q}(\theta) = \alpha [E_{s \sim D, a \sim \pi(\cdot|s)}[Q_\theta(s, a)] - E_{s,a \sim D}[Q_\theta(s, a)]] + L_{TD}(\theta)$.
While effective in preventing overestimation, CQL can suffer from unbounded pessimism, leading to excessively large Q-value drops and gradients for OOD actions, which can destabilize training and require significant online "unlearning." It also uniformly prefers any dataset action over any policy action, even if dataset actions are suboptimal.

**Calibrated Q-Learning (Cal-QL)**
Cal-QL improves upon CQL by introducing a "calibration" mechanism, preventing policy action Q-values from being pushed below a reference policy's value $V_\mu(s)$:
$L_{Cal-QL_Q}(\theta) = \alpha [E_{s \sim D, a \sim \pi(\cdot|s)}[\max(Q_\theta(s, a), V_\mu(s))] - E_{s,a \sim D}[Q_\theta(s, a)]] + L_{TD}(\theta)$.
This mitigates unbounded pessimism. However, Cal-QL, similar to CQL, still maintains a uniform preference for dataset actions over other actions, regardless of their actual quality.

**RankQ: Self-Supervised Action Ranking**
RankQ's core idea is to enforce structured action ordering to shape the Q-landscape. It involves the following steps:
1. **Dataset Partitioning:** The offline dataset $D$ is partitioned into $D_{success}$ and $D_{failure}$ based on trajectory outcomes.
2. **Suboptimal Action Generation:** For each successful transition $(s, a) \sim D_{success}$, several classes of suboptimal actions are generated:
 - **Noisy actions ($a_\epsilon$):** Small perturbations $a + \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma)$.
 - **Very noisy actions ($a_{2\epsilon}$):** Larger perturbations $a + 2\epsilon$.
 - **Random actions ($a_r$):** Arbitrary actions sampled uniformly from the action space.
 - **Permuted actions ($a_p$):** Actions sampled from unrelated states in the dataset.
3. **Ranking Constraints:**
 - **Successful Action Preference (Eq. 4):** For $(s, a) \sim D_{success}$, successful actions are preferred over all generated suboptimal variants: $Q(s, a) > Q(s, a')$, where $a' \in \{a_\epsilon, a_{2\epsilon}, a_r, a_p\}$.
 - **Suboptimal Action Chain (Eq. 5):** Ordering is enforced among suboptimal actions based on their proximity to the successful action: $Q(s, a_\epsilon) > Q(s, a_{2\epsilon}) > Q(s, a_r)$.
 - **Failure Action Preference (Eq. 6):** For $(s, a) \sim D_{failure}$, failure dataset actions are preferred over random actions: $Q(s, a) > Q(s, a_r)$. Stronger ordering is avoided for failure trajectories due to less clear notions of relative quality among their variations.
4. **Multi-term Pairwise Ranking Loss:** These constraints are formulated as a loss using the softplus function $sp(x) = \log(1 + e^x)$ for pairwise ranking $R(s, a^+, a^-) = sp(Q_\theta(s, a^-) - Q_\theta(s, a^+))$.
 - $L_{succ_Q}(\theta)$: Enforces preferences of successful actions over their suboptimal variants.
 - $L_{chain_Q}(\theta)$: Enforces the chain ordering among noisy, very noisy, and random actions derived from successful trajectories.
 - $L_{fail_Q}(\theta)$: Enforces preference of failure dataset actions over random actions.
5. **Final RankQ Objective:** The total RankQ objective combines these ranking losses with the TD loss:
 $L_{RankQ_Q}(\theta) = \alpha_0 (L_{succ_Q}(\theta) + L_{chain_Q}(\theta)) + \alpha_1 L_{fail_Q}(\theta) + L_{TD}(\theta)$.
 Hyperparameters $\alpha_0$, $\alpha_1$ control the influence of success and failure trajectory ranking terms, respectively, and $\sigma$ controls the noise scale.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Environments:** Evaluated on D4RL benchmarks (antmaze-medium/large, adroit-pen/door/relocate) and vision-based robot learning tasks (VLA) in low-data and high-data (sim-to-real) regimes (carrot-onto-plate, cube-stacking, spoon-into-bowl).
- **Baselines:** Compared against CQL, CQL+SAC, Cal-QL, Cal-QL+SAC, SAC+OFF, SAC (pure online), and Hybrid RL.
- **RL Algorithms:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) for D4RL. For VLA tasks, SACFlow, an off-policy formulation of PPOFlow based on a pretrained $\pi_0$ VLA model, was used. SACFlow employs a VLM backbone and a flow-matching action head, with learnable noise injected for stochastic policy.
- **Data Regimes:** D4RL uses standard datasets. VLA low-data uses 200 self-collected rollouts offline and 8 online rollouts/update. VLA high-data/sim-to-real uses 800 self-collected rollouts offline, 192 online rollouts/update, and extensive domain randomization for robustness.
- **Metrics:** Success rate, average trajectory length, and time-to-finish.
- **Computational Efficiency:** RankQ typically requires fewer critic evaluations per training iteration (4 batched evaluations) compared to pessimistic methods like CQL/Cal-QL (20 batched evaluations), leading to faster training times for large VLA models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings across diverse environments and data regimes, indicating the performance characteristics of RankQ.

**D4RL Benchmarks:**
- RankQ demonstrated competitive or superior performance compared to seven baseline methods across four antmaze and three adroit sparse-reward environments.
- Pure online RL (SAC) exhibited inefficiency or failure in long-horizon tasks like antmaze. Naive offline-to-online baselines (SAC+OFF, Hybrid RL) improved over pure online RL but were insufficient for more challenging tasks.
- Only the full offline-to-online variants (CQL, Cal-QL, RankQ) achieved non-zero success across all environments. Among these, RankQ showed consistent overall performance.
- RankQ matched the strongest baseline in success rate on antmaze-medium, pen, and door tasks. It outperformed all baselines on more challenging tasks such as antmaze-large and relocate.
- Trajectory efficiency varied by environment; for example, RankQ exhibited slightly slower trajectories on antmaze-medium but substantially faster execution on relocate.

**VLA Low-Data Evaluation:**
- In a challenging low-data setting (200 offline self-rollouts, 8 online rollouts/update) on three manipulation tasks (carrot-on-plate, cube-stacking, spoon-into-bowl), RankQ was observed to be the only method that consistently improved the policy substantially beyond its imitation baseline.
- Other approaches largely plateaued near initial performance levels.
- RankQ achieved higher average success rates than the next best non-RankQ baseline by 9.5% for carrot-on-plate, 64.3% for cube-stacking, and 54.2% for spoon-into-bowl. This indicates RankQ's ability to extract learning signals from sparse successes and abundant failures in data-constrained environments.

**VLA High-Data Evaluation:**
- For the cube stacking task in a high-data regime (800 offline self-rollouts, 192 online rollouts/update, extensive domain randomization), pessimistic methods (CQL, Cal-QL) improved, unlike in the low-data setting.
- RankQ, however, demonstrated higher sample efficiency, improving more rapidly and achieving a higher final success rate (13.7% higher than Cal-QL).
- RankQ also yielded faster task completion times, reducing average execution time to just over 8 seconds, compared to 10.5-11.5 seconds for CQL and Cal-QL. This suggests improved efficiency in policy behavior.

**Sim-to-Real Evaluation:**
- Policies trained with Cal-QL and RankQ were deployed zero-shot on a real robot for cube stacking.
- The RankQ (0.4) checkpoint, corresponding to 40% of online training, improved real-world cube stacking success from an initial 43.1% (VLA baseline) to 88.9%. Cal-QL achieved 84.7%.
- Partial success rate (picking up the first cube) increased from 77.8% to 98.6% for RankQ and 100% for Cal-QL.
- RankQ checkpoints also reduced average task completion time by 13.6% and 20.1% for the 0.4 and 1.0 checkpoints, respectively, while Cal-QL achieved similar speeds to the baseline.

**Q-landscape Analysis and Critic Accuracies:**
- Analysis of the Q-value landscape for vla-sim2real-cube-stacking showed that CQL and Cal-QL produced sharper gradient landscapes during offline RL due to their pessimism, with CQL exhibiting the largest spikes.
- RankQ generated more stable ∂Q/∂a statistics and minimal distributional shift between offline and online training.
- RankQ achieved the highest accuracies in ranking successful actions above four categories of suboptimal actions (noisy, very noisy, random, permuted), attributed to its explicit ordering constraints.
- CQL and Cal-QL achieved comparable accuracies for random and very noisy actions but lower accuracies for noisy and permuted actions initially, gradually approaching RankQ's performance.

**Ablation Studies:**
- Ablations on D4RL environments (e.g., varying noise scale, omitting specific ranking terms) indicated that the impact of each component varied by environment difficulty. While the optimal configuration might differ, the default RankQ formulation consistently provided strong baseline performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces RankQ, an offline-to-online reinforcement learning objective that represents an alternative to prevailing methods based on uniform pessimism. By augmenting temporal-difference learning with self-supervised action ranking, RankQ explicitly models relative action quality to shape the Q-value landscape. This approach aims to direct action gradients towards higher-quality behaviors, which can result in more effective policy improvement during both offline pretraining and online fine-tuning.

The significance of this work is demonstrated through its performance characteristics:
- **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** RankQ achieved competitive or superior results on D4RL benchmarks and demonstrated consistent improvements in robotic manipulation tasks, particularly in sparse-reward and low-data environments where pessimistic methods can be overly restrictive. This suggests that explicitly modeling relative action quality can offer advantages over uniformly penalizing OOD actions.
- **Effective VLA Fine-tuning:** The method proved effective for fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in both low-data and high-data regimes, leading to higher success rates and faster task completion times. This indicates its potential applicability in complex robot learning scenarios where pre-trained models require efficient adaptation.
- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The demonstration of strong zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for cube stacking, with substantial increases in real-world success rates, highlights the practical utility of RankQ-trained policies. This is a critical step towards deploying RL policies in real-world robotic systems.
- **Q-Value Landscape Shaping:** The analysis of Q-value landscape statistics and critic accuracies suggests that RankQ's explicit ranking objectives lead to more stable gradient dynamics and a more accurate understanding of action quality relationships, which can contribute to more stable and efficient learning.

The potential impact of RankQ extends to several areas:
- **Improving Sample Efficiency:** By extracting more signal from limited and potentially suboptimal offline datasets, RankQ could enable more rapid learning in real-world applications where data collection is costly.
- **Advancing Robot Learning:** Its effectiveness in VLA fine-tuning and sim-to-real transfer positions RankQ as a potentially valuable tool for developing more capable and adaptable robotic systems.
- **Guiding Future Research:** The study proposes that explicitly modeling relative action ordering offers a viable alternative to uniform pessimism. This may motivate further investigation into structured value shaping objectives, which could lead to new directions in scalable offline-to-online RL and real-world robot learning algorithms.
