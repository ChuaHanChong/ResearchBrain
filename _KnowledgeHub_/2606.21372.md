# NAC: Neural Action Codec for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The Neural Action Codec (NAC) adapts advanced neural audio codec architectures to tokenize continuous robot actions for Vision-Language-Action models. It achieves superior downstream policy success rates, such as 49.73% on LIBERO-10 and 50% on real-world tasks, by efficiently compressing action trajectories into discrete tokens while maintaining kinematic fidelity.

## Problem

- Representing continuous robot actions as discrete tokens for autoregressive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models without incurring high computational costs or losing critical kinematic detail.
- Existing action tokenization methods often face trade-offs between compression efficiency, latency, and the fidelity of the reconstructed action trajectories, leading to suboptimal policy performance.
- The need to capture statistical regularities of robot action distributions while adapting architectures to robotic control's multi-channel, lower-frequency nature, distinct from audio processing needs.

## Method

- **NAC** develops a learned action tokenizer by adapting multi-scale ==Residual Vector Quantization Generative Adversarial Network (RVQGAN)== architectures from neural audio codecs, treating action trajectories as multi-channel 1D signals.
- Key adaptations include replacing audio-specific mel-spectrogram losses with kinematic-appropriate reconstruction losses (e.g., Mean Squared Error) and incorporating ==adversarial training== with a ==DAC discriminator== to preserve fine-grained temporal details.
- An autoregressive behavioral cloning policy (**NACPolicy**) is designed to leverage NAC's structured token space, using ==offset codebooks== to enable efficient ==causal next-token prediction== conditioned on visual and language inputs.

## Results

- NAC achieved a **49.73%** success rate on LIBERO-10, outperforming the best baseline (OAT) by **5.56** percentage points, and showed similar performance gains on the RoboMimic benchmark.
- The method demonstrated strong real-world transferability, achieving a **50%** overall success rate across eight physical manipulation tasks, which surpassed competing tokenizers like OAT and FAST (both **40%**).
- NAC compresses each action chunk into **12** tokens, a significant reduction compared to Bin (**224** tokens) and FAST (**36** tokens), while maintaining a practical reconstruction latency of **3.536 ms** on an Nvidia RTX 4090 GPU.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Neural audio codec architectures, specifically SEANet encoders, multi-scale RVQ, adversarial training, and ISTFT decoders, can be effectively repurposed for robot action tokenization, provided their loss functions are specifically tailored for kinematic fidelity rather than human auditory perception.
> - Adversarial training is crucial for preserving the high-frequency components and fine-grained temporal details of robot actions, which are essential for successful control, even if simpler reconstruction losses might yield low overall reconstruction error.
> - Multi-scale Residual Vector Quantization with temporal pooling at each stage allows for efficient and high-fidelity compression by progressively capturing coarse and then fine-grained structural information from action trajectories.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The advancement of large-scale vision-language-action (VLA) models represents a significant direction in robotics, aiming to enable robots to understand natural language instructions and execute complex tasks by mapping visual observations directly to low-level control signals. A fundamental challenge in this paradigm is the representation of continuous robot actions within the discrete token space required by autoregressive sequence models, such as transformers, which form the backbone of many VLA architectures. This process is known as action tokenization.

Existing action tokenization methods often face inherent trade-offs. Early approaches like uniform per-dimension binning, while simple, generate excessively long token sequences, leading to high computational costs and latency. Subsequent methods, such as FAST, improved compression by employing frequency-domain techniques (e.g., Discrete Cosine Transform) and byte-pair encoding. While these methods demonstrated the benefit of compressing the action space, they rely on hand-designed priors that may not capture complex, non-linear dynamics inherent in robot control. More recently, learned tokenizers have emerged, utilizing techniques like vector quantization (VQ-VLA) or residual vector quantization (ActionCodec, FASTer, OAT), often combined with transformer autoencoders or specific regularization to enforce structured token spaces. However, consistently achieving high-fidelity compression, low latency, and robust downstream policy performance remains an active research area.

This paper identifies a parallel domain that has extensively addressed similar challenges: neural audio codecs. These codecs, standard front ends for audio foundation models, compress continuous audio waveforms into discrete codes, demonstrating success in high-fidelity compression, low latency, and modeling complex temporal distributions. The authors observe the structural similarity between audio waveforms and robot action trajectories as continuous time-series signals. However, they also acknowledge key differences, such as the lower frequency range and multi-channel nature of robot actions compared to audio, and the domain-specific optimization objectives (e.g., mel-frequency domain for human auditory perception in audio).

NAC positions itself in this landscape by proposing to adapt the mature and successful architectures of neural audio codecs to the domain of robotic action tokenization. It seeks to bridge the gap by retaining the strengths of these architectures (convolutional encoder-decoders, residual vector quantization, adversarial training) while critically adapting their loss functions and specific components to the kinematic nature of robot actions. By doing so, this work aims to provide a more effective and practical action tokenizer that overcomes some limitations of prior methods, leading to improved VLA model performance and efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized challenges in designing optimal action tokenizers for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The efficacy of VLA models, particularly those based on autoregressive sequence modeling, is heavily dependent on how continuous robot actions are discretized and represented as tokens. The authors identify several critical issues with existing tokenization approaches:

1. **Trade-off between Compression, Latency, and Fidelity:**
 - **Compression:** Naive methods like uniform binning result in prohibitively long token sequences for high-frequency control, increasing computational demands. While methods like FAST offer better compression, their hand-designed frequency priors may not universally capture the nuanced, non-linear dynamics of diverse robotic tasks.
 - **Latency:** The length of the token sequence directly impacts the inference speed of autoregressive policies. Shorter, more compact representations are crucial for achieving practical, low-latency closed-loop control.
 - **Fidelity:** The tokenizer must accurately represent the continuous action trajectories, preserving critical kinematic details that are essential for precise and robust robot control. Loss of fidelity during tokenization can lead to compounding errors in policy execution and suboptimal task performance.

2. **Capturing Statistical Regularities:** An effective tokenizer needs to capture the underlying statistical regularities of the action distribution. This allows the downstream VLA policy to learn and generate action sequences that are coherent, dynamically feasible, and lead to successful task completion. Many existing methods struggle to learn these implicit structures purely from data.

3. **Domain-Specific Adaptation:** While neural audio codecs offer a promising paradigm for compressing continuous time-series data, their direct application to robot actions is problematic due to fundamental differences. Audio models are optimized for human auditory perception using mel-spectrogram losses, which are ill-suited for kinematic signals. This highlights the need for tailored adaptations.

Motivated by the success of neural audio codecs in addressing similar high-fidelity compression and temporal modeling challenges in the audio domain, the key objectives of this work are:

- **To develop a novel learned action tokenizer, the Neural Action Codec (NAC),** based on a multi-scale RVQGAN architecture. This involves adapting the convolutional encoder-decoder structure and Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) principles from state-of-the-art audio codecs to multi-channel robot trajectories.
- **To identify and implement crucial adaptations for applying neural audio codecs to robotic actions.** Specifically, this includes replacing audio-specific mel-spectrogram losses with kinematic-appropriate reconstruction losses (e.g., time-domain Mean Squared Error or non-mel spectral losses) and incorporating adversarial training to maintain fine-grained temporal detail.
- **To formulate an autoregressive behavioral cloning policy that effectively utilizes NAC's structured token space.** This involves leveraging NAC's offset codebooks to enable causal, next-token prediction in a systematic manner.
- **To empirically validate NAC's performance** in terms of both reconstruction fidelity (how accurately it can recover continuous actions from tokens) and, more importantly, downstream policy success rates across a range of simulated and real-world manipulation tasks.
- **To evaluate the practical utility of NAC** by analyzing its compression rates and latency characteristics, demonstrating that it offers a favorable balance for enabling fast and effective closed-loop autoregressive policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Neural Action Codec (NAC) is designed as a learned action tokenizer that adapts state-of-the-art neural audio codec architectures for robotic control. Its core methodology involves treating short robot action trajectories as multi-channel 1D signals and processing them through a specialized convolutional encoder-decoder architecture with Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) and adversarial training.

**1D Signal Representation:**
A continuous robot action chunk, initially represented as a tensor $a \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H_a \times D_a}$ (Batch size, Action horizon, Action dimensionality), is first transformed into a 1D pseudo-waveform. This is achieved by flattening the temporal and feature dimensions, resulting in $a_{flat} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times (H_a \cdot D_a)}$. This flattened representation is then unsqueezed into a single-channel 1D signal $w \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 1 \times L}$, where $L = H_a \cdot D_a$. Optionally, per-dimension linear normalization is applied based on dataset statistics prior to flattening.

**SEANet Encoder:**
The pseudo-waveform $w$ is fed into a fully convolutional SEANet-style encoder. This encoder architecture comprises stacked 1D convolutions that perform strided downsampling, defined by a set of ratios $R = [r_1, \ldots, r_k]$. The total temporal downsampling factor, or hop length, is the product of these ratios, denoted as $\prod R$. The encoder utilizes residual blocks with ELU activations, weight normalization, and reflection padding to extract a continuous latent representation $z \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times D_{enc} \times T'}$, where $D_{enc}$ is the latent dimension and $T'$ is the temporally downsampled length $L / \prod R$.

**Multi-Scale Residual Vector Quantization (MRVQ):**
To discretize the continuous latent $z$, NAC employs Multi-Scale Residual Vector Quantization (MRVQ), a technique adapted from SNAC. This process involves $n_q$ quantization stages, each with its own codebook of size $V_{bins}$ (e.g., 1024). A key departure from standard RVQ is the application of temporal pooling *before* quantization at each stage. This design compels earlier stages to capture coarse, global structures of the trajectory over wider temporal windows, while successive stages focus on refining fine-grained, high-frequency residuals.

At each stage $s$, the unquantized residual $z_s$ (with $z_0 = z$) is temporally pooled. The quantized output $c_s$ is determined by finding the nearest neighbor in the $s$-th codebook $e^{(s)}_j$:
$c_s = \arg \min_{j \in V_{bins}} \| \text{pool}(z_s) - e^{(s)}_j \|^2$
Codebooks are initialized using k-means on the first training batch and updated via exponential moving average (EMA) in subsequent forward passes. The selected codebook embedding $e^{(s)}_{c_s}$ is then upsampled to the encoder’s resolution and subtracted from $z_s$ to form the residual $z_{s+1}$ for the next stage. The final quantized latent representation is the sum of all upsampled embeddings.

**Commitment Loss:**
To prevent the encoder's continuous latent outputs from drifting away from the learned codebook embeddings, a commitment loss is applied: $L_{commit} = \|z - \text{sg}(e_c)\|^2_2$, where $\text{sg}(\cdot)$ is the stop-gradient operator. This regularization helps maintain codebook utilization and prevents collapse, with NAC employing an aggressive scaling factor (e.g., 1000) for this loss.

**Vocos-style Decoder and ISTFT Head:**
The sum of quantized latents is passed to a Vocos-style decoder backbone. This decoder typically includes a Conv1D embedding layer, ResNet blocks with attention, and ConvNeXt blocks. To reconstruct the 1D action signal, an Inverse Short-Time Fourier Transform (ISTFT) Head is utilized. This head predicts STFT magnitude and phase components, which are then combined via the ISTFT operation to recover the continuous 1D signal. Crucially, the ISTFT hop length is synchronized with the encoder’s hop length. This decoder functions as the detokenizer $T^{-1}$, converting discrete latents back into continuous action space.

**Discriminator and Adversarial Loss:**
Neural audio codecs often leverage adversarial training with Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to enhance high-frequency fidelity. NAC adopts this approach by employing discriminators, similar to those found in audio models (e.g., Multi-Period Discriminators, Multi-Resolution Discriminators, or DAC discriminators), adapted for 1D action signals. The DAC discriminator was found to provide the most effective adversarial signal for robotic actions. Adversarial training helps the generator learn to produce smooth, detailed trajectories that are indistinguishable from real action data, capturing rapid corrective motions or sharp velocity changes important for control.

**Reconstruction Loss and Frequency Domain Shift:**
A critical adaptation for NAC is the modification of the reconstruction loss. Unlike audio codecs that rely on mel-spectrogram losses optimized for human auditory perception, NAC entirely drops this component for robotic actions. Mel-spectrogram losses were found to severely degrade control signal representations. Instead, the generator loss $L_{gen}$ is formulated as:
$L_{gen} = L_{reconst} + \lambda_{commit} L_{commit} + L_{adv}$
Here, $L_{reconst}$ can be Mean Squared Error (MSE), L1 loss, or an unscaled spectrogram loss. MSE was found to yield the strongest downstream control performance, while spectrogram loss provided the best reconstruction MSE. $L_{adv}$ is the adversarial loss derived from the DAC discriminator.

**Behavioral Cloning Policy (NACPolicy):**
Once the NAC tokenizer is trained and frozen, an autoregressive behavioral cloning policy, "NACPolicy," is trained. This policy predicts the discrete token sequence directly, conditioned on visual observations and language instructions. NAC introduces **offset codebooks** to structure the generation process. If the tokenizer has $n_q$ scales, the policy's vocabulary size is $|V| = n_q \times V_{bins} + 1$ (including a Beginning-Of-Sequence (BOS) token). Token IDs for scale $s$ are offset to fall within a specific range $[s \cdot V_{bins}, (s+1) \cdot V_{bins})$.

During training, ground truth discrete codes (a list of $n_q$ tensors) are offset and concatenated into a flat 1D sequence. The policy predicts tokens for scale 0 first, followed by scale 1, and so on: $C_{flat} = [\text{BOS}, C^{(0)}_1, \ldots, C^{(0)}_{L_0}, C^{(1)}_1, \ldots, C^{(1)}_{L_1}, \ldots]$. The policy is optimized using a standard causal cross-entropy loss over this structured sequence.
During inference, the policy autoregressively generates tokens based on this fixed layout. These tokens are then partitioned into per-scale segments, code indices are recovered via modulo arithmetic, and passed to the frozen NAC detokenizer $T^{-1}$ to yield continuous actions. These actions are executed in a receding horizon fashion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of NAC aimed to address its ability to model robot actions using audio codec architectures, the necessary adaptations, its impact on downstream control, transferability to real-world scenarios, and its compression-latency trade-offs.

**Experimental Setup:**
The evaluation was conducted across three benchmarks: LIBERO-10 and RoboMimic for simulated manipulation tasks, and a suite of eight real-world physical manipulation tasks. NAC was compared against several baselines: Bin (uniform per-dimension binning), Diffusion Policy (continuous control), FAST (frequency-domain compression), VQ-VLA (vector quantized VLA), and OAT (ordered action tokenization). All policy comparisons shared observation inputs, action horizons, and training protocols. Performance was primarily measured by task success rate (%) for policies and reconstruction Mean Squared Error (MSE) for tokenizer ablations.

**1. Viability of Audio Codec Architectures for Robot Actions:**
The initial findings confirmed that neural audio codecs can indeed provide a viable foundation for action tokenization, but only with critical adaptations.
- **Reconstruction Loss:** Table 1a demonstrates that directly using audio-specific mel-spectrogram training objectives results in near-zero downstream policy performance (0% success rate), indicating a fundamental mismatch for kinematic signals. In contrast, simple signal-domain losses (L1, MSE) or non-mel frequency-domain losses (Spectrogram) produced robust policies. MSE loss yielded the highest downstream control performance (49.2% success), while spectrogram loss achieved the lowest reconstruction MSE (0.0002). This highlights the importance of optimizing for kinematic fidelity rather than human perceptual priors.
- **Adversarial Training:** Table 1b showed that removing the discriminator entirely led to a complete failure in downstream policy performance (0% success), despite maintaining a low reconstruction MSE (0.35). This suggests that adversarial training is crucial for preserving the fine-grained temporal details and high-frequency components necessary for precise and successful robot control. Among discriminator types, the DAC discriminator provided the strongest adversarial signal and highest policy performance (49.45%).
- **Decoder Head:** The choice of decoder head also proved significant. As shown in Table 1c, replacing the ISTFT head with a simpler linear decoder worsened both reconstruction fidelity and downstream policy success (42.1% vs 48.3% for ISTFT). This indicates that the ISTFT-style decoder, with its ability to recover phase information, is valuable for reconstructing smooth and detailed action trajectories.

These results collectively demonstrate that the core convolutional encoder, residual vector quantization, adversarial training, and ISTFT-style decoder recipe from audio codecs effectively transfers to actions when its optimization objectives are tailored for kinematic signals.

**2. Downstream Performance Improvement in Simulation:**
NAC consistently achieved superior performance across simulated manipulation benchmarks.
- **LIBERO-10:** As detailed in Table 2, NAC achieved a success rate of 49.73% ± 1.0, outperforming all baselines. This represents a significant improvement over FAST (38.02% ± 1.3) by 11.71 percentage points and OAT (44.17% ± 1.2) by 5.56 percentage points.
- **RoboMimic:** Similar trends were observed on the RoboMimic benchmark, where NAC obtained 33.94% ± 1.86 success, surpassing OAT (31.94% ± 2.15) and FAST (28.38% ± 2.37).
These findings indicate that NAC's learned multi-scale residual quantization strategy creates a more effective interface for autoregressive policy learning compared to both hand-designed compression schemes and other learned tokenizers, leading to better policy performance.

**3. Transferability to Real-World Tasks:**
The gains observed in simulation also translated to real-world performance, indicating the robustness of NAC's learned token space under practical conditions, including noise, calibration errors, and execution mismatch.
- **Overall Real-World Success:** Across eight diverse physical manipulation tasks, NAC achieved the highest overall real-world success rate of 50%, as shown in Table 2. This outperformed OAT and FAST, both of which achieved 40% success.
- **Task-Specific Gains:** NAC demonstrated notable improvements on tasks requiring high precision and localized corrections, such as "Grapes" (100% success for NAC vs. 80% for FAST and OAT) and "Two Blocks" (30% success for NAC, while all other methods failed). While no single tokenizer dominated every task, NAC exhibited superior performance on average, suggesting better generalization.

**4. Compression and Latency Characteristics:**
Action tokenization aims to reduce sequence length for efficient policy processing.
- **Compression Rate:** Table 3 illustrates that NAC compresses each action chunk into 12 tokens. This matches the compression of other top learned tokenizers like OAT and VQ-VLA, but represents a substantial reduction in token count compared to Bin (224 tokens, ~19x less) and FAST (36 tokens, ~3x less). This compact representation is critical for reducing the computational burden on autoregressive policies.
- **Latency:** NAC's total reconstruction time (encoder + decoder) on a single Nvidia RTX 4090 GPU was 3.536 ms (1.270 ms for encoding, 2.183 ms for decoding). While this is slower than hand-designed methods like FAST (0.290 ms) and Bin (0.079 ms), it is significantly faster than VQ-VLA (11.049 ms) and falls within a practical range for real-time control.

In summary, NAC provides a favorable balance between high-fidelity reconstruction, significant compression, and acceptable latency, making high-frequency autoregressive policies more viable.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of the Neural Action Codec (NAC) represents a notable advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Cross-Domain Architectural Transfer:** NAC successfully demonstrates that advanced neural architectures and principles developed for one continuous time-series domain (neural audio codecs) can be effectively adapted and repurposed for another (robot action control). This highlights a broader potential for knowledge transfer between seemingly disparate domains in machine learning, potentially accelerating innovation by leveraging mature solutions.

2. **Enhanced Action Tokenization:** By meticulously adapting multi-scale RVQGAN architectures from audio codecs, NAC provides a learned action tokenizer that addresses critical limitations of previous methods. It achieves a superior balance of compression, fidelity, and low latency, which are often conflicting objectives in action representation. The high-fidelity reconstruction capabilities, validated across simulation and real-world tasks, indicate that NAC better preserves the kinematic details essential for precise robot control.

3. **Improved VLA Model Performance:** The empirical results consistently show NAC outperforming both hand-designed and other learned action tokenizers in downstream policy success rates across diverse manipulation benchmarks. This suggests that NAC's compact and structured discrete action space provides a more effective latent representation for autoregressive VLA policies to learn from, leading to more capable and robust robot behaviors. Its structured token space, via offset codebooks, simplifies the causal next-token prediction task for policy models.

4. **Practicality for Real-World Robotics:** The successful transfer of NAC's benefits from simulation to physical robot tasks is a crucial indicator of its practical utility. Its ability to handle the complexities and noise of real-world environments, leading to higher average success rates, positions NAC as a strong candidate for deployment in real robotic systems. The demonstrated gains on tasks requiring fine-grained control are particularly impactful.

5. **Enabling Efficient Autoregressive Policies:** The significant compression achieved by NAC (12 tokens per action chunk) directly contributes to reducing the sequence length processed by autoregressive VLA policies. This reduction in token count lowers computational costs during inference, making it more feasible to run high-frequency, closed-loop control policies in real-time. While not the fastest in terms of pure tokenization latency, its overall balance of performance and compression makes it a practical solution for contemporary VLA architectures.

6. **Foundation for Future Robot Foundation Models:** By providing a robust and efficient action tokenization backbone, NAC can facilitate the development and scaling of next-generation generalist robot foundation models. These models often rely on large-scale datasets and require effective discrete action representations to generalize across numerous tasks and embodiments. NAC's approach, drawing inspiration from successful audio foundation models, aligns with this future trajectory.

**Potential Future Work:** The authors acknowledge limitations, such as the requirement for action chunk lengths to be strictly divisible by downsampling ratios and the need for explicit knowledge of target action dimensionality. Future work aims to address these by exploring cross-embodiment shared token spaces without naive padding, leveraging massive diverse action data for scaling gains similar to audio foundation models, and applying NAC to even higher-frequency control domains given its superior compression. These directions suggest that NAC could serve as a foundational element for further innovation in robot learning.
