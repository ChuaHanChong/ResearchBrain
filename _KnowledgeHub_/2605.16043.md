# Learning Sim-Grounded Policies for Bimanual Rope Manipulation from Human Teleoperation Data

> [!summary] Summary
> A Snapshot-to-Action paradigm for bimanual rope untangling: a single multi-view RGB-D observation is segmented, skeletonized into 100 ordered 3D particles, and evolved in an XPBD Cosserat-rod simulation that conditions an ACT policy. Against an identical vision-based ACT baseline on a held-out stiffer rope, the particle policy cuts L1 action error 30.8% with 7.2x faster inference.

## Problem

- End-to-end visual imitation policies for deformable linear objects break when wrist-mounted cameras are occluded by the robot's own end-effectors during bimanual manipulation.
- Prior untangling pipelines rely on topological keypoints plus fixed motion primitives, or keep humans in the loop, rather than learning continuous bimanual trajectories.
- Evidence that structured 3D observations beat pixels under small data exists only for rigid and articulated objects, not for one-dimensional deformable manifolds.

## Method

- Snapshot initialization: SAM2 masks plus aligned depth fuse multi-view clouds, ==Voronoi skeletonization== and depth-gradient crossing resolution yield 100 arc-length-resampled ordered 3D particles.
- Simulation playback re-executes recorded teleoperation actions inside an ==XPBD Cosserat-rod== environment, producing fully observed particle-state trajectories even during heavy gripper occlusion.
- A **ParticleEncoder** replaces the ResNet backbone in ==ACT==: a two-layer GELU/LayerNorm MLP tokenizes each particle with 1D sinusoidal encodings, sharing the ==CVAE== decoder and action head.

## Results

- The particle policy reaches L1 error **9.70e-3** versus **14.02e-3** for the vision baseline on the held-out stiffest rope, a **30.8%** reduction over a 20-step chunk.
- Inference time drops from **21.34 ms** to **2.95 ms** per step (**86.2%** faster) and peak VRAM from **623.66 MB** to **334.40 MB** (**46.4%** lower).
- Trained on only **64** of **96** unscripted teleoperation demonstrations across **four** ropes, the policy resolves an unseen overhand knot in one open-loop grasp-and-pull macro action.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When demonstrations are scarce, the observation space, not architecture or data volume, is the binding constraint on generalization; identical backbones diverge purely on input structure.
> - Physics simulation can serve as a state estimator, not just a data source, sustaining a coherent object representation through occlusions where pixel tracking loses the trace.
> - The gain is confounded: the particle pipeline exploits depth while the baseline is RGB-only, and fixed simulation parameters will drift without online system identification.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The manipulation of Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs) such as ropes and cables presents a persistent challenge in robotics, primarily due to their high-dimensional configuration spaces, inherent visual homogeneity, and frequent self-occlusions during manipulation. Traditional imitation learning (IL) approaches often rely on mapping continuous visual streams from egocentric cameras directly to robot actions. However, these "end-to-end" visual policies commonly encounter limitations, including computational inefficiency, vulnerability to perceptual noise, and particularly, the occlusion of the object by the robot's own end-effectors (EEFs).

Prior work in DLO manipulation, particularly for tasks like rope untangling, has explored various strategies. These include topological state estimation combined with discrete action primitives, where keypoints are predicted for pull-and-pin actions, or learned components are used for grasp refinement and failure recovery. Some methods generalize to complex multi-cable knots by planning over topological graphs. Other assistive teleoperation approaches keep a human in the control loop for real-time planning. These methods often abstract the DLO state into discrete elements or rely on human input for topological reasoning. In contrast, the current work aims to learn continuous bimanual trajectories directly from diverse human demonstrations, utilizing a particle-based representation to capture finer geometric details and bypass the need for predefined action primitives.

Furthermore, this research contributes to the broader discussion on the efficacy of 3D versus 2D observation spaces for imitation learning. Recent studies have indicated that structured 3D representations can outperform raw 2D images, especially when training datasets are limited. This has been demonstrated for rigid and articulated objects, where 3D diffusion policies or object-centric 3D priors have shown improved generalization across variations in space, viewpoint, appearance, and instance. This paper extends this line of inquiry to DLOs, a category of objects where the relevant 3D structure is a continuous manifold rather than discrete entities, and where self-occlusion from the gripper is a dominant factor.

Finally, this work builds upon the Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) framework, which has advanced bimanual imitation learning by predicting synchronized action chunks using a CVAE Transformer architecture. While standard ACT policies typically rely on continuous visual streams throughout a trajectory, this study adapts the framework to a "Snapshot-to-Action" paradigm. This involves grounding the ACT policy in a physics-based particle state, initialized from an initial multi-view observation, to enhance spatial equivariance and robustness to occlusions inherent in bimanual DLO manipulation.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to address the persistent challenges in robotic manipulation of Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs), particularly the task of untangling ropes. DLOs are ubiquitous in various applications but remain difficult for robots to manipulate due to their high-dimensional configuration spaces and frequent self-occlusion, especially during bimanual operations where robot end-effectors can obscure the object of interest.

The core objective of this study is to investigate whether the limitations in generalization observed in egocentric visual policies for knot untangling tasks stem primarily from the choice of observation space, rather than from policy architecture or the quantity of training data. The researchers hypothesize that a more robust and physically consistent state representation can lead to improved policy performance and generalization from limited human demonstrations.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:
- **Quantifying the Observability Gap:** To measure and quantify the performance difference, termed the "observability gap," between policies conditioned on raw pixel-level observations and those conditioned on physics-consistent state representations. This aims to empirically demonstrate the advantages of a structured, physics-grounded state.
- **Enhancing Robustness to Occlusion:** To develop a manipulation paradigm that explicitly addresses the challenge of self-occlusion by robot end-effectors. The motivation is to shift from continuous visual tracking, which is prone to failure when occlusions occur, to a method where a physically consistent state can be maintained even without direct visual input.
- **Improving Computational Efficiency:** To reduce the computational burden associated with processing high-bandwidth continuous visual data. By employing a compact state representation derived from an initial snapshot, the aim is to achieve faster inference times and lower memory usage, making policies more suitable for real-time operation on resource-constrained robotic platforms.
- **Achieving Data-Efficient Learning and Generalization:** To explore a path towards more data-efficient robot learning for DLO manipulation. The hypothesis is that by grounding observations in a physics-based simulation, policies can generalize more effectively across different instances of DLOs, even with variations in physical properties, from smaller and more diverse human demonstration datasets.
- **Proposing a "Snapshot-to-Action" Paradigm:** To introduce and validate a novel approach where the rope's topological state is extracted once from a multi-view observation and then evolved within a particle-based eXtended Position-Based Dynamics (XPBD) simulation. This paradigm aims to replace the continuous visual feedback loop with an initial state anchor that preserves physical integrity.
- **Benchmarking State Representation:** To conduct a controlled ablation study comparing two Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policies – one visual-based and one state-based – trained on identical bimanual teleoperation data. This comparison is designed to isolate the impact of the observation space itself on a policy's ability to generalize to unseen rope configurations.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to provide a more practical and scalable approach to bimanual DLO manipulation, particularly in scenarios where human teleoperation data is limited.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The study's methodology focuses on learning a policy that predicts bimanual manipulation actions for tangled ropes, comparing the performance of a policy conditioned on raw visual input against one conditioned on simulation-grounded particle coordinates.

**A. Data Acquisition and Bimanual Setup:**
Human teleoperation demonstrations were collected for the task of bimanual overhand-knot untangling. The dataset comprised 96 demonstrations, executed on four distinct ropes that varied in length, color, and stiffness. These demonstrations included diverse and unconstrained human manipulation strategies, explicitly incorporating sub-optimal actions and exploratory movements to capture a broad distribution of human topological reasoning. A bimanual teleoperation interface recorded 6-DoF gripper poses (position and quaternion) and continuous gripper openness at 100 Hz, subsequently resampled to 30 Hz to align with the simulation rate. The objective for policy learning was to predict only the initial "macro action" to capture human intent, rather than attempting to untangle the rope fully.

The experimental setup utilized two 7-DoF Franka Panda arms, each equipped with a Robotiq 2F-140 gripper and a wrist-mounted ZEDX stereo camera. Human teleoperation was performed using an HTC Vive Pro VR system, which provided 6-DoF end-effector commands. The 96 demonstrations were partitioned into 64 for training, 15 for validation, and 17 for testing. The test set was specifically curated to consist entirely of demonstrations performed on a held-out rope, selected for being the stiffest and thus dynamically most distinct from the training distribution, to assess zero-shot generalization to unseen physical properties.

**B. Ground-Truth Generation via Simulation Playback (for the particle-based policy):**
To obtain continuous particle states from the teleoperation data, a Simulation Playback pipeline was developed:
- **Snapshot Initialization:** Multi-view RGB-D frames were captured just before the gripper initiated a grasp, a phase chosen to avoid occlusion by the robot's end-effectors. The Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM2) was used to generate binary masks of the rope. These masks were then combined with aligned depth maps and aggregated into a unified, voxel-downsampled 3D point cloud. To extract the rope's one-dimensional topology, this point cloud was projected onto a 2D table plane, and a Voronoi-based skeletonization algorithm was applied to estimate its centerline. The keypoints of this centerline were then lifted back into a 3D ordered structure, with ambiguous self-intersections resolved by analyzing the relative depth gradient. Finally, this centerline was uniformly resampled to a fixed sequence of N=100 particles via arc-length interpolation, resulting in a particle state representation G_3D ∈ R^(100x3). This canonicalization ensured consistent input dimensionality, where each particle index corresponded to a specific fractional position along the rope's arc length.
- **Action Re-execution and Labeling:** Within an XPBD (eXtended Position-Based Dynamics) environment, a simulated gripper was spawned. The recorded human teleoperation actions (a_human(t)) were then re-executed in this simulation. The resulting trajectory of the simulated rope's particles, {G_3D(t)} from t=0 to T, was recorded and synchronized with the actions. This process yielded a continuous, fully observed state signal throughout each demonstration, including phases where visual tracking would fail due to gripper occlusion, providing the (state, action) pairs necessary for training the particle-based policy.

**C. Policy Learning with Action Chunking (ACT):**
The study utilized the ACT framework, which models the policy π as a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE). During training, a Transformer encoder compresses the ground-truth action sequence into a latent style variable (z), and a Transformer decoder conditions on z, the current observation, and learned action queries to predict a chunk of k future bimanual actions. At inference time, z is sampled from the prior, and overlapping action chunks are smoothed.

Two input variants for the observation (s) were defined, sharing the decoder, action head, and CVAE latent structure:
- **Vision-based State (s_vis):** This variant used multi-view RGB images from both wrist-mounted cameras as input. A shared, pre-trained ResNet-18 backbone independently extracted spatial feature maps from each view. These features were then projected to the Transformer’s hidden dimension (d=512) via a 1x1 convolution, concatenated along the spatial axis, and augmented with sinusoidal 2D position encodings.
- **Particle-based State (s_part):** This variant replaced image processing with a custom ParticleEncoder. The N=100 resampled 3D particle coordinates from the simulation playback pipeline served as input. These coordinates were projected through a two-layer MLP with GELU activation and LayerNorm, generating one token per particle. Sinusoidal 1D positional encodings were added to maintain the sequential ordering along the rope’s arc length.

Both variants additionally received current bimanual proprioception (q ∈ R^16), consisting of 8-dimensional state information (3D position, 4D quaternion, and 1D gripper openness) for each arm, as an embedded input token. Both policies were trained using the standard ACT loss, which combines L1 action reconstruction and KL regularization.

**D. Evaluation:**
Policies were quantitatively evaluated offline against human teleoperation trajectories from the held-out test set. For the particle-based policy, an initial multi-view RGB-D snapshot was used to initialize the rope's geometry in the simulation. The simulation was then propagated forward using Newton Physics, incorporating an XPBD solver based on the Cosserat rod model, to continuously provide updated particle state information. In contrast, the baseline vision policy processed continuous multi-view RGB observations directly at each evaluation timestep. Both policies predicted open-loop action chunks. Performance was measured using the L1 error, defined as the mean absolute distance between predicted and ground-truth bimanual end-effector poses at the end of a k=20-step trajectory. Computational metrics, including inference time per single step and peak VRAM usage during evaluation, were also recorded on an NVIDIA RTX 4060 Ti GPU.

### 4. Main findings and results

The study's quantitative and qualitative evaluations provided a comparison between the vision-based ACT policy and the sim-grounded particle-based ACT policy.

**Quantitative Performance Metrics:**
Table I presents a summary of the action prediction performance and computational metrics.
- **L1 Error:** The particle-based policy (S) achieved a mean L1 error of 9.70 ± 7.95 (x 10^-3), while the vision-based policy (V) yielded an L1 error of 14.02 ± 8.44 (x 10^-3). This indicates a 30.8% reduction in L1 error for the particle-based policy compared to its visual counterpart, demonstrating its superior ability to predict the initial grasp-and-pull action on unseen rope configurations.
- **Inference Time:** The particle-based policy exhibited an average inference time of 2.95 ± 0.05 ms per single step, significantly faster than the vision-based policy's 21.34 ± 0.21 ms. This represents an 86.2% reduction in inference time for the particle-based approach, approximately a 7.2x speedup.
- **Peak VRAM Usage:** The particle-based policy consumed a peak VRAM of 334.40 MB, which is 46.4% less than the vision-based policy's 623.66 MB. This demonstrates notable memory efficiency.

These quantitative results indicate that the sim-grounded particle-based policy outperforms the vision-based policy in terms of accuracy, inference speed, and memory efficiency when predicting macro actions for untangling knots on unseen rope instances. The observed reduction in L1 error is attributed to the "observability gap" between raw pixel observations and a physics-consistent state, which the study aimed to quantify.

**Qualitative Performance:**
Figure 1 illustrates an open-loop rollout of the particle-based policy on a held-out rope, which possessed distinct physical properties (stiffness and appearance) not present in the training set. From an initial particle state, the policy successfully predicted a single grasp-and-pull macro action that resolved an overhand knot. This qualitative observation suggests that the particle representation can encode sufficient information regarding the initial knot topology to enable a geometrically valid untangling strategy, even for rope instances with physical characteristics unseen during training. While this level of success was not universally achieved across all test samples, the example demonstrates the policy's potential for zero-shot transfer.

**Summary of Findings:**
The main findings establish that grounding the initial visual observation of a DLO into a particle-based simulation provides a more robust, efficient, and generalizable state representation for bimanual rope manipulation compared to continuous egocentric visual streams. The particle-based approach demonstrated improved accuracy in action prediction, significantly faster inference, and reduced memory footprint, thereby addressing limitations related to occlusion and computational overhead in traditional visual policies.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The findings of this research present several points of significance and potential impact on the field of robotic manipulation, particularly concerning Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs).

**Improved Robustness and Generalization:** The work demonstrates that a physics-grounded particle-based state representation offers enhanced robustness against common challenges in DLO manipulation, notably self-occlusion by robot end-effectors. By converting an initial multi-view observation into a physically consistent particle state, the policy can maintain an accurate understanding of the rope's configuration even when direct visual tracking fails. This approach enables better zero-shot generalization to unseen rope instances, including those with different physical properties (e.g., stiffness), which is a critical step towards deploying robots in varied real-world environments.

**Quantified Observability Gap:** The study quantifies a significant "observability gap" between raw pixel observations and physics-consistent states. This empirical measurement provides concrete evidence that for complex, high-degree-of-freedom tasks like knot untangling, the quality and structural clarity of the observation space can be a more substantial bottleneck than the volume of human demonstration data or the capacity of the policy architecture (e.g., Transformer models). This insight challenges the assumption that increasing data alone will solve generalization issues in visual-based learning for DLOs.

**Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** The particle-based approach results in a substantial reduction in inference time (7.2x speedup) and peak VRAM usage (46.4% reduction). This computational efficiency is crucial for real-time robotic applications and enables the deployment of complex manipulation policies on resource-constrained edge systems. This can broaden the accessibility and practical applicability of advanced DLO manipulation capabilities.

**Paradigm Shift for DLO Learning:** The research proposes and validates a "Snapshot-to-Action" paradigm, which shifts the burden of continuous visual tracking and representation learning from end-to-end visual policies to an initial state grounding and subsequent physics-based simulation. This approach suggests a more data-efficient pathway toward generalizable manipulation by encoding physical priors directly into the state representation, reducing the need for extensive and diverse visual training data that covers all possible environmental and object variations.

**Future Research Directions:** The study also identifies limitations and suggests future research avenues. The reliance on a fixed physics model parameters (e.g., for Cosserat rod stiffness, friction) implies potential drift between simulated and physical rope states over time. This highlights the need for integrating active System Identification modules to estimate material properties online or implementing periodic re-grounding strategies to re-initialize the particle state from fresh visual snapshots. Furthermore, comparing against an RGB-D ACT baseline would help isolate the specific gains attributed to explicit topological structure versus just depth information.

Overall, this work contributes to making robotic DLO manipulation more practical, robust, and scalable by providing a method that is less susceptible to visual occlusions and more efficient computationally, opening doors for broader applications in both household and industrial settings.
