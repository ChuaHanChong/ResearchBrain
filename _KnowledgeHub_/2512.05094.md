# From Generated Human Videos to Physically Plausible Robot Trajectories

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley and NYU developed GenMimic, a two-stage framework that enables humanoid robots to mimic human actions from synthetically generated, noisy videos in a zero-shot manner. The system achieved an 86.77% success rate in simulation on a new benchmark and successfully reproduced coherent upper-body motions on a physical Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to generalize to novel tasks specified by abstract, high-level commands without extensive task-specific training.
- Video generative models produce outputs with inherent noise, morphological inconsistencies, and physical implausibilities, making them unsuitable for direct robot control.
- Current humanoid control approaches often rely on expensive, high-quality human motion capture data, limiting scalability and adaptability to diverse, unforeseen scenarios.

## Method

- **GenMimic** utilizes a two-stage pipeline: first, a ==4D human reconstruction== model extracts body poses and shapes from generated RGB videos, which are then retargeted to the robot's morphology as 3D keypoint trajectories.
- A robust, physics-aware reinforcement learning policy is trained in simulation using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== to mimic these retargeted 4D humanoid trajectories.
- The policy incorporates weighted tracking rewards, prioritizing critical keypoints, and an auxiliary ==bilateral symmetry loss== to enhance robustness against noisy, imperfect motion cues from generated videos.

## Results

- GenMimic's teacher policy achieved an **86.77%** success rate in simulation on the GenMimicBench dataset, significantly outperforming baselines (e.g., GMT **4.29%**, TWIST **2.69%**) on generated video inputs.
- The policy successfully deployed on a **23-DoF** Unitree G1 humanoid robot, reproducing a wide range of upper-body motions (e.g., waving, pointing) with a **1.0** visual success rate for in-place actions.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both weighted keypoint rewards (increasing SR from **40.0%** to **77.4%**) and the symmetry loss (further increasing SR to **86.8%**) were crucial for GenMimic's superior robustness and performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Video generative models can serve as scalable high-level action planners for robots, provided the robot control policy is specifically designed to handle their inherent noise and morphological inaccuracies.
> - Using 3D keypoints as the goal observation space and applying weighted tracking rewards significantly improves a robot's ability to robustly mimic motions from noisy, generated sources.
> - Incorporating an inductive bias like bilateral symmetry loss into the policy training objective further enhances robustness, allowing the robot to perform more reliably even when presented with inconsistent or physically implausible motions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the crucial intersection of several rapidly advancing fields: **humanoid robot control, zero-shot generalization in robotics, and video generative models.** It aims to bridge a significant gap between the impressive capabilities of generative AI and the complex requirements for real-world physical robot execution.

**In humanoid robot control**, the field has seen tremendous progress, largely driven by reinforcement learning (RL) and physics-based simulation. Previous works have enabled robots to master diverse locomotion skills, perform contact-based tasks, and even achieve complex maneuvers like parkour. A prominent paradigm involves learning from human motion capture (MoCap) data, giving rise to generalizable whole-body controllers for teleoperation and high-fidelity motion reproduction (e.g., H2O, OmniH2O, HOVER, GMT, TWIST). More recent efforts, like VideoMimic, have extended these capabilities to incorporate scene and object awareness. This paper builds upon this foundation but distinguishes itself by tackling the challenge of **novel, synthetic inputs** rather than relying on high-quality, real-world motion data.

**Zero-shot generalization** has been a hallmark of large-scale language and vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP and modern multimodal LLMs. These models can perform tasks on unseen categories or generate content without specific fine-tuning, demonstrating remarkable transfer capabilities. In robotics, this concept has evolved from perception (open-vocabulary object detection) to action, giving rise to Vision-Language-Action (VLA) frameworks (e.g., RT-2, SayCan, OpenVLA). These frameworks ground robot control in multimodal representations to execute novel tasks. While generalist humanoid policies have also achieved zero-shot behavior in full-body imitation, they typically depend on high-quality motion capture. This paper pushes the boundary by enabling zero-shot imitation from motions *reconstructed from noisy generated videos*, thus extending VLA concepts to physically embodied imitation from highly diverse, abstractly described prompts.

**Video generative models** have witnessed an explosion of progress, now capable of synthesizing human actions with impressive temporal coherence and semantic consistency from text or visual prompts (e.g., Wan, Hunyuan-Video, MovieGen, Cosmos-Predict2). These models can generate diverse scenarios, identities, and action sequences. The prevailing trend is towards controllable synthesis, identity preservation, and multimodal conditioning. This paper reframes these powerful video generative models not just as content creators but as **potential high-level action planners for robotic control.** This shift in perspective is critical, as it unlocks the ability for robots to "imagine" and then execute a vast array of actions specified by abstract commands, moving beyond pre-programmed or strictly learned behaviors from real data.

The work directly addresses a key barrier to realizing the full potential of these generative models for robotics: their outputs are often noisy, morphologically inconsistent, and physically implausible, making direct use for robot control challenging. By developing a robust policy specifically designed to handle these imperfections, GenMimic represents a significant step towards enabling general-purpose robots that can flexibly interpret and execute high-level, generatively specified plans, ultimately paving the way for more autonomous and adaptable robotic agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **enable humanoid robots to faithfully reproduce human actions depicted in generated videos in a zero-shot manner.** This means the robot should be able to execute motions from videos it has never encountered before, without any task-specific fine-tuning.

The motivation for this objective stems from the rapid advancements in two distinct but related fields:

- **Emergence of Powerful Video Generative Models:** The authors observe that video generation models are becoming increasingly sophisticated at synthesizing human actions in novel contexts. These models hold immense potential to serve as "high-level planners" for contextual robot control, allowing users to describe desired robot behaviors through natural language or abstract visual prompts, which are then translated into video demonstrations.
- **Need for General-Purpose Robotic Agents:** The long-term goal in robotics is to develop general-purpose agents capable of planning and adapting to a wide range of unforeseen tasks and contexts. Current humanoid control approaches often rely on hand-crafted objectives or extensive training on real motion capture data, limiting their adaptability to truly novel scenarios. Leveraging video generative models could provide a scalable and flexible source of diverse task specifications.

However, a critical challenge arises: **generated videos are often noisy and exhibit morphological distortions or physical implausibilities** that make direct imitation difficult for robots, especially when compared to using high-quality real video or motion capture data. The paper is motivated to overcome this "inherent noise and morphological inaccuracies" to unlock the potential of generative AI for physically embodied agents. The key question is not *if* video generation can create human actions, but *how* a robot can robustly *execute* those imperfect, generated actions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **GenMimic**, a two-stage pipeline designed to translate generated human videos into physically plausible robot trajectories in a zero-shot manner.

**Stage 1: From Pixels to 4D Humanoid Reconstruction**
The first stage focuses on converting the raw, generated RGB video into a robot-understandable motion trajectory:
1. **4D Human Reconstruction:** Given an input RGB video (generated by models like Wan2.1 or Cosmos-Predict2), a state-of-the-art human reconstruction model (e.g., TRAM) is used. This model detects and extracts per-frame global pose ($\theta_t \in \mathbb{R}^7$) and SMPL parameters (shape $\beta_t \in \mathbb{R}^{16}$ and per-joint angle-axis $J_t \in \mathbb{R}^{J \times 3}$). The SMPL model provides a high-fidelity 3D representation of the human body.
2. **Retargeting to Humanoid Morphology:** Because the reconstructed human morphology does not perfectly match the robot's physical structure, the SMPL trajectory is "retargeted" to the robot's joint space ($q_t^{goal}$) using a method like PHC. This process adapts the human motion to the robot's kinematics and physical constraints. The retargeted joint angles, combined with the global pose, yield global 3D keypoints ($p_t^{goal}$) in robot space, which serve as the primary goal input for the next stage. The authors specifically choose 3D keypoints over joint angles as keypoints are "more robust to variations and noise is more directly observable in this representation."

**Stage 2: From 4D Humanoid to Actions (The GenMimic Policy)**
This stage involves training a robust, physics-aware policy using reinforcement learning (RL) to mimic the retargeted 4D humanoid trajectory.

1. **Policy Formulation (POMDP):** The problem is framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). A privileged teacher policy ($\pi_s$) is trained in simulation using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). This teacher has access to full state information. The learned behaviors are then distilled into a student policy ($\pi_o$) using DAgger, which only observes proprioceptive information and goal 3D keypoints.
2. **Observation Space:**
 - **Student Policy ($π_o$):** Receives proprioceptive information (joint positions and velocities, root angular velocity, projected gravity vector, previous action) over past $\ell_{proprio}$ steps, concatenated with goal 3D keypoints over $\ell_{goal}$ future steps.
 - **Teacher Policy ($π_s$):** In addition to the student's observations, it has access to complete simulator state information (robot's global 3D position, orientation, linear/angular velocities, and simulation parameters), as well as the difference between goal and robot positions.
3. **GenMimic's Core Robustness Features:**
 - **Weighted Tracking Rewards:** To handle noise and morphological mismatches, GenMimic employs a novel tracking reward that uses a weighted combination of per-keypoint errors. Critical keypoints, such as end-effectors (hands, head), are given higher weights, while less reliable or less task-relevant points (e.g., lower body, torso, where generated video noise can be more pronounced) receive lower weights. This allows the policy to selectively attend to the most important features for stable mimicry. The reward function $R_{weighted}^t = \exp(-\sum_{j=1}^n w_j \|p_{t,j} - p_{t,j}^{goal}\|^2_2 / \sigma^2)$ quantifies this.
 - **Symmetry Loss:** Leveraging the inherent bilateral symmetry of the human body, an auxiliary symmetry loss ($L_{SYM}(\pi)$) is incorporated into the PPO training objective. This loss encourages the policy to learn connections between symmetric keypoints (e.g., left and right limbs). If one side of the motion is noisy, the policy can implicitly leverage information from its mirror reflection, enhancing robustness to localized errors. This introduces an "inductive bias for robustness."
4. **Policy Learning Details:**
 - **Training Data:** GenMimic is trained solely on human motion trajectories from AMASS, a large-scale real motion capture dataset. These trajectories are retargeted to the robot's morphology.
 - **Rewards:** A comprehensive reward function includes the weighted tracking rewards (for joint position, joint velocity, body positions, and body rotations), energy regularization terms (acceleration, velocity, action rate), simulator-specific penalties (feet slippage, joint limits), an orientation penalty for stability, termination penalties, and alive rewards. Additional feet penalties are included for safer real-world deployment.
 - **Domain Randomization:** To facilitate sim-to-real transfer and improve robustness to external perturbations, extensive domain randomization is applied during training. This includes randomizing friction, mass, PD controller gains, motor strength, control delay, and applying external impulses to the robot. Input noise is also added to states and observations.
5. **Real-world Execution:** The trained GenMimic policy outputs desired joint angles ($q_t^{des}$), which are then passed to a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller to generate actionable torques for the physical robot (Unitree G1 humanoid).

**Evaluation Benchmark (GenMimicBench):**
To systematically evaluate the zero-shot generalization and policy robustness on generated videos, the authors curated **GenMimicBench**. This synthetic dataset comprises 428 videos generated using two state-of-the-art video generative models: Wan2.1-VACE-14B and Cosmos-Predict2-14B-Sample-GR00T-Dreams-GR1.
- **Wan2.1 Videos:** 217 clean, multi-view videos generated from NTU RGB+D frames, covering diverse subjects, structured action compositions (simple upper-body, upper-body + walking, composite, composite + walking), and camera viewpoints.
- **Cosmos-Predict2 Videos:** 211 "in-the-wild" videos generated from PennAction frames, featuring various subjects performing simple gestures and object-interaction motions in realistic, cluttered environments.
This dataset is specifically designed to stress-test policies under visual, morphological, and motion distribution shifts, exposing them to the inherent noise and artifacts of generated videos.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides strong evidence for GenMimic's effectiveness through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world robot deployment.

#### 4.1. Simulated Experiments

GenMimic was rigorously evaluated against strong baselines (GMT, TWIST, BeyondMimic) on the GenMimicBench dataset. Key metrics included:
- **Success Rate (SR):** Percentage of rollouts where the robot doesn't fall and stays within 0.5m of the goal.
- **Mean Per-Keypoint Positional Error (MPKPE):** Global positional error (cm).
- **Local Mean Per-Keypoint Positional Error (LMPKPE):** Local pose error (cm).
- **MPKPE-NT / LMPKPE-NT (No Termination):** Errors computed over the entire motion rollout, regardless of early termination, providing an unbiased comparison on the same input distribution.

**Key Findings in Simulation (Table 1):**
- **Superior Generalization:** GenMimic significantly outperformed all baselines in both unprivileged (student policy) and privileged (teacher policy) settings.
 - GenMimic student achieved an SR of 29.78%, compared to 4.29% for GMT and 7.52% for TWIST (student). It also showed better MPKPE-NT (62.48 cm) compared to GMT (132.32 cm) and TWIST (98.08 cm).
 - GenMimic teacher achieved an impressive SR of 86.77%, far surpassing BeyondMimic (23.81%) and TWIST (teacher) (2.69%). Its MPKPE (16.63 cm) and MPKPE-NT (20.46 cm) were also substantially lower, indicating higher tracking fidelity over longer, challenging trajectories.
- **Robustness to Noise:** The results demonstrate GenMimic's superior robustness to the noisy and morphologically inaccurate inputs from generated videos, which typically cause other methods (designed for high-quality MoCap) to fail early or exhibit high variance.
- **Comparison Insights:** GMT struggled with global tracking despite decent local pose tracking, likely due to its reliance on DoF conditioning. TWIST showed high variance and poor performance on generated videos, reinforcing that it's designed for clean MoCap. BeyondMimic, while conceptually similar in using global positional information, was outperformed by GenMimic's specific enhancements.

#### 4.2. Real-world Experiments

The GenMimic policy was successfully deployed on a 23-DoF Unitree G1 humanoid robot for 43 motions from GenMimicBench.
- **Coherent and Physically Stable Mimicry:** The robot successfully reproduced a wide range of upper-body motions, including simple gestures (waving, pointing, reaching) and composite sequences (e.g., touch head → fold arms). The Visual Success Rate (VSR), which assesses visual resemblance and physical stability, was 1.0 for these "in-place action" categories.
- **Challenges with Complex Locomotion:** While the policy reliably followed upper-body motions, integrating them with complex lower-body movements (stepping, turning) proved more challenging. For stepping compositions (e.g., "step+action"), the VSR was 0.40, and for turning compositions (e.g., "turn+action"), it was 0.41. The robot often stumbled or failed to consistently lift its legs. The authors hypothesize these issues stem from inaccurate or physically infeasible motion cues in the generated videos, particularly for dynamic full-body actions.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablations confirmed the importance of GenMimic's key design choices:
- **Goal Observation Choice:** Using 3D keypoints (3DP) as the goal observation space significantly improved performance on noisy inputs compared to using Degrees of Freedom (DoFs), especially in terms of SR (40.0% for 3DP vs. 23.8% for DoFs).
- **Weighted Keypoint Rewards:** Adding weighted rewards (3DP+Weights) substantially boosted performance, increasing SR from 40.0% (3DP baseline) to 77.4% on GenMimicBench. A self-supervised approach to learning weights achieved comparable performance to fixed weights on noisy inputs but was less effective on clean or difficult ones.
- **Symmetry Loss:** Incorporating the symmetry loss (3DP+Weights+Symmetry, equivalent to GenMimic teacher) further enhanced robustness, increasing SR from 77.4% to 86.8% on GenMimicBench, and also improving MPKPE-NT and LMPKPE-NT. This confirms that leveraging bilateral symmetry provides a crucial inductive bias for handling noise. The additional ablations in the supplementary material also showed that the benefits of symmetry loss are most pronounced when combined with 3D keypoint conditioning, highlighting their synergistic effect.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GenMimic represents a significant leap forward in robot control and embodied AI, with several profound implications:

- **Pioneering Vision-Based Generative Planning:** This work is the *first generalist framework* that empowers humanoid robots to execute motions derived from video generative models. It effectively transforms these models from mere content generators into high-level planners, opening up a novel pathway for robots to synthesize and execute diverse behaviors from abstract text prompts or visual cues. This could dramatically reduce the effort traditionally required for task specification and robot programming.
- **Robustness to Imperfect Inputs:** By explicitly tackling the inherent noise, morphological distortions, and physical implausibilities present in generated videos, GenMimic addresses a critical bottleneck. Its design—incorporating weighted keypoint rewards and symmetry regularization—provides a blueprint for creating robot policies that are robust enough to operate with "imperfect vision" or noisy references, a common challenge in real-world AI systems.
- **Scalable Task Acquisition:** The ability to generate an endless variety of human motion videos means that robots are no longer limited by the availability of real-world motion capture data, which is expensive and time-consuming to collect. This work introduces a highly scalable method for expanding the robot's behavioral repertoire, facilitating the learning of novel skills and adaptations to new contexts.
- **Establishment of a New Benchmark:** The curation of **GenMimicBench** is a crucial contribution. It provides a standardized, diverse, and challenging dataset for evaluating zero-shot generalization and robustness of humanoid policies using synthetic motion. This benchmark will accelerate future research by allowing fair and systematic comparison of new methods against the complexities introduced by generated video data.
- **Advancing General-Purpose Humanoid Agents:** This research brings us closer to the vision of general-purpose humanoid robots that can truly adapt to unseen tasks and contexts. By enabling humanoids to "imagine" and then physically execute motions, GenMimic pushes the boundaries of autonomous learning and control, paving the way for more intelligent, versatile, and human-like robotic assistants capable of performing a wide range of complex tasks in unstructured environments.
- **Bridging AI Modalities:** The work successfully bridges advances in generative AI (video generation), computer vision (4D human reconstruction), and reinforcement learning (physics-aware control), demonstrating a powerful synergy between these distinct research domains for practical robotic applications.

In summary, GenMimic offers a promising new paradigm for robot control, moving beyond imitation of real-world examples to robust execution of generatively "imagined" actions. This represents a significant step towards more autonomous, flexible, and intelligent robots capable of operating in dynamic and uncertain human-centric environments.
