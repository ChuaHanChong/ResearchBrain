# The Surprising Effectiveness of Negative Reinforcement in LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Virginia and Princeton University dissected Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for LLMs into positive and negative reinforcement components. Their analysis revealed that negative reinforcement, which penalizes incorrect responses, surprisingly enhances reasoning and maintains output diversity, often outperforming or matching full RL methods.

## Problem

- The precise mechanisms by which Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) improves LLM reasoning, particularly how it utilizes correct versus incorrect samples, remain underexplored.
- Many RL-trained LLMs show improved greedy accuracy (Pass@1) but often suffer from degraded inference-time scaling and reduced output diversity at higher sampling budgets (Pass@k).
- A clear understanding of the individual contributions of positive sample reinforcement (reinforcing correct outputs) and negative sample reinforcement (penalizing incorrect outputs) within RLVR was lacking.

## Method

- The standard RLVR objective was mathematically decomposed into two distinct learning signals: ==Positive Sample Reinforcement== (PSR) and Negative Sample Reinforcement (NSR).
- LLMs were trained exclusively with PSR-only and NSR-only objectives on mathematical reasoning tasks and evaluated comprehensively using Pass@k metrics (from k=1 to k=256).
- A token-level gradient analysis was conducted to mechanistically understand how PSR and NSR alter the model's output probability distributions and influence knowledge refinement.

## Results

- NSR-only training consistently improved Pass@k performance across the entire spectrum of `k` values, often matching or surpassing complex PPO/GRPO algorithms, even achieving Pass@1 scores comparable to or better than explicit positive reinforcement.
- PSR-only training improved Pass@1 accuracy but caused a significant degradation in Pass@k performance for `k > 8`, indicating a loss of output diversity and exploration capacity.
- A proposed Weighted-REINFORCE (W-REINFORCE) objective, which moderately down-weights positive reward contributions (λ=**0.1**) while fully retaining negative reinforcement, achieved superior overall Pass@k performance on multiple math benchmarks, effectively balancing accuracy and diversity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Negative Sample Reinforcement (NSR) is surprisingly effective, improving Pass@k performance by implicitly preserving prior knowledge and constructively redistributing probability mass, which helps maintain output diversity.
> - Positive Sample Reinforcement (PSR) can lead to diversity collapse by overly concentrating probability on a few correct responses, resulting in degraded Pass@k performance at higher sampling rates.
> - The effectiveness of RL training is highly dependent on the LLM backbone, with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct showing degradation from RL, while NSR caused the least decline in scaling behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is situated at the intersection of large language models (LLMs), reinforcement learning (RL), and complex reasoning tasks, specifically mathematical problem-solving. It addresses a critical, yet underexplored, aspect of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), a powerful paradigm for training LLMs on tasks where the correctness of an outcome can be objectively verified (e.g., using a code interpreter or a mathematical solver).

The broader research landscape for LLMs has seen remarkable progress in reasoning capabilities across domains like mathematics, coding, and scientific inquiry. A key driver for this progress has been RL techniques, especially RLVR, which offers advantages like conceptual simplicity, sample efficiency, and the ability to induce emergent behaviors such as long chains of thought (CoTs). Prior work, such as DeepSeek-R1 and Kimi K1.5, has demonstrated RLVR's capacity to elicit these complex reasoning patterns and achieve strong performance.

However, despite empirical successes, the precise *mechanisms* by which RLVR improves LLM reasoning have remained opaque. Specifically, how RLVR leverages both correct (positive) and incorrect (negative) samples to update model behavior is not well understood. This gap in understanding is exacerbated by the fact that many prior studies primarily focus on Pass@1 (greedy decoding accuracy), which may mask underlying changes in model behavior, particularly concerning *inference-time scaling*—the ability of a model to improve performance with increased computational budget (e.g., by sampling multiple responses, denoted as Pass@k). Recent work by Yue et al. and Hochlehnert et al. has even raised questions about whether RL-trained models truly possess superior inference-time scaling compared to their base models, with some findings suggesting a degradation in diversity at higher `k` values. Concurrently, Dang et al. observed that diversity collapse during supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can negatively impact Pass@k performance.

This paper directly intervenes in this landscape by:
1. **Decomposing RLVR's Learning Signals:** It dissects the RLVR objective into Positive Sample Reinforcement (PSR) and Negative Sample Reinforcement (NSR) to isolate and understand their individual contributions. This is a novel approach to dissecting RLVR mechanics.
2. **Emphasizing Inference-Time Scaling:** Unlike many previous studies that focused solely on Pass@1, this work adopts a comprehensive Pass@k evaluation across a wide spectrum of `k` values. This allows for a deeper understanding of how different reinforcement strategies affect both exploitation (Pass@1) and exploration/diversity (Pass@k for large `k`).
3. **Highlighting the Role of Negative Reinforcement:** The most significant contribution to the landscape is the *rediscovery* and *re-evaluation* of negative reinforcement. While "unlikelihood training" has existed to suppress undesirable generations, this paper provides a fine-grained, token-level gradient analysis within the context of RLVR, revealing that NSR implicitly preserves prior knowledge and guides the redistribution of probability mass in a surprisingly effective way.

By meticulously analyzing these components, the paper challenges prevailing assumptions and offers new insights into how to design more effective and robust RL algorithms for LLM reasoning, especially those that balance accuracy with the crucial aspect of output diversity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to **uncover the underlying mechanisms that drive the effectiveness of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)** in training large language models (LLMs) for complex reasoning tasks, specifically focusing on how models utilize correct and incorrect samples.

The primary motivations and specific objectives stemming from this goal are:

- **Understanding Underexplored Mechanisms:** The authors acknowledge RLVR's empirical success but highlight that its "precise mechanisms driving its effectiveness remain underexplored, particularly how it utilizes correct and incorrect samples." This lack of clarity motivates a deeper dive into the learning dynamics.
- **Decomposition of Learning Signals:** To disentangle the complex interactions within RLVR, the paper aims to formally **decompose the RLVR objective into two distinct learning paradigms:** Positive Sample Reinforcement (PSR), which focuses on reinforcing correct responses, and Negative Sample Reinforcement (NSR), which penalizes incorrect ones. The core question is: "What roles do PSR and NSR play in shaping model behavior and generalization?"
- **Empirical Isolation and Evaluation:** The research seeks to **empirically isolate the effects of PSR and NSR** by training LLMs with each sub-objective independently. A crucial objective is to evaluate their impact on *inference-time performance across a range of Pass@k metrics*, which offers a more comprehensive view of model capabilities (both exploitation and exploration) than traditional single-shot accuracy.
- **Token-Level Gradient Analysis:** To explain observed empirical trends, a key objective is to **conduct a token-level gradient analysis** for both PSR and NSR. This aims to reveal how these different learning signals directly alter the model's token probabilities during generation, providing a mechanistic understanding of their effects on knowledge refinement and diversity.
- **Balancing Accuracy and Diversity:** The paper is motivated by the observation that improving Pass@1 (greedy accuracy) often comes at the cost of Pass@k (diversity and exploration). A core objective is to **identify strategies to better balance these two aspects** by leveraging insights from the PSR/NSR decomposition.
- **Proposing an Improved RL Objective:** Based on the insights gained, the authors aim to **propose a simple yet effective variant of the RL objective**, specifically focusing on how to weight positive and negative reinforcement contributions to achieve superior overall performance across the Pass@k spectrum.

In essence, the research is motivated by a desire to move beyond black-box empiricism in RLVR, providing a principled understanding of its components to enable the development of more robust, diverse, and generalizable LLM reasoning systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper employs a systematic methodology combining theoretical decomposition, rigorous empirical experimentation, and fine-grained gradient analysis to achieve its objectives.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Decomposition of RLVR Objective

The core of the approach is the mathematical decomposition of the standard RLVR objective. The authors start with the objective function for RLVR, which minimizes the expected reward-weighted likelihood over sampled responses `y` for prompts `x`, where rewards `r(x,y)` are binary (+1 for correct, -1 for incorrect). They then algebraically separate this objective into two distinct sub-objectives:
- **L_PSR (Positive Sample Reinforcement):** Minimizes the negative expected likelihood of *correct* responses, effectively increasing their probabilities. This resembles supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for positive samples.
- **L_NSR (Negative Sample Reinforcement):** Minimizes the negative expected negative likelihood of *incorrect* responses, which translates to minimizing the likelihood of incorrect responses, thereby reducing their probabilities.
This decomposition provides the theoretical framework for isolating and studying the effects of positive and negative feedback.

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup and Algorithms

- **Models:** Three distinct LLM backbones were chosen: Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen3-4B (used in its non-thinking mode to simulate a strong underlying but unactivated prior), and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. This diversity allows for assessing the generalizability of findings across different model architectures and capabilities.
- **Training Data:** The MATH dataset, a benchmark for mathematical reasoning problems, served as the training corpus.
- **Training Framework:** The `verl` framework was used for implementation, which is designed for RLHF/RLVR applications.
- **Comparison Algorithms:**
 - **Base Model:** The pre-trained LLM without any RL fine-tuning, serving as a baseline.
 - **PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) & GRPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization):** State-of-the-art RL algorithms commonly used in RLVR, representing full RLVR training.
 - **PSR-only:** Training exclusively with the L_PSR objective, using only correct samples.
 - **NSR-only:** Training exclusively with the L_NSR objective, using only incorrect samples.
 - **REINFORCE (Vanilla):** The fundamental policy gradient algorithm, which is the basis for the decomposition.
 - **Weighted-REINFORCE (W-REINFORCE):** The proposed method, a simple variant of REINFORCE that scales down the positive reward contribution (λ * L_PSR + L_NSR).
- **Hyperparameters:** Consistent training parameters were used across methods where applicable, including prompt batch size (1024), rollouts per prompt (8), sampling temperature (1.0), mini-batch size (256), and learning rate (1e-6). Notably, KL penalties were used for PPO/GRPO but omitted for PSR/NSR, which was found to improve their performance. An entropy bonus (1e-4) was applied to all objectives.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Methodology

- **Benchmarks:** Models were evaluated on three challenging math reasoning benchmarks: MATH (test set), AIME 2025, and AMC23.
- **Metric: Pass@k:** A crucial aspect of the evaluation is the use of Pass@k, which measures the fraction of problems for which at least one correct response is produced within `k` independent trials. The authors used the unbiased estimator from Chen et al. to mitigate variance. This metric is key because:
 - `k=1` approximates greedy decoding accuracy (exploitation).
 - Larger `k` values (up to 256 for some models) assess the model's ability to generate diverse correct responses and its exploration capacity.
- **Inference Sampling:** Multiple responses were sampled per prompt (256 for Qwen2.5/Llama, 64 for Qwen3) with specific temperature and top-p/top-k settings to thoroughly assess Pass@k.

#### 3.4. Analysis Techniques

- **Training Dynamics Tracking:**
 - **Test Set Entropy:** Quantified model diversity on a held-out test set to understand how different objectives influence the output distribution's breadth.
 - **Correct Sample Ratio (training):** Proportion of correct responses per batch.
 - **Fully Solved Ratio (training):** Proportion of problems where all rollouts in a batch were correct. These provided insights into how quickly and confidently models learn.
- **Token-Level Gradient Analysis:** Derived the gradients of L_PSR and L_NSR with respect to token logits. This mathematical analysis, illustrated by Figure 6, explains *how* each learning signal alters the probability distribution over tokens, showing PSR's sharpening effect versus NSR's redistributive and diversity-preserving effects. This analysis was extended to qualitatively discuss its applicability to PPO and GRPO, and compared against entropy regularization and unlikelihood training.

This comprehensive approach allows the researchers to not only observe *what* happens under different RLVR components but also to understand *why* these behaviors emerge, leading to principled recommendations for objective design.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several significant and surprising findings regarding the roles of positive and negative reinforcement in LLM reasoning:

#### 4.1. The Surprising Effectiveness of Negative Sample Reinforcement (NSR)

- **Consistent Performance Gains:** NSR-only training consistently improved Pass@k performance over the base model across the *entire spectrum* of `k` values (from Pass@1 up to Pass@256), for Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Figure 2) and Qwen3-4B (Figure 3).
- **Competitive with Full RL:** In many cases, NSR-only training matched or even surpassed the performance of more complex, full RL algorithms like PPO and GRPO, particularly at larger `k` values where diversity is critical.
- **Indirect Reinforcement:** Despite being trained *solely* on incorrect samples (i.e., without direct positive reinforcement), NSR achieved Pass@1 scores comparable to, or even better than, methods that explicitly reinforce correct responses. This suggests that suppressing incorrect paths implicitly raises the probability of correct alternatives.
- **Preservation of Diversity and Exploration:** NSR maintained Pass@k performance at large `k` values (e.g., Pass@256) at or above the base model, and generally outperformed other RL algorithms in this regard. This indicates that NSR promotes exploration and preserves output diversity, a key factor for strong Pass@k performance.
- **Unlocking Latent Capabilities:** For Qwen3-4B in non-thinking mode (Figure 3), NSR (and GRPO) significantly improved performance, reaching capabilities comparable to the model's dedicated "thinking mode." This highlights NSR's ability to activate strong underlying knowledge even without direct positive examples of the desired behavior.

#### 4.2. Limitations of Positive Sample Reinforcement (PSR)

- **Pass@1 Improvement, Pass@k Degradation:** PSR-only training improved Pass@1 accuracy, but this came at a significant cost: Pass@k performance *degraded* at larger `k` values (e.g., for `k > 8` on Qwen2.5, Figure 2).
- **Diversity Collapse:** This degradation indicates a loss of output diversity and exploration capacity. PSR overly concentrates probability mass on the few observed correct responses, leading to an overconfident, collapsed output distribution, as evidenced by a rapid drop in model entropy (Figure 5b).
- **Failure to Generalize/Unlock:** PSR failed to improve or even degraded performance for Qwen3-4B in non-thinking mode (Figure 3), suggesting it struggles to unlock latent reasoning abilities and instead overfits to currently dominant (though suboptimal) behaviors.

#### 4.3. Insights from Gradient Analysis

- **PSR's Sharpening Effect:** Token-level gradient analysis confirmed that PSR increases the logits of tokens in sampled *correct* responses while decreasing all others. This aggressive sharpening of the output distribution leads to reduced entropy and potential overfitting.
- **NSR's Prior-Guided Redistribution:** In contrast, NSR suppresses the logits of tokens in sampled *incorrect* responses and *proportionally redistributes probability mass to other candidate tokens based on their current likelihoods*. This has several desirable properties:
 - It preserves high-confidence priors (i.e., less aggressive updates for highly probable incorrect tokens).
 - It guides exploration toward plausible alternatives already favored by the model's prior beliefs.
 - It acts as an implicit regularizer, halting updates once errors are avoided, preventing overfitting.

#### 4.4. Model-Specific Responses to RL

- **Llama Models Degradation:** RL training (PPO, GRPO, PSR, NSR) consistently *degraded* the inference-scaling performance (Pass@k for large `k`) of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct compared to its base model (Figure 4). This highlights that the base model's backbone and its pre-trained characteristics significantly influence the efficacy of RL. However, even in this challenging scenario, NSR caused the *least degradation* to the base model's scaling behavior.

#### 4.5. Effectiveness of Weighted-REINFORCE (W-REINFORCE)

- **Optimal Balance:** Based on the insights that NSR preserves diversity and PSR improves Pass@1, the proposed W-REINFORCE, which moderately down-weights PSR (λ=0.1) while fully retaining NSR, achieved the best trade-off.
- **Superior Overall Performance:** W-REINFORCE consistently delivered strong Pass@k performance across MATH, AIME 2025, and AMC23, often matching or surpassing the best Pass@1 scores and maintaining superior performance across the entire `k` spectrum compared to PPO, GRPO, and vanilla REINFORCE (Table 1).

These findings collectively highlight that merely penalizing incorrect responses, without explicitly rewarding correct ones, plays a much more critical and beneficial role in fostering robust and diverse reasoning capabilities than previously recognized, especially for maintaining inference-time scaling performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for both the fundamental understanding of LLM learning and the practical development of more effective AI reasoning systems.

#### 5.1. Deeper Understanding of RL for LLMs

The most immediate impact is a **profoundly deeper understanding of how Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) truly works** at a mechanistic level. By decomposing RLVR into Positive Sample Reinforcement (PSR) and Negative Sample Reinforcement (NSR) and analyzing their token-level gradient dynamics, the paper demystifies the black box of RL fine-tuning. It reveals that the "negative" signal is not merely about punishment but about *refining existing knowledge* and *redistributing probability mass constructively* toward plausible alternatives, guided by the model's prior. This insight challenges the often-intuitive notion that positive reinforcement is the sole or primary driver of desirable behaviors.

#### 5.2. Improved LLM Reasoning and Generalization

The findings demonstrate that focusing on negative reinforcement, or carefully balancing it with positive reinforcement, can **significantly enhance LLM reasoning capabilities while preserving crucial generation diversity**. This addresses a critical trade-off observed in many RL-trained models, where improvements in greedy accuracy (Pass@1) often come at the expense of broader exploration and performance at higher inference budgets (Pass@k). By preserving diversity, models are better equipped to explore solution spaces and generalize to varied problem types. The ability of NSR to "unlock" latent reasoning capabilities (as seen in the Qwen3-4B non-thinking mode) further emphasizes its potential for more robust model activation.

#### 5.3. Simplified and More Effective Algorithm Design

The proposal of **Weighted-REINFORCE (W-REINFORCE)**, a simple modification to the classic REINFORCE algorithm, offers a pragmatic and highly effective alternative to more complex RL methods like PPO and GRPO. The fact that W-REINFORCE consistently outperforms these sophisticated baselines across multiple benchmarks by simply down-weighting positive rewards is a substantial contribution. This suggests that for tasks with verifiable rewards, particularly when the base model possesses strong underlying priors, the computational and conceptual overhead of complex RL architectures might not always be necessary. This simplification could accelerate research and deployment by making advanced RL fine-tuning more accessible.

#### 5.4. Guiding Future Research Directions

The paper opens several exciting avenues for future research:
- **Robustness of NSR:** Investigating why extended NSR-only training can lead to degradation (as noted in limitations) and how to mitigate this instability, potentially through intelligent integration with PSR or as a "warm-up" phase.
- **Beyond Binary Rewards:** Exploring the applicability of PSR/NSR decomposition and Weighted-REINFORCE to tasks with dense, continuous, or more subjective reward signals.
- **Model-Specific RL Strategies:** Further understanding why different backbone models (e.g., Qwen vs. Llama) respond differently to RL, which could lead to tailored fine-tuning strategies.
- **Theoretical Foundations:** Deepening the theoretical understanding of NSR's implicit regularization and prior-guided redistribution properties to formally guarantee its benefits.

In conclusion, this work fundamentally shifts the perspective on how LLMs learn from feedback. By spotlighting the often-underestimated power of negative reinforcement, it provides actionable insights for developing LLMs that are not only more accurate but also more adaptable, diverse, and robust in their reasoning abilities, thus contributing significantly to the advancement of artificial general intelligence.
