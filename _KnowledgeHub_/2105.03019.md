# Imitation Learning via Simultaneous Optimization of Policies and Auxiliary Trajectories

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at NVIDIA, University of Washington, and Georgia Institute of Technology introduce Collocation for Demonstration Encoding (CoDE), an imitation learning method that improves policy learning from fixed offline demonstrations. It achieves this by simultaneously optimizing the policy and auxiliary trajectories, leading to reduced long-horizon rollout errors and enhanced data efficiency by up to an order of magnitude on robotic manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Existing imitation learning methods like Behavior Cloning (BC) suffer from covariate shift, where compounded one-step errors cause policies to diverge from expert trajectories over long horizons.
- Many advanced imitation learning algorithms, such as DAgger, require impractical online interaction with an 'oracular expert' to correct policy deviations.
- Inconsistencies often exist between raw demonstrations (e.g., due to noise or non-smoothness) and the learner's policy class, leading to irreducible one-step errors even with optimal BC.

## Method

- The proposed CoDE (Collocation for Demonstration Encoding) framework jointly optimizes both the policy parameters and a set of parameterized auxiliary trajectories.
- CoDE minimizes two objectives: the deviation between original demonstrations and auxiliary trajectories, and the one-step prediction error of the policy with respect to the auxiliary trajectories.
- For acceleration-based robotic systems, auxiliary trajectories are derived from parameterized continuous-time position trajectories, and a single neural network generates all auxiliary trajectories for parameter efficiency.

## Results

- CoDE policies exhibited substantially smaller error growth over long horizons compared to BC and BC+NOISE, with state deviation remaining nearly flat, validating its ability to mitigate covariate shift.
- The method achieved high performance with an order of magnitude fewer training demonstrations; for instance, CoDE with 10 trajectories matched BC+NOISE with over 150 trajectories on a lifting task.
- On robotic manipulation tasks, CoDE yielded significantly lower mean target errors (e.g., 0.01m vs. 0.89m for BC) and drastically reduced collision rates (**5.3%** vs. **49.0%** for BC) in obstacle avoidance scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - CoDE theoretically bounds trajectory deviation, demonstrating that by finding auxiliary trajectories consistent with the policy class, the uniform one-step error can be significantly reduced compared to standard BC, mitigating exponential error growth.
> - Optimizing auxiliary trajectories creates a 'smoother' and policy-compatible version of the demonstrations, effectively providing an easier learning problem for the policy network.
> - The joint optimization balances faithfulness to original demonstrations with consistency for the policy, allowing the policy to learn robustly without online expert interaction or assumptions about expert actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous systems, particularly robots, frequently involves programming complex behaviors, a process that can be time-consuming. Machine learning approaches, such as Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL), have been investigated as alternatives for automatically synthesizing desired behaviors. RL, while capable of learning complex policies, often suffers from poor data efficiency and necessitates extensive, sometimes risky, exploration on physical systems.

Imitation learning offers a more data-efficient and safer alternative, as it leverages expert demonstrations. The simplest form of IL is Behavior Cloning (BC), which involves training a policy to directly map observed states to expert actions or next states through supervised learning. However, BC is known to be susceptible to "covariate shift." This phenomenon occurs when the learned policy, during execution, encounters states not present in the training demonstrations, leading to accumulating prediction errors and divergence from the desired expert trajectory.

To address covariate shift, methods like Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) reduce imitation learning to online learning, where an "oracular expert" is queried to provide correct actions at newly encountered states during policy rollouts. While effective in theory, the assumption of continuous online access to an oracle is often impractical in real-world scenarios, where expert demonstrations are typically collected offline through processes like lead-through or teleoperation. Other advanced IL techniques, such as Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) and Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) (e.g., GAIL, AIRL), aim to infer reward functions or match state-action distributions. These methods often introduce computational complexity, require internal planners, or suffer from sample inefficiency and training instability.

This paper introduces Collocation for Demonstration Encoding (CoDE) to enhance imitation learning for scenarios where only a fixed, offline set of trajectory demonstrations is available. It seeks to mitigate the challenges of covariate shift and the inherent inconsistencies between demonstrations and the learner's policy class (e.g., due to observation noise, dynamics mismatch, or limited policy expressivity) without relying on online expert interaction. CoDE draws inspiration from optimal control's collocation methods and can be contextualized as a principled approach to improve upon practical BC fixes like demonstration smoothing or noise injection, which often lack theoretical grounding and are task-dependent. It contrasts with optimal tracking methods that optimize for long-horizon performance but typically face issues with vanishing gradients during back-propagation-through-time. Furthermore, while related to Data as Demonstrator (DAD) in its use of data to generate easier learning problems, CoDE explicitly learns *consistent* auxiliary demonstrations, avoiding potential conflicts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel imitation learning technique, Collocation for Demonstration Encoding (CoDE), capable of learning effective policies solely from a fixed set of offline trajectory demonstrations. This objective is motivated by several limitations of existing imitation learning methods:

1. **Reliance on Oracular Experts**: Many advanced imitation learning algorithms, such as DAgger, necessitate online interaction with an expert to provide corrective actions when the learned policy deviates from the demonstrated behavior. This assumption is often unrealistic in practical robotic applications, where expert demonstrations are costly, time-consuming to obtain, and typically collected in an offline manner (e.g., via teleoperation or lead-through). The development of a method that can learn robustly without such online queries is a significant practical motivation.

2. **Covariate Shift in Behavior Cloning (BC)**: While BC is simple and widely used, it suffers from the problem of covariate shift. Even small, one-step prediction errors by the learned policy can compound over time, causing the policy's rollout trajectories to diverge significantly from the expert demonstrations. This cascading error phenomenon limits BC's effectiveness for long-horizon tasks and out-of-distribution states.

3. **Inconsistencies Between Demonstrations and Policy Class**: The paper highlights that one-step prediction errors in BC are not solely due to optimization failures. A fundamental source of error arises from inherent inconsistencies between the provided demonstrations and the learning agent's policy class. Examples include:
 - **Observation Noise**: Demonstrations may contain measurement noise.
 - **Dynamics Mismatch**: The dynamics under which the demonstrations were generated might differ from the learner's dynamics.
 - **Limited Policy Expressivity/Inductive Bias**: The policy class chosen for the learner often has inductive biases (e.g., favoring smooth motions for robot hardware safety) that may not align with potentially non-smooth or 'jerky' demonstrations. In such cases, even an optimally trained BC policy will exhibit non-trivial one-step errors, leading to substantial rollout deviations.

4. **Challenges of Optimal Tracking**: Approaches that directly optimize for long-horizon trajectory matching (optimal tracking) face their own set of difficulties. These methods typically involve back-propagation-through-time, which can suffer from vanishing or exploding gradients, making optimization challenging for general dynamics functions and policy classes. Moreover, optimal tracking focuses on matching training data rather than generalizing to unseen scenarios.

To address these motivations, CoDE aims to provide the BC learner with a set of "auxiliary trajectories." These auxiliary trajectories are designed to be both near the original demonstrations and highly compatible with the learner's policy class. By jointly optimizing the policy and these auxiliary trajectories, CoDE intends to reduce the effective one-step prediction error of the policy and, consequently, improve its long-horizon rollout performance and generalization capabilities, even when operating solely on a fixed set of demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Collocation for Demonstration Encoding (CoDE), addresses the limitations of standard imitation learning by simultaneously optimizing both the policy parameters and a set of auxiliary trajectories. The core idea is to provide the policy learner with an auxiliary imitation problem that is more compatible with the policy class, while ensuring this auxiliary problem still accurately tracks the desired demonstrated behavior.

**Core Formulation:**
CoDE introduces parameterized auxiliary trajectories, denoted {˜s_t,φ}, where φ represents their parameters. These auxiliary trajectories are constrained such that their initial states match the initial states of the original demonstration trajectories {s_t}, i.e., ˜s_0,φ = s_0. The method then solves a joint optimization problem:

$min_{θ,φ} L(θ, φ; λ) := \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t=0}^{T_i} `(s_t^{(i)}, \tilde{s}_{t,φ}^{(i)}) + λ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t=0}^{T_i-1} `(\tilde{s}_{t+1,φ}^{(i)}, f(\tilde{s}_{t,φ}^{(i)}, π_θ(\tilde{s}_{t,φ}^{(i)})))$

Here, `(·, ·) is a symmetric, smooth, and continuously differentiable loss function (e.g., L2 norm squared) that measures state deviation. The objective function comprises two main terms:
1. **Deviation from Demonstrations**: The first term minimizes the deviation between the original demonstration trajectories {s_t} and the auxiliary trajectories {˜s_t,φ}. This ensures that the auxiliary trajectories remain faithful to the expert behavior.
2. **Policy Consistency with Auxiliary Trajectories**: The second term minimizes the one-step prediction error of the learned policy π_θ when executed from states along the auxiliary trajectories. This encourages the policy to accurately reproduce the auxiliary trajectories, which are designed to be inherently consistent with the policy class.

The hyperparameter λ (λ > 0) controls the trade-off between these two objectives. A larger λ emphasizes consistency between the policy and the auxiliary trajectories, effectively forcing the auxiliary trajectories to be highly reproducible by policies within the defined class.

**Assumptions:**
- **Known Dynamics Model**: The approach assumes a deterministic discrete-time system with a known dynamics model, s_t+1 = f(s_t, a_t).
- **State-Only Demonstrations**: Only state trajectories are assumed to be available; expert actions are not explicitly provided. In this setup, knowledge of the dynamics model becomes necessary to infer actions or predict next states.

**Theoretical Implication (Theorem 4.1):**
The paper presents a theorem showing that the trajectory deviation between the demonstrated trajectories and the rollout trajectories of the learned policy (π_θ) is bounded by O(κT_i + e^(T_i log(L))δ). Here, κ represents the uniform deviation between demonstrations and auxiliary trajectories, and δ is the uniform one-step error of the policy with respect to the auxiliary trajectories. This is compared to the BC bound of O(e^(T_i log(L))`), where ` is the one-step error with respect to the original demonstrations. The key insight is that while both bounds contain an exponential term, δ can be made significantly smaller than ` by the choice of auxiliary trajectories and the hyperparameter λ. CoDE effectively separates the intrinsic inconsistencies (captured by κ) from the trainable one-step prediction error (captured by δ), allowing for a much smaller δ compared to ` in BC, thus mitigating the exponential growth of rollout error.

**Realization for Acceleration-based Systems:**
For robotic systems, which are often acceleration-based, the state s_t consists of position q_t and velocity ˙q_t. The dynamics model is given by:
$s_{t+1} = \begin{bmatrix} q_t + \dot{q}_t T_s \\ \dot{q}_t + \pi_θ(q_t, \dot{q}_t)T_s \end{bmatrix}$
In this context, the one-step prediction error is proportional to the action (acceleration) error. Therefore, the CoDE objective can be re-expressed with the second loss term directly minimizing the difference between the acceleration generated by the auxiliary trajectory and the action predicted by the policy:
$min_{θ,φ} L(θ, φ; ν) := \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t=0}^{T_i} ||s_t^{(i)} - \tilde{s}_{t,φ}^{(i)}||_2^2 + ν \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t=0}^{T_i-1} ||\tilde{a}_{t,φ}^{(i)} - π_θ(\tilde{s}_{t,φ}^{(i)})||_2^2$
where ã_t,φ are actions (accelerations) derived from the auxiliary trajectories, and ν = λT_s^2.

**Auxiliary Trajectory Parameterization:**
Auxiliary trajectories are generated from parameterized continuous-time position trajectories {ρ_φ(τ)}. Velocities (˙q) are obtained through finite differencing of ρ_φ(τ), and accelerations (actions ã) are similarly derived from velocities. A key aspect of the parameterization (Equation 10 in the paper) ensures that the initial and final states of the auxiliary position trajectory match those of the demonstration.
The auxiliary trajectories are parameterized using a "single-neural-net" approach, where one neural network (ψ_φ) generates all auxiliary trajectories. This network takes continuous time (τ), the initial configuration (q_0), and external features (e.g., goal position, obstacle information) as input to output the state vector. This shared parameterization promotes learning correlations across demonstrations and is more parameter-efficient than using a separate network for each trajectory.

**Overall Architecture (Figure 2):**
The CoDE system employs two distinct neural networks:
1. **Auxiliary Trajectory Network**: Takes time, start/goal end-effector poses, and obstacle information as input, and outputs a state vector (position and velocity). Accelerations are derived through numerical differentiation.
2. **Policy Network**: Takes the state (position and velocity) from the auxiliary trajectory network as input and outputs a 7-dimensional joint acceleration vector (action).
These networks are optimized jointly based on the CoDE loss function, enabling the policy to learn to reproduce behaviors that are consistent with a smoothed, policy-compatible version of the demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of CoDE was conducted on a simulated 7-degrees-of-freedom (DoF) Franka robotic manipulator across two distinct tasks: 1) end-effector lifting and random goal reaching, and 2) random goal reaching around a randomly oriented object (incorporating obstacle avoidance). The demonstrations (530 trajectories) were collected from a state machine-driven Riemannian Motion Policy (RMP) system. CoDE's performance was compared against Behavior Cloning (BC) and Behavior Cloning with Noise Injection (BC+NOISE), a common practical enhancement for BC.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Multi-step Error Accrual (Long-Horizon Performance):**
 - **BC:** Policies trained with standard BC exhibited a rapid increase in rollout error (measured as state deviation in L2 norm) as the trajectory horizon lengthened. This empirically confirms the issue of covariate shift and the exponential error growth discussed in the theoretical analysis.
 - **BC+NOISE:** The addition of noise to BC training data attenuated the error growth, indicating some improvement in robustness.
 - **CoDE:** Policies learned with CoDE demonstrated the smallest error growth over long horizons. The state deviation remained nearly flat, indicating a substantial reduction in the compounding effect of one-step errors. This result supports the theoretical expectation that CoDE effectively mitigates covariate shift by finding auxiliary trajectories consistent with the policy class.
 - **Training Precision:** CoDE also showed significantly lower performance variation across different random initializations compared to BC and BC+NOISE, suggesting improved training stability and reliability.

2. **Task Performance and Data Efficiency (Overall Rollout Error):**
 - CoDE consistently produced policies that were more accurate and less sensitive to random seeds (exhibiting smaller mean RMSE and standard deviations across testing trials) than both BC and BC+NOISE.
 - **Significant Data Efficiency:** CoDE achieved high performance with a substantially lower number of training demonstrations. For Task 1, CoDE using only 10 training trajectories matched the performance of BC+NOISE trained on over 150 trajectories. For Task 2, CoDE with approximately 40 trajectories achieved performance comparable to BC+NOISE using over 200 trajectories. This implies that CoDE can reduce the data requirement by an order of magnitude.
 - Furthermore, even with the maximum number of training demonstrations, policies from either BC variant could not match the performance achieved by CoDE.

3. **Specific Performance Metrics (with 240 Demonstrations):**
 - **Task 1 (End-effector Lifting & Reaching):** Mean target error (distance between end-effector and target at trajectory end) for CoDE was 0.01m, significantly lower than BC (0.89m) and BC+NOISE (0.41m).
 - **Task 2 (Reaching with Obstacle Avoidance):** Mean target error for CoDE was 0.02m, superior to BC (0.78m) and BC+NOISE (0.41m). Crucially, CoDE-learned policies also exhibited a much lower mean collision rate of 5.3%, compared to BC (49.0%) and BC+NOISE (38.3%), indicating improved safety.

4. **Baseline Performance Analysis:** The paper notes that the suboptimal performance of BC, particularly its sensitivity to the number of demonstrations, stemmed partly from insufficient data coverage in the velocity space of the expert trajectories. While noise injection (BC+NOISE) offered some mitigation, CoDE's direct approach to learning consistent auxiliary trajectories proved more effective in fundamentally addressing the mismatch between demonstrations and the learner.

5. **Performance with Structured Policy Classes (Appendix C):** Experiments using Riemannian Motion Policies (RMPs) as a structured policy class yielded similar trends, with CoDE again outperforming baselines. RMP policies, due to their inductive biases, generally offered better error rates than unstructured neural network policies, especially with fewer demonstrations, further enhancing data efficiency in combination with CoDE.

6. **Auxiliary Trajectory Parameterization (Appendix D):** A comparison between the "single-neural-net" (joint) parameterization used in the main paper and a "multi-neural-net" (independent) parameterization showed similar performance when trained with 20 or more demonstrations. For smaller datasets (<20 trajectories), the multi-neural-net approach demonstrated slightly better performance, offering an alternative for very low-data regimes, though the single-neural-net is generally preferred for its implementation convenience and parameter efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, through the development of Collocation for Demonstration Encoding (CoDE), offers several significant contributions and has potential impacts across robotic learning and related fields:

1. **Robust Imitation Learning from Offline Data**: CoDE provides a principled method for robust imitation learning that operates solely on fixed, offline sets of trajectory demonstrations. This is a critical advancement for real-world robotic applications, where acquiring online expert feedback is often impractical, expensive, or risky. By removing the reliance on an "oracular expert," CoDE makes imitation learning more accessible and applicable in diverse industrial and research settings.

2. **Mitigation of Covariate Shift and Improved Long-Horizon Performance**: The method directly addresses the fundamental problem of covariate shift in behavior cloning. By learning auxiliary trajectories that are both close to demonstrations and consistent with the policy class, CoDE significantly reduces the accumulation of one-step prediction errors. This leads to more accurate and stable policy rollouts over long horizons, which is crucial for complex, multi-step tasks typical in robotics. The empirical validation of reduced error growth rates supports the method's effectiveness in generating reliable long-term behaviors.

3. **Enhanced Data Efficiency**: CoDE demonstrates an ability to achieve high policy performance with substantially fewer training demonstrations compared to standard behavior cloning and its variants. The reported reductions in required data, by an order of magnitude in some cases, can dramatically lower the cost and time associated with collecting expert demonstrations. This data efficiency is a major practical advantage for deployment in resource-constrained environments or for tasks where expert data is inherently scarce.

4. **Increased Safety and Effectiveness in Robotic Tasks**: The experimental results on a robotic manipulator, particularly for obstacle avoidance, show that policies learned with CoDE exhibit significantly lower target errors and drastically reduced collision rates. This directly translates to safer and more effective robotic operations, lessening the risk of damage to hardware or disruptions in task execution. The ability to learn robust, safe policies from limited demonstrations can accelerate the adoption of autonomous systems in sensitive applications.

5. **Principled Framework for Addressing Demonstration Inconsistencies**: CoDE offers a formal and principled framework to handle inherent inconsistencies between expert demonstrations (e.g., due to noise, dynamics mismatch, or non-smoothness) and the learner's policy class (e.g., inductive bias towards smoothness). By explicitly learning intermediate, policy-compatible auxiliary trajectories, CoDE effectively formalizes and improves upon ad-hoc methods like demonstration smoothing or data augmentation, providing a theoretically grounded approach to overcome these challenges.

6. **Foundation for Future Research**: The work opens avenues for future research, including extending CoDE to scenarios with only approximate dynamics models or purely visual demonstrations (requiring integration with pre-trained latent state encoders and dynamics models). The concept of jointly optimizing auxiliary trajectories and policies could also be applied to learn dynamics models simultaneously. The consistent performance across both unstructured neural network policies and structured Riemannian Motion Policies suggests broad applicability to various policy representations.

In summary, CoDE represents a significant advancement in offline imitation learning, offering a robust, data-efficient, and safe method for training robotic policies. Its ability to learn from limited, imperfect demonstrations without online expert interaction positions it as a valuable tool for practical robotic autonomy.
