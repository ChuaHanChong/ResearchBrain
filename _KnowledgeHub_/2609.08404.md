# Environments as Scaffold: Enriching Feedback to Bootstrap Self-Evolving Agents in Long-Horizon Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> Feedback-Enriched Environments (FEEs) attack reward sparsity in long-horizon agentic RL from the environment side rather than by SFT warm-up. A pilot study fixes the recipe: action guidance early, observation enrichment late, across both intra-episode steps and inter-episode training. On SciWorld and BFCL with Qwen3-4B/8B under GRPO, DAPO and GSPO, FEEs beat standard environments by 2.82% average and stabilize entropy.

## Problem

- RL on long-horizon agent tasks hits severe reward sparsity: agents get trapped in zero-reward trajectories, so gradients vanish and no learning signal remains.
- Supervised warm-up on expert trajectories is expensive and hard to scale, and over-optimizing it constrains agent behavior, shrinking the exploration needed for later RL.
- Prior scaffolding work adds linguistic hints to static single-turn datasets; systematically reconstructing multi-turn dynamic environments for agent evolution stays under-explored.

## Method

- **Action Guidance** injects procedural next-step hints into feedback to prune the combinatorial search space; **Observation Enrichment** augments observations with hidden state such as task-progress checklists or absolute paths.
- A two-axis design schedule, intra-episode exploration and inter-episode evolution, yielding the **AG-Early then OE-Late** strategy: guidance at steps 1-3 and training steps 1-100, enrichment at steps 6-10 and 101-200.
- **FEEs** instantiate this on SciWorld and BFCL-v3 Multi-Turn with 0.5 enrichment probability, intra-group-consistent feedback, and agent-side ==GRPO==, ==DAPO== or ==GSPO== left unchanged.

## Results

- Hybrid AG-Early plus OE-Late beat every single-strategy variant in the pilot; overall FEEs gained **2.82%** average, e.g. Qwen3-8B GSPO on SciWorld **53.91%** to **60.94%**, Qwen3-4B GRPO on BFCL-Base **58%** to **68%**.
- Policy entropy in standard SciWorld collapsed to zero near step **250** (near step **130** with entropy regularization); FEE training held steady through **300** steps (near **200** with regularization).
- On BFCL difficulty tiers, FEE-trained agents improved hard environments **6.5%** to **10.9%** (**+4.3**) while intra-group stochastic feedback caused erratic success-rate plunges versus consistent feedback.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Match scaffold type to learner competence: procedural hints bootstrap a weak policy, richer state semantics only pay off once a foundational policy can interpret them.
> - Environment-side adaptation is orthogonal to the RL algorithm, so it composes with GRPO, DAPO or GSPO without touching the optimizer.
> - Guidance-rich feedback breeds compliance: agents lost accuracy on missing-parameter and missing-function cases needing clarification, and gains vanished on extremely sparse AppWorld.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The current research landscape for Large Language Models (LLMs) is characterized by their advanced capabilities in static reasoning tasks, such as mathematical problem-solving and code generation. However, a significant challenge arises when deploying LLMs as autonomous agents in dynamic, long-horizon environments, such as web navigation or terminal-based computer usage. These tasks inherently involve numerous sequential interaction steps and often suffer from severe reward sparsity, where positive feedback is rare and delayed.

Traditional approaches to mitigate reward sparsity in Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLMs frequently involve "agent-side warming up" through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on expert trajectories. While SFT can provide an initial performance boost and increase the likelihood of encountering positive rewards, it is constrained by several factors: the high cost and difficulty of acquiring large volumes of ground-truth expert data, and the risk of over-optimizing the SFT objective, which can overly constrain the agent's policy and hinder the exploration necessary for effective RL.

This paper proposes a paradigm shift by focusing on "environment-side adaptation" rather than solely on agent-side modifications. Instead of seeking a better-initialized agent, the work investigates the construction of more informative and adaptive environments. This approach complements existing agent-centric RL algorithms and aims to address the fundamental issues of reward sparsity and limited exploration by systematically enriching the feedback signals provided by the environment. This positions the work within the growing field of agentic language models and the development of interactive, gym-style environments for training such agents, particularly for challenges in scaling environments and modulating task difficulty through scaffolding.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the prevalent challenge of reward sparsity in training Large Language Model (LLM) agents for long-horizon tasks using Reinforcement Learning (RL). In such complex, multi-step environments, agents frequently encounter long sequences of actions before receiving any meaningful reward signal, leading to vanishing gradients and ineffective learning. Current agent-side warming-up strategies, such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT), are limited by the scarcity of expert data and can restrict the agent's exploration capabilities.

To overcome these limitations, the paper proposes a fundamental shift in perspective: instead of primarily adapting the agent, it advocates for adapting the environment itself. The key objectives are:

1. **To develop a systematic feedback design strategy for enriching environments:** This involves exploring two primary dimensions:
 - **What information to provide:** Distinguishing between "action guidance" (direct hints for immediate next steps) and "observation enrichment" (supplementary semantic information about the state).
 - **When to deliver the information:** Analyzing its temporal application across "intra-episode exploration" (within a single trajectory) and "inter-episode evolution" (across the entire training lifecycle).
 The goal here is to identify an optimal strategy for delivering various types of feedback to maximize its impact on RL training.

2. **To construct Feedback-Enriched Environments (FEEs) based on the derived strategy:** The intention is to apply the identified optimal feedback strategy to established benchmarks to create environments that provide more informative signals to the agent during training.

3. **To empirically validate the effectiveness of FEEs:** This involves conducting large-scale experiments across various LLM scales (Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B) and different state-of-the-art RL algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, GSPO) on widely adopted benchmarks (SciWorld, BFCL) to demonstrate that training in FEEs consistently leads to superior performance compared to standard environments.

4. **To conduct an in-depth analysis of the impacts of FEEs on RL training dynamics:** Beyond mere performance gains, the research aims to understand the mechanisms through which FEEs influence agent learning. This includes investigating:
 - How FEEs contribute to training stability by analyzing policy entropy.
 - Whether FEEs promote more effective state-space exploration, especially in difficult tasks.
 - If the environmental feedback is internalized into the agent's policy weights or merely acts as a temporary inference-time prior.
 - The importance of intra-group feedback consistency for stable optimization in group-based RL settings.

Ultimately, the motivation is to facilitate more effective, stable, and sample-efficient reinforcement learning for LLM agents in challenging long-horizon tasks by providing a richer and more structured learning scaffold from the environment itself.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on a paradigm shift from agent-side policy adaptation to environment-side feedback enrichment for training LLM agents in long-horizon tasks.

#### 3.1. Feedback Design Strategy for FEEs (Pilot Study)

A pilot study was conducted to empirically determine an effective feedback design strategy. This involved investigating two key dimensions:

- **Type of Information:**
 - **Action Guidance (AG):** Integrates procedural hints into environmental feedback to narrow the search space, suggesting valid next steps. Example in SciWorld: explicitly prompting an agent to "enter the laboratory."
 - **Observation Enrichment (OE):** Augments raw observations with supplementary semantic information addressing partial observability. Example in SciWorld: indicating "the cathode remains unpowered" instead of just "wire connected."
- **Timing of Delivery:**
 - **Intra-episode Exploration:** When to provide feedback within a single interaction trajectory (early vs. late steps).
 - **Inter-episode Evolution:** When to provide feedback across the overall training lifecycle (early vs. later training phases).

The pilot study was conducted on the SciWorld environment using a Qwen3-4B model trained with GRPO for 200 steps. Different combinations of AG and OE, delivered at early or late stages, were compared. The findings informed the final strategy.

#### 3.2. Construction of Feedback-Enriched Environments (FEEs)

Based on the pilot study, the strategy adopted for constructing FEEs is to apply **Action Guidance (AG) during early interaction steps and training phases, and transition to Observation Enrichment (OE) in the later stages** of both processes (referred to as AG-Early and OE-Late).

- **SciWorld:**
 - AG utilizes ground-truth expert trajectories to provide hints (e.g., valid actions).
 - OE uses SciWorld's internal task progress tracking logic to add supplementary state information (e.g., "Full task progress: [1/4 Completed]").
- **BFCL-v3 Multi-Turn:**
 - AG appends suggested actions to user queries.
 - OE augments tool outputs with additional execution information (e.g., whether an invocation succeeded, intermediate status, or contextual details like current absolute path in Gorilla File System).

During training, enrichment operations are applied stochastically with a 0.5 probability. The transition from AG-Early to OE-Late occurs at step 100 of the 200-step training process. Evaluation tasks are strictly disjoint from training tasks.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup for Validation

- **Benchmarks:** SciWorld and BFCL-v3 Multi-Turn. For BFCL, Gorilla File System and Vehicle Control domains were used for training, while Trading Bots and Travel Booking were reserved for held-out evaluation.
- **LLM Backbones:** Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B.
- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithms:** Three representative group-based policy optimization algorithms were used:
 - **GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization):** Estimates advantage by computing relative scores within a group, using token-level importance ratios and clipping.
 - **DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization):** Modifies GRPO's reduction and clipping, using token-level loss reduction and decoupled clipping ranges, alongside dynamic sampling to filter non-degenerate trajectory groups.
 - **GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization):** Aligns the optimization unit with trajectory-level rewards by defining importance ratios at the sequence level, with clipping applied once per trajectory.
- **Training Details:** Training was conducted for 200 steps, with a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁶. Evaluations were performed every 5 steps, and the peak performance was recorded. Proprietary models (GPT-5.4, Kimi-K2-Thinking, Qwen3-235B-Thinking) were included as baselines.

#### 3.4. Analytical Investigations

Beyond performance metrics, four specific experiments were conducted to understand the impact of FEEs:

1. **Training Stability (Exp 1):** Policy entropy was tracked for Qwen3-4B on SciWorld environments, comparing standard and enriched settings, both with and without explicit entropy regularization, over 300 training steps.
2. **State-Space Exploration (Exp 2):** Monitored Qwen3-4B on BFCL by calculating average success rates across 8 rollouts per sampled environment during training. For evaluation, 400 environments were partitioned into "easy," "medium," and "hard" tiers based on success rates in standard environments, and FEE-trained models were evaluated across these tiers.
3. **Feedback Internalization (Exp 3):** A controlled prediction experiment using multiple-choice probe questions on the GorillaFileSystem environment in BFCL. Models were asked to predict tool outputs, and the probability assigned to original vs. enriched feedback options was tracked across training steps.
4. **Intra-group Feedback Consistency (Exp 4):** Compared two training configurations for Qwen3-4B on SciWorld with GRPO: one with stochastic assignment of enriched feedback within a sampling group, and another with consistent feedback within a group, monitoring validation performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Feedback Design Strategy

- **Action Guidance vs. Observation Enrichment:** Action guidance generally demonstrated superior performance compared to observation enrichment in the pilot study. This was attributed to AG's explicit pruning of the search space, reducing exploration bottlenecks, while OE requires the agent to learn complex mappings from supplementary information to actions.
- **Timing of Enrichment:** The optimal timing for feedback delivery depends on the type of enrichment. Action guidance was more effective in the early stages of both intra-episode exploration and inter-episode evolution. Conversely, observation enrichment showed greater benefit in the later stages, likely due to its higher cognitive load requiring a more foundational policy to interpret.
- **Combined Strategy:** A hybrid approach, applying AG-Early for the initial 100 training steps and transitioning to OE-Late for the subsequent 100 steps, yielded the highest success rate, confirming that distinct strategies are suitable for different training stages. This formed the basis for the FEEs.

#### 4.2. Main Evaluation Results

- **RL Performance Improvement:** RL training significantly enhanced the capabilities of Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B models on multi-turn interactive tasks. Initially, these models achieved average scores of 23.58% and 27.18%, respectively. Post-RL training, they reached 47.81% and 48.11%, surpassing Qwen3-235B-Thinking and approaching proprietary models like GPT-5.4.
- **Consistent Superiority of FEEs:** Training agents in Feedback-Enriched Environments consistently resulted in superior performance across all evaluated model scales (Qwen3-4B/8B), optimization algorithms (GRPO, DAPO, GSPO), and benchmarks (SciWorld, BFCL). The average performance improvement across settings was 2.82%. Specific examples include Qwen3-8B with GSPO on SciWorld improving from 53.91% to 60.94% and Qwen3-4B with GRPO on BFCL-Base tasks showing a 10.00% absolute increase.
- **Limitations in Critical Thinking Tasks:** Despite widespread gains, FEEs exhibited slight performance regressions in tasks requiring agents to question input sufficiency rather than blindly execute commands. For instance, Qwen3-4B with DAPO dropped from 41.00% to 34.00% on BFCL "Miss Param" tasks, and Qwen3-8B with GRPO declined from 52.00% to 48.00% on "Miss Func" tasks. This suggests a potential over-reliance on proactive guidance, leading agents to prioritize execution over necessary clarification.

#### 4.3. Training Stability (Exp 1)

- FEEs significantly stabilized RL training dynamics. Training in FEEs delayed or prevented premature policy collapse, maintaining steady entropy throughout 300 steps without explicit entropy regularization, whereas standard environments showed a sharp drop at approximately 250 steps. Even with entropy regularization, FEEs sustained stable training for nearly 200 steps, outperforming standard environments which collapsed around 130 steps. This indicates FEEs act as a stabilizer by reducing gradient volatility.

#### 4.4. State-Space Exploration (Exp 2)

- FEEs facilitated more effective and persistent state-space exploration. During training on BFCL, FEE-trained agents showed a higher density of high success rates across diverse environments, indicating broader state exploration. In evaluation, FEE-trained models consistently outperformed baselines across all difficulty levels of standard environments, achieving a 4.3% improvement on "hard" environments. This suggests that the exploration capability learned in FEEs is internalized and transfers to challenging scenarios without enriched feedback.

#### 4.5. Feedback Internalization (Exp 3)

- The enriched information provided by FEEs was internalized into the agent’s policy weights rather than serving as a temporary inference-time hint. In probe questions testing GorillaFileSystem operations, models trained with FEEs progressively assigned higher probabilities to enriched feedback options when tested in standard environments. Conversely, standard-trained models remained biased towards original tool outputs.

#### 4.6. Intra-group Feedback Consistency (Exp 4)

- Intra-group feedback consistency was identified as crucial for stable optimization. When all agents within a sampling group received identical feedback for a given state, the success rate increased steadily. In contrast, providing diverse (stochastic) feedback within a single group led to severe instability and erratic performance fluctuations, likely due to distorted advantage estimation and noisy optimization signals inherent in group-based RL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Model (LLM) agents, particularly in the context of long-horizon tasks.

The primary significance lies in the **introduction of a paradigm shift towards environment-side adaptation**. By systematically designing and constructing Feedback-Enriched Environments (FEEs), the work offers a complementary approach to the prevalent agent-side training methods like supervised fine-tuning (SFT). This addresses critical limitations of current RL training, such as severe reward sparsity and constrained exploration, by providing a scaffolded learning experience directly within the environment.

**Key impacts include:**

- **Improved Performance and Efficiency:** The consistent performance gains demonstrated across various LLM scales, RL algorithms, and benchmarks indicate that FEEs can significantly enhance the ability of agents to tackle complex, multi-step tasks. This has the potential to make RL training for LLM agents more effective and possibly more sample-efficient.
- **Enhanced Training Stability:** The finding that FEEs stabilize training dynamics by reducing policy entropy volatility is crucial. Stable training is a prerequisite for successful deep reinforcement learning, and FEEs provide an independent mechanism to achieve this, even under conditions that typically lead to premature policy collapse.
- **Robust Exploration Capabilities:** FEEs facilitate more proactive state-space exploration, allowing agents to discover and master a broader range of environment states. The demonstration that these capabilities are internalized and transfer to difficult standard environments suggests that FEEs promote genuine learning and generalization rather than mere reliance on explicit hints.
- **Internalization of Knowledge:** The evidence that environmental feedback is internalized into the agent's policy weights, rather than acting as a temporary prior, indicates a deeper integration of the guidance provided by FEEs. This suggests that the agents are learning to implicitly leverage the enriched information, which is vital for developing truly autonomous and capable agents.
- **Guidance for Environment Design:** The identification of an optimal feedback design strategy (AG-Early and OE-Late) and the critical importance of intra-group feedback consistency provide concrete guidelines for future environment engineering efforts. These principles can inform the development of more effective training environments for a wide range of agentic LLM applications.

**Potential broader impact** includes accelerating the development of robust and generalizable LLM agents capable of solving real-world, long-horizon problems in dynamic digital environments such as web automation, code execution, and complex decision-making systems. By providing a structured and informative learning environment, FEEs could enable more accessible and scalable RL for advanced agent intelligence.

**Limitations** acknowledged by the authors include the environment-specific nature of design choices (e.g., manual specification of early/late phases), the lack of broader evaluation across a wider range of agent benchmarks, and the observation that FEEs may not be universally effective in extremely sparse long-horizon settings (e.g., AppWorld), where even enriched feedback failed to yield positive rewards. Furthermore, a potential negative impact is the observed tendency for FEEs to cause agents to rely excessively on environmental feedback, potentially weakening their ability to identify and question insufficient input context in certain tasks. These limitations indicate avenues for future research in generalizing and refining the environment enrichment strategies.
