# Complexity Scaling Laws for Neural Models using Combinatorial Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> This study from Virginia Tech and Kensho Technologies investigates how neural model performance scales with fundamental measures of problem complexity, using the Traveling Salesman Problem to independently vary solution space and representation space sizes. It establishes predictable power law and exponential decay trends for model suboptimality as problem complexity increases, providing insights into model limits and drawing analogies to classical local search algorithms.

## Problem

- Existing neural scaling laws primarily focus on resource-centric dimensions like computational budget, model size, and dataset size, lacking a similar understanding for intrinsic problem complexity.
- Developing generalizable measures of problem complexity, beyond task-specific metrics, is challenging for diverse deep learning applications.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) often struggles to exhibit smooth, predictable scaling laws due to high variance in performance metrics in complex environments.

## Method

- The Symmetric Euclidean ==Traveling Salesman== Problem (TSP) was used as a case study, allowing independent scaling of solution space complexity (number of nodes) and representation space complexity (spatial dimensions).
- Transformer-based encoder-decoder models were trained with both Reinforcement Learning (PPO) and Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) on the TSP task, using an identical architecture.
- A large-scale dataset of 128 million optimal 2D TSP solutions was generated and publicly released, with near-optimal solutions approximated for higher dimensions, to enable comprehensive evaluation.

## Results

- Model suboptimality displayed smooth power decay with respect to both model parameters and compute budget for both RL and SFT, with SFT showing greater compute efficiency at tested scales.
- For fixed-capacity models, suboptimality grew as a superlinear power law (α ≈ **1.86** for RL, α ≈ **1.69** for SFT) with increasing TSP nodes, predicting eventual performance worse than random at extreme scales.
- Suboptimality for fixed-capacity RL models smoothly increased but exhibited bounded, negative exponential decay towards a fitted asymptote when scaling spatial dimensions, consistent with the asymptotic behavior of random and optimal tour lengths.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Neural model suboptimality demonstrates predictable scaling laws with problem complexity, exhibiting superlinear power growth with solution space size and bounded, negative exponential decay with representation space size for fixed-capacity models.
> - Fixed model capacity in deep networks may implicitly constrain inference in a manner analogous to imposing a fixed search depth in classical local search algorithms, explaining similar scaling trends.
> - Token-level cross-entropy loss (SFT NLL) for next-node prediction can show a counterintuitive exponential decay even as overall task suboptimality increases with problem complexity, indicating a potential disconnect between local and global performance metrics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of neural scaling laws (NSLs) has established that the performance of deep learning models can be predictably correlated with computational budget, model size, and dataset size. These observed trends suggest a fundamental order in how neural networks behave across diverse tasks. Existing research predominantly focuses on resource-centric scaling, where performance improves as more computational resources are allocated.

This work contributes to the landscape by proposing and investigating a complementary dimension of scaling: **problem complexity**. While previous studies have occasionally considered task-specific complexity metrics (e.g., Hex board size in AlphaZero), this paper aims to derive scaling laws based on more fundamental and generalizable measures of complexity: the size of the solution space and the size of the representation space. By decoupling these complexity measures using a combinatorial optimization problem (Traveling Salesman Problem, TSP), the authors explore how model suboptimality behaves when facing intrinsically harder problems, rather than simply having more or less data/compute.

The research also distinguishes itself by directly comparing two distinct deep learning paradigms, Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT), on the same combinatorial task. Furthermore, it delves into the limits of performance for fixed-capacity models under increasing problem complexity, a perspective often overshadowed by studies focusing on scaling up model and compute resources. The observed trends are then analyzed against analogous behaviors in classical local search algorithms, aiming to provide theoretical insights into the empirical scaling laws.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to ascertain whether model performance scales predictably with fundamental measures of problem complexity, analogous to how it scales with computational resources. This overarching goal is driven by several detailed motivations and specific objectives:

1. **Isolate Fundamental Problem Complexity Measures**: The authors hypothesize that predictable scaling laws can emerge when considering fundamental measures of problem complexity. They aim to distill two such measures applicable to any deep learning task: the size of the solution space and the size of the representation space. This approach seeks to avoid task-specific complexity metrics that may hinder generalization.
2. **Decouple Complexity Factors using TSP**: In many domains like generative modeling or computer vision, solution and representation complexity are intrinsically coupled. The research leverages the Symmetric Euclidean Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) as a case study because its node count directly scales the combinatorial solution space (O(n!)), while its spatial dimensionality independently scales the representation complexity without altering the solution space size. This allows for isolated study of each complexity measure.
3. **Investigate Performance Limits for Fixed-Capacity Models**: The work focuses on predicting the limit of performance for parameter-constrained, fixed-size models when compute and data are considered unconstrained. This is a crucial step towards understanding the inherent capabilities of a deep learning algorithm and a model's capacity relative to the properties of a given task.
4. **Compare RL and SFT on Combinatorial Optimization**: A significant motivation is to enable a direct, quantitative comparison between the scaling behaviors of Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) on the same combinatorial optimization problem. This comparison aims to shed light on their relative efficiencies and performance characteristics under various scaling regimes.
5. **Promote Smooth Cost Trends for Scaling Laws**: For RL, performance metrics (like reward) in complex environments often exhibit high variance, making smooth scaling laws difficult to observe. The authors seek to demonstrate that in combinatorial optimization, objective metrics like TSP tour length can promote smooth cost trends, thereby enabling meaningful scaling laws even for RL, where an interpretable loss may be absent.
6. **Analyze Suboptimality Growth with Complexity**: A core objective is to analyze how the suboptimality gap (difference between model and optimal tour length) predictably grows for fixed-size models when the number of TSP nodes or spatial dimensions increases. This analysis is conducted independently of whether the model was trained with RL or SFT.
7. **Explore Scaling Law Breakdown**: The research also investigates the conditions under which simple scaling trends might break down. By scaling problem complexity to extreme levels, the authors aim to identify limits where initial predictable behavior gives way to more nuanced or divergent trends.
8. **Provide Insights through Local Search Analogy**: To offer potential explanations for the observed complexity scaling laws in deep models, the study draws an analogy to classical local search algorithms (e.g., 2-opt gradient descent). By observing similar trends in a "white-box" algorithm, the authors aim to identify mechanisms that might constrain deep model performance.
9. **Generate and Share a Large-Scale Dataset**: A practical motivation is to contribute a new, large-scale dataset of 128 million optimal TSP solutions for academic use, addressing the scarcity of such resources for supervised learning in this domain.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a systematic experimental design focused on the Symmetric Euclidean Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) to investigate complexity scaling laws.

#### 3.1. Data Design and Optimal Tour Generation

- **Problem Instances**: TSP instances consist of `n` nodes with `d` coordinates, uniformly sampled between \ for each dimension. The objective is to find the minimum-length tour visiting all nodes exactly once.
- **Optimal 2D Solutions**: For 2-dimensional TSP, 128 million optimal solutions were generated using PyConcorde (a Python wrapper for the Concorde TSP solver). This large dataset, with 12.8 million solutions for each node scale studied, is publicly available.
- **Near-Optimal Higher-D Solutions**: For TSP with `d > 2` dimensions, exact optimal solutions are computationally intensive to obtain. The authors approximated optimality using extensive local search: for each problem instance, `k` random starting tours were generated, and 2-opt local search was applied to each. The lowest-cost tour found among these `k` descents served as the surrogate optimal solution. Datasets of 128,000 (for 10-node, d-scaled problems) and 64,000 (for 20-node, d-scaled problems) such solutions were generated. Validation showed these approximations were statistically indistinguishable from Concorde-generated optimal solutions in 2D.

#### 3.2. Model Architecture and Training

- **Neural Network Architecture**: Transformer-based encoder-decoder model. The policy network outputs tour sequences autoregressively using a Pointer Network head, generating a policy distribution over unvisited nodes. An encoding of the sampled visitation is added to the Transformer decoder memory, repeating until a full tour is formed. A separate critic network, also a Transformer decoder, learns problem-level state values or node-level compositional values as a baseline for policy gradients. The architectures for RL and SFT models were kept identical.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Training**: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was used. The environment was framed as a bandit problem where the return is the negative length of the solution tour. Models were trained for 1 million gradient updates, incorporating a hyperparameter optimization (HPO)-informed learning rate schedule (brief linear warm-up, cosine decay, then slow linear decay to zero). Node-level compositional values were used for the PPO baseline.
- **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) Training**: Models were trained by minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss between their edge selections and the optimal edge selections, using teacher forcing. Training proceeded for one epoch over the generated optimal solution dataset, involving approximately 73,000 gradient updates with a faster learning rate decay than RL.

#### 3.3. Suboptimality Estimation

- Model performance was quantified using the "suboptimality gap" (`s = μ_model - μ_opt`), defined as the difference between the mean model tour length and the mean optimal tour length. This metric accounts for variations in optimal tour length across different problem scales.
- Evaluations utilized 1.28 million problems for parameter and node scaling, 12,800 problems for compute scaling (evaluated throughout training), and full approximate datasets for spatial dimension scaling. One model tour was sampled per optimal tour.

#### 3.4. Scaling Experiments

- **Neural Scaling Laws (2D, 20-node TSP)**:
 - **Parameter Scaling**: Twelve distinct model sizes were trained, geometrically progressing from approximately 60,000 to 6 million parameters by scaling model width.
 - **Compute Scaling**: Performance was tracked throughout training, specifically focusing on the compute-efficient frontier.
- **Problem Complexity Scaling Laws (Fixed Model Capacity)**:
 - **Node Scaling (Solution Space)**: Models with fixed non-embedding parameters were trained and evaluated across ten node scales (`n ∈ {5, 10, 15..., 50}`).
 - **Spatial Dimension Scaling (Representation Space)**: RL models with fixed non-embedding parameters were evaluated across various spatial dimensions (`d ∈ {2, 3..., 100}`) for two node scales (`n=10` and `n=20`). SFT training was omitted due to the lack of sufficient optimal data in higher dimensions.
- **Scaling Law Fitting**: Power laws (`s = k * X^α`) and exponential decay functions (`s = β - k * ψ^(-X^ϕ)`) were fitted to the observed performance trends. Experiments failing to converge to a stable trend were noted and discussed as indicators of trend breakdown.

#### 3.5. Local Search Comparison

- To provide an interpretable comparison, 2-opt local search was used to generate solutions for the same TSP problem instances. The algorithm started with random tours and performed gradient descent on the cost landscape until a local optimum was reached.
- The impact of constraining the maximum search depth (`M`) for local search was also investigated, as was a simpler 2-exchange search.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding both neural scaling and problem complexity scaling in the context of TSP.

#### 4.1. Neural Scaling Laws for Combinatorial Optimization

- **Predictable Suboptimality**: Model suboptimality consistently exhibited smooth power decay with respect to both model parameters and compute budget. This trend held for both RL and SFT.
- **Efficiency Comparison (RL vs. SFT)**: At the tested scales, SFT demonstrated greater compute efficiency than RL, achieving faster decay toward optimal performance. Their parameter efficiencies were similar, though projections from SFT's fit suggested it might surpass RL in parameter efficiency with larger models.
- **Optimal Model Size**: The relationship between optimal model size and compute budget showed power growth, consistent with findings in other deep learning domains (e.g., language models, vision models, RL Procgen).
- **Suitability of TSP for RL Scaling**: TSP tour length proved to be a natural and effective performance metric for RL, enabling smooth scaling laws with high precision, which is often not the case for reward-based metrics in more complex RL environments.
- **Loss Behavior**: While mean PPO actor loss did not show a clear trend, its variance exhibited smooth power decay. PPO critic loss, in contrast, smoothly power decayed. This indicates that predictability in variance can exist even without a trend in the mean, and that interdependent loss components can exhibit differing scaling behaviors.

#### 4.2. Complexity Scaling Laws at the Infinite-Compute Limit

- **Solution Space Scaling (Number of Nodes `n`)**:
 - **Superlinear Suboptimality Growth**: For fixed-capacity models (both RL and SFT), suboptimality demonstrated smooth *superlinear power growth* with increasing numbers of TSP nodes. The growth rates (α ≈ 1.86 for RL, α ≈ 1.69 for SFT) were similar.
 - **Scaling Law Breakdown**: This superlinear growth implied that model performance would eventually become worse than random performance at sufficiently large node scales (e.g., beyond 40,000 nodes for RL), indicating a necessary breakdown of the simple power law at extreme complexities.
 - **Reference Trends**: Mean optimal tour length grows sublinearly (asymptotically proportional to `√n`), while mean random tour length grows linearly (O(n)). Consequently, random tour suboptimality grows approximately linearly with nodes.
 - **SFT NLL Loss Behavior**: Counterintuitively, supervised NLL loss for next-node prediction exponentially decayed with increasing node scale, implying improved node-level prediction as task suboptimality increased. This suggests that token-level cross-entropy loss may not directly correlate with overall task performance when scaling problem complexity.
- **Representation Space Scaling (Spatial Dimensions `d`)**:
 - **Bounded Suboptimality Growth**: For fixed-capacity RL models, suboptimality smoothly increased but exhibited *bounded growth*, characterized by negative exponential decay towards a fitted asymptote. The 20-node experiments provided a more discriminating fit than 10-node experiments, favoring exponential decay.
 - **Asymptotic Behavior Explanation**: This convergence is supported by analysis showing that both expected random tour length and optimal tour length diverge at similar rates (proportional to `√d`) as `d → ∞`. This results in random tour suboptimality becoming *provably constant* in the limit.
 - **Embedding Layer Role**: The observation suggests that problem complexity, when scaled by spatial dimensions, approaches a stationary state, with increased computational cost primarily confined to the embedding layer. This finding aligns with the concept of embedding parameters being separable from core model capacity in neural scaling laws.
 - **Critic Loss Behavior**: Self-supervised critic loss components mirrored the exponential decay observed for suboptimality, while PPO actor loss remained zero-centered.

#### 4.3. Interpretable Complexity Scaling with Local Search

- **Dimension Scaling Alignment**: 2-opt local search replicated the subexponential decay trend observed for deep models when scaling spatial dimensions. This alignment suggests that fixed model capacity might act as a bottleneck, limiting a model's ability to navigate the increasingly dense local optima landscape, similar to the constraints faced by local search.
- **Node Scaling under Depth Constraint**: Unconstrained 2-opt search yielded an unclear, non-power-law trend for node scaling. However, by *constraining the maximum search depth (M)* for 2-opt, the suboptimality followed a smooth *superlinear power growth*, a functional form consistent with parameter-constrained deep models. This analogy proposes that fixed model capacity in deep networks might implicitly constrain inference to approximate local optima, analogous to fixed-depth local search.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the understanding of deep learning model behavior and performance.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Expansion of Neural Scaling Laws**: The study expands the scope of neural scaling laws beyond resource-centric dimensions to encompass fundamental measures of problem complexity. This suggests a more general underlying order governing how neural networks perform, implying that predictable performance is not solely a function of computational investment but also of intrinsic problem hardness.
- **Framework for Problem Complexity Analysis**: By systematically decoupling solution space and representation space complexity using TSP, the work provides a framework for analyzing the distinct impacts of these factors on model performance. This is valuable for understanding how model capabilities are challenged by different types of problem growth.
- **Direct Comparison of Learning Paradigms**: The direct comparison of RL and SFT on the same combinatorial optimization task offers quantitative insights into their relative efficiencies under scaling, demonstrating contexts where SFT may be more compute-efficient.
- **Theoretical Insights from Local Search**: The analogy to classical local search algorithms provides plausible mechanistic explanations for observed scaling trends in deep models, suggesting that concepts like fixed model capacity might act as implicit search depth constraints or limit the ability to navigate complex cost landscapes. This bridges empirical observations in deep learning with established optimization theory.
- **Understanding Asymptotic Behavior**: The characterization of scaling law breakdown at extreme problem scales (node scaling) and convergence to constant suboptimality (dimension scaling) contributes to a more nuanced understanding of neural model limits.
- **Public Data Contribution**: The release of a large-scale dataset of optimal TSP solutions facilitates further research in combinatorial optimization and neural network design.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Predicting Performance Limits**: The development of complexity scaling laws could enable researchers and practitioners to better predict the performance limits of existing deep learning architectures when applied to new, harder problem instances, even with fixed model capacity. This is particularly relevant for applications where computational resources or model size are constrained.
- **Algorithm Benchmarking**: The methodology could be extended to benchmark different deep learning algorithms or architectural choices across a range of problem complexities, providing insights into their robustness and efficiency profiles without requiring extensive training on every scale.
- **Architectural Design Principles**: A deeper theoretical understanding of how model capacity interacts with problem complexity could inform the design of more efficient and scalable neural network architectures, especially for combinatorial optimization and other problems with explicit complexity measures.
- **Real-world Applications**: While currently focused on TSP, the principles explored could, with further generalization, aid in understanding and predicting performance in real-world scenarios where problem complexity is a dynamic factor (e.g., adaptive routing problems, dynamic resource allocation).
- **Guidance for Resource Allocation**: For model deployment on edge devices or in resource-constrained environments, complexity scaling laws could help optimize the trade-offs between model size, computational budget, and acceptable performance degradation for varying task difficulties.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Work

- **Resource Constraints**: The study acknowledges limitations due to modest computational resources, which restrict experimentation at extremely large model and problem scales, particularly impacting the precision of infinite-compute performance estimates and the characterization of trend breakdowns.
- **Single-Seed Training**: Training with a single seed per scale introduces imprecision in comparing RL and SFT fits and assessing statistical significance.
- **Problem Specificity**: The primary focus on Euclidean TSP, an NP-hard problem that is comparatively easier due to properties like the triangle inequality and the existence of Polynomial-Time Approximation Schemes (PTAS), limits the immediate generalizability of findings to other, harder combinatorial problems or real-world tasks where complexity factors are tightly coupled.
- **Theoretical Gaps**: A formal underlying theory explaining parameter-constrained complexity scaling laws remains a significant gap, necessary for understanding the conditions for their applicability and generalization.

Future work could involve extending these studies to more elaborate Euclidean combinatorial optimization problems (e.g., Vehicle Routing Problem, Orienteering Problem) where solution and representation spaces are independently scalable. Further investigation into algorithm insensitivity for complexity scaling and developing a theoretical framework to explain how model size influences scaling parameters (growth rates, limits) are crucial. Exploring counter-examples and the nature of embedding bottlenecks are also suggested. Finally, extending the analysis to finite-compute complexity scaling laws holds practical implications for real-world constrained environments.
