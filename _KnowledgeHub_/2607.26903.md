# From Passive Video to Editable Experience: Physically Grounded Experience Synthesis for Embodied Intelligence

> [!summary] Summary
> Pegasus, developed by Jia Luo from Huazhong University of Science and Technology, presents a framework to transform passive human demonstration videos into physically grounded, robot-executable training data. The system bridges the human-robot embodiment gap by abstracting task semantics and affordances into a graph-based representation, leading to an 11.3% absolute improvement in robot success rates when augmenting real-robot data and reducing collision rates by 90.9% through iterative physics-constrained verification.

## Problem

- Scaling robot manipulation capabilities is limited by the scarcity of diverse, large-scale training data.
- Existing data acquisition methods face challenges: real-robot collection is costly, and physics simulation has a "sim-to-real gap."
- The "embodiment gap" prevents direct use of abundant human videos for robot learning due to differences in physical morphology and action spaces.

## Method

- **Pegasus** introduces a low-resource framework for cross-embodiment knowledge transfer, translating human videos into robot-learnable data by focusing on structured experience.
- It employs a multi-stage graph-based approach, including ==Task Graph Extraction==, ==Hierarchical Affordance Latent== representation, ==Cross-Embodiment Graph Translation==, and ==Physics-Constrained Verification==.
- The framework generates physically executable robot trajectories by progressively transforming abstract task representations into robot-specific, kinematically feasible motion plans.

## Results

- Graph-conditioned generation improved Task Correctness by **12.3%** and Executability by **15.7%** compared to prompt-only methods, confirming the importance of structured representation.
- Augmenting real-robot data with Pegasus-generated data boosted robot policy success rates by an absolute **11.3%** across various manipulation tasks.
- The closed-loop physics verification mechanism reduced IK errors by **93.5%** and collision rates by **90.9%**, achieving a **94.1%** pass rate for generated trajectories within **five** refinement iterations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective cross-embodiment transfer relies on abstracting human actions into embodiment-invariant task semantics and affordances, rather than pixel-level visual matching.
> - Graph-based representations serve as a powerful intermediate abstraction for modularly adapting tasks from human demonstrations to diverse robotic platforms.
> - Iterative, closed-loop physics verification is essential for ensuring generated robot trajectories are not only semantically correct but also kinematically feasible and collision-free.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robot manipulation capabilities requires large-scale and diverse training data. Existing paradigms for data acquisition each present specific limitations. **Real-robot data collection**, while yielding high-quality demonstrations, is resource-intensive and challenging to scale. **Physics simulation** can provide extensive data but often suffers from a "sim-to-real gap," particularly for vision-based policies, due to discrepancies between simulated and real-world physics and appearances. **Learning from human videos** leverages the abundance of internet-scale visual data; however, current approaches primarily extract visual representations and do not typically produce data directly executable by robots.

This work addresses the fundamental challenge known as the **embodiment gap**. Human morphology, kinematics, and action spaces differ significantly from those of robots, preventing direct transfer of human demonstrations to robot execution. The paper argues that effective data transfer should occur at the level of structured experience—task semantics, object affordances, and robot constraints—rather than solely through pixel-level matching.

Pegasus introduces a **fourth paradigm** for embodied data acquisition: translating human experience into robot-learnable data through structured cross-embodiment knowledge transfer. This positions it distinctly from existing methods.

In the realm of **video generation for robotics**, while advanced video diffusion models and inpainting techniques achieve photorealism, they often lack direct applicability to robot policy learning or cross-embodiment transfer. Pegasus differentiates itself by conditioning generation on a graph representation of task structure rather than raw pixels or text prompts, with the primary quality metric being policy improvement, not visual fidelity.

For **cross-embodiment transfer**, existing research explores policy transfer across robot morphologies (e.g., Octo, OpenVLA) but typically requires robot data for training. Other works extract affordances from human videos (e.g., VRB) but do not translate them into executable robot trajectories. Pegasus bridges this gap by uniquely converting human video content into robot-executable data through a graph-structured task translation and an affordance-centric representation.

Furthermore, the work builds upon the established utility of **graph-based representations in robot manipulation**. Task graphs, scene graphs, and affordance graphs are recognized abstractions for planning, scene understanding, and action reasoning. Pegasus extends these by employing graph representations as an intermediate abstraction for cross-embodiment translation, using them to facilitate embodiment transfer within a video generation pipeline rather than primarily for perception or planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognition that the key bottleneck in advancing embodied AI is not solely model architecture, but the scarcity of diverse, large-scale training data suitable for robot learning. Despite billions of human manipulation videos being available online (e.g., EPIC-Kitchens, Ego4D, DexYCB, H2O), these cannot be directly utilized by robots due to the inherent embodiment gap. This gap refers to the fundamental differences in physical morphology, kinematic structures, and action capabilities between humans and robots.

The central objective of this work is to develop a framework, named Pegasus, that effectively **bridges this embodiment gap**. Specifically, the framework aims to:

1. **Translate human demonstrations into robot-learnable data:** Convert passive human video observations into active, executable demonstrations for robotic agents.
2. **Preserve task semantics:** Ensure that the generated robot demonstrations faithfully retain the high-level task goals, sub-goal ordering, object interactions, and causal dependencies observed in the original human demonstrations. This includes maintaining the intent and logic of the task across different embodiments.
3. **Ensure physical executability and adherence to robot constraints:** Generate motions that are kinematically feasible, collision-free, within joint limits, and compliant with the target robot's end-effector specifications and workspace constraints. This ensures the synthesized data is genuinely useful for training physical robots.
4. **Facilitate scalable data generation:** Develop a low-resource framework that can leverage abundant internet human videos to generate diverse robot training data, thereby reframing data acquisition from a hardware-intensive collection problem to a scalable knowledge transfer problem.
5. **Enhance generalization:** Move beyond object-specific identities to enable generalization to unseen objects and novel task instances by learning a hierarchical representation of object states, affordances, and task semantics.
6. **Provide effective supervision for downstream policy learning:** Ensure that the synthesized demonstrations are of sufficient quality and relevance to serve as robust training data for learning visuomotor policies, leading to improved robot performance.

By addressing these objectives, Pegasus aims to unlock the potential of internet-scale human video data as a significant resource for developing general-purpose robot manipulation skills, mitigating the high costs and scalability issues associated with real-robot data collection or the reality gap challenges of simulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Pegasus is proposed as a low-resource framework for cross-embodiment translation, structured around a series of graph-based transformations and physics-constrained verification. The core idea is to decompose the problem into embodiment-invariant task representation and embodiment-dependent execution, thereby bridging the gap at an "experience level" rather than a "pixel level." The framework operates in five sequential stages: Task Graph Extraction, Hierarchical Affordance Latent representation, Cross-Embodiment Graph Translation, Physics-Constrained Verification, and Experience Rendering.

The overall flow can be summarized as:
$V_h \rightarrow G_T \rightarrow Z \rightarrow G_P \rightarrow D_r$
where $V_h$ is a human demonstration video, $G_T$ is a task graph, $Z$ is a hierarchical affordance latent, $G_P$ is a robot planning graph, and $D_r$ is the synthesized robot demonstration.

#### 3.1. Task Graph Extraction

Given an egocentric human demonstration video $V_h = \{I_1, \ldots, I_T\}$, Pegasus first extracts a structured representation in the form of a Task Graph $G_T = (V, E)$.
- **Nodes (V)** represent semantic entities such as sub-goals ($g_i$), manipulated objects ($o_j$), and spatial regions ($r_k$).
- **Edges (E)** encode three types of relationships: temporal ordering ($e_{temp}$), causal dependency ($e_{cause}$), and spatial interaction ($e_{spatial}$).
This graph is constructed using a structured vision-language parser that processes video observations to predict semantic actions, manipulated objects, spatial relationships, and temporal dependencies. The purpose of this step is to abstract away visual pixel data and establish an embodiment-independent representation of the task topology.

#### 3.2. Hierarchical Affordance Latent

To create a representation invariant across embodiments, Pegasus introduces a hierarchical affordance latent space $Z = \{Z_s, Z_a, Z_t\}$. This structure progressively removes embodiment-specific details while preserving causal task semantics.
- **Object-state level ($Z_s$)**: This level captures the physical state of objects in the environment, derived from the Task Graph ($Z_s = \Phi_s(G_T)$). Examples include whether an object is grasped, opened, or displaced.
- **Affordance level ($Z_a$)**: This level abstracts object-specific observations into manipulation capabilities ($Z_a = \Phi_a(Z_s)$). Examples include graspability, containment, support, or pourability, enabling generalization beyond specific object identities.
- **Task level ($Z_t$)**: This highest level represents embodiment-independent intentions or high-level tasks ($Z_t = \Phi_t(Z_a)$), such as transporting, stacking, or pouring.

**Cross-Embodiment Alignment:** To align human and robot demonstrations within this shared affordance space, Pegasus employs a contrastive objective ($L_{align}$). This objective encourages geometric consistency in the affordance space, ensuring that semantically equivalent manipulations from different embodiments are represented similarly, irrespective of visual disparities.

#### 3.3. Cross-Embodiment Graph Translation

Once the shared latent representation is established, Pegasus transforms the Task Graph ($G_T$) into an executable Robot Planning Graph ($G_P$). This transformation is modularly decomposed into three graph operators:
$G_T \rightarrow G_A \rightarrow G_C \rightarrow G_P$
- **Affordance Graph ($G_A$)**: Augments the semantic nodes of the Task Graph with specific manipulation affordances derived from the affordance latent.
- **Constraint Graph ($G_C$)**: Incorporates embodiment-dependent feasibility constraints, including suitable grasp types for the robot's end-effector, inverse kinematics (IK) solutions, collision avoidance constraints, and workspace limits of the target robot.
- **Planning Graph ($G_P$)**: Converts the feasible manipulation primitives from the constraint graph into executable robot trajectories. These trajectories are represented as motion waypoints and temporal transitions, forming a step-by-step plan.

The complete translation is parameterized as $G_P = g_\phi(G_T, R)$, where $R$ denotes the target robot embodiment and $g_\phi = g_{emb} \circ g_{con} \circ g_{aff}$ represents the sequence of graph transformations. This modularity allows the same task graph to be instantiated across diverse robotic platforms.

#### 3.4. Physics-Constrained Verification

Visual plausibility of generated trajectories does not guarantee physical executability. Therefore, Pegasus integrates a closed-loop verification module, $\Psi(G_P)$, which validates the generated trajectories against several physical constraints:
- **Inverse kinematics (IK) solvability:** Ensures that the robot can reach the desired end-effector poses.
- **Joint limits:** Checks if the robot's joints operate within their specified range.
- **Collision avoidance:** Verifies that the robot path is free from self-collisions or collisions with the environment.
- **Grasp stability:** Assesses the feasibility and stability of grasping actions.
- **Trajectory smoothness:** Evaluates the jerk and overall fluidity of the motion.

If any constraint is violated ($\Psi(G_P) = 0$), the verifier generates structured error signals $\epsilon$. These signals are fed back into the planning module ($G^{(k+1)}_P = g_\phi(G_T, R, \epsilon^{(k)})$), initiating an iterative refinement process. This closed-loop optimization continues for a predefined number of iterations (e.g., up to five) until all constraints are satisfied, effectively treating embodiment adaptation as a constraint satisfaction problem.

#### 3.5. Experience Rendering

Finally, the verified and refined Planning Graph ($G_P$) is rendered into task-specific supervision suitable for downstream robot learning. Pegasus supports three output modalities depending on the target policy architecture:
- **Video demonstrations ($D_{video}$)**: For training vision-language policies.
- **Discrete action tokens ($D_{action}$)**: For training autoregressive action models.
- **Continuous trajectories ($D_{trajectory}$)**: For classical control pipelines or trajectory following.

The rendering process is conditioned on the executable planning graphs, ensuring that the generated demonstrations maintain both semantic consistency with the original human task and physical feasibility for the target robot embodiment. The paper mentions using Wan2.1-1.1-T2V through the Aliyun DashScope API for synthesizing robot demonstration videos at 720P resolution and 5-second duration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Pegasus was structured to address four primary questions concerning cross-embodiment data generation, along with studies on affordance generalization and component contributions.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:**
 - **GTEA Gaze+ (Li, Liu, and Rehg 2020):** 37 egocentric cooking videos with 5,026 temporally annotated actions.
 - **Epic-Kitchens-100 (Damen et al. 2022):** 67,217 narrated action timestamps for prompt extraction, with the P01_04 subset for hand segmentation.
 - **Internet Manipulation Video Set:** A curated benchmark of 1,000 first-person and third-person manipulation videos from various household, kitchen, tool-use, and object interaction scenarios, collected and filtered from an initial set of 10,000. This dataset was used to evaluate open-world task diversity.
- **Robot Embodiments:** Five diverse robotic manipulators were used for evaluation: Franka Emika Panda (7-DoF, parallel-jaw gripper), xArm 7 (7-DoF), UR5e (6-DoF, RG2 gripper), Kinova Gen3 (7-DoF, adaptive three-finger gripper), and SO-ARM101 (6-DoF).
- **Video Generation:** Robot demonstrations were synthesized at 720P resolution and 5-second duration. The closed-loop generation involved up to five iterations of regeneration with structured verification feedback for failed trajectories.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Robot-oriented data quality was assessed using four metrics:
 - **Task Correctness (TC):** Semantic consistency, measured by VLM-based classification and human evaluation (N=10 raters).
 - **Executability (EX):** Physical feasibility, including inverse-kinematics solvability, collision-free execution, and joint-limit satisfaction.
 - **State Consistency (SC):** Temporal consistency of manipulated object states throughout the demonstration.
 - **Learnability (LE):** Downstream policy performance, measured by success rates on a Franka Panda robot after training OpenVLA and ACT models on generated data.

#### 4.2. Q1: Can Internet Human Videos Become Robot Videos?

- **Setup:** Task graphs were extracted from 100 human videos (GTEA Gaze+ + Epic-Kitchens) and translated across the five robot embodiments to generate robot demonstration videos.
- **Finding:** Graph-conditioned generation, as implemented by Pegasus, significantly outperformed prompt-only generation. It showed a **12.3% improvement in Task Correctness (TC)** and a **15.7% improvement in Executability (EX)** (p < 0.01, paired t-test), confirming that structured task representation is critical for effective cross-embodiment transfer. Mean Task Correctness (VLM/Human) was 84.4%/81.5%, and Executability was 89.6% across tested robots (Table 1).

#### 4.3. Q2: Does Generation Improve Robot Policy?

- **Setup:** OpenVLA and ACT policies were trained on a Franka Panda robot for five manipulation tasks (pick-and-place, pouring, drawer opening, tool use, stacking) under three data conditions: (1) real-robot data only (50 demos), (2) real + generated data (50 real + 200 generated demos), and (3) generated data only (200 demos).
- **Finding:** Robot policies trained solely on generated robot videos achieved a **55.2% mean success rate**, which is approximately 84% of the performance achieved by 50 real demonstrations (65.9%). Critically, **augmenting real-robot data with generated data yielded an 11.3% absolute improvement** in success rate over using real data alone (Table 2).

#### 4.4. Q3: Is It Better Than Training on Human Videos?

- **Setup:** An ablation study compared the performance of policies trained on various data sources: (1) Human video features (R3M/VIP pretrained), (2) Generated robot video (Pegasus, graph-conditioned), (3) Generated robot video (prompt-only, no graph), (4) Mixed: human + generated, (5) Real-robot only (upper bound).
- **Finding:** Graph-conditioned generated data demonstrated a **+25.8% increase in success rate over human video features** and a **+13.5% increase over prompt-only generation** (Table 3). This indicates that the graph-structured representation, rather than the video generation model itself, is the primary driver for improved policy learning.

#### 4.5. Q4: Do Videos Satisfy Robot Kinematics?

- **Setup:** 500 generated trajectories across the five embodiments were evaluated using the closed-loop physics verifier. Metrics included IK error (mm), collision rate (%), and joint limit violations (%).
- **Finding:** The closed-loop verification mechanism significantly enhanced kinematic validity. It reduced **IK error by 93.5%** and **collision rates by 90.9%**. The proportion of trajectories satisfying all constraints (Pass@1) improved from 51.2% without verification to **94.1% (Pass@5)** within five iterations of refinement (Table 4).

#### 4.6. Affordance Generalization: Unseen Objects

- **Setup:** The ability of the Hierarchical Affordance Latent to generalize was tested on objects unseen during training (watermelon, screwdriver, sponge, teapot, tennis ball) versus training objects (cup, bottle, plate, spoon, knife, pan).
- **Finding:** The Affordance Latent enabled **78.5% Task Correctness on completely unseen objects**, a **+33.4% absolute improvement** compared to an object-identity-based latent (45.1%). For instance, for a watermelon, which the model had not seen, it inferred handling based on affordance composition (graspable + heavy + fragile surface), leading to appropriate robot behavior (slow approach, power grasp, gentle lift) (Table 5).

#### 4.7. Ablation: Graph Component Contributions

- **Setup:** Each component of the Pegasus graph-based framework was individually removed to assess its contribution to Task Correctness (TC) and Executability (EX).
- **Finding:** All graph components contributed measurably to the overall performance. The **Constraint Graph was most critical for Executability** (–11.2% when removed). The **Affordance Graph was most critical for Task Correctness** (–5.1% when removed). The closed-loop verification module was critical for both metrics (Table 6).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Pegasus presents a paradigm shift in robot data acquisition, re-conceptualizing the generation of robot training data from a hardware-centric collection problem to a scalable, low-resource knowledge transfer problem. The work's primary significance lies in demonstrating that the vast repository of human manipulation videos available online can be effectively transformed into executable, robot-learnable demonstrations, thereby addressing a critical bottleneck in embodied AI development.

**Key Impacts:**

- **Scalability of Data Generation:** By leveraging internet-scale human videos, Pegasus offers a path to generate diverse and extensive robot training data without the prohibitive costs, time, and logistical challenges associated with real-robot data collection. This democratizes access to large-scale data for robot learning.
- **Bridging the Embodiment Gap:** The framework successfully bridges the morphology and action space differences between humans and robots by transferring structured *experience* (task semantics, affordances, constraints) rather than relying on pixel-level visual matching. This "experience-level transfer" is a foundational insight.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The hierarchical Affordance Latent representation enables robots to generalize manipulation skills to novel and unseen objects based on their functional properties (affordances), rather than being limited to learned object identities. This is a crucial step towards more adaptable and robust robot intelligence.
- **Physically Grounded Demonstrations:** The closed-loop physics verification mechanism is vital for ensuring that generated robot trajectories are not only visually plausible but also kinematically feasible, collision-free, and within the robot's physical limits. This ensures the practical utility of the synthesized data for training real-world robot policies.
- **Improved Robot Policy Learning:** The empirical results demonstrate that robot policies trained on Pegasus-generated data achieve higher success rates compared to those trained solely on human video features, and that augmenting real data with generated data significantly boosts performance. This validates the effectiveness of the generated data as high-quality supervision.
- **Reduced Sim-to-Real Gap Issues:** Unlike simulation-based data generation, Pegasus directly works with real-world human video data, avoiding the need for extensive 3D asset construction and mitigating the sim-to-real gap for vision-based policies, while still benefiting from the diversity and realism of modern video generation models.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, including the dependence on VideoLLMs for accurate task decomposition (where reasoning errors can propagate), the physics verifier's current focus on kinematic consistency rather than full contact dynamics (making contact-rich manipulation challenging), and the current scope being limited to single-view generation and moderate-scale real-robot evaluation. Future work could explore multi-view synthesis, richer affordance discovery, and broader embodiment validation.

**Ethical Considerations:**
The paper includes an ethical statement, emphasizing responsible use of human manipulation videos, including adherence to licenses and consent. It also highlights the risk of reproducing biases or unsafe behaviors from source videos and generative models, stressing the necessity of independent safety checks, human oversight, and validation before deploying generated demonstrations on physical systems.

In conclusion, Pegasus offers a significant contribution towards making large-scale robot learning more accessible and effective by transforming readily available human video data into physically grounded, executable robot experiences. This approach lays groundwork for future research in structured cross-embodiment representation learning, potentially accelerating the development of general-purpose embodied intelligence.
