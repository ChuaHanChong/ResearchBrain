# Outcome-Based Online Reinforcement Learning: Algorithms and Fundamental Limits

> [!summary] Summary
> A collaboration from MIT and UWM introduces the first comprehensive theoretical framework for online reinforcement learning with outcome-based feedback and general function approximation. It includes a provably sample-efficient algorithm and establishes a fundamental exponential separation in sample complexity between outcome-based and per-step reward feedback for specific MDPs.

## Problem

- Online reinforcement learning often lacks per-step reward feedback, relying instead on a single outcome reward at the end of an entire trajectory, as seen in LLM training or clinical trials.
- This outcome-based feedback exacerbates the credit assignment problem, making it difficult to attribute final rewards to specific actions within a sequence.
- Prior theoretical analyses of trajectory-level feedback typically rely on restrictive assumptions like structured reward functions or tabular MDPs, limiting their applicability to general function approximation settings.

## Method

- An ==optimism-in-the-face-of-uncertainty== (OFU) algorithm (Algorithm 1) is proposed, which jointly optimizes value functions and reward models using a layer-wise exploration strategy.
- For deterministic MDPs, a simplified algorithm (Algorithm 2) is introduced that leverages the structure to reduce computational complexity and assumptions.
- The framework is extended to preference-based feedback (Algorithm 3), utilizing a modified optimism objective and logistic loss for learning from pairwise comparisons.

## Results

- Algorithm 1 achieves a sample complexity of 	ilde{O}(C_{	ext{cov}} H^3 / 	ext{epsilon}^2) episodes for learning an 	ext{epsilon}-optimal policy under outcome-based feedback and general function approximation.
- For deterministic MDPs, Algorithm 2 achieves a sample complexity of 	ilde{O}(C'_{	ext{cov}} H^4 / 	ext{epsilon}^2), offering a simpler and more efficient approach.
- The framework extends to preference-based RL (Algorithm 3), demonstrating equivalent statistical efficiency to explicit outcome-based feedback.
- A fundamental statistical lower bound reveals an exponential separation in sample complexity, requiring 	ext{Omega}(e^d) episodes for outcome-based feedback versus 	ilde{O}(d^2/	ext{epsilon}^2) for per-step rewards in certain MDPs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling reward model learning from value function estimation can lead to catastrophic failure and infinite sample complexity in outcome-based online RL.
> - Outcome-based feedback is not always statistically equivalent to per-step feedback, as specific MDPs can exhibit an exponential separation in sample complexity.
> - The severe credit assignment problem in outcome-based RL necessitates a holistic approach that simultaneously refines value and reward estimates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Executive Summary

The paper "Outcome-Based Online Reinforcement Learning: Algorithms and Fundamental Limits" presents a pioneering theoretical analysis of online reinforcement learning (RL) under outcome-based feedback, a setting prevalent in applications like large language model (LLM) training and clinical trials. Addressing the fundamental credit assignment problem inherent in such scenarios, the authors introduce the first comprehensive framework for this problem with general function approximation. Their main contribution is a provably sample-efficient algorithm (Algorithm 1) that achieves a complexity bound scaling with the MDP's coverability coefficient, without relying on structured reward functions. They also present a simplified algorithm for deterministic MDPs (Algorithm 2) and extend their approach to preference-based feedback (Algorithm 3), demonstrating equivalent statistical efficiency. Crucially, the paper establishes fundamental statistical limits, revealing an unavoidable exponential separation in sample complexity between outcome-based and per-step reward feedback for certain MDPs, highlighting the unique challenges of this paradigm. This work lays a robust theoretical foundation for understanding and tackling outcome-based RL, offering crucial insights into its tractability and limitations.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of several critical areas within reinforcement learning: **online RL, general function approximation, and alternative feedback models**. It specifically addresses the significant theoretical gap in understanding online exploration when rewards are only observed at the end of complete trajectories (outcome-based feedback), rather than after each action (per-step feedback).

**Contrast with Traditional RL:** Traditional online RL, as exemplified by algorithms like Q-learning or SARSA, heavily relies on immediate, per-step reward signals. This granular feedback allows for effective credit assignment, directly linking actions to their consequences. However, in many real-world applications, per-step rewards are unavailable or impractical to obtain.

**Motivation from Real-World Applications:** The paper highlights the increasing relevance of outcome-based feedback in modern AI.
- **Large Language Models (LLMs):** Training LLMs often involves Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), where humans provide preferences or ratings for entire generated outputs (trajectories of tokens) rather than evaluating each individual token.
- **Clinical Trials:** Patient outcomes are typically observed only after a full treatment regimen, making per-step reward observation impossible.
- Other domains such as robotics (successful task completion), recommender systems (user satisfaction after a session), and game AI (win/loss at the end of a game) also fit this paradigm.

**Addressing the "Credit Assignment Problem":** The core challenge in outcome-based RL is the "credit assignment problem" – determining which specific actions within a long trajectory contributed to the final observed outcome. This is significantly harder without immediate feedback. Prior work in this area (e.g., Efroni et al., 2021; Pacchiano et al., 2021) often assumes well-structured reward functions (e.g., linear), which simplifies the problem but may not hold in general settings. This paper moves beyond such assumptions.

**General Function Approximation:** A crucial aspect of this work is its use of general function approximation. Many theoretical RL results are confined to tabular MDPs (finite states and actions) or linear function approximation. However, real-world problems often involve large or infinite state spaces where only complex function approximators (like neural networks) can represent value or reward functions. This paper tackles this by leveraging recent advancements in online RL with general function approximation (e.g., Jin et al., 2021a; Xie et al., 2022), which use notions like Bellman completeness and coverability to bound complexity.

**Relationship to Offline RL:** The authors explicitly mention that prior work (Jia et al., 2025) has shown the sufficiency of outcome-based feedback for *offline* RL under certain conditions. This paper extends that understanding to the more challenging *online* setting, where active exploration is essential for gathering new data in dynamic environments.

**Novelty in Statistical Limits:** Beyond proposing algorithms, the paper critically examines the fundamental statistical limits of outcome-based feedback. The surprising finding of an *exponential separation* from per-step rewards in certain cases challenges the intuitive notion that outcome-based feedback might always be statistically equivalent to per-step rewards, highlighting a unique and inherent difficulty in this paradigm.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to comprehensively understand the statistical implications and tractability of online reinforcement learning when agents receive rewards exclusively at the end of complete trajectories, rather than at individual steps. This leads to the overarching question: **"When is online exploration with outcome-based reward statistically tractable?"**

The motivations driving this inquiry are multi-faceted:

1. **Bridging the Theory-Practice Gap:** The authors observe that outcome-based feedback naturally arises in numerous practical applications, including the training of large language models (RLHF), where human preferences are provided for entire outputs, and clinical trials, where patient outcomes are only observable after a full treatment regimen. Despite its prevalence, the theoretical understanding of online exploration in such settings, especially with general function approximation, remains nascent. This paper aims to close this gap.

2. **Addressing the Credit Assignment Problem:** Without granular, per-step rewards, the fundamental challenge of "credit assignment" becomes significantly more pronounced. It is difficult to pinpoint which specific actions contributed positively or negatively to the final observed outcome, especially in sequential decision-making tasks with long horizons. The paper is motivated to develop algorithms that can implicitly or explicitly address this problem.

3. **Moving Beyond Simplistic Reward Structures:** Previous theoretical work on trajectory-level feedback often relies on strong assumptions about the reward function's structure, such as linearity or other well-behaved properties. These assumptions might not hold in complex real-world scenarios. A key motivation is to develop a framework that supports *general function approximation* for both value and reward functions, allowing for broader applicability.

4. **Extending from Offline to Online Learning:** While some work has explored outcome-based feedback in the offline RL setting, where data is pre-collected, the challenges of online learning—where an agent actively explores and adapts in a dynamic environment—are distinct and more complex. The paper aims to investigate the feasibility of efficient *online exploration* with only trajectory-level feedback.

5. **Characterizing Fundamental Statistical Limits:** Beyond developing algorithms, the authors are motivated to characterize the inherent statistical limits of learning in this outcome-based setting. This includes understanding when efficient exploration is possible and, critically, identifying scenarios where outcome-based feedback fundamentally differs in its informational content from per-step feedback, leading to unavoidable statistical barriers. This quest for lower bounds provides a deeper theoretical understanding of the problem's intrinsic difficulty.

In essence, the research seeks to provide a comprehensive theoretical foundation for outcome-based online reinforcement learning, offering both provably efficient algorithms and a clear understanding of the fundamental conditions under which learning is (or isn't) statistically tractable.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The authors tackle outcome-based online RL by developing novel algorithms grounded in the principle of **optimism in the face of uncertainty (OFU)**, combined with advanced techniques for **general function approximation** and **instance-dependent complexity measures**.

#### 4.1. General Framework: Optimism and Joint Optimization (Algorithm 1)

The core of their approach is Algorithm 1, designed for general MDPs and function approximation. It operates in an iterative manner, alternating between computing optimistic estimates and collecting data.

- **Joint Optimization of Q-functions and Reward Models:** Unlike approaches that might try to learn a reward model separately, Algorithm 1 performs a *joint maximization* over both the value function class $\mathcal{F}$ and the reward function class $\mathcal{R}$. This is a critical design choice, as the authors later show that a decoupled approach can lead to catastrophic failure (Remark 1). The optimization objective is:
 $\max_{f \in \mathcal{F}, R \in \mathcal{R}} \lambda f_1(s_1) - \mathcal{L}_{\text{BE}}^D(f; R) - \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}^D(R)$
 This objective balances maximizing the estimated initial state value ($f_1(s_1)$) with minimizing two error terms:
 - **Bellman Error Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{BE}}^D(f; R)$):** Measures how well the proposed Q-function $f$ and reward model $R$ satisfy the Bellman equation, adapted for outcome-based feedback. It involves a minimization over a comparator function class $\mathcal{G}$ to handle the "double-sampling" problem common in function approximation RL.
 - **Reward Model Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}^D(R)$):** Measures how well the proposed reward model $R$ explains the observed trajectory-level outcomes, by comparing $\sum_{h=1}^H R_h(s_h, a_h)$ with the single observed reward $r$.

- **Layer-wise Exploration:** To ensure sufficient data collection across all time steps, the algorithm employs a unique "layer-wise" exploration strategy. In each round $t$, after selecting an optimistic policy $\pi^{(t)}$, it executes $\pi^{(t)} \circ_h \pi_{\text{ref}}$ for each horizon step $h \in [H]$. This means $\pi^{(t)}$ is executed for the first $h$ steps, followed by a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ for the remaining steps. This ensures that even early steps of $\pi^{(t)}$ are explored in combination with a diverse range of subsequent behaviors.

- **Theoretical Tools:**
 - **Function Classes:** Relies on value function classes $\mathcal{F}$, reward function classes $\mathcal{R}$, and a comparator class $\mathcal{G}$, whose complexity is measured by covering numbers.
 - **Assumptions:** Requires standard **Realizability** (Assumption 1: $Q^\star \in \mathcal{F}$ and $R^\star \in \mathcal{R}$ with bounded approximation error $\epsilon_{\text{app}}$) and **Bellman Completeness** (Assumption 2: Bellman operator applied to $\mathcal{F}$ can be approximated by $\mathcal{G}$).
 - **Coverability Coefficient ($C_{\text{cov}}$):** An instance-dependent measure of MDP complexity (Xie et al., 2022) that quantifies how well the state-action distributions induced by the policy class $\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}$ can be "covered" by a single distribution. This plays a crucial role in bounding sample complexity, allowing the results to be independent of specific reward structures.
 - **Uniform Concentration:** The analysis heavily relies on uniform concentration bounds (e.g., extensions of Freedman's inequality) to ensure that the estimated losses are close to their true expected values over the entire function classes.

#### 4.2. Simplification for Deterministic MDPs (Algorithm 2)

For the special, but practical, case of deterministic MDPs (Assumption 3), the methodology simplifies significantly.

- **Bellman Residual Loss:** When transitions are deterministic, the Bellman equation can be rewritten such that the per-step reward $R_h(s_h, a_h)$ is precisely $Q_h(s_h, a_h) - V_{h+1}(s_{h+1})$. This allows the outcome reward $R(\tau)$ to be expressed purely in terms of Q-functions: $\sum_{h=1}^H [Q_h(s_h, a_h) - V_{h+1}(s_{h+1})]$.
- Algorithm 2 leverages this by introducing a **Bellman Residual Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{BR}}^D(f)$)**, which directly measures how well a Q-function $f$ explains the outcome reward:
 $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BR}}^D(f) := \sum_{(\tau,r) \in D} \left( \sum_{h=1}^H [f_h(s_h, a_h) - f_{h+1}(s_{h+1})] - r \right)^2$.
- This removes the need for a separate reward function class $\mathcal{R}$ and comparator class $\mathcal{G}$, simplifying the optimization problem to $\max_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \lambda f_1(s_1) - \mathcal{L}_{\text{BR}}^D(f)$.
- It also requires only a single episode collection per round, making it computationally more efficient than Algorithm 1.

#### 4.3. Extension to Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (Algorithm 3)

The methodology further extends to the challenging scenario of preference-based RL, where feedback is binary (e.g., trajectory A is preferred over trajectory B).

- **Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTR) Model:** The authors assume the BTR model (Definition 3), which states that the probability of preferring trajectory A over B depends on the exponential difference of their true underlying rewards.
- **Preference-based Reward Model Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{PbRM}}^D(R)$):** Instead of a squared error for rewards, a logistic loss is used to learn the reward model from pairwise preferences.
- **Modified Optimism Objective:** The optimism objective in Algorithm 3 adjusts to account for the relative nature of preferences, including a term that estimates the advantage of the current policy over a reference policy based on estimated rewards.
- The overall structure remains similar to Algorithm 1, incorporating the Bellman error loss alongside the preference-based reward model loss.

#### 4.4. Lower Bound Construction

To establish fundamental limits, the authors construct a specific class of MDPs (two-layer, d-dimensional generalized linear reward) where they can demonstrate a stark difference in information content. This construction highlights scenarios where the aggregated nature of outcome-based feedback fundamentally obscures local information that per-step rewards would provide. This technique involves carefully designing reward functions and transitions to maximize the ambiguity for outcome-based learners while keeping it simple for per-step learners.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key theoretical findings that collectively advance the understanding of outcome-based online reinforcement learning:

1. **Sample-Efficient Algorithm for General Outcome-Based RL (Theorem 1):**
 - The authors develop Algorithm 1, a model-free approach for online RL with outcome-based feedback and general function approximation.
 - It achieves a provably sample-efficient bound of $\tilde{O}(C_{\text{cov}} H^3 / \epsilon^2)$ episodes to learn an $\epsilon$-optimal policy. Here, $C_{\text{cov}}$ is the coverability coefficient of the MDP (an instance-dependent complexity measure), and $H$ is the horizon.
 - This bound holds under standard realizability and Bellman completeness assumptions for the function classes.
 - **Significance:** This is the first comprehensive result of its kind, demonstrating that efficient online exploration with only trajectory-level feedback is possible even in large or infinite state spaces and without assuming specific structure on the reward functions (e.g., linearity). The dependency on $C_{\text{cov}}$ indicates that the complexity scales with the inherent diversity of reachable state-action distributions, which aligns with modern understanding of RL complexity.

2. **Necessity of Joint Optimization (Remark 1):**
 - The paper demonstrates that decoupling the optimization process (i.e., first learning a reward model, then optimizing value functions based on it) can lead to catastrophic failure.
 - In the worst case, this decoupled approach may result in *infinite* sample complexity, as the algorithm can become "trapped" in regions where the exploratory policy fails to gather informative data due to reward model mismatch.
 - **Significance:** This finding justifies the intricate joint optimization scheme of Algorithm 1, highlighting that the credit assignment problem in outcome-based RL requires a more holistic approach that simultaneously refines value and reward estimates.

3. **Simplified Algorithm for Deterministic MDPs (Theorem 2):**
 - For deterministic MDPs, Algorithm 2 is introduced, offering a simpler and more computationally efficient approach.
 - It eliminates the need for a separate reward function class and the Bellman completeness assumption, relying only on the realizability of the Q-function class.
 - It achieves a similar sample complexity of $\tilde{O}(C'_{\text{cov}} H^4 / \epsilon^2)$, where $C'_{\text{cov}}$ is a related coverability measure.
 - **Significance:** This simplification makes the theoretical guarantees more robust and potentially more amenable to practical implementation in deterministic or near-deterministic environments, by reducing the complexity of the optimization objective.

4. **Extension to Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning (Theorem 3):**
 - The framework is successfully extended to preference-based RL (Algorithm 3), where feedback consists of binary comparisons between trajectory pairs (under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model).
 - It proves that this more limited feedback model can achieve *equivalent statistical efficiency* to outcome-based RL, matching the sample complexity bound of Theorem 1.
 - **Significance:** This result directly bridges to real-world RLHF applications, showing that even weaker forms of human feedback (preferences) can be leveraged efficiently in online learning, without incurring a significant statistical penalty compared to explicit outcome rewards.

5. **Fundamental Exponential Separation (Theorem 4):**
 - Perhaps the most striking finding is the establishment of a fundamental statistical lower bound, demonstrating an *exponential separation* between outcome-based and per-step reward feedback.
 - For a specific class of MDPs (H=2, with a d-dimensional generalized linear reward model), learning an $\epsilon$-optimal policy requires $\tilde{O}(d^2/\epsilon^2)$ episodes with per-step rewards, but $\Omega(e^{d})$ episodes with only outcome-based rewards.
 - **Significance:** This negative result is profound. It clarifies that while outcome-based feedback can be efficient in many settings (as shown by the upper bounds), it is not *always* statistically equivalent to per-step feedback. It reveals that the aggregated nature of trajectory-level rewards can sometimes fundamentally obscure critical information, leading to exponentially harder learning problems, particularly when the underlying reward structure is complex or "ill-conditioned" from the perspective of outcome observation. This highlights that "delicate analysis based on well-behaved reward structure can fail catastrophically when only outcome reward feedback is available."

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the theoretical understanding of reinforcement learning under challenging feedback models, with profound implications for both theory and practice.

1. **Foundational Theoretical Framework:** The paper provides the first comprehensive theoretical framework for online outcome-based reinforcement learning with general function approximation. By offering provably sample-efficient algorithms and characterizing their statistical complexity using measures like the coverability coefficient, it establishes a robust foundation for future research in this area. This fills a crucial gap, extending theoretical guarantees from simpler settings (tabular, linear) or offline scenarios to the more realistic and challenging online, non-linear function approximation domain.

2. **Guiding Algorithm Design for Real-World Applications:** The insights derived from this work are directly relevant to real-world applications where per-step rewards are unavailable or impractical.
 - **Large Language Models (LLMs) and RLHF:** The results on preference-based learning provide theoretical backing for online RLHF algorithms, confirming that even binary preference feedback can be statistically efficient. This can guide the development of more robust and sample-efficient alignment techniques for LLMs.
 - **Clinical Trials and Healthcare:** In settings where patient outcomes are observed only after a full treatment regimen, the proposed algorithms offer principled ways to learn optimal treatment policies without relying on speculative intermediate rewards.
 - **Robotics and Complex Systems:** For tasks defined by successful task completion rather than fine-grained step-by-step rewards, this framework offers a theoretical basis for online learning.

3. **Clarifying the Role of Feedback Granularity:** The paper's most impactful contribution might be its characterization of the fundamental limits of outcome-based feedback. The proven *exponential separation* between outcome-based and per-step rewards in certain MDPs is a critical finding. It demonstrates that outcome-based feedback is not always an "almost equivalent" substitute for per-step feedback. This result:
 - **Challenges Intuition:** It refines our understanding of the informational content of different feedback types, showing that aggregation can sometimes lead to fundamental loss of identifiability.
 - **Informs Problem Formulation:** It provides guidance on when outcome-based feedback might be inherently difficult and suggests that in such cases, investing in more granular (even if noisy) per-step reward estimation or alternative exploration strategies might be necessary.
 - **Highlights the Credit Assignment Problem's Depth:** The exponential gap underscores the severity of the credit assignment problem in specific settings, especially when reward functions are not "well-behaved" or easily decomposable from a trajectory perspective.

4. **Necessity of Joint Optimization:** The demonstration that decoupled reward model learning can fail catastrophically for outcome-based RL is a significant practical and theoretical insight. It emphasizes that for these challenging feedback regimes, algorithms must intricately link value function estimation with reward modeling, reflecting the deep interdependencies that arise when feedback is sparse and delayed.

5. **Reinforcing the Importance of Coverability:** By using the coverability coefficient, the paper reinforces its utility as a relevant instance-dependent complexity measure in online RL. Its presence in the sample complexity bounds indicates that the hardness of learning is tied to the intrinsic properties of the MDP and the richness of the policy class.

In summary, this research provides a comprehensive theoretical map for navigating outcome-based online reinforcement learning. It equips researchers and practitioners with both powerful algorithms and a deeper understanding of the statistical hurdles involved, ultimately advancing the frontier of RL applicability in complex, real-world scenarios.
