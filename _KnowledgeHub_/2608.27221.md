# Tensegrity Continuum Robots Enable Task-Adaptive Morphologies for Cooperative Behaviors

> [!summary] Summary
> TeCoBot is a modular self-reconfigurable robot whose body stacks four icosahedral tensegrity modules into a compliant continuum backbone driven by three cables, with a motorized self-locking claw at each end serving as both gripper and docking interface. Single units bend omnidirectionally, collapse axially, manipulate, and locomote; multiple units dock claw-to-claw or claw-to-body into chains, loops, H-shapes, and branches for cooperative manipulation, locomotion, and loco-manipulation.

## Problem

- Most modular reconfigurable robots use rigid cuboidal or spherical modules that stay rigid after docking, so they cannot conform to objects, terrain, or docking misalignment.
- Because they lack structural compliance, such rigid modular systems typically excel at either manipulation or locomotion, leaving combined loco-manipulation capability largely unaddressed.
- Continuum robots supply backbone compliance but operate as standalone systems that cannot dock, detach, and reassemble into task-adaptive multi-robot morphologies.

## Method

- ==Continuum tensegrity backbone==: four stacked icosahedral modules of six carbon-fiber rods plus a printed TPU tendon lattice, actuated by three cables spaced 120 degrees for bending and axial collapse.
- Dual modular claws driven through ==self-locking worm gears== act as grippers and connectors, supporting claw-to-claw, claw-to-body, and claw-to-docking-station attachment with swappable rubber or soft-pad fingers.
- ==Constant-curvature kinematic model== maps cable displacements to backbone shape, plus wireless ESP32 control and an ==ArUco-marker sense-correct-move loop== for closed-loop autonomous docking.

## Results

- Stacking modules raises maximum axial displacement linearly from **60 mm** to **260 mm** across one to four modules while peak collapsing force stays near constant at **23-28 N**.
- Docking holds substantial loads: **42.9 N** symmetric claw-to-claw with latex sleeves versus **38.9 N** silicone, **29.8 N** claw-to-tensegrity, and roughly **18-20 N** at docking stations.
- Constant-curvature predictions match reconstructed shapes within **1.9-6.4 mm** tip error (**0.8-3.0%** of backbone length); rolling reaches **30 mm/s** with cost of transport **7.4**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Serial stacking of compliant tensegrity units decouples workspace from actuation effort: reach grows with module count while required cable force stays flat.
> - Compliance is itself a docking aid, absorbing misalignment during connection so assemblies need less sensing and active alignment than rigid modular counterparts.
> - Reported speeds reflect slow gearmotors and teleoperated reconfiguration, not architectural limits; autonomous morphology selection remains the open bottleneck for such collectives.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Robots capable of adapting their morphology and behaviors to different tasks and environments represent a significant area of research aimed at creating versatile and multifunctional robotic systems. Within this field, two primary architectures have been explored: Modular Reconfigurable Robots (MRRs) and Continuum Robots.

MRRs achieve functional adaptability by docking and rearranging individual units. These modules typically employ rigid structures, such as cuboidal blocks, spherical units, or triangular forms. While MRRs demonstrate broad task capabilities, including manipulation, diverse locomotion modes (crawling, rolling, walking, snake-like), and object transportation, their reliance on rigid modules limits structural compliance. This lack of compliance restricts their ability to conform to objects, terrain, or docking misalignments, thereby limiting contact-rich interactions and often confining their excelence to either manipulation or locomotion, but rarely both concurrently.

In contrast, continuum robotic systems provide inherent body compliance through flexible or extensible backbones. This compliance makes them suitable for navigating constrained and complex environments. Such systems utilize various mechanisms like flexible spines, origami structures, or soft bodies, and are actuated by methods including tendons, magnetic fields, pneumatics, or shape memory alloys. However, most existing continuum robots operate as standalone systems. They are generally not designed as self-contained modules that can dock, detach, and reassemble into multi-robot configurations.

The current work addresses a gap in the broader research landscape by seeking to unify the advantages of both MRR and continuum robot architectures. It aims to develop a modular self-reconfigurable robot that integrates body compliance with modular reconfigurability. This approach diverges from existing tensegrity robots that primarily actuate a single tensegrity unit for locomotion. Instead, this research explores stacking multiple tensegrity modules into a compliant continuum body that facilitates both axial deformation and omnidirectional bending, while enabling multi-robot reconfigurations for cooperative behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a class of robots that can dynamically change their physical morphology and behavior to adapt to varying tasks and environmental conditions, thereby enabling adaptable, multifunctional robotic systems. This overarching goal is motivated by the recognized limitations of existing robotic paradigms:

1. **Addressing the Compliance Gap in Modular Reconfigurable Robots (MRRs):** Current MRRs predominantly use rigid modules. While effective for global reconfiguration, these rigid bodies exhibit limited structural compliance. This deficiency restricts their ability to conform to irregular objects, adapt to uneven terrain, or tolerate docking misalignments. Consequently, rigid MRRs often struggle with contact-rich interactions and typically specialize in either manipulation or locomotion, rather than excelling in combined loco-manipulation tasks. The motivation is to overcome this limitation by introducing inherent body compliance into an MRR system.

2. **Enhancing Reconfigurability in Continuum Robots:** Continuum robots offer excellent body compliance, which is beneficial for navigating complex and constrained environments. However, most current continuum robot designs function as monolithic, standalone systems. They generally lack the modularity and self-reconfiguration capabilities necessary to form multi-robot configurations, which limits their adaptability to tasks requiring collective behaviors or significant changes in overall morphology. The motivation here is to extend the benefits of continuum robotics to a modular, reconfigurable framework.

To address these limitations, the paper introduces "TeCoBot" (Tensegrity-based Continuum robot). The specific objectives for TeCoBot and its capabilities are:

- **Integrate Tensegrity-based Compliance:** Design a robot body composed of stacked tensegrity modules that allows for significant recoverable deformation, including axial collapse/extension and omnidirectional bending, while maintaining load-bearing stiffness. This aims to provide embodied intelligence, offloading some sensing and control burden to the robot's physical structure.
- **Enable Modular Self-Reconfiguration:** Equip each robot with connection mechanisms (claws) at both ends that allow individual robots to manipulate, locomote, and dock with other robots. This enables self-reconfiguration into diverse multi-robot morphologies (e.g., chains, loops, branches).
- **Demonstrate Individual Robot Capabilities:** Validate that a single TeCoBot can perform a range of manipulation tasks (e.g., grasping, pick-and-place within a 3D workspace) and diverse locomotion modes (e.g., axial extension–compression, rolling, pipe climbing) across various terrains.
- **Achieve Cooperative Behaviors:** Demonstrate how multiple TeCoBots, through self-reconfiguration, can perform complex cooperative tasks, including:
 - **Cooperative Manipulation:** Coordinating to grasp, lift, and manipulate large or complex objects.
 - **Cooperative Locomotion:** Reconfiguring into different assemblies to navigate varied environments (e.g., traversing ducts, rolling around pipes).
 - **Cooperative Loco-Manipulation:** Integrating manipulation and locomotion for multi-phase objectives in real-world scenarios (e.g., climbing, object transport, handover).
- **Establish a Foundation for Adaptive Robotic Collectives:** The ultimate motivation is to lay groundwork for the development of adaptable and multifunctional robotic collectives with broad potential applications in fields such as manufacturing, space exploration, and search-and-rescue.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research outlines a methodology centered on the design, characterization, and experimental validation of TeCoBot, a modular self-reconfigurable robot.

**Robot Design and Fabrication:**
TeCoBot consists of a compliant, continuum body formed by stacking multiple icosahedral tensegrity modules. Each module comprises six rigid carbon fiber rods and a continuous, stretchable tendon network 3D-printed from thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU). Spacer disks between modules maintain structural stability and guide three actuation cables. These cables, spaced 120° apart and driven by geared DC motors (Pololu 380:1 Micro Metal Gearmotor) housed in the base module, enable omnidirectional bending and axial extension/collapse through closed-loop control. The robot measures 587 mm in length and 110 mm in diameter, weighing approximately 600 g (excluding batteries).

At each end of the robot, modular, swappable claws are mounted. Two designs were employed: a triangular claw with rubber sleeves (latex or silicone) for enhanced friction in locomotion and multi-point docking, and a TPU-padded claw for handling delicate objects. Each claw is actuated by a motor via a self-locking worm gear, ensuring secure grasping and stable docking. Passive roller wheels are integrated into the claws for smooth ground contact during locomotion. Wireless communication and control are managed by onboard ESP32 microcontrollers, receiving commands via Wi-Fi from a PC-connected ESP32 transmitter, allowing for both independent and synchronized control of multiple robots.

**Characterization Experiments:**
The mechanical properties of the tensegrity modules and the performance of the claws were systematically characterized.
- **Tensegrity Module Characterization:** Axial collapsing and bending tests were performed on single and stacked tensegrity modules. Tendons with different cross-sectional sizes (1×2 mm, 2×2 mm, 3×2 mm) were evaluated for their force-displacement and force-angle responses. This allowed for the selection of a 2×2 mm tendon cross-section and a stack of four modules, identified as offering a balance between deformation capability and actuation force requirements.
- **Claw Docking Characterization:** The maximum holding force (docking force) was quantified for various docking configurations: claw-to-claw (symmetric and asymmetric), claw-to-body (grasping a spacer disk or a TPU tendon), and claw-to-docking station. Tests evaluated the impact of sleeve material (latex, silicone), sleeve length, and gripper motor current on docking strength. Latex sleeves with medium length were selected due to their performance in claw-tensegrity docking.
- **Kinematic Modeling and Validation:** A constant-curvature kinematic model was developed to predict TeCoBot's deformation under cable actuation. The model's predictions were validated against experimental data, including robot tip position, bending angle, and bending plane direction, measured using images, inertial measurement units (IMUs), and a time-of-flight sensor.

**Locomotion and Manipulation Experiments:**
A series of experiments were conducted to demonstrate TeCoBot's capabilities:
- **Single-Robot Behaviors:** Manipulation capabilities were shown through pick-and-place tasks, demonstrating the robot's 3D workspace. Locomotion capabilities included axial extension–compression, inchworm, crawling, rolling, turning, and pipe climbing. Performance was quantified across different indoor and outdoor terrains (e.g., smooth wood, grass, gravel), measuring locomotion speed and Cost of Transport (CoT).
- **Multi-Robot Cooperative Behaviors:**
 - **Self-Reconfiguration:** The ability of multiple TeCoBots to detach, reposition, and re-dock (claw-to-claw or claw-to-body) to form different morphologies (e.g., linear chains, loops, H-shapes, Y-shapes) was demonstrated, with transitions shown to be reversible.
 - **Cooperative Manipulation:** Experiments using a cuboidal frame with docking stations showcased cooperative object manipulation, such as lifting a large ball, multi-step tasks like cork removal and liquid pouring, stabilizing objects, and extended-reach tasks.
 - **Cooperative Locomotion:** Different reconfigured morphologies were used for cooperative locomotion in specific environments, including anchored extension-contraction, object-assisted inchworm motion, traversing rectangular ducts, and rolling around cylindrical pipes.
 - **Cooperative Loco-Manipulation:** Integrated behaviors were demonstrated in multi-phase real-world scenarios, such as study table cleanup (climbing, grasping, handover, waste disposal) and cabinet organization (drawer opening, object retrieval, handover, drawer closing).
- **Automation Demonstrations:** While most reconfigurations were teleoperated, open-loop autonomous docking (pre-scripted) and closed-loop autonomous docking (using onboard vision with ArUco markers for alignment and current-aware claw closure) were demonstrated to illustrate the potential for future autonomous operation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the design, performance, and capabilities of TeCoBot:

**TeCoBot Design and Characterization:**
- **Scalable Deformation without Increased Actuation Force:** The tensegrity module design demonstrated linear scaling of maximum axial displacement and bending angle with an increasing number of modules (e.g., 260 mm axial displacement for four modules vs. 60 mm for one). Crucially, the peak collapsing force (~23–28 N) and peak bending force (~16–18 N) remained approximately constant, indicating that large deformations can be achieved through structural scaling without demanding proportionally higher actuation effort. The 2×2 mm TPU tendon cross-section was identified as an optimal choice, providing a balance between flexibility and force requirements.
- **Robust and Versatile Docking:** Characterization of the claw-based end effectors revealed robust docking capabilities. Latex sleeves generally outperformed silicone for critical claw-to-claw symmetric docking (mean peak force 42.9 ± 4.5 N) and claw-to-tensegrity body docking (29.8 N). Docking forces increased monotonically with motor current, demonstrating controlled and secure attachment.
- **Accurate Kinematic Modeling:** A constant-curvature kinematic model was validated, showing close agreement with experimentally reconstructed robot shapes. Tip position errors ranged from 1.9 to 6.4 mm (0.8–3.0% of backbone length), confirming the model's accuracy for predicting and controlling robot deformation.

**Single-Robot Capabilities:**
- **Broad Manipulation Workspace:** A single TeCoBot, leveraging its omnidirectional bending and axial extension/collapse, demonstrated the ability to generate a three-dimensional workspace for pick-and-place tasks, such as grasping and relocating objects. The modular and swappable claw designs further expanded its manipulation versatility.
- **Multimodal Locomotion and Terrain Adaptability:** TeCoBot exhibited diverse locomotion modes, including axial extension–compression, inchworm, crawling, rolling, turning, and pipe climbing. Rolling proved effective on unstructured outdoor terrains (e.g., rocks, hay) and achieved higher speeds (up to 30 mm/s on smooth wood) compared to axial extension–compression (up to 5 mm/s on smooth wood), which was less effective on soft or loose terrains.
- **Competitive Cost of Transport:** On smooth wood, rolling locomotion achieved a Cost of Transport (CoT) of 7.4, which is comparable to or lower than many representative modular robots, indicating energy efficiency for this mode.
- **Potential for Speed Enhancement:** Analysis suggested significant improvements in locomotion speed (e.g., 2.4× for axial extension–compression, up to 5.5× overall) could be achieved by upgrading motors without altering the robot's morphology or gait.

**Multi-Robot Cooperative Behaviors:**
- **Dynamic Self-Reconfiguration:** Multiple TeCoBots demonstrated the ability to self-reconfigure between various morphologies (e.g., vertical docking, two-vertical-one-horizontal, fully serial chains, loops, H-shapes) through sequential claw detachment, repositioning, and redocking (both claw-to-claw and claw-to-body connections). These transitions were shown to be reversible.
- **Enhanced Cooperative Manipulation:** Different reconfigured morphologies facilitated a range of cooperative manipulation tasks:
 - A triple-vertical configuration (M1) successfully lifted a large spherical object (200 mm diameter).
 - A two-vertical-one-horizontal configuration (M2) performed multi-step tasks like test tube manipulation and liquid pouring.
 - A one-vertical-two-horizontal configuration (M3) stabilized objects for tearing and facilitated cooperative jar opening.
 - A serial chain morphology (M4) extended the system's reach for distant object retrieval and transport.
- **Adaptive Cooperative Locomotion:** Specific multi-robot configurations enabled adaptation to different environments and locomotion challenges:
 - A serial chain (L1) achieved anchored extension–contraction on flat ground.
 - A loop-with-trailer (L2) performed object-assisted inchworm motion around large objects.
 - An H-shaped morphology (L3) traversed rectangular ducts using coordinated push–pull cycles.
 - A closed-loop configuration (L4) rolled along large cylindrical pipes.
 - Two- and four-robot assemblies further demonstrated gap traversal and collective translation.
- **Integrated Cooperative Loco-Manipulation:** In real-world indoor scenarios, three TeCoBots dynamically reconfigured to achieve complex multi-phase objectives. These included navigating onto a table, performing object retrieval and handover, forming a spanning chain to transport waste to a bin (Task A), and opening a cabinet drawer, retrieving an object, and performing a vertical-to-ground handover for placement (Task B).

**Automation Potential:**
- Demonstrations of both open-loop autonomous docking (pre-scripted) and closed-loop autonomous docking (using onboard vision with ArUco markers for alignment and a sense–correct–move cycle) indicated the feasibility of automating the self-reconfiguration process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a significant contribution to the field of robotics by developing TeCoBot, a modular self-reconfigurable robot that unifies two typically distinct robotic properties: local body compliance and global multi-robot reconfiguration. This integration addresses a critical gap in current robotic systems, which largely excel in one area but not the other.

The **significance** of this unification lies in its ability to enable "task-adaptive morphologies" for cooperative behaviors.
1. **Embodied Intelligence:** The tensegrity-based compliant body allows TeCoBot to passively conform to objects, tolerate docking and contact misalignments, and distribute forces across uneven terrains. This embodied intelligence reduces the need for complex active sensing and control mechanisms, distinguishing it from rigid MRRs that require explicit sensing and actuation for similar interactions.
2. **Expanded Functionality:** By combining compliance with modular docking, TeCoBot can form a wide array of deformable assemblies (chains, loops, branches). These assemblies can not only change their overall shape but also leverage the inherent compliance of individual modules to bend, collapse, and extend, enabling a broader range of manipulation, locomotion (e.g., rolling, inching, pipe climbing), and support functions without hardware modification. This expands the functional workspace and adaptability of robotic collectives.
3. **Demonstrated Versatility:** The detailed experimental results across individual and cooperative tasks, including manipulation, diverse locomotion modes, and integrated loco-manipulation in complex real-world scenarios, validate the practical utility and robustness of this design framework. The demonstrations of dynamic self-reconfiguration further highlight the system's adaptability to changing task requirements and environments.
4. **Foundation for Future Autonomy:** The successful demonstration of both open-loop and closed-loop autonomous docking indicates a clear pathway towards fully autonomous self-reconfiguration and task execution, paving the way for advanced decision-making frameworks for morphology selection.

The **potential impact** of TeCoBot's capabilities is broad and extends to various application domains:
- **Manufacturing:** The ability to form task-adaptive morphologies could enhance flexible assembly lines, enabling robots to handle diverse components and reconfigure for different manufacturing processes without extensive retooling.
- **Space Exploration:** In uncertain and unstructured extraterrestrial environments, robots capable of adaptive navigation, manipulation, and structural support through reconfiguration would be invaluable for tasks like habitat construction, equipment maintenance, and sample collection.
- **Search-and-Rescue Operations:** TeCoBots could navigate complex and confined spaces, adapt their form to traverse debris, lift obstacles, and provide assistance in disaster zones where traditional rigid robots struggle.
- **Logistics and Inspection:** The system could be applied in warehouses or industrial settings for adaptive inspection of infrastructure, transport of varied payloads, and navigating dynamic environments.

Overall, this work lays a foundational framework for developing highly adaptable and multifunctional robotic collectives, moving towards a future where robots can seamlessly integrate morphological changes with cooperative behaviors to address complex challenges in diverse, unstructured environments.
