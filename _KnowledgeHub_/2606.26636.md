# FracEvent: Event-Camera Simulation via Fractional-Relaxation Pixel Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> FracEvent is an event-camera simulator that converts a supplied log-intensity trajectory into asynchronous ON/OFF events by modeling each pixel's lifecycle: a stack of fractional-relaxation memory modes forms a voltage state, bisection localizes threshold crossings in continuous time, and firing advances only the reference while memory persists. It attains the lowest inter-event-interval distance and the best downstream reconstruction and optical-flow transfer among simulators.

## Problem

- Real event data are hard to scale: specialized sensors, careful synchronization and task-specific annotation limit collection across new scenes, motions and sensor assumptions.
- Practical simulators built on contrast-threshold rules plus filtering, stochastic noise or hand-tuned parameters simplify each pixel's temporal lifecycle, distorting event timing and weakening downstream transfer.
- Prior formulations discard residual sub-threshold state after firing, so repeated same-pixel triggering, sub-frame crossing times and ON/OFF asymmetry are represented poorly.

## Method

- **FracEvent**'s ==fractional-relaxation pixel dynamics==: M first-order memory channels with log-spaced time constants, weighted by a ==Cole-Cole-style fractional kernel== of shape alpha, sum into the pixel voltage.
- ==Continuous-time crossing localization==: endpoint counts fix ON/OFF levels, then ==bisection== over closed-form mode updates timestamps each level for active pixels only, recursively splitting intervals during bursts.
- ==Retained-memory update==: after an event only the reference advances by the ON or OFF threshold while mode states carry over unreset; separate thresholds encode polarity asymmetry.

## Results

- Against real DAVIS240C windows FracEvent gives lowest IEI distance **0.0964** and polarity error **0.0244** versus DVS-Voltmeter **0.1271**; ablating multi-mode or retained memory raises IEI to **0.1369**/**0.1244**.
- E2VID-style models trained on its events reach best DAVIS240C MSE **0.030** and LPIPS **0.460** against v2e **0.065**/**0.575** and ESIM **0.054**/**0.496**, though ESIM leads SSIM.
- EV-FlowNet trained on FracEvent events reaches **1.24** mean AEE at dt=1, near the **1.17** real-MVSEC control, beating DVS-Voltmeter **1.30**, ESIM **1.49**, v2e **1.53**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating the upstream brightness trajectory from sensor-side conversion makes pixel dynamics reusable across renderers and video sources; better dynamics cannot repair a flawed input trajectory.
> - Carrying sub-threshold state across firings, rather than resetting it, is what fixes repeated-trigger timing; ablations show timing metrics, not event counts, are the sensitive signal.
> - Metrics disagree: best time-surface or structural scores need not accompany best timing or perceptual transfer, so simulator choice should follow the intended downstream task.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Event cameras, also known as dynamic vision sensors (DVS), represent a distinct paradigm in computer vision by reporting asynchronous pixel events in response to local brightness changes, offering microsecond-level temporal resolution and high dynamic range. These characteristics make event cameras relevant for applications such as robotics, autonomous driving, optical flow estimation, and video reconstruction. However, the acquisition of large-scale real event data is challenging due to the specialized hardware, synchronization requirements, and annotation efforts involved. This limitation has propelled the field of event-camera simulation, which aims to convert conventional frame-based video data into synthetic event streams.

Existing event simulators typically rely on contrast-threshold event generation, often incorporating elements like filtering, stochastic noise, or hand-tuned sensor parameters. While these approaches have demonstrated utility, they frequently simplify the temporal dynamics associated with the lifecycle of individual pixels. This simplification can lead to inaccuracies in event timing and may negatively impact the transferability of models trained on synthetic data to real-world event camera applications. The research landscape therefore identifies a need for more physically accurate, sensor-side simulation models that can capture the nuanced temporal behavior of event pixels, such as repeated triggering, sub-frame interval event timing, and ON/OFF asymmetry, without over-reliance on extensive parameter tuning or complex learning architectures. FracEvent addresses this gap by proposing a model that specifically focuses on the pixel-level event generation process through fractional-relaxation voltage dynamics, aiming to produce event streams with a temporal structure more akin to real event camera output. This work positions itself as a development in sensor-side conversion, allowing it to be integrated with various upstream brightness trajectory sources (e.g., renderers, video interpolation modules).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an event-camera simulator, named FracEvent, that generates synthetic event streams with enhanced temporal fidelity compared to existing methods. This enhancement is sought by modeling the intrinsic pixel-level event lifecycle through a fractional-relaxation voltage dynamics approach. The motivation for this objective stems from several identified limitations in current event simulation practices:

1. **Simplification of Pixel Lifecycle:** Most practical simulators simplify the internal temporal structure and state evolution of individual pixels. Real event camera pixels accumulate brightness changes, trigger an event, update their internal reference, and then continue processing from a residual state influenced by recent activity. Simplistic models often treat event generation as independent threshold crossings, which can distort event timing, misrepresent event density, and ultimately weaken the transferability of event-based models trained on such simulated data to real sensor deployments.
2. **Difficulty in Real Data Collection:** The collection of large, diverse, and annotated datasets from real event cameras is resource-intensive. It requires specialized sensors, precise synchronization with ground truth (e.g., for optical flow), and often specific scene setups, making it difficult to scale across varying scenes, motion conditions, and sensor configurations. Consequently, high-quality simulated data is crucial for advancing event-based vision research by complementing scarce real datasets.
3. **Distorted Event Timing and Weaker Downstream Transfer:** The inaccuracies arising from simplified pixel dynamics, particularly concerning event timing, can lead to synthetic event streams that do not sufficiently resemble real ones. This discrepancy can manifest as models trained on simulated data performing suboptimally when deployed on real event camera footage. Improved temporal structure in simulated events is hypothesized to lead to stronger downstream transfer results for tasks like image reconstruction and optical flow estimation.
4. **Addressing Key Real-World Event Camera Properties:** The authors aim to explicitly model three properties observed in real event data:
 - **Repeated Same-Pixel Triggering:** The ability of a single pixel to fire multiple events in quick succession due to continuous or oscillating brightness changes, where residual state influences subsequent triggers.
 - **Threshold-Crossing Times within Frame Intervals:** The asynchronous nature of event cameras means events can occur at any microsecond-level timestamp within a frame interval, unlike frame-based systems. Accurate simulation requires localization of these continuous-time crossings.
 - **ON/OFF Asymmetry:** Real event cameras often exhibit different thresholds or responses for positive (ON) and negative (OFF) brightness changes.

By addressing these motivations and objectives, FracEvent seeks to provide a more robust and realistic event simulation core, which can be paired with various upstream intensity trajectory sources, thereby improving the utility of synthetic data for training and evaluating event-based vision algorithms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FracEvent's methodology focuses on a sensor-side conversion model that transforms a log-intensity trajectory into an asynchronous event stream by simulating pixel-level dynamics. This process involves three main components: fractional-relaxation voltage memory, continuous-time threshold crossing, and retained-memory updates.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup for Event-stream Simulation

The simulator takes a normalized intensity trajectory `I(t)` as input, which is converted to log-intensity `L(t) = log(I(t) + i_dark + ε)` to align with common contrast-threshold event camera models. Given `L(t)`, FracEvent generates an event stream `E = {(x_i, y_i, t_i, p_i)}`, where `(x_i, y_i)` are pixel coordinates, `t_i` is the timestamp, and `p_i ∈ {+1, -1}` is the polarity (ON/OFF). This setup isolates the sensor-side conversion, allowing the same pixel dynamics model to be used with different upstream intensity sources.

#### 3.2. Fractional-Relaxation Pixel Dynamics

FracEvent models pixel voltage memory using multiple fractional-relaxation modes. These modes, indexed by `m = 1, ..., M`, are characterized by log-time offsets `u_m` sampled uniformly over a configured relaxation log range `R`. A fractional shape parameter `α ∈ (0, 1)` defines the normalized mode weights `w_m`. For `α = 1`, this simplifies to a single-mode, first-order relaxation.

Each mode `m` has a memory channel `z_m(t)` with a time constant `τ_m`. The dynamics of each channel follow a first-order relaxation equation: `dz_m(t)/dt = (L(t) - z_m(t))/τ_m`. The pixel voltage `v(t)` is then computed as the weighted sum of these memory channels: `v(t) = Σ_m w_m z_m(t)`. This distributed-exponential construction approximates a fractional-memory kernel, where fast modes respond to immediate brightness changes and slow modes retain residual response over longer durations.

The time constants `τ_m` are dynamically set for each frame interval based on the mid-interval brightness `(I(t_k) + I(t_{k+1}))/2`, a reference time constant `τ_ref`, a reference brightness `I_ref`, and a brightness dependence parameter `β`. `τ_0` is bounded to prevent unrealistic responses.

For continuous-time evaluation, the log-intensity `L(t)` within a frame interval is approximated as a linear segment: `L(t_k + ∆) = L_0 + s∆`. This allows for a closed-form update equation for each `z_m(t_k + ∆)`, enabling the calculation of the voltage state at any point within the interval without a dense temporal grid. At the start of a sequence, all modes and the reference voltage are initialized from the first log-intensity frame.

#### 3.3. Continuous-Time Threshold Crossing

Events are generated when the pixel voltage `v(t)` crosses predefined ON or OFF thresholds relative to a reference voltage `r`. The reference-relative voltage is defined as `u(t) = g(v(t) - r)`, where `g` is a scalar gain. An ON event is emitted if `u(t) ≥ θ_on`, and an OFF event if `u(t) ≤ -θ_off` (with `θ_off` as a positive magnitude).

For each frame interval, the system first determines the number of ON (`n_+`) and OFF (`n_-`) events by evaluating the endpoint reference-relative voltage `u(t_{k+1})` against the thresholds. These counts indicate how many threshold levels have been spanned. For each active pixel and each spanned threshold level, the exact continuous-time timestamp `t*` of the crossing is determined using a bisection method on the interval `[t_k, t_{k+1}]`. If the number of events to be localized exceeds a predefined solver cap, the interval is recursively split into subintervals to handle dense bursts without requiring a uniform substep grid.

Crucially, after an event is emitted, only the reference state `r` is updated (e.g., `r ← r + θ_on/g` for an ON event). The memory mode states `z_m(t)` are *not* reset, meaning they carry their residual voltage response to the next crossing. This "retained memory state" is designed to preserve sub-threshold responses and support repeated triggering, linking the residual voltage to subsequent event timing. This lifecycle contributes to generating asynchronous event times, handling repeated same-pixel triggers, and representing ON/OFF polarity asymmetry through separate threshold magnitudes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FracEvent was conducted through event-stream comparison and its utility in downstream event-based vision tasks, specifically image reconstruction and optical flow estimation. Comparisons were made against established simulators: ESIM, v2e, and DVS-Voltmeter.

#### 4.1. Event-Stream Comparison

- **Quantitative Comparison (DAVIS240C):** FracEvent demonstrated competitive performance across several metrics when comparing simulated event streams to real DAVIS240C events over matched 50 ms windows. It achieved the lowest Inter-Event Interval (IEI) distance (0.0964) and the lowest polarity error (0.0244), indicating a closer match to the same-pixel timing structure and ON/OFF balance of real events. DVS-Voltmeter recorded the closest event count ratio (1.101, where 1 is ideal) and the lowest time surface distance (0.4791), which evaluates the similarity of recent event occurrences. ESIM and v2e showed significantly higher IEI distances and larger event count ratios, particularly ESIM, which produced an event count ratio of 9.221, indicating much denser event streams.
- **Qualitative Comparison (DAVIS240C and DAVIS346):** Visualizations showed that FracEvent produced event patterns continuously distributed over time and spatially consistent with real DAVIS240C data. In contrast, v2e and ESIM tended to concentrate events around discrete timestamps, with ESIM generating overly dense events. On DAVIS346 real-life captures, FracEvent better preserved event contours and ON/OFF polarity structure compared to v2e (which missed parts of patterns) and ESIM (which produced denser edge responses).
- **Ablation Study:** An ablation study on FracEvent components revealed the contribution of each design choice. Full FracEvent achieved the best overall performance in terms of event-count match, IEI distance, and polarity error. Removing multi-mode memory or the retained memory state primarily degraded the IEI distance, suggesting these components are crucial for accurate temporal structure. Removing separate ON/OFF thresholds significantly worsened polarity balance while having a minimal effect on IEI distance.

#### 4.2. Image Reconstruction

- **DAVIS240C and HQF Datasets:** For image reconstruction, an E2VID-style recurrent model was trained on events generated by each simulator from the GoPro training split and evaluated on real DAVIS240C and HQF sequences. FracEvent-generated events led to models with the lowest Mean Squared Error (MSE) (0.030 on DAVIS240C, 0.052 on HQF) and lowest LPIPS (0.460 on DAVIS240C, 0.443 on HQF) among simulated training sources. LPIPS measures perceptual similarity, while MSE indicates pixel-level accuracy. ESIM achieved the highest SSIM (Structural Similarity Index Measure) on both datasets (0.411 on DAVIS240C, 0.372 on HQF), suggesting better structural preservation in some cases, despite higher MSE and LPIPS compared to FracEvent. DVS-Voltmeter and v2e generally performed less effectively in reconstruction metrics.

#### 4.3. Optical Flow Estimation

- **MVSEC Dataset:** For optical flow estimation, an EV-FlowNet-style self-supervised model was trained on MVSEC outdoor day2 events generated by each simulator. The trained models were evaluated on MVSEC outdoor day1 and indoor flying sequences at `dt = 1` and `dt = 4` frame intervals. FracEvent yielded models with the lowest mean Average Endpoint Error (AEE) among all simulated event training sources, performing closest to the model trained on real MVSEC events. Across the four MVSEC evaluation sequences at both `dt = 1` and `dt = 4`, FracEvent achieved a mean AEE of 2.68, which was closer to the Real MVSEC control (2.42) than DVS-Voltmeter (2.87), ESIM (3.38), or v2e (3.39). FracEvent also demonstrated the best AEE in six out of eight sequence AEE columns, indicating consistent performance. DVS-Voltmeter performed strongest on two outdoor day1 columns. This suggests that FracEvent provides a more consistent training source for optical flow estimation under the specified protocol.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of FracEvent contributes to the field of event-based vision by offering a refined approach to event-camera simulation. Its significance lies in its focus on modeling the pixel-level event lifecycle through fractional-relaxation voltage dynamics, which addresses identified limitations in existing simulators that often oversimplify the temporal structure of event generation.

The **primary impact** is the generation of synthetic event streams that exhibit a closer resemblance to real event data, particularly in terms of inter-event interval timing, polarity balance, and spatiotemporal patterns. This is supported by direct event-stream comparisons where FracEvent demonstrated lower IEI distance and polarity error. By preserving residual sensor state across events and localizing threshold crossings in continuous time, FracEvent produces more nuanced event timing and density, which are critical for robust event-based algorithms.

**Practically, FracEvent's utility** is evidenced by its performance in downstream vision tasks. Models trained on event streams generated by FracEvent achieved lower MSE and LPIPS in image reconstruction and lower mean AEE in optical flow estimation compared to models trained on events from other prominent simulators. This indicates that the improved temporal fidelity of FracEvent's output translates into stronger sim-to-real transfer capabilities, reducing the gap between synthetic and real-world performance for event-based vision systems. This can accelerate the development and training of algorithms for tasks where real event data is scarce or challenging to collect, such as in robotics and autonomous driving.

Furthermore, by **decoupling the sensor-side conversion from the input trajectory source**, FracEvent offers a reusable event-generation core. This modularity allows researchers to pair its physically-inspired pixel dynamics with various upstream brightness signals from renderers, interpolation modules, or existing frame sequences. This flexibility enhances its adaptability and potential for integration into diverse simulation pipelines.

The work also highlights the **ongoing challenges and future directions** in event-camera simulation. While FracEvent improves sensor-side dynamics, the fidelity of the overall simulation still depends on the quality of the supplied brightness trajectory, which can be affected by interpolation, rendering, exposure handling, and camera-specific effects. Future research can build upon FracEvent's compact core by integrating it with more advanced trajectory models and incorporating additional camera mechanisms and noise regimes, potentially expanding its applicability to an even broader range of tasks and sensor types. The development of FracEvent represents a step towards making synthetic event data a more reliable and effective resource for advancing event-based vision.
