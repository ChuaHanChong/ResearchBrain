# Code-as-Room: Generating 3D Rooms from Top-Down View Images via Agentic Code Synthesis

> [!summary] Summary
> Code-as-Room is an agentic framework that turns a single top-down room image into executable Blender code. A ten-stage pipeline parses a scene graph, generates layout, geometry, materials and lighting, and shares a cross-stage memory. Visual feedback loops refine the layout. With Gemini 3.1 Pro, it raises layout IoU from 16.8% to 73.2% over direct generation and beats VIGA in human ratings.

## Problem

- Text-only room generation cannot express precise spatial information such as object counts, exact positions and orientations, so generated rooms often diverge from what users intended.
- Existing image-conditioned agents such as VIGA, extended naively from perspective to top-down views, lose fine-grained spatial detail and fall into infinite loops, giving unstable, unreliable holistic room generation.
- Long multi-stage agent workflows suffer context forgetting, and direct single-call VLM generation of a full Blender scene gives incomplete, spatially inconsistent rooms with unreliable code execution.

## Method

- Structured ==coarse-to-fine harness== of ten specialized stages: scene structuring, layout code, ==layout-grounded object profiling==, object geometry code, and material, texture and lighting code, each conditioned on prior outputs.
- ==Cross-stage memory== stores each stage's typed artifact and gives downstream stages only a predefined view, preserving consistency and cutting prompt noise. A ==scene graph== with a minor-object sidecar seeds layout.
- ==Render-critique-revise loop== on ==bounding-box layout proxies==, with sanitized critic feedback and at most 5 iterations. Hybrid procedural-plus-retrieval tiny objects and a deterministic post-hoc ==collision and boundary correction== complete the scene.

## Results

- On a 41-scene benchmark, Gemini 3.1 Pro with CaR reaches **73.2%** layout IoU, **79.8%** spatial-relation accuracy and **93.6%** rotation accuracy, versus **16.8%**, **54.7%** and **78.0%** for direct generation.
- Human study with 20 experts: CaR with Gemini 3.1 Pro scores **9.0** similarity and **7.5** acceptability, versus VIGA at **5.5** and **4.0** and direct GPT-5.5 at **7.0** and **5.0**.
- Ablations: removing memory drops layout IoU from **73.2%** to **58.0%** and object recall from **55.5%** to **48.2%**. Visual feedback peaks at **5 iterations**, and 10 lowers layout IoU from **66.2%** to **64.2%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A stable execution harness matters as much as the base model: staged decomposition, memory and feedback lifted weak direct-prompt backbones such as Gemini 3.1 Pro to the most reliable ones.
> - Persisting typed intermediate outputs in shared memory fixes context forgetting in long agent pipelines, and it helps most on global spatial consistency.
> - More critique-and-revise rounds are not always better: too many iterations cause layout drift and over-correction, so cap refinement loops and sanitize critic feedback.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The generation of realistic and functional 3D indoor rooms is a critical task for applications in interior design, virtual reality, gaming, and embodied AI. Historically, this domain has been addressed by traditional computer graphics methods, including procedural generation, rule-based layout optimization, and constraint-driven object placement. While these methods reduce manual effort, they are often constrained by hand-crafted rules and predefined categories, which limits their ability to model complex real-world spatial relationships and adapt to flexible user requirements.

Recent advancements in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have opened new avenues for 3D scene generation. Early MLLM-based approaches often represent indoor scenes using structured formats like JSON, leveraging MLLMs to predict spatial layouts, inter-object relations, and object attributes from textual descriptions. However, these text-based methods frequently lack the precision required for specifying fine-grained spatial information, such as exact object counts, precise locations, or detailed orientations, leading to generated scenes that may not accurately reflect user preferences.

In parallel, researchers have begun exploring MLLM agents for 3D room synthesis from reference images, often employing iterative generator-critic feedback loops. A notable work in this area is VIGA, which utilizes a coding agent to generate executable Blender code for 3D scenes from a perspective image. VIGA demonstrated the potential of a "code-as-scene" paradigm. However, when attempts are made to extend such approaches to holistic 3D room generation from top-down view images, existing image-conditioned agents encounter limitations. These include difficulties in recovering fine-grained spatial details, susceptibility to instability, and tendencies to enter infinite loops, which undermine the reliability and consistency of the generation process.

The current work, Code-as-Room, addresses these identified limitations by specifically focusing on top-down view images as input. Top-down layouts, sketches, and floor plans are widely used in real-world design workflows because they inherently encode rich spatial priors and holistic scene appearance. By leveraging this type of input, Code-as-Room aims to bridge the gap between abstract textual descriptions and the detailed spatial requirements of 3D room design, while simultaneously improving the stability and coherence of agent-based MLLM generation through a structured framework and robust execution harness. This positions the work as an advancement in controllable, image-conditioned 3D scene generation, particularly for complete indoor environments represented as editable code.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges associated with 3D indoor room design and the limitations of existing automated generation methods.

**Motivation:**
1. **Labor-Intensive Manual Design:** Manually constructing realistic and functional 3D rooms is a time-consuming and expert-dependent process, involving detailed 3D object modeling, spatial arrangement, material design, and lighting adjustments.
2. **Limitations of Traditional Graphics Methods:** While procedural, rule-based, and constraint-driven methods have reduced manual effort, they are inherently limited by predefined rules and categories. This restricts their ability to handle the complexity of real-world spatial relationships and adapt to diverse user needs.
3. **Inadequacy of Text-based MLLM Approaches:** Although MLLMs have shown promise in scene generation from textual descriptions, text inputs often fall short in conveying precise spatial information (e.g., exact object locations, orientations, or counts). This leads to generated 3D rooms that may not fully align with user-specified spatial requirements.
4. **Instability of Existing Image-conditioned Agents for Top-Down Views:** Real-world design workflows frequently utilize top-down layouts, floor plans, or sketches because these visual references intrinsically encode comprehensive spatial priors. Existing MLLM agent frameworks, such as VIGA, designed for perspective images, exhibit instability and a propensity for infinite loops when extended to complete 3D room generation from top-down views. They struggle to recover fine-grained spatial details necessary for holistic room synthesis. The absence of a stable, image-guided agent for generating complete, editable 3D rooms from top-down views represents a notable gap.
5. **Need for Editable and Interpretable 3D Representations:** Current image-conditioned 3D generation methods often focus on reconstruction or visual synthesis rather than structured, editable representations of complete indoor rooms. Executable code, like Blender scripts, offers interpretability and editability, which is valuable for design applications.

**Key Objectives:**
To address the aforementioned motivations, the research establishes the following key objectives for the Code-as-Room (CaR) framework:

1. **Develop a Top-Down Image-Guided 3D Room Synthesis Paradigm:** Propose a novel approach where a single top-down room image serves as the explicit global spatial prior to guide the generation of a complete 3D indoor room.
2. **Implement an MLLM-based Agentic Framework with a Structured Execution Harness:** Design a robust framework that orchestrates an MLLM agent to synthesize 3D rooms. This harness is intended to ensure stability and coherence throughout the generation process, specifically targeting the issues of instability and infinite loops observed in prior agent-based methods.
3. **Represent 3D Rooms as Executable Blender Code:** Generate Blender code that specifies the room structure, object placement, object geometry, materials, and lighting, thereby producing editable, renderable, and structured 3D scenes.
4. **Integrate a Cross-Stage Memory Module:** Introduce a memory system that persists scene information across various stages of the generation pipeline. This is crucial for mitigating context forgetting, a common challenge in long, multi-stage agentic workflows.
5. **Construct a Dedicated Benchmark for Code-based 3D Room Synthesis:** Create a comprehensive benchmark to systematically evaluate MLLM models and agentic frameworks. This benchmark is designed to assess visual content understanding, spatial relationship reasoning, vision-to-code generation capabilities, and overall scene quality.
6. **Conduct Comprehensive Comparisons and Validation:** Perform extensive experiments to compare Code-as-Room against existing agent-based methods and direct VLM generation, thereby validating the effectiveness of the proposed structured execution harness for robust 3D room generation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Code-as-Room (CaR) framework addresses the problem of generating executable Blender code for a complete 3D indoor scene from a single top-down view image. The approach decomposes this complex task into a coarse-to-fine, multi-stage pipeline, orchestrated by an MLLM agent within a structured execution harness.

#### 3.1. Problem Definition

Given a top-down room image *I*, the objective is to generate executable Blender code *C* that constructs a 3D indoor scene accurately aligned with *I*. This process is formalized as *C = A(I)*, where *A* is the VLM-agent harness. The task is decomposed into:
- **Coarse Stage:** Understanding the scene and generating a layout program (*D_CU* = *U_CU*(*I, M*), *C_layout* = *G_CG*(*I, D_CU, M*)), focusing on global spatial alignment.
- **Fine Stage:** Enriching the layout with detailed object descriptions and synthesizing the final room program (*D_FU* = *U_FU*(*I, C_layout, M*), *C* = *G_FG*(*I, C_layout, D_FU, M*)), adding editable geometry, appearance, and rendering code.
A cross-stage memory *M* is maintained throughout the process.

#### 3.2. Cross-Stage Memory

A shared memory *M* functions as a persistent state. Each stage *s* produces a typed artifact *e_s* (containing stage ID, type, output, and metadata), which updates the memory (*M_s = M_{s-1} ⊕ e_s*). Downstream stages access specific, predefined views of this memory, ensuring consistency and reducing irrelevant context.

#### 3.3. Image-based Scene Structuring (Phase 1)

This phase converts the input image into a structured scene state.
- **Stage 1: Spatial Semantic Analysis:** An MLLM extracts a schema-constrained description (*D1*) from the input image *I*, including functional zones, object hierarchies, and architectural elements (walls, doors, windows). Objects are assigned identifiers, categories, placement types, and parent relationships. A perimeter-aware prompt aids in identifying wall-mounted and peripheral objects.
- **Stage 2: Object-centric Scene Graph Construction:** Using *I* and *D1*, this stage builds a deterministic skeleton scene graph *G = (V, E)*, comprising architectural features (*V_arch*) and layout-defining major objects (*V_major*). Parent relations (*E_parent*) are derived, and minor objects are stored in a sidecar (*M_minor*). The MLLM further completes attributes, geometry hints, and forward relations for existing nodes. The graph is refined by filtering invalid edges and adding inverse and architectural-anchor relations before being stored in memory.

#### 3.4. Layout Code Generation (Phase 2)

This phase generates a coarse layout program (*C_layout*). Objects are represented as bounding-box proxies with approximate position, scale, and orientation, deferring fine details.
- **Stage 3: Major Layout with Visual Feedback:** The major layout for floor-level furniture is iteratively refined. An initial code (*C^(0)*) is generated based on *I*, *D1*, and *G*. In a render-critique-revise loop, the current code is rendered, and a VLM-based critic evaluates the rendered image against the input and scene graph. The critic provides a layout quality score (*s_t*) and textual feedback (*A^(t)*) on issues like missing objects, overlaps, or boundary violations. Feedback is sanitized, and the code is revised. This loop continues for a maximum of 5 iterations or until a satisfactory score is achieved, producing *C_major_layout*.
- **Stage 4: Auxiliary Layout for Walls and Salient Minors:** Wall-mounted objects from *D1* and visually salient minor objects from *M_minor* (e.g., rugs, floor lamps) are appended to *C_major_layout*. Tiny, surface-bound items are deferred. The resulting *C_layout* serves as a scaffold.

#### 3.5. Object-level Code Generation (Phase 3)

This module enriches each layout proxy with appearance, procedural geometry, and small objects.
- **Stage 5: Layout-grounded Object Description:** The MLLM parses *C_layout* and, conditioned on *I* and memory, generates fine-grained object descriptions (*D_FU*) for each object, including color, material, function, structure, and style, along with a global room-style description.
- **Stage 6: Object Geometry Replacement:** For each object, an agent predicts a semantic 3D geometry primitive decomposition (*P_i*), defining part types, names, sizes, offsets, and rotations within the object's local frame. Proxy constructors in *C_layout* are replaced with these part-based constructors (*C_geom*). This decomposition is stored as a geometry dictionary.
- **Stage 7: Tiny Object Instantiation (Implied/Auxiliary):** Visually distinctive tiny objects are handled by creating simple geometric placeholders, followed by retrieval of matching 3D assets from a library (*B*). The asset selection considers semantic relevance and size compatibility, scaling and aligning the asset to the placeholder while preserving support surfaces and footprints.

#### 3.6. Interior Decoration Code Generation (Phase 4)

This final phase completes the scene's appearance and illumination through geometry-preserving code rewriting.
- **Stage 8: Material Assignment:** Using the part dictionary, fine-grained descriptions, and global room style, the agent assigns part-level PBR (Physically Based Rendering) materials. This includes predicting material type, base color, roughness, metallic value, and specular strength, which are injected into the Blender script. Special handling for glass, mirrors, floors, and walls is included.
- **Stage 9: Texture and Decorative Surfaces:** For large or patterned surfaces, a high-capacity image generation model synthesizes texture maps for elements like floors, walls, rugs, and paintings. These textures augment corresponding material node graphs with image-texture nodes, with explicit UV mappings for planar elements. Failed generations fall back to simplified prompts.
- **Stage 10: Lighting, Rendering, and Post-hoc Correction:** The VLM infers the overall lighting style (dominant direction, natural light, artificial sources, ambient intensity) from *I* and the generated scene. This is translated into Blender light objects and renderer settings, producing raw executable scene code (*C_raw*). A deterministic post-hoc correction pass is applied to fix common implementation issues (e.g., missing materials, invalid texture paths, unreasonable light intensities, camera coverage, geometric artifacts). For movable objects with boundary or overlap violations, a local search finds a nearby feasible placement, producing the final program *C = PostHoc(C_raw)*.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Code-as-Room (CaR) involved a dedicated benchmark, human studies, scene re-rendering, and ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Benchmark for Top-down View Image to 3D Room

- **Benchmark Models:** CaR was evaluated with three MLLM backbones: Gemini-3 Flash, Gemini-3.1 Pro, and GPT-5.5. For comparison, direct (single-pass) generation using Gemini-3.1 Pro and GPT-5.5, and the agentic image-to-3D pipeline VIGA, were also assessed.
- **Benchmark Settings:** A test suite of 41 diverse scenes (residential, specialized; Simple, Middle, Hard complexity; photorealistic, synthetic, abstract line drawings) was used. Ground truth annotations were created via a human-in-the-loop pipeline, leveraging initial Gemini 3.1 generations corrected by human annotators.
- **Benchmark Metrics:** Evaluation encompassed four aspects:
 - **Visual Understanding:** Object Recall, Functional Accuracy.
 - **Spatial Reasoning:** Self Overlap, Layout IoU, Spatial Relation, Rotation Accuracy, Support Accuracy.
 - **Code Generation:** Agent Completion Rate, Blender Execution Rate.
 - **Overall Scene Quality:** VLM-based scores (Image Similarity, Scene Usability, Aesthetic Quality) and human study ratings (Usability, Error Acceptability).
- **Benchmark Results (Table 1):**
 - **Effectiveness of CaR:** The agentic design of Code-as-Room consistently improved MLLM performance across various metrics.
 - **VLM Backbone Performance:** While direct GPT-5.5 showed strong visual understanding and holistic quality, it suffered from spatial inconsistencies and unstable Blender execution. Gemini models, particularly Gemini3.1-pro, were less capable under direct prompting but emerged as the most stable and competitive backbones when integrated with Code-as-Room. For instance, Gemini3.1-pro with CaR achieved high Layout IoU (73.2%), Spatial Relation accuracy (79.8%), Rotation Accuracy (93.6%), and a 95.5% Blender execution rate, significantly outperforming its direct generation (Layout IoU 16.8%, Spatial Relation 54.7%, Rotation Acc. 78.0%, Exec. Rate 57.8%).
 - **Importance of Staged Workflow:** The results indicate that staged decomposition, memory-guided reasoning, and visual feedback are crucial for achieving reliable 3D scene synthesis from top-down images.
 - **Qualitative Analysis (Figures 3, 4, 5):** Direct VLM generation often produced incomplete or spatially unstable rooms. Code-as-Room, in contrast, yielded more complete structures, clearer functional regions, and better-aligned furniture layouts. Gemini-based CaR variants recovered richer local structures (shelves, cabinets, decorations), while GPT-5.5 with CaR preserved coarse layouts effectively but sometimes simplified small details.

#### 4.2. Human Evaluation

- **Methodology:** 20 expert raters evaluated scene quality based on similarity to the input, usability, lighting alignment, and acceptability (usability after minor corrections). Comparisons included direct VLM generation, VIGA, and CaR variants.
- **Results (Table 2):**
 - Code-as-Room consistently surpassed direct generation and VIGA in human-perceived quality.
 - CaR with Gemini3.1-Pro achieved the highest scores for similarity (9.0), usability (8.0), and acceptability (7.5).
 - VIGA, despite comparable lighting scores, showed lower similarity (5.5) and usability (4.5), suggesting weaker layout preservation and practical utility.
 - **Qualitative Observation (Figure 6):** VIGA tended to generate template-like scenes with missing details and inaccurate object placement, whereas Code-as-Room better preserved room proportions, furniture arrangements, and local semantics.

#### 4.3. Scene Re-rendering

- **Demonstration:** To illustrate the potential of the generated 3D scenes, image-level re-rendering was applied using GPT-5.5.
- **Outcome (Figure 7):** Although the procedurally generated 3D scenes might have limited visual realism, they provided strong 3D priors (room structure, object layout, spatial relations, camera-consistent geometry). The re-rendering model utilized these priors to generate more realistic materials, lighting, and object details, while maintaining geometric and semantic consistency across multiple views. This indicates that the 3D scenes produced by CaR can serve as effective structural foundations for high-quality visual refinement.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Effect of Memory Mechanism (Table 3):**
 - Removing the cross-stage memory module resulted in a degradation across all evaluated metrics (Object Recall: 48.2% vs. 55.5%; Layout IoU: 58.0% vs. 73.2%; Rotation Accuracy: 88.4% vs. 93.6%).
 - The significant drop in Layout IoU highlights the memory's importance in maintaining spatial consistency and effectively reusing image-derived information across the pipeline.
- **Effect of Visual Feedback Iterations (Table 3):**
 - Performance, particularly in Layout IoU and Rotation Accuracy, improved from 0 iterations (no feedback) up to 5 iterations. For example, Layout IoU increased from 64.0% (0 iter.) to 66.2% (5 iter.).
 - This confirms that intermediate renderings and the critique-revise loop are effective in correcting object omissions, placement errors, and rotational misalignments.
 - However, increasing iterations beyond 5 (e.g., to 10) led to a slight reduction in Layout IoU (64.2%) and rotation accuracy (72.6%), suggesting that excessive revision might introduce layout drift or over-correction. Thus, 5 iterations were selected as an optimal balance.
 - **Qualitative Observation (Figure 8):** Visual examples further support these findings, demonstrating improved coherence with memory and optimal feedback iterations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Code-as-Room framework presents a notable advancement in the field of 3D indoor scene generation, particularly for applications requiring structured, editable, and visually consistent environments.

**Significance:**
1. **Bridging the Gap in 3D Room Generation:** This work addresses critical limitations of prior methods, including the labor-intensiveness of manual design, the rigidity of rule-based systems, the lack of spatial precision in text-driven MLLMs, and the instability of existing image-conditioned agents when dealing with top-down views. By leveraging top-down images as explicit spatial priors, it aligns with practical design workflows.
2. **Enhancing Stability and Coherence of MLLM Agents:** The introduction of a structured execution harness, a multi-stage pipeline, and a cross-stage memory module significantly improves the stability and coherence of MLLM-based 3D scene synthesis. This mitigates issues like infinite loops and context forgetting, which have hindered previous agentic frameworks.
3. **Generating Editable and Interpretable 3D Scenes:** Representing 3D rooms as executable Blender code provides a highly interpretable and editable output. This differs from methods that generate static 3D models, offering greater utility for designers and developers who need to modify or interact with the generated scenes.
4. **Establishing a Benchmark for Future Research:** The creation of a dedicated benchmark for code-based 3D room synthesis, covering visual understanding, spatial reasoning, vision-to-code generation, and overall scene quality, provides a valuable resource for guiding future research and enabling standardized evaluation in this domain.
5. **Robust Integration of Vision-Language Capabilities:** The framework effectively orchestrates the visual understanding, spatial reasoning, and code generation capabilities of MLLMs, demonstrating a principled approach to complex, multi-modal tasks.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Interior Design and Architecture:** Designers could rapidly generate initial 3D room layouts and designs from simple top-down sketches or reference images, significantly accelerating the ideation and prototyping phases. The editable nature of the Blender code allows for subsequent refinement.
- **Virtual Reality and Gaming:** Content creators can quickly populate virtual environments with diverse and realistic indoor scenes, reducing the manual effort involved in asset creation and scene assembly. This could lead to richer and more varied virtual experiences.
- **Embodied AI and Robotics:** The ability to generate functional and geometrically consistent 3D room environments is crucial for training and evaluating embodied AI agents and robots. These agents require realistic simulation environments for learning navigation, object interaction, and complex task execution.
- **Architectural Visualization:** Rapid generation of 3D scenes from floor plans could streamline architectural visualization processes, allowing for quick creation of photorealistic renderings and walkthroughs.
- **Advancement in MLLM Applications:** This work contributes to the broader field of MLLM applications by demonstrating how structured agentic design can overcome inherent limitations of large models in complex, multi-step generation tasks.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
1. **Input View Dependency:** The current framework is optimized for top-down view images, limiting its applicability to more general, arbitrary-view inputs. Future work could explore extending its capabilities to diverse camera perspectives.
2. **Geometric Fidelity of Procedural Code:** While effective, procedural code generation may struggle to faithfully represent the complex geometric details of all real-world objects. Asset retrieval is still required for higher geometric fidelity in certain cases. Future research could focus on improving the alignment between code-generation models and detailed 3D assets.
3. **Realism of Visualizations and Temporal Consistency:** Although image re-rendering enhances visual realism, current video generation models face challenges in producing high-quality and temporally consistent re-renderings, especially for longer trajectories. Future work aims to explore video generation models as neural renderers to produce more realistic and coherent scene visualizations from Code-as-Room outputs.
