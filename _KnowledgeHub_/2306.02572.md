# Introduction to Latent Variable Energy-Based Models: A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence

> [!summary] Summary
> A conceptual framework for autonomous machine intelligence is presented, built upon latent variable energy-based models and Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs). This framework aims to enable self-supervised learning of world models that handle multimodality and uncertainty, supporting hierarchical planning.

## Problem

- Current supervised learning (SL) requires extensive, costly labeled datasets and struggles with generalization beyond training distributions.
- Reinforcement learning (RL) demands numerous trial-and-error episodes, making it impractical for many real-world applications where mistakes are costly.
- Existing AI systems lack the flexibility, common sense reasoning, and generalization abilities that characterize human intelligence.

## Method

- Proposing a conceptual framework centered on ==energy-based== models (EBMs) with latent variables to define compatibility between variables, supporting multimodal predictions and uncertainty.
- Introducing Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) that learn representations of inputs and outputs in a common embedding space, trained via self-supervised learning.
- Developing Hierarchical JEPAs (H-JEPAs) to enable planning and prediction at multiple temporal or abstraction levels, akin to human hierarchical planning.

## Results

- The framework enables learning rich world models directly from unlabeled data, mirroring human observational learning processes.
- It supports multimodal predictions and structured representation of uncertainty, allowing AI systems to account for multiple plausible outcomes.
- The approach facilitates hierarchical planning, allowing systems to break down complex long-term goals into subgoals and engage in 'mental imagination' to evaluate action sequences.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-supervised learning is critical for autonomous intelligence, allowing systems to learn rich world models from unlabeled data by predicting parts of inputs from other parts.
> - Energy-based models inherently handle multimodal outputs and provide a measure of uncertainty through their energy landscape, unlike deterministic models.
> - Latent variables within energy-based models enable the representation of multiple plausible futures or interpretations, facilitating reasoning under ambiguity and efficient representation of multimodal distributions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Achieving autonomous machine intelligence—systems that can learn efficiently, reason, and plan complex action sequences—remains one of the most ambitious goals in artificial intelligence research. The paper by Anna Dawid and Yann LeCun presents a conceptual framework for building such systems using latent variable energy-based models.

Current AI systems, despite impressive capabilities in specific domains, lack the flexibility and generalization abilities that characterize human intelligence. They typically require extensive labeled data (supervised learning) or massive trial-and-error experiences (reinforcement learning) to learn even simple tasks. They also struggle with tasks requiring "common sense" and generalization to new situations.

The proposed framework, centered around energy-based models with latent variables, aims to address these limitations by drawing inspiration from how humans and animals learn through observation and self-supervised learning. This approach could enable AI systems to build rich world models from unlabeled data, represent uncertainty in predictions, handle multimodal predictions, and learn hierarchical planning abilities.

### 2. Limitations of Current Machine Learning Approaches

Current machine learning paradigms face several significant limitations:

**Supervised Learning (SL)** requires extensive labeled datasets, which are often expensive, time-consuming to create, and may not be available in many domains. While SL has achieved remarkable results in image recognition, machine translation, and other tasks, it struggles with generalization beyond its training distribution.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL)** requires numerous trial-and-error episodes to learn even relatively simple tasks, making it impractical for many real-world applications where mistakes are costly. RL also faces challenges with sparse rewards and exploration in large state spaces.

**Self-Supervised Learning (SSL)** addresses some of these limitations by learning from unlabeled data, essentially creating its own supervision signal. SSL works by predicting parts of the input from other parts, such as predicting future frames in a video from past frames or masked portions of an image from visible portions.

The authors argue that SSL is a key enabler for autonomous intelligence because it allows systems to learn rich representations of the world without explicit supervision, similar to how humans and animals learn through observation.

### 3. Energy-Based Models

Energy-Based Models (EBMs) form the foundation of the proposed approach. An EBM defines a scalar energy function that maps each configuration of variables to a scalar energy value. Lower energy values indicate more compatible configurations, while higher energies represent incompatible or unlikely configurations.

Formally, an energy function $F_W(x, y)$ assigns an energy to each pair of input $x$ and output $y$, parameterized by weights $W$. The most compatible $y$ for a given $x$ is the one that minimizes the energy:

$$\hat{y} = \arg\min_y F_W(x, y)$$

EBMs offer several advantages over traditional feed-forward models:

1. They can naturally handle multimodal outputs by having multiple energy minima for a given input
2. They provide a measure of uncertainty through the shape of the energy landscape
3. They can represent complex dependencies between variables that may not be easily factorizable

However, training EBMs presents challenges. The most direct training approach involves minimizing the energy for observed (x,y) pairs while raising the energy for all other y values. This is computationally expensive and often intractable for high-dimensional outputs.

### 4. Latent Variable Models

To address the limitations of basic EBMs and enhance their expressiveness, the authors introduce latent variables. A latent variable model incorporates unobserved variables $z$ that can capture underlying factors of variation in the data.

An energy-based latent variable model defines an energy function $F_W(x, y, z)$ that includes the latent variables. The effective energy for a pair $(x, y)$ is obtained by minimizing or marginalizing over the latent variables:

$$F_W(x, y) = \min_z F_W(x, y, z) \text{ or } F_W(x, y) = -\log \int_z e^{-F_W(x, y, z)} dz$$

Latent variables allow the model to:

1. Capture underlying factors that explain observations
2. Represent multimodal distributions more efficiently
3. Incorporate uncertainty in a structured way
4. Enable inference of missing data through latent space

An important property of these models is that latent variables can represent multiple plausible futures or interpretations of inputs, allowing systems to reason about uncertainty and plan under ambiguity.

### 5. Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures

The paper introduces Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) as a concrete implementation of the latent variable EBM framework. JEPAs learn representations of inputs and outputs in a common embedding space, where compatibility is measured by a divergence function.

A JEPA consists of:

1. **Encoder networks** that map inputs $x$ and outputs $y$ to representations $s_x$ and $s_y$ in embedding space
2. **Predictor network** that predicts the output representation $\hat{s}_y$ from input representation $s_x$, conditioned on latent variable $z$
3. **Divergence function** $D(s_x, s_y)$ that measures compatibility between representations
4. **Energy function** derived from this divergence

The overall energy function for a JEPA is:

$$F_W(x, y, z) = D(\text{Pred}_W(s_x, z), s_y) + R(z)$$

where $R(z)$ is a regularization term that prevents the model from relying too heavily on the latent variables.

JEPAs differ from traditional predictive models in several key ways:

1. They predict representations rather than raw outputs
2. They incorporate latent variables to handle uncertainty and multimodality
3. They use a divergence measure rather than a direct reconstruction loss
4. They can be trained using contrastive or regularized methods

### 6. Hierarchical Planning with H-JEPAs

Building on JEPAs, the authors propose Hierarchical JEPAs (H-JEPAs) that enable planning at multiple temporal or abstraction levels. H-JEPAs stack JEPA modules hierarchically, with higher levels making predictions at longer time scales or higher levels of abstraction.

This hierarchical structure allows the system to:

1. Plan at multiple time scales simultaneously
2. Break down complex long-term goals into manageable subgoals
3. Handle uncertainty differently at different time scales
4. Make efficient use of computation by focusing on relevant details at each level

The H-JEPA architecture bears resemblance to model-predictive control (MPC) systems from optimal control theory, but incorporates uncertainty handling and multimodal predictions through latent variables.

For planning, the system can use the learned world model to simulate and evaluate different action sequences through a process the authors liken to "mental imagination." This involves:

1. Generating potential action sequences using the actor module
2. Predicting their outcomes using the world model (H-JEPA)
3. Evaluating the outcomes using an intrinsic cost function
4. Selecting the sequence with the lowest predicted cost

### 7. Training Challenges and Regularization

Training energy-based models with latent variables presents several challenges. One of the most significant is "energy collapse," where the model learns to assign low energy to all input-output pairs by exploiting the latent variables.

To address these challenges, the authors discuss several training approaches:

**Contrastive methods** push the energy of observed data points down while pushing the energy of carefully selected negative samples up. These methods include Noise-Contrastive Estimation (NCE), Contrastive Divergence (CD), and various forms of self-supervised contrastive learning.

**Regularized methods** prevent energy collapse by adding regularization terms that:
1. Limit the capacity or information content of latent variables
2. Encourage the model to use different latent configurations for different inputs
3. Push the energy up everywhere except near training samples

The paper also discusses connections to other models like Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs), Hopfield networks, and autoencoders.

### 8. Applications and Future Directions

The proposed framework has potential applications across numerous domains:

1. **Robotics and autonomous systems** that can learn world models from observation and plan complex actions
2. **Computer vision systems** that understand scene dynamics and can predict multiple plausible futures
3. **Natural language processing** models that capture the inherent ambiguity in language
4. **Multimodal learning** systems that integrate information from different sensory modalities

Future research directions include:

1. Scaling H-JEPA architectures to more complex environments
2. Developing more efficient training methods for latent variable EBMs
3. Incorporating intrinsic cost functions that capture task-relevant preferences
4. Integrating these models with real-world robotic systems
5. Exploring the connections between these models and theories of human cognition

### 9. Conclusion

The paper presents a comprehensive conceptual framework for building autonomous intelligence systems using latent variable energy-based models. The key innovations include the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) and its hierarchical extension (H-JEPA), which enable self-supervised learning of world models capable of handling uncertainty and multimodality.

While still largely conceptual, this approach offers a promising direction for addressing the limitations of current machine learning paradigms and moving towards more human-like AI systems. The integration of self-supervised learning with energy-based modeling provides a framework that could potentially support common-sense reasoning, efficient learning from limited data, and hierarchical planning capabilities.

The challenges in training these models are significant, but the proposed regularization techniques and architectural innovations offer potential solutions. As computational resources continue to expand and training methods improve, these models may provide a foundation for the next generation of artificial intelligence systems that can learn, reason, and plan with greater autonomy and flexibility.
