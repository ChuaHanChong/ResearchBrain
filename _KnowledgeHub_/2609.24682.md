# Think Like a World Model, Act Like a VLA: Distilling World-Model Representations into Compact Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> This method distills a frozen world model's internal visual features into a compact VLA through one cosine alignment loss against an offline-cached teacher feature, so no teacher runs during training and the deployed policy is unchanged. A 0.8B student reaches 97.9% on LIBERO (+2.6 points), 50.5% on RoboCasa-GR1 (from 48.2%), runs in 32 ms and 1.86 GB, and transfers gains to real robots.

## Problem

- Standard VLA training maps observations to actions with no objective modeling how the world responds, so robustness to viewpoint, lighting, and clutter shifts depends mainly on data coverage.
- World-model and world-action policies are more physically grounded, but rolling the future forward is costly: DreamZero needs 3 s per inference on an H100 and 45.9 GB memory.
- Compact VLAs such as SmolVLA and Seer trade accuracy for deployability, while representation-alignment work like FitNets and REPA aligned to generic teachers, not world models carrying temporal, causal structure.

## Method

- **World-model representation alignment**: mean-pool the student's final-layer image tokens per camera view, map through a two-layer ==MLP== projector, and apply a ==cosine loss== (weight 0.5) against the stop-gradient teacher feature.
- **Offline teacher cache**: the frozen teacher runs once over training frames, writing one pooled vector per view to a memory-mapped cache, so no teacher weights load during training and one cache serves many students.
- Student and teacher: a 0.8B **QwenGR00T** policy (Qwen VL backbone plus ==GR00T-style flow-matching== action expert) aligned to **Cosmos3-Nano**'s understanding tower at layer 24 (4096-d); projector dropped after training, no teacher action targets.

## Results

- LIBERO four-suite mean rises from **95.3%** to **97.9%** with the same 0.8B student; every other sub-4B policy in the comparison scores between **78.7%** and **95.3%**.
- RoboCasa-GR1 improves from **48.2%** to **50.5%**, within **4.3 points** of the 4B QwenGR00T; real-robot success rises **83.3** to **93.3%** (fruit), **46.7** to **60.0%** (egg), **40.0** to **46.7%** (bimanual).
- Ablations: all teachers help on LIBERO (V-JEPA2-AC **+1.2**, Fast-WAM **+1.6**, Cosmos3-Nano **+2.6**); gains hold at InternVL 1B (**+2.4**) and Qwen3-VL 4B (**+1.6**); all alignment layers stay within **four points**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A world model's value to control can live in its internal features, not its rollouts; generation is the training objective that shapes them, and can be discarded at deployment.
> - Caching a frozen teacher's pooled features offline makes representation distillation nearly free, decouples teacher and student environments, and keeps deployed-architecture parity, so gains are attributable to representation alone.
> - Gains are modest and variance-sensitive: RoboCasa-GR1 swings several points across GPUs, and a representational prior helps least with contact failures like egg slippage; report multi-seed, multi-device means.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot must pick up fruit and place it in a basket, or coordinate a handover between two arms. Ordinary vision-language-action (VLA) policies do not explicitly learn how the world responds to actions. A VLA is a model that takes observations and a language instruction and predicts robot actions. Using a world model as the policy instead can require seconds and large memory per decision. Dao and colleagues propose a middle route. They use a world model's visual features to train a small policy and leave the future-generating model behind at deployment. In matched tests, the same small student achieves higher success rates, and its deployed graph is unchanged.

Author-described compilation of THAW-VLA 0.8B real-robot rollouts on AgileX Nero single-arm fruit and egg pick-and-place, and TRIP-Bag bimanual fruit handover; inference runs on one RTX 5090.

### 2. A cached lesson, not a deployed world model

A world model is trained to predict how a scene evolves. The student learns from the world model's representation of the scene rather than from its predictions of the future. The teacher is the frozen understanding tower of Cosmos3-Nano, specifically Cosmos3's Qwen3-VL-8B reasoner at layer 24, with 4096 feature dimensions per view.

The recipe has three steps:

1. The teacher runs once on the training images.
1. Its image-token features are mean-pooled separately for each camera view and cached.
1. During ordinary student training, the student's image-token features for the same views are mean-pooled and passed through a two-layer MLP projector. A cosine loss then aligns their direction with the cached features.

The training objective is the usual action loss plus this alignment term, weighted 0.5. The action targets remain the demonstrations, not teacher actions. In plain terms, the small policy learns to make its view of a scene point in the same representational direction as the teacher's, while it still learns actions from demonstrations.

The closest precedent, [[2410.06940|REPA: Representation Alignment for Generation]], also uses a projection-mediated cosine alignment objective. REPA aligns diffusion-model features to a pretrained self-supervised visual encoder to aid image generation. This paper uses a world-model teacher for robot action learning, and the student never learns to generate futures.

Figure 2. Left, "Offline teacher precompute": student frames from views 1 through C pass through a frozen world model and per-view mean-pooling into a teacher-feature cache. Right, "Student training": the Qwen-VL backbone's image features are pooled per view and projected for alignment with the cached features. Its hidden states also condition a flow-matching action expert trained with the action loss.

Because the targets are cached offline, the teacher does not need to be loaded during student training. The extra offline pass took about an hour on four GPUs for LIBERO, and the cache can be reused. At inference, the projector is removed. The policy is architecturally identical to the undistilled one and uses the same four flow steps. It runs in 32 ms and 1.86 GB on an RTX 5090.

### 3. The matched policy is the test

The most informative comparison pits the same student, trained with and without alignment, on RoboCasa-GR1. The benchmark has 24 humanoid task environments. Success is the mean of per-environment success rates over 20 episodes each. Each reported number averages four runs, crossing two seeds and two GPUs. The benchmark is nondeterministic, and results can move by several points when the GPU changes.

Under this protocol, the 0.8B QwenGR00T student rises from 48.2% ± 2.1 to 50.5% ± 2.3 with alignment. The matched 4B QwenGR00T reaches 54.8% ± 2.0. Several stronger published entries also remain ahead.

Table II, RoboCasa-GR1 success rates (%). "QwenGR00T, no distillation" (0.8B) scores 48.2 ± 2.1, and "Ours" (0.8B) scores 50.5 ± 2.3. The matched 4B QwenGR00T, at 54.8 ± 2.0, was re-evaluated by the authors under the same four-run protocol because success rates shift across seeds and GPU devices.

On LIBERO, success is averaged over four suites: SPATIAL, OBJECT, GOAL, and LIBERO-10, with 500 trials per suite. The distilled 0.8B student scores 97.9% ± 0.5, versus 95.3% ± 0.7 without distillation. Stronger, generally larger policies still lead on both benchmarks.

### 4. Borrow the features

The student's gains come from teacher-feature supervision added during training. Future generation is not part of the deployed policy. The authors read successful tests across different teachers, student sizes, backbones, and alignment layers as a sign of a broad representational prior. That is their interpretation, not a direct measurement of what the features encode. They describe the observed gains as "modest but consistent."
