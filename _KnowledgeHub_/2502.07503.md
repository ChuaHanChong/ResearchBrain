# Recursive Inference Scaling: A Winning Path to Scalable Inference in Language and Multimodal Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Recursive Inference Scaling (RINS) is an architectural approach from Google DeepMind that enhances the performance of language and multimodal models by intelligently applying recursion during inference. It achieves superior results by repeatedly applying an early portion of the network, outperforming other parameter-sharing methods under equivalent training compute, and is validated across language modeling and vision-language tasks.

## Problem

- Traditional neural scaling laws primarily focus on training performance, with less emphasis on efficient and effective ways to scale models at inference time.
- Prior recursive and parameter-sharing architectures often lacked conclusive evidence of superior scaling exponents or were not rigorously compared under compute-matched training conditions.
- There was a need to intrinsically leverage the inherent properties of data, such as the hypothesized fractal nature of language, within model architectures to enhance performance.

## Method

- Introduces ==Recursive INference Scaling (RINS)==, a novel architectural paradigm defined by a degree 1 and A^rB signature, where an initial block (A) is ==recursively applied to its own output== 'r' times before passing to a final block (B).
- Employs a ==compute-matched training regime==, ensuring that recursive models are trained on fewer examples to equalize total training FLOPs with non-recursive baselines for fair comparison.
- Develops ==stochastic RINS== with lightweight linear adapters to enable flexibility in inference-time compute and establish a "no-regret" pretraining strategy, where training with RINS improves performance even at baseline inference costs.

## Results

- RINS consistently outperforms over **55** other parameter-sharing variants, including RAO and latent recurrent thinking, in language modeling tasks under compute-matched training, with the performance gap widening at higher training compute budgets.
- The approach successfully generalizes to multimodal systems (SigLIP-B/16), improving zero-shot classification accuracy (e.g., ImageNet 0-shot from **73.4%** to **74.1%**) and retrieval performance, and pushing state-of-the-art in overtraining regimes.
- Stochastic RINS, combined with linear adapters, provides a "no-regret" pretraining strategy, enhancing baseline performance even when recursive inference is not utilized, and improves both asymptotic performance limits and scaling exponents for language modeling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Language exhibits a self-similar or fractal nature, which can be effectively exploited by recursive decoding mechanisms to improve model performance.
> - Intelligent architectural design focusing on inference-time compute, such as RINS, can yield greater performance gains and more efficient scaling laws compared to simply increasing model size, data, or training compute.
> - Rigorous compute-matched training comparisons are essential to accurately attribute performance improvements to architectural innovation rather than merely increased training resources.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of several rapidly evolving areas within deep learning, particularly concerning the efficiency and effectiveness of large language models (LLMs) and multimodal AI.

Firstly, it builds upon the foundational understanding of **neural scaling laws**. Pioneering works by Hestness et al. (2017), Kaplan et al. (2020), and Hoffmann et al. (2022) established that the performance of deep neural networks often follows predictable power-law relationships with increasing dimensions like model size, dataset size, and training compute. The current paper extends this paradigm by identifying **inference compute** as another crucial scaling dimension. While previous scaling laws primarily focused on *training* performance, RINS explicitly targets improvements achievable by increasing compute during the *inference* phase.

Secondly, the paper directly contributes to the burgeoning field of **inference-time scaling and "thought" processes in LLMs**. The successes of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2024), critique-then-generate approaches (Ankner et al., 2024), and repeated sampling methods (AlphaCode, Codex, Brown et al., 2024) have demonstrated that allocating more computational effort during inference can significantly enhance model capabilities, particularly in reasoning and complex task completion. The survey by Welleck et al. (2024) highlights the growing importance of these inference-time algorithms. RINS introduces a **complementary architectural approach** to this landscape, offering a mechanism to intrinsically scale inference within the model's structure rather than solely relying on external prompting or sampling strategies.

Thirdly, RINS re-evaluates and advances the concept of **model recursion and parameter sharing**. Techniques like Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019) and ALBERT (Lan et al., 2020) previously explored parameter sharing to reduce model size or improve sample efficiency. However, as noted by Tay et al. (2022a, 2022b), these earlier recursive architectures sometimes exhibited less favorable scaling exponents compared to vanilla transformers, limiting their widespread adoption. RINS distinguishes itself by demonstrating that it **improves *both* the asymptotic performance limits and the scaling exponents**, addressing a key limitation of prior work in this area. Crucially, the paper rigorously compares RINS against recent recursive architectures like "repeat-all-over" (RAO) from MobileLLM (Liu et al., 2024) and "latent recurrent thinking" (Geiping et al., 2025). Unlike these prior works, RINS performs all comparisons under a **compute-matched training regime**, meaning the total FLOPs used during training are equivalent across different architectures. This rigorous control ensures that observed performance gains are due to the architectural innovation of RINS itself, not merely an artifact of increased training compute, a critical distinction that clarifies the true efficacy of recursive designs.

Finally, the work is uniquely inspired by the emerging understanding of the **fractal or self-similar nature of language** (Alabdulmohsin et al., 2024a). This theoretical grounding posits that similar patterns repeat across different scales of linguistic representation. RINS translates this insight into an architectural design, proposing that recursively applying an early portion of a neural network can effectively exploit this inherent scale-invariant property of language. This theoretical motivation, combined with empirical validation, sets RINS apart from purely empirical architectural searches and suggests a deeper connection between data structure and optimal model design. Its extension to multimodal systems further underscores its potential broad applicability, recognizing that such systems also process and generate language.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce **Recursive INference Scaling (RINS)** as a novel and highly effective method for enhancing the performance of language and multimodal systems by intelligently leveraging additional compute at inference time.

The motivations driving this research are multifaceted:

1. **Exploiting Inherent Language Structure:** A core motivation stems from recent findings suggesting that language exhibits a "self-similar" or "fractal" nature. This implies that patterns observed at one level of linguistic granularity (e.g., words) are mirrored at higher levels (e.g., sentences, paragraphs). The authors hypothesize that a recursive (scale-invariant) decoding mechanism could naturally align with and exploit this inherent property, leading to more efficient and powerful models. This connection provides a theoretical grounding for the architectural choices made in RINS.

2. **Efficient Inference Scaling without Training Compute Increase:** While increasing model size, data, or training compute has been the traditional path to better performance, there's a growing interest in improving models via inference-time compute. However, many current methods (e.g., complex prompting, multiple sampling) can be computationally expensive at test time. The key motivation for RINS is to achieve significant performance gains by extending the computational path length during inference *without* requiring an increase in the model's parameter count or the overall training compute budget. This makes RINS an attractive option for deploying high-performing models in resource-constrained environments.

3. **Rigorous Architectural Comparison:** Previous work on recursive architectures (e.g., MobileLLM, latent recurrent thinking) demonstrated promising results but often compared models based on similar training tokens, not necessarily equivalent training FLOPs. This left open the question of whether gains were due to the recursion itself or simply increased training compute. A critical motivation for RINS is to address this ambiguity by conducting **compute-matched comparisons** across a wide range of parameter-sharing strategies. This ensures that RINS's superior performance is a genuine architectural advantage, not a hidden consequence of more compute.

4. **Generalizability Beyond Language:** Recognizing that many advanced AI systems, particularly multimodal ones, incorporate natural language processing, the authors were motivated to investigate if the benefits of recursive inference scaling extend beyond pure language tasks. Demonstrating gains in multimodal systems would significantly broaden RINS's impact and applicability.

5. **Developing a "No-Regret" Strategy:** A practical concern with inference-time scaling is the potential trade-off between peak performance (when scaled inference is used) and baseline performance (when it's not). The authors were motivated to develop a "no-regret" strategy for RINS, ensuring that pretraining with RINS offers benefits even if the recursive inference path is not utilized at test time. This would make RINS a universally beneficial component of model pretraining, regardless of deployment inference budget.

6. **Improving Scaling Laws:** Beyond merely achieving better performance, the research aims to understand if RINS can fundamentally alter the scaling behavior of models, specifically by improving both the asymptotic performance limits (the best performance achievable with infinite data/compute) and the scaling exponents (how quickly performance improves with compute). This would imply a more efficient learning trajectory and higher ultimate potential.

In essence, the authors seek to provide a highly efficient, rigorously validated, and theoretically inspired method for improving large neural networks by optimizing their inference-time computation, making them both more performant and more practical for real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper is characterized by a systematic exploration of recursive architectures under rigorously controlled computational budgets, ensuring fair comparisons.

#### 3.1. Formalizing Recursive Architectures: Taxonomy

The authors introduce a precise taxonomy for classifying parameter-sharing architectures based on two key concepts:
- **Signature:** Describes how unique blocks (e.g., A, B, C) are composed and where parameter sharing occurs. For example, `AB` is a baseline feedforward, `AAB` applies block A twice then block B, and `AB^2C` applies B twice recursively. Critically, these blocks are partitioned to be of *equal size* in terms of layers, and the signature reflects the computational flow.
- **Degree:** Specifies if recursive depth is nested. A degree of 1 implies simple sequential application of blocks according to the signature. A degree of 2 means each block within the signature itself has the same recursive pattern applied (e.g., `(ABB)^2` implies A, B, and B themselves follow the ABB pattern recursively).

#### 3.2. Defining Recursive Inference Scaling (RINS)

From this taxonomy, RINS is formally defined as an architecture with **degree 1 and signature A^rB**, where `r > 1`. This means the model is depth-wise partitioned into two equally sized blocks, A and B. Block A is recursively applied `r` times to its own output before being passed to block B. This specific configuration was identified empirically as the "clear winner" among a vast search space.

#### 3.3. The Compute-Matched Regime: A Critical Control

Unlike prior works that often compared models based on similar numbers of *training tokens*, this study meticulously controls for the *total training compute FLOPs*. This means that if a recursive model performs more operations per forward pass (due to repeated block applications), it is trained on *fewer examples or steps* to ensure its total training FLOPs are equivalent to a non-recursive baseline model of the same parameter count. This rigorous control is fundamental to attributing performance gains directly to the architectural benefits of recursion rather than merely to increased training compute.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup for Language Modeling

- **Model Architecture:** Decoder-only Transformer models (Vaswani et al., 2017) with relative position embeddings and sequence packing.
- **Tokenizer:** C4/English tokenizer with a 32K vocabulary.
- **Training Data:** A mixture of C4 (Raffel et al., 2020) and SlimPajama (Soboleva et al., 2023) with equal weighting.
- **Training Configuration:** Batch size of 1,024, context length of 1,024 (or 1,536 for the long-sequence baseline). The non-recursive baseline was trained for 200K steps (approx. 200B tokens).
- **Optimizer:** Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014) with a learning rate of 5x10^-4 and weight decay of 5x10^-5, using an inverse square root decay schedule.
- **Hardware:** Experiments conducted on 16x16 TPUv5 chips, with each model consuming approximately 2.2K core-hours.
- **Scope:** 59 different models were trained across two sizes (300M and 600M parameters), including specific implementations of RAO and latent recurrent thinking. A 1B parameter model was used for specific analyses.
- **Evaluation:** Primary metric was C4 Log-Perplexity (PPL) and SlimPajama Log-PPL. Downstream zero-shot common sense reasoning tasks (OpenBookQA, BoolQ, PIQA, SIQA, HellaSwag, CommonSenseQA) were also used.

#### 3.5. Stochastic Recursive Inference Scaling

To address the trade-off between inference flexibility and performance, the authors introduced stochastic RINS. During training, a skip probability (`p_s`) is applied to the recursive block A, meaning the number of recursion rounds is sampled. This approach aims to make the model robust to varying inference-time computational budgets.
- **Linear Adapters:** To enhance the "no-regret" property, lightweight linear adapters (comprising <1% of model parameters) are added. The choice of adapter depends on the number of recursion rounds used at inference time.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup for Multimodal Systems

- **Model:** SigLIP-B/16 models (Zhai et al., 2023), which are contrastive vision-language models.
- **Training Protocol:** Images resized to 256x256 (16x16 patch sizes), texts tokenized using C4 (32K vocab, max 64 tokens). Similar Adam optimizer configuration.
- **Compute Matching:** Recursive models were trained on fewer examples to match the baseline's total training compute.
- **Architectures Compared:** Non-recursive baseline (AB), RAO (ABAB), and RINS (A^2B).
- **Evaluation:** Zero-shot classification (ImageNet, CIFAR100, Pet) and retrieval tasks (COCO, Flickr).
- **Overtraining and Cultural Diversity:** Additional experiments involved training SigLIP-RINS-B/16 (A^3B) on a larger dataset (40B examples) with a multilingual mixture, evaluating on cultural diversity metrics (Dollar Street, GeoDE, GLDv2) and multilinguality (XM3600).

#### 3.7. Further Analysis Techniques

- **Vision-Only Experiment:** To test the self-similarity hypothesis, encoder-only Vision Transformers (ViT-B/16) were trained on ImageNet-ILSRCV2012. This served as a control to see if RINS benefits extended to domains without hypothesized self-similarity.
- **Data Scaling Law Analysis:** Power laws of the form ε(x) = βx⁻ᶜ + ε∞ were fitted to the log-perplexity loss as a function of training FLOPs. This allowed for analyzing the impact of RINS on the scaling exponent (`c`) and the asymptotic performance limit (`ε∞`).
- **Optimal Recursion Rounds Study:** The optimal value of `r` in A^rB was plotted against training compute to understand its relationship with increasing computational budgets.
- **KV Cache Sharing:** The impact of sharing Key-Value (KV) cache during recursion to further reduce memory footprint was analyzed, assessing its effect on performance.

This comprehensive and controlled experimental methodology allows for robust claims about RINS's effectiveness and its underlying principles.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents a compelling body of evidence supporting the efficacy of Recursive Inference Scaling (RINS), particularly under compute-matched training regimes.

#### 4.1. RINS as the Superior Recursive Architecture

- **Outperformance:** RINS (with signature A^rB, degree 1) consistently and significantly outperforms over 55 other parameter-sharing variants, including the "repeat-all-over" (RAO) strategy from MobileLLM and latent recurrent thinking, when compared on a compute-matched basis for language modeling tasks.
- **Comparison to Long Context:** RINS also yields better language modeling performance than simply scaling inference by increasing the context length (e.g., from 1,024 to 1,536 tokens) while maintaining the same training FLOPs. This indicates that intelligent recursion is more effective than just providing more input context.
- **Widening Performance Gap:** The performance advantage of RINS (lower log-perplexity) grows larger with increased training compute budget. As models are trained longer (e.g., from 200B to 500B tokens for 600M models), RINS's lead over baselines and RAO continues to expand, suggesting its benefits become more pronounced in well-trained models. This improvement also translates to downstream zero-shot common sense reasoning tasks (e.g., OpenBookQA, BoolQ, HellaSwag), where RINS models consistently achieve higher accuracy.

#### 4.2. Generalization to Multimodal Systems

- **Significant Multimodal Gains:** RINS successfully generalizes beyond pure language tasks to multimodal systems. In SigLIP-B/16, a contrastive vision-language model, RINS (signature A^2B) significantly improves zero-shot classification accuracy on ImageNet (e.g., from 73.4% to 74.1%) and CIFAR100 (from 68.9% to 72.2%), as well as enhancing retrieval performance on COCO and Flickr datasets.
- **Advancing State-of-the-Art in Overtraining Regimes:** When SigLIP-RINS-B/16 (A^3B) is trained on a larger dataset (40B examples, matching a public SigLIP checkpoint), it substantially outperforms the standard SigLIP-B/16 across all evaluated benchmarks, including ImageNet 0-shot classification (from 77.3% to 79.0-79.6%) and cultural diversity/multilinguality metrics. This demonstrates RINS's ability to push state-of-the-art for a given model size in an overtraining scenario.

#### 4.3. Stochastic RINS: The "No-Regret" Strategy

- **Inference Flexibility:** Stochastic RINS, where recursive blocks are stochastically dropped during training (controlled by `p_s`), allows for flexibility in inference compute. A higher `p_s` reduces the performance degradation when RINS is not applied at test time (RINS@1x).
- **Linear Adapters Enable No-Regret:** Crucially, when combined with lightweight linear adapters (<1% of parameters), stochastic RINS offers a "no-regret" strategy. This means that models pretrained with RINS and adapters *improve* performance in language modeling even when recursive depth is *not* applied at inference time (i.e., at 1x inference cost relative to baseline). This makes RINS-enabled pretraining beneficial regardless of whether the extra inference compute is later utilized.

#### 4.4. Impact on Data Scaling Laws

- **Improved Asymptotics and Exponents:** RINS improves *both* the asymptotic performance limits (`ε∞`) and the scaling exponents (`c`) when log-perplexity is modeled as a power law of training FLOPs. This indicates that RINS not only achieves better ultimate performance but also converges faster, providing a more efficient learning trajectory. This finding directly counters the possibility that RINS's gains could be matched simply by overtraining non-recursive models.

#### 4.5. Optimal Recursion Rounds and Model Size

- **Compute-Dependent Optimality:** The optimal number of recursion rounds (`r`) in RINS (A^rB) monotonically increases with the total training compute. This suggests that as more training resources become available, higher recursive depths become more advantageous.
- **Greater Benefit for Smaller Models:** Smaller models (e.g., 300M parameters) appear to benefit *more* from RINS than larger ones, indicating its particular utility for compact model development and deployment.

#### 4.6. KV Cache Sharing and Hypothesis Validation

- **Memory Efficiency with Retained Gain:** While sharing the KV cache during recursion (to reduce memory footprint) slightly diminishes the performance gains of RINS, it still maintains an advantage over the non-recursive baseline, indicating it remains a viable strategy for memory-constrained scenarios.
- **Support for Self-Similarity Hypothesis:** The authors' hypothesis, linking RINS's success to the fractal nature of language, is strongly supported by an experiment in the vision domain. In supervised image classification (using ViT on ImageNet), recursive architectures, including RINS, offer *no advantage* over the non-recursive baseline. This contrasting result suggests that the benefits of RINS are indeed tied to specific properties of the data domain, such as the self-similarity hypothesized for language.

In summary, the results unequivocally establish RINS as a highly effective and robust method for improving neural network performance through intelligent inference scaling, offering both empirical superiority and theoretical insights into the nature of language processing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Recursive Inference Scaling (RINS) holds significant implications for the future of large language models and multimodal AI, promising advancements in performance, efficiency, and fundamental understanding.

#### 5.1. A New Dimension for Scaling AI Performance

RINS proposes and validates a crucial, yet previously underexplored, dimension for scaling AI: **inference compute through intrinsic model recursion**. While traditional scaling laws focus on model size, data quantity, and training compute, RINS demonstrates that strategically increasing computational effort *within* the model's forward pass at inference time can yield substantial performance gains. This complements existing techniques like Chain-of-Thought prompting, offering an architectural solution that can potentially be combined with other inference-time methods for even greater impact.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Efficiency and Resource Optimization

One of the most profound impacts of RINS is its ability to deliver significant performance improvements **without increasing the model's parameter count or the total training compute budget**. This is a game-changer for several reasons:
- **Resource Constrained Environments:** For on-device deployment, mobile LLMs, or scenarios with stringent memory and computational constraints, RINS provides a path to higher accuracy without needing larger models or more expensive pretraining. This makes powerful AI more accessible and deployable.
- **Cost-Effectiveness:** By achieving better performance with the same training budget and model size, RINS offers a more cost-effective way to develop and deploy advanced AI systems. It implies that for a given investment, better models can be obtained.

#### 5.3. Practicality and "No-Regret" Adoption

The "plug-in" nature of RINS, especially its stochastic variant combined with lightweight linear adapters, makes it highly practical. It can be readily integrated into existing pretraining pipelines. More importantly, the demonstration of a "no-regret" strategy is a critical contribution. This means that enabling RINS during pretraining *improves* the model's performance even if recursive inference is *not* utilized at test time (i.e., at the baseline inference cost). This eliminates a major hurdle for adoption, as developers can pretrain RINS-enabled models without committing to higher inference costs for all deployment scenarios, effectively getting an "upgrade for free" in baseline performance and an "even bigger upgrade" if they choose to scale inference.

#### 5.4. Deeper Theoretical Understanding of Language

The success of RINS, particularly its contrasting performance in vision tasks, strongly supports the hypothesis that language possesses a "fractal" or "self-similar" geometry. This is a significant step towards a more fundamental understanding of why certain architectural patterns work well for language. It suggests that designing models to inherently recognize and process recurring patterns at different scales (scale-invariant decoding) is a powerful inductive bias. This insight could inspire future architectural designs that are more aligned with the intrinsic properties of the data they process.

#### 5.5. Advancing Multimodal AI Capabilities

By demonstrating that RINS can substantially improve vision-language models like SigLIP, the research expands its potential impact beyond pure language. The ability to enhance zero-shot classification, retrieval, and even cultural diversity metrics in multimodal settings suggests RINS could be a valuable component for a wide range of applications involving integrated language and vision understanding. The performance gains in "overtraining" regimes further show its capacity to push the state-of-the-art for a given model size.

#### 5.6. Re-evaluation of Architectural Design and Scaling Laws

RINS challenges the simplistic notion that "bigger is always better" or that gains only come from more training compute. It emphasizes that *how* compute is utilized, particularly at inference time through intelligent architectural choices, can significantly alter scaling behaviors. By improving both scaling exponents and asymptotic performance limits, RINS suggests that architectural innovation can fundamentally make models learn more efficiently and reach higher ultimate performance ceilings. This calls for renewed focus on architectural design in the era of large models, moving beyond simple layer stacking to more nuanced and data-aligned structures.

In conclusion, RINS offers a powerful, efficient, and theoretically grounded approach to building more capable and adaptable AI systems. Its potential to enhance performance without increasing model size or training cost, coupled with its "no-regret" nature and applicability across language and multimodal domains, positions it as a significant advancement in the pursuit of scalable and deployable artificial intelligence.
