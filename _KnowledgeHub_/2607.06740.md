# A Continual Learning Framework for Adaptive Control of Modular Soft Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> The Soft Modular Progressive Learning (SMPL) framework presents a continual learning method for modular soft robots, allowing controllers to adapt to changes in robot morphology and supporting distributed control. It exhibited lower trajectory tracking errors compared to benchmark methods in both simulated and real-world experiments, while also mitigating catastrophic forgetting and enabling adaptive module activation.

## Problem

- Learning-based controllers for Modular Soft Robots (MSRs) typically require complete retraining when the robot's morphology changes, hindering practical adaptability and efficiency.
- Many existing MSR control methods rely on a single, centralized controller, which limits modularity, scalability, and increases the potential for system-wide error propagation.
- Designing effective controllers for MSRs is challenging due to their hyper-redundancy, nonlinear material properties, complex dynamics, and difficulties in accurate modeling.

## Method

- Introduces the **Soft Modular Progressive Learning (SMPL)** framework, which utilizes a ==Progressive Neural Network (PNN)== architecture to incrementally learn control policies and mitigate ==catastrophic forgetting==.
- Integrates the PNN as an inverse model within a closed-loop control system, where an ==LSTM==-based forward dynamics model provides real-time error feedback to enhance stability and accuracy.
- Employs a supervised training strategy based on 'motor babbling' data, adapted for both incremental morphological changes (Exp1S) and distributed control of fixed-morphology MSRs (Exp2S, Exp2R).

## Results

- SMPL successfully learned control for MSRs with **1 to 5** modules, demonstrating mitigation of catastrophic forgetting and maintaining accurate trajectory tracking capabilities for all configurations.
- In distributed control scenarios, SMPL achieved the lowest average position and orientation errors, outperforming Bi-LSTM, VAE-LSTM, standard LSTM, and MLP in both simulation (Table 2) and real-world experiments (Table 3).
- An adaptive module activation experiment showed that selectively activating modules incrementally reduced task-space error, indicating potential for improved computational and energy efficiency by engaging only necessary components.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Progressive Neural Networks effectively enable continual learning in soft robot control, allowing adaptation to new robot configurations while explicitly preserving knowledge from previously learned morphologies.
> - Distributed control, implemented through dedicated sub-networks for each module, enhances modularity, scalability, and robustness by localizing control and error management.
> - Incorporating closed-loop error feedback into the control architecture significantly improves trajectory tracking accuracy and overall system stability for both simulated and real-world soft robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Soft robots, inspired by biological organisms, are characterized by their inherent compliance, flexibility, and numerous degrees of freedom. These properties enable them to operate in unstructured environments and interact safely with their surroundings, leading to applications in medical interventions, rehabilitation, wearable technologies, and manipulation. Modular soft robots (MSRs) represent a class of these systems composed of interconnected segments, offering reconfigurability and enhanced adaptability to diverse task requirements. MSRs typically possess a higher number of active degrees of freedom compared to single-module robots, facilitating access to larger workspaces and the execution of complex tasks.

Despite their advantages, designing effective controllers for MSRs presents significant challenges. These arise from their hyper-redundancy, nonlinear material properties, complex dynamics, and the difficulty in accurately modeling their behavior. Traditional control strategies for soft robots are broadly categorized into model-based and model-free approaches. While model-based methods can offer faster convergence, their performance often degrades in unpredictable environments or when system dynamics change. The nonlinear and hysteretic behavior of soft materials, combined with the high dimensionality of modular configurations, further complicates model-based control.

To address these modeling limitations, learning-based approaches have been increasingly explored to capture the kinematics and dynamics of soft robots. Methods such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, bi-directional LSTMs, echo state networks, and reinforcement learning (RL) have shown promise in handling time-dependent and nonlinear behaviors. However, a significant limitation of most existing learning-based controllers for MSRs is the assumption of a fixed robot morphology during training. Consequently, these controllers typically require complete retraining from scratch whenever the robot's configuration changes due, for instance, to the addition or removal of modules. This necessity hinders the practical deployability and adaptability of MSRs.

Furthermore, for MSRs with a fixed morphology, many current methods rely on a single, centralized controller trained across all modules. This centralized paradigm imposes limitations on modularity and scalability, particularly for robots with a large number of modules. It also increases the susceptibility to error propagation across the entire system.

This work addresses these identified gaps by proposing a Continual Learning (CL) framework. CL, also known as lifelong learning, specifically tackles the challenge of incrementally integrating new knowledge from non-stationary data while explicitly preserving previously acquired information, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting. By leveraging CL, specifically a Progressive Neural Network (PNN) architecture, this research aims to develop MSR controllers that can adapt to morphological changes and facilitate distributed control, thus advancing the state of the art in adaptive and scalable control for modular soft robotic systems. The work builds upon prior research in PNNs for various learning tasks and in data-driven control strategies for soft robots, extending these concepts to address the specific challenges of MSR reconfigurability and distributed control.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to develop and validate a continual learning (CL)-based adaptive control framework for modular soft robots (MSRs) that addresses two main challenges identified in the existing literature:

1. **Adaptive Control for Evolving MSR Morphology:**
 - **Objective:** To enable a controller to incrementally adapt to changes in robot morphology, such as the attachment or detachment of modules, without requiring complete retraining from scratch. The framework should preserve knowledge acquired from previously learned MSR configurations while integrating information from new configurations.
 - **Motivation:** Existing MSR control approaches typically assume a fixed robot morphology. When the robot's structure changes (e.g., a module is added or removed to suit a new task), the controller must often be retrained entirely. This process is computationally expensive, time-consuming, and limits the practical utility and flexibility of reconfigurable MSRs. An effective MSR controller needs to dynamically adjust to structural variations, efficiently incorporating new data while retaining functional capabilities for previous configurations.

2. **Distributed Control for Fixed-Morphology MSRs:**
 - **Objective:** To employ the same CL framework in a distributed manner for MSRs with a fixed number of modules, where each module is controlled by a dedicated sub-network. This aims to achieve localized, module-specific control.
 - **Motivation:** Many current methods for fixed-size MSRs rely on a single, centralized controller that manages all modules. This approach often limits the inherent modularity of MSRs, reduces scalability as the number of modules increases, and can lead to error propagation across the entire system. A distributed control strategy, where each module's dynamics are learned and controlled locally, offers several advantages:
 - **Improved Precision and Interpretability:** By focusing on individual module dynamics, control can be made more precise.
 - **Enhanced Robustness:** In scenarios involving degradation or failure of a specific actuator or module (e.g., a puncture), only the corresponding module-specific sub-network would require retraining or adjustment, rather than the entire centralized system.
 - **Increased Scalability:** The modular nature of the controller would facilitate easier expansion to MSRs with a larger number of modules.
 - **Reduced Computational Overhead:** Selective activation of modules can lead to more efficient control and reduced computational load, as demonstrated in scenarios where only necessary modules are engaged for a task.

To further enhance performance and ensure stability across all experimental settings, a critical motivation is the integration of the proposed control framework within a **closed-loop configuration**. This involves feeding the trajectory error (the difference between the target trajectory and the trajectory predicted by a forward dynamics model) back into the inverse model. This feedback mechanism is designed to reduce task-space error and improve the robustness of the overall control architecture during both training and inference. Both forward and inverse models are motivated to be trained in a supervised manner using motor babbling data to capture the complex, nonlinear dynamics of soft robots empirically.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces the **Soft Modular Progressive Learning (SMPL)** framework, a continual learning (CL)-based adaptive control system designed for modular soft robots (MSRs). The framework leverages a Progressive Neural Network (PNN) architecture to address both incremental morphological changes and distributed control for fixed-morphology MSRs.

**A. Progressive Neural Network (PNN) Architecture:**
The core of SMPL is an adaptation of the PNN architecture, which intrinsically mitigates catastrophic forgetting—the degradation of performance on previously learned tasks after training on new ones.
- **Structural Expansion:** When a new MSR configuration (Exp1S) or a new module (Exp2S, Exp2R) is introduced, an additional sub-network is instantiated.
- **Knowledge Preservation:** The parameters of previously trained sub-networks are frozen to prevent overwriting learned knowledge.
- **Knowledge Transfer:** The hidden layers of the newly added sub-network receive inputs not only from their preceding layers within the same sub-network but also from the corresponding layers of all earlier sub-networks via lateral connections. These lateral connections are trainable and facilitate the transfer of relevant features and representations from prior knowledge to new learning tasks.

**B. Closed-Loop Control Architecture:**
The SMPL framework operates within a closed-loop control system (Figure 3), which integrates an inverse model (the CL-based PNN) and a forward dynamics model.
- **Inverse Model:** The SMPL PNN serves as the inverse model, mapping from the robot's task space (end-effector pose: position and orientation) to its actuation space (actuation commands). Inputs to the inverse model are the target pose (x<sub>t+1</sub>), the current task-space error (ϵ<sub>t</sub>), and the previous actuation (τ<sub>t-1</sub>). The output is the current actuation (τ<sub>t</sub>).
- **Forward Dynamics Model:** An LSTM-based architecture is employed as the forward model. It is trained to approximate the robot's forward dynamics, mapping from actuation inputs to predicted poses. For Exp1S, a separate forward model is trained for each MSR configuration. For Exp2S and Exp2R, individual forward models are trained for each module, capturing module-specific dynamics and considering inter-module coupling (actuation signals of preceding modules are fed into subsequent module models). The forward model is pretrained and its parameters are fixed during the training and evaluation of the inverse model.
- **Error Feedback:** The actuation predicted by the SMPL inverse model is passed through the fixed forward dynamics model to predict the robot's resulting pose. The error between this predicted pose and the target pose (ϵ<sub>t</sub>) is then fed back as an additional input to the inverse model. This closed-loop mechanism is designed to enhance stability and reduce task-space errors.

**C. SMPL Training Strategy:**
The training procedure is supervised and relies on "motor babbling" data.
- **Exp1S (Morphology Adaptation):** Training starts with a single sub-network for a single-module MSR. As new modules are attached, new sub-networks are progressively added, and lateral connections are established. Each new sub-network is trained on data corresponding to the current MSR configuration (e.g., a two-module sub-network is trained on two-module data), while parameters of prior sub-networks remain frozen. The outputs of the sub-networks are actuation commands for all modules in the current configuration.
- **Exp2S & Exp2R (Distributed Control):** For MSRs with fixed morphology (e.g., three modules), each sub-network is dedicated to learning the control policy for an individual module. Each sub-network is trained independently using motor babbling data specific to its corresponding module, producing localized and module-specific actuation commands. Lateral connections still enable knowledge transfer across module sub-networks.
- **Network Details:** Each column (sub-network) of the PNN consists of two LSTM layers with 32 hidden units, followed by a linear output layer. A sequence length of 15 is used for LSTM inputs. The network is trained using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻³, optimized for 800 epochs.

**D. Experimental Setup and Data Collection:**
- **Simulation Environment (Exp1S, Exp2S):** A cable-driven soft robot modeled using the PyElastica framework (based on Cosserat rod theory) is used. Modules are 50 mm long, with four cable-driven actuators per module. For Exp1S, experiments involve MSRs with 1 to 5 modules. For Exp2S, a fixed three-module configuration is used.
- **Real-World Robot (Exp2R):** A three-module pneumatic soft robotic arm is used. The proximal module has four linear bellow actuators, while subsequent modules have three each. Actuators are 80 mm long, fabricated from silicon (30A shore hardness), capable of 120% elongation and 70% contraction.
- **Data Collection:** 14,000 motor babbling samples are generated through random actuation to explore the workspace. An additional 1,000 samples each are collected for predefined circular, rectangular, and spiral trajectories. Motion capture (OptiTrack PrimeX13) tracks reflective markers on the real robot to record module poses (position and orientation). All actuation and pose values are normalized to the range [−1, 1].

**E. Evaluation Metrics and Benchmarking:**
- **Metrics:**
 - **Position Error:** L2-norm between target and predicted positions (mm).
 - **Orientation Error:** Measured in degrees using the arccosine of the dot product of normalized predicted and target orientation vectors.
 - **Normalized Tip Error (Exp1S):** Ratio of end-effector position error to the total MSR length (%).
 - Errors for Exp2S and Exp2R are averaged across all three modules.
- **Benchmarking:** The proposed SMPL framework is compared against four baseline models, all trained in a closed-loop configuration:
 1. Bi-directional LSTM (as in)
 2. Standard LSTM (as in)
 3. Variational Autoencoder (VAE)-LSTM (as in)
 4. Three-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)
- **Procedure:** Models are evaluated on a 20% test subset of babbling data and on the three custom trajectories. Each algorithm is run over five independent trials with different random seeds, and mean and standard deviation are reported. Hyperparameters are optimized using the Optuna framework.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents findings from three experimental categories: Exp1S (simulation, morphological adaptation), Exp2S (simulation, distributed control for fixed morphology), and Exp2R (real robot, distributed control for fixed morphology), complemented by an adaptive module activation experiment.

**A. Trajectory Tracking Experiment (Exp1S: Morphological Adaptation)**
- **Incremental Learning and Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation:** The SMPL framework demonstrated the ability to incrementally learn control for MSR configurations ranging from one to five modules. Following sequential training on the five-module setup, the framework successfully retained accurate trajectory tracking capabilities for all previously encountered configurations (Figure 4). The normalized tip error (E<sub>L</sub><sup>tip</sup>), measured after each training stage on the "test babbling" dataset, indicated that the model acquired control over new configurations while preserving performance on learned ones, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting (Figure 5).
- **Precision Across Configurations:** Table 1 summarizes the position and orientation errors across various trajectories (Test Babbling, Circle, Rectangle, Spiral) for all five MSR configurations after incremental learning. The SMPL framework maintained precision, with errors remaining relatively low even for more complex configurations, although minor deviations increased with robot size and complexity.
- **Advantage of Closed-Loop Control:** Consistently across all configurations and trajectories, closed-loop training yielded lower position and orientation errors compared to open-loop control (Table 1). This finding reinforces the benefit of integrating error feedback into the control loop for robustness and accuracy.

**B. Trajectory Tracking Experiment (Exp2S: Distributed Control in Simulation)**
- **Superior Performance:** In the Exp2S setting, where the framework controlled a fixed three-module MSR in a distributed manner (each sub-network controlling a specific module), SMPL achieved the lowest average position and orientation errors across all evaluated trajectories (Table 2).
- **Benchmark Comparison:** SMPL outperformed all benchmark methods: Bi-LSTM, VAE-LSTM, standard LSTM, and MLP. The MLP baseline exhibited the highest errors due to its inability to effectively model temporal dependencies, while temporal neural networks (LSTMs, VAE-LSTM, Bi-LSTM) performed better, but still below SMPL. This superior performance is attributed to SMPL's distributed architecture, which enables localized control and better exploitation of module-specific dynamics.

**C. Trajectory Tracking Experiment (Exp2R: Distributed Control on Real Robot)**
- **Real-World Validation:** The real-world experiments on a three-module pneumatic soft robotic arm (Exp2R) corroborated the simulation findings. SMPL demonstrated the lowest position and orientation errors among all compared methods across all trajectories (Table 3).
- **Accurate Trajectory Following:** Figure 7 illustrates that the predicted trajectories closely followed the target trajectories along the X, Y, and Z axes for all three modules. Minor deviations were observed at the initial time step, attributed to the initialization of previous actuation and pose error to zero, which are subsequently updated.
- **Robustness to Physical Constraints:** For complex trajectories like the rectangular shape, SMPL maintained good tracking accuracy (Figure 8, left). While slight deviations from an ideal rectangular shape were observed for both target and predicted trajectories, these were attributed to inherent structural constraints and asymmetric actuator distribution in the physical robot. The overall position error remained below 6 mm, with occasional peaks potentially due to mechanical vibrations (Figure 8, right).
- **Closed-Loop Benefits on Real Robot:** Similar to simulation, closed-loop control significantly reduced pose error for all three modules compared to open-loop control in the real-world setup (Figure 6).

**D. Adaptive Module Activation Experiment**
- **Selective Module Engagement:** This experiment showcased the adaptive and distributed capabilities of SMPL. The task was to reach a virtual target point in space using the second module, with the option to selectively activate other modules if needed.
- **Error Reduction through Sequential Activation:** Initially, with only the second module active, the reach error was relatively high (Figure 9, left). Activating the preceding module (first module) substantially improved performance, allowing the robot to approach the target more closely. Subsequent activation of the third module further reduced the error, albeit to a smaller extent.
- **Computational Efficiency Potential:** This demonstrates the framework's ability to dynamically activate only the necessary modules, implying potential benefits in scenarios with limited computational or power resources by engaging only relevant pressure valves for a given task.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The continual learning framework proposed in this research, Soft Modular Progressive Learning (SMPL), contributes to the field of soft robotics by addressing critical challenges in the control of Modular Soft Robots (MSRs).

**Enhanced Adaptability to Morphological Changes:**
A key significance of this work is its capability to enable MSR controllers to adapt incrementally to changes in robot morphology. By leveraging a Progressive Neural Network (PNN) architecture, the SMPL framework allows for the addition or removal of modules without necessitating a complete retraining of the controller from scratch. This ability to integrate new knowledge while preserving previously acquired control policies directly addresses the significant limitation of catastrophic forgetting prevalent in traditional learning-based methods. This improved adaptability is crucial for MSRs designed for reconfigurability, allowing them to dynamically adjust to new task requirements or environmental conditions efficiently.

**Improved Modularity, Scalability, and Robustness through Distributed Control:**
The research demonstrates the effectiveness of SMPL in a distributed control paradigm for MSRs with fixed morphology. By assigning dedicated sub-networks to control individual modules, the framework enables localized learning of module-specific dynamics. This approach yields several benefits:
- **Increased Precision and Interpretability:** Localized control can lead to finer control over individual modules and a more interpretable understanding of their behaviors.
- **Enhanced Scalability:** As MSRs grow in complexity with more modules, a distributed architecture inherently offers better scalability compared to a single, centralized controller, which can become unwieldy.
- **Improved Robustness:** In the event of localized actuator degradation or failure within a specific module (e.g., a puncture in a pneumatic actuator), only the corresponding sub-network would require retraining or adjustment. This localized repair mechanism significantly enhances the overall robustness and operational uptime of the robotic system, avoiding the need for full system recalibration.

**Demonstrated Efficiency through Adaptive Module Activation:**
The adaptive module activation experiment highlights a practical impact: the potential for improved computational and energy efficiency. By enabling the controller to selectively activate only the modules necessary to achieve a specific task (e.g., reaching a target position), the framework can reduce computational overhead and energy consumption. This capability is particularly relevant for autonomous soft robots operating with limited onboard resources, extending their operational duration and improving their responsiveness.

**Validation Across Multiple Domains:**
The rigorous validation of the SMPL framework in both simulation (tendon-driven MSR) and on a real-world pneumatic soft robotic arm demonstrates its generalizability and practical applicability. The consistent outperformance against established benchmark algorithms across various trajectory tracking tasks in both simulated and real environments lends credibility to its potential for deployment in real-world scenarios.

**Foundational for Future Research:**
This work lays a foundation for future advancements in adaptive soft robot control. The authors indicate future directions, such as integrating meta-learning for faster adaptation to new modules and exploring the framework's robustness to actuator degradation through selective retraining of affected sub-networks. Expanding to actuator-level modular control also suggests potential for even finer control and further optimized energy efficiency.

In summary, this research offers a substantial step towards more autonomous, adaptable, and robust modular soft robots. By overcoming limitations related to morphological changes and centralized control, the SMPL framework advances the design of intelligent controllers for complex soft robotic systems, with potential implications for a wide range of applications, including advanced manufacturing, medical devices, and exploration in complex environments.
