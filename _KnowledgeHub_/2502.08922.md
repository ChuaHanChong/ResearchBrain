# Self-Consistency of the Internal Reward Models Improves Self-Rewarding Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The Self-Consistent Internal Rewards (SCIR) framework improves Large Language Model alignment by enforcing consistency between the model's generative and implicit reward mechanisms. This approach leads to more reliable preference judgments and demonstrates superior performance, including a 14% improvement in length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0 and higher MT-Bench scores.

## Problem

- Ensuring LLM outputs align with human preferences traditionally requires expensive, time-consuming human-annotated preference data.
- Self-Rewarding Language Models (SRLMs) mitigate this by using the model itself to generate preference judgments, but different internal reward models often produce inconsistent evaluations.
- This internal inconsistency leads to unreliable self-generated training data, hindering the overall alignment performance of SRLMs.

## Method

- The ==Self-Consistent Internal Rewards (SCIR)== framework enforces consistency between an LLM's generative and implicit internal reward models.
- It employs =="Consistency Training"== to penalize disagreements between these internal models and =="Dynamic Consistency Preference Optimization (DCPO)"== to select only consistently judged preference data for training.
- The iterative process refines the model by generating response pairs, evaluating them, and optimizing with a combination of ==consistency and preference optimization losses==.

## Results

- SCIR significantly increases the consistency rates between internal reward models compared to standard SRLM approaches, improving across training iterations.
- The framework improves the model's reward modeling accuracy on benchmarks like RewardBench and demonstrates superior overall performance on alignment benchmarks.
- Achieved a **14%** improvement in length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0 and higher MT-Bench scores, consistently outperforming various baseline methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A fundamental limitation in Self-Rewarding Language Models (SRLMs) is the inconsistency between different internal reward mechanisms (e.g., explicit LLM-as-a-Judge and implicit DPO-derived rewards).
> - Improving the self-consistency of these internal reward models directly translates to more reliable self-generated preference data for training.
> - Explicitly encouraging consistency through training and dynamically selecting highly consistent preference pairs can significantly enhance LLM alignment with human preferences without reliance on new human annotations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have become increasingly capable, but ensuring they produce outputs aligned with human preferences remains challenging. Self-Rewarding Language Models (SRLMs) have emerged as a promising approach to reduce dependency on human-annotated preference data by leveraging the model itself to evaluate and improve its outputs. However, these self-rewarding approaches face a critical limitation: inconsistency between different internal reward models.

The research paper "Self-Consistency of the Internal Reward Models Improves Self-Rewarding Language Models" by Zhou and colleagues from Fudan University addresses this challenge by introducing a novel framework called Self-Consistent Internal Rewards (SCIR). This framework aims to improve the alignment of LLMs by enforcing consistency between different types of internal reward models, leading to more reliable preference judgments and ultimately better performance.

#### 1.2. Background and Context

To understand the significance of this work, we need to consider the context of LLM alignment research:

1. **Alignment Methods**: Traditional approaches like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) require substantial human-annotated preference data, which is expensive and time-consuming to gather.

2. **Self-Rewarding Models**: SRLMs attempt to mitigate this issue by using the LLM itself to generate preference judgments between different outputs, thereby creating self-supervised training data.

3. **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)**: This algorithm has become popular for fine-tuning LLMs based on preference data without explicitly modeling rewards, offering a more stable optimization approach.

4. **Internal Reward Models**: Within an LLM, different methods can be used to evaluate and compare responses:
 - The LLM-as-a-Judge approach (generative reward model)
 - Reward signals derived from the DPO training objective (implicit reward model)

The key insight of this paper is that these different internal reward models often produce inconsistent preference judgments, undermining the quality of self-generated preference data and limiting the overall alignment performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper identifies a fundamental inconsistency issue in SRLMs. When asked to evaluate the quality of different responses, the same LLM may produce contradictory judgments depending on how the evaluation is conducted:

1. **Generative Reward Model (GRM)**: When explicitly prompted to evaluate responses (LLM-as-a-Judge approach), the model might prefer Response A over Response B.

2. **Implicit Reward Model (IRM)**: When evaluating based on the DPO-derived reward function, the same model might prefer Response B over Response A.

This inconsistency creates unreliable training signals for the model during self-improvement. The authors hypothesize that improving the consistency between these internal reward models would lead to more reliable preference data and better alignment with human preferences.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. The SCIR Framework

The Self-Consistent Internal Rewards (SCIR) framework addresses this inconsistency problem through a well-structured approach. The framework consists of two main components:

1. **Consistency Training**: This encourages agreement between different internal reward models by applying an inconsistency penalty.

2. **Dynamic Consistency Preference Optimization (DCPO)**: This selectively uses preference data where internal reward models show consistent judgments for training.

The training process begins with unlabeled prompts. The LLM generates multiple responses for each prompt, creating potential preference pairs. These pairs are then evaluated by both internal reward models. The framework applies consistency training to improve agreement between these models and uses DCPO to select high-quality preference data for subsequent training iterations.

#### 3.2. Methodology

The SCIR methodology follows an iterative process:

1. **Generating Unlabeled Preference Pairs**: From a prompt pool, the model at iteration t (Mt) generates response pairs for each prompt.

2. **Self-Consistent Internal Rewards Training**:
 - The model evaluates these response pairs using both the Generative Reward Model (GRM) and Implicit Reward Model (IRM)
 - Consistency training applies penalties to reduce disagreement between the models
 - DCPO selects preference pairs with consistent judgments for training
 - The model is optimized using both consistency loss and preference optimization loss

3. **Iterative Refinement**: This process repeats over multiple iterations, with each iteration using the improved model from the previous step.

The mathematical framework for consistency training uses symmetrical Kullback-Leibler divergence with entropy regularization:

```
L_consistency = λ_KL * (KL(p_IRM || p_GRM) + KL(p_GRM || p_IRM)) + λ_ENT * (H(p_IRM) + H(p_GRM))
```

Where:
- p_IRM and p_GRM are the preference probabilities from each reward model
- KL is the Kullback-Leibler divergence
- H is the entropy function
- λ_KL and λ_ENT are balancing hyperparameters

#### 3.3. Key Components

The SCIR framework includes several important components to address specific challenges:

1. **Bias Mitigation Techniques**:
 - **Prompt and Position Bias Mitigation**: Using multiple judge prompt templates and alternating positions of responses to reduce position sensitivity.
 - **Length Bias Mitigation**: Adding length regularization to the reward models' prediction logits to prevent favoring longer responses.

2. **Adaptive Reference Model**: The implicit reward model uses an adaptive reference model that evolves during training, enhancing consistency with the generative reward model.

3. **Confidence-based Masking**: The consistency loss is only applied to preference pairs where at least one reward model shows high confidence, focusing training on the most informative examples.

4. **Dynamic Consistency Selection**: Only preference pairs with consistent predictions across all internal reward models are used for preference optimization, ensuring high-quality training data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of the SCIR framework:

The results show that SCIR significantly improves:

1. **Internal Reward Model Consistency**: As shown in the figure, models trained with SCIR (solid lines) show much higher consistency rates between internal reward models compared to standard SRLM approaches (dashed lines), with consistency rates improving across training iterations (M1 to M3).

2. **Reward Modeling Accuracy**: SCIR improves the model's ability to accurately predict human preferences, as measured on benchmarks like RewardBench.

3. **Overall Performance**: Models trained with SCIR demonstrate superior performance on alignment benchmarks:
 - 14% improvement in length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0
 - Higher MT-Bench scores
 - Better performance on various evaluation benchmarks

4. **Ablation Studies**: The authors conducted comprehensive ablation studies confirming that each component of SCIR (consistency training, DCPO, bias mitigation, adaptive reference model) contributes positively to the overall performance.

5. **Comparison to Baselines**: SCIR consistently outperforms several baseline approaches, including traditional Self-Rewarding methods using either LLM-as-a-Judge or Implicit Reward Models alone, and even models trained with external reward models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Broader Significance

The significance of this research extends beyond the immediate technical results:

1. **Identifying a Critical Problem**: The paper highlights internal reward model inconsistency as a fundamental limitation in self-rewarding approaches, a problem that was previously underexplored.

2. **Practical Solution**: SCIR provides a practical framework to address this inconsistency without requiring additional human annotation, making it scalable and efficient.

3. **Reduced Human Annotation Dependency**: By improving self-rewarding approaches, SCIR reduces the need for expensive human preference data while achieving better alignment.

4. **Broader Implications**: The principles of consistency training could potentially be applied to other aspects of LLM development beyond preference learning.

5. **Advancement of Self-Improving AI**: The research contributes to developing AI systems that can reliably evaluate and improve their own outputs, a step toward more autonomous AI improvement.

#### 5.2. Conclusion

The SCIR framework represents a significant advancement in the development of Self-Rewarding Language Models. By addressing the critical issue of internal reward model inconsistency, it enables more reliable self-evaluation and consequently better alignment with human preferences.

The approach is both theoretically grounded and empirically effective, demonstrating substantial improvements across various benchmarks. The consistency training and dynamic consistency preference optimization components provide a solid foundation for developing more reliable self-improving AI systems.

This research highlights that effective self-rewarding models need not only good reward signals but also consistent ones. The consistency between different internal evaluation mechanisms proves to be just as important as the quality of each individual evaluator. As language models continue to evolve, ensuring self-consistency in how they evaluate their own outputs will likely remain a crucial aspect of developing aligned AI systems.
