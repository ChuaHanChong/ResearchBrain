# Symmetry in language statistics shapes the geometry of model representations

> [!summary] Summary
> A theoretical framework demonstrates that translation symmetry in pairwise word co-occurrence statistics determines the geometric structures in language model representations. This principle accounts for phenomena like circular embeddings for cyclical concepts and robust, rippled 1D manifolds, consistently observed across various model architectures.

## Problem

- A clear, unifying theoretical explanation for widely observed geometric patterns (e.g., circles, 1D manifolds) in language model representations has been largely absent.
- The lack of an overarching "organizing principle" to explain the apparent universality of these geometric structures across diverse LLM architectures and tasks.
- Prior theoretical work did not adequately address how data distribution and unsupervised learning settings drive these specific representational geometries.

## Method

- Proposed that ==translation symmetry== within ==pairwise word co-occurrence statistics== serves as the universal organizing principle for representational geometry.
- Developed a mathematical theory to analytically derive specific ==manifold geometries== (e.g., ==circles==, ==rippled 1D manifolds==) from this symmetry.
- Empirically validated these theoretical predictions using both simple ==word embedding models== and complex ==transformer-based large language models==.

## Results

- Analytically derived and empirically confirmed that translation symmetry in co-occurrence statistics leads to circular representations for periodic concepts (e.g., months) and rippled 1D manifolds for continuous sequences (e.g., years).
- Demonstrated that linear decoding error for spatiotemporal coordinates scales predictably with data statistics, validated across both simple and complex, deep-learning models.
- Representational geometry proved robust to perturbation: removing all direct month-month co-occurrences still allowed circular structure to be reconstructed via indirect influence from seasonal "helper" words.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Translation symmetry in pairwise word co-occurrence statistics is the causal mechanism shaping the geometric structures (e.g., circles, 1D manifolds) in language model representations.
> - The robustness of these geometric representations stems from a "collective phenomenon," where latent variables broadly influence co-occurrence statistics across many words.
> - These symmetry-driven principles apply universally, explaining geometric patterns in both simple word embeddings and complex, deep transformer models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has witnessed a surge in empirical observations of fascinating geometric structures within their internal representations. Researchers have consistently found that concepts like calendar months organize into circles, historical years form smooth one-dimensional manifolds with "ripples," and geographical coordinates can be linearly decoded from representations. Examples include observations of prisms (Mikolov et al., 2013; Park et al., 2023; Merullo et al., 2024), loops (Engels et al., 2024; Modell et al., 2025), and continuous 1D manifolds (Gurnee et al., 2025), along with linear decoding capabilities (Gurnee & Tegmark, 2023). While these geometric patterns are striking and appear universally across different model architectures and tasks, a unifying, fundamental explanation for *why* they arise has been largely absent.

This paper directly addresses this gap by proposing and rigorously testing a universal organizing principle. It builds upon and distinguishes itself from prior theoretical work:
- **Distinction from Saxe et al. (2019):** While Saxe et al. showed circular geometry in linear models trained on synthetic periodic data, their work did not address the unsupervised learning setting, where the data distribution itself is key. This paper focuses on how corpus statistics drive these patterns.
- **Advancement over Prieto et al. (2025):** Prieto et al. observed cyclic correlations and found that nonlinear autoencoders approximately perform PCA, leading to circular representations. This work goes further by providing an explicit mechanism linking symmetry to geometry, demonstrating the phenomenon in simpler word embedding models, extending analysis to open boundary conditions and 2-dimensional lattices, offering a more detailed mathematical description of manifolds, and crucially, identifying the collective nature of the phenomenon.
- **Connection to Low-Order Statistics:** Recent work (Cagnetta & Wyart, 2024; Favero et al., 2025; Rende et al., 2024) suggests that LLMs first learn low-order token correlations (like pairwise statistics), before higher-order ones. This provides a strong justification for why an analysis rooted in pairwise co-occurrence statistics can explain fundamental aspects of LLM representations, even for complex transformer models.
- **Extension of Robustness Theories:** The paper also connects to research on the robustness of word embeddings, such as the persistence of linear analogies even after explicit perturbations (Chiang et al., 2020; Korchinski et al., 2025). This work extends this understanding by showing how the robustness of geometric structures emerges from the collective influence of latent variables on co-occurrence statistics.

By providing a unified, analytical framework for understanding the geometric structures in language model representations, this research offers a significant contribution to the mechanistic interpretability of LLMs. It shifts the discourse from mere observation to a causal, principled explanation rooted in the underlying statistics of natural language.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the consistent empirical observation of highly structured geometric patterns in the internal representations of language models, without a clear, unifying theoretical explanation. These intriguing structures, such as circles for cyclical concepts (e.g., months), "rippled" 1D manifolds for continuous sequences (e.g., historical years), and linearly decodable spatiotemporal coordinates (e.g., latitudes and longitudes), are found across various model architectures and tasks, suggesting a deeper, universal origin. The authors highlight the lack of an "organizing principle" to explain this apparent universality.

The overarching objective of this work is to provide such an organizing principle. Specifically, the authors aim to:

- **Hypothesize a Causal Link:** Propose that the representational geometry of language models directly reflects pairwise co-occurrence statistics between words, and that *translation symmetry* within these statistics is the driving force behind the formation of observed representational manifolds.
- **Develop a Mathematical Theory:** Analytically derive the specific manifold geometries from this translation symmetry, predicting phenomena like circular representations, rippled 1D manifolds, and linear coordinate decoding.
- **Explain Robustness:** Account for the surprising robustness of these representational geometries, even when the underlying co-occurrence statistics are significantly perturbed. They hypothesize this is due to a "collective phenomenon" involving many words modulated by shared latent variables.
- **Validate Across Models:** Empirically test these theoretical predictions against both simple word embedding models (where representation learning is driven *exclusively* by pairwise co-occurrence) and complex, deep transformer-based large language models.

In essence, the research seeks to move beyond describing *what* geometric structures appear in LLMs to explaining *why* they appear, grounding these phenomena in the fundamental statistical properties of the language data itself.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper combines theoretical derivations with empirical validation across multiple types of language models. The core of their approach is built upon a fundamental hypothesis: translation symmetry in pairwise word co-occurrence statistics dictates the geometric structure of learned representations.

**Theoretical Framework:**
1. **Foundation in Word Embeddings:** The authors begin by leveraging recent theoretical results (Karkada et al., 2025) which show that word embedding models (like word2vec) learn to represent the top eigenmodes of a normalized co-occurrence matrix, M* (an approximation of the Pointwise Mutual Information, PMI, matrix). This establishes a crucial analytical link between raw co-occurrence statistics and the learned embedding vectors.
2. **Assumption of Translation Symmetry:** A key assumption (Assumption 3.1 or its refined version B.1) is that for words representing a continuous concept (e.g., months, years, geographic locations), their pairwise co-occurrence probability depends *only* on the distance between their positions on the semantic continuum. This induces a circulant-like matrix structure for periodic boundary conditions (e.g., months) and a Toeplitz-like structure for open boundary conditions (e.g., historical years).
3. **Analytical Diagonalization:** Under this symmetry assumption, the authors analytically derive the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the co-occurrence matrix.
 - **Periodic Lattices (D=1):** For concepts like calendar months, the resulting embeddings exhibit *Fourier geometry* (Proposition 1, Corollary 2). Each principal component corresponds to a sinusoidal function, with loops and circles arising from long-wavelength modes and "ripples" from higher harmonics. An exponential co-occurrence kernel is used for tractability and empirical fit.
 - **Open Lattices (D=1):** For concepts like historical years, the derivations (Proposition 3) show similar sinusoidal modes, but with different quantization conditions due to open boundaries.
 - **2D Lattices:** While not analytically diagonalized in general, the theory suggests that even for unevenly distributed points (like US states), top directions will encode slow spatial variations.
4. **Linear Coordinate Decoding:** Proposition 4 analytically predicts the error scaling for linearly decoding continuous coordinates from these Fourier-like embeddings, showing it decays as r^(-1/D), where r is the embedding rank and D is the latent dimension.
5. **Collective Effects for Robustness:** To explain the robustness of these geometries to perturbations, the authors introduce a continuous latent variable model (Section 4). In this model, many words in the vocabulary are collectively affected by a shared latent variable (e.g., "seasonality"). This "collective phenomenon" leads to large eigenvalues in the PMI matrix that are robust to local perturbations, thus preserving the geometric structure even when direct co-occurrences between target words are removed.

**Empirical Validation:**
1. **Data Corpus:** All word embedding models are trained on the November 2023 dump of English Wikipedia (2.72 billion tokens, 25,000 most frequent words).
2. **Model Types:**
 - **Word Embeddings:** Explicitly trained embeddings from Wikipedia using methods approximating matrix factorization of the M* matrix.
 - **Large Language Models (LLMs):** Internal representations (hidden activations) extracted from Gemma 2 2B, a deep transformer model, and EmbeddingGemma, a text embedding model, are analyzed.
3. **Analysis Techniques:** Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is used to visualize and analyze the geometric structures in the high-dimensional representation spaces. Gram matrices of the representations are also visualized to show co-occurrence patterns.
4. **Perturbation Experiments:** To test robustness, experiments are conducted where specific co-occurrence statistics (e.g., all month-month co-occurrences) are deliberately removed from the input data, and the resulting embedding geometry is then analyzed. The ability to reconstruct the original geometry using "helper" words (e.g., seasonal words) is also tested.
5. **Coordinate Decoding Task:** Linear probes are trained to decode numerical coordinates (e.g., historical years) from the learned representations, and the decoding error is measured across different embedding ranks.

This comprehensive approach allows the authors to not only hypothesize a mechanism but also to provide strong analytical backing and extensive empirical evidence for their claims, connecting fundamental data properties to complex neural network behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings that collectively support the hypothesis that translation symmetry in language statistics fundamentally shapes the geometry of learned representations in language models:

1. **Empirical Evidence of Translation Symmetry in Co-occurrence Statistics:** The authors demonstrate that for words representing continuous concepts like calendar months, historical years, and US states, their pairwise co-occurrence probabilities in natural text (Wikipedia corpus) indeed exhibit translation symmetry. This is visualized by showing that the M* matrix (or its components |M*|, M+, M-) for these words approximates a circulant (for periodic concepts like months) or Toeplitz (for open concepts like years) matrix, where interactions depend primarily on semantic distance (Figures 5 and 6).

2. **Analytical Derivation of Representational Geometries:**
 - **Circles for Cyclical Concepts:** For D=1 semantic lattices with periodic boundary conditions (e.g., calendar months), the theory analytically predicts that the embeddings form circular geometries in low-dimensional PCA projections. This is because the top principal components encode the slowest (fundamental frequency) Fourier modes. This prediction shows excellent agreement with both word embeddings and Gemma 2 2B internal representations (Figure 1, left column, and Figure 8).
 - **Rippled 1D Manifolds for Continuous Sequences:** For D=1 semantic lattices with open boundary conditions (e.g., historical years), the theory predicts that embeddings form continuous 1D manifolds with "ripples" or extrinsic curvature. This is explained as a consequence of different principal components encoding sinusoids with varying frequencies, leading to Lissajous curves when projected onto multiple PCA axes. This phenomenon is observed empirically in word embeddings for historical years (Figure 1, right column, Figure 2, left).
 - **Slow Spatial Variation for 2D Concepts:** For 2D semantic continua (e.g., geographic locations like US states), the theory qualitatively predicts that the top PCA modes exhibit slowly-varying spatial oscillations across the map. This is confirmed by numerical diagonalization of the theoretical model and observed in word embeddings and LLM representations (Figure 3 and Figure 12).

3. **Explainable Linear Coordinate Decoding:** The paper provides a theoretical explanation for the ability of linear probes to decode spatiotemporal coordinates from LLM representations. Proposition 4 predicts that the decoding error scales as ε² ~ r^(-1/D) (where r is the embedding rank and D is the dimension of the semantic continuum). This prediction is empirically validated, showing that the error decreases as more Fourier modes (corresponding to higher resolution) are included in the probe, and also demonstrating the "double descent" phenomenon in test error (Figure 2, right).

4. **Robustness as a Collective Phenomenon:** A significant finding is the surprising robustness of these representational geometries to perturbations. Even when all direct co-occurrence statistics between target words (e.g., month-to-month co-occurrences) are completely removed from the corpus, the circular geometry of months can still be reconstructed in word embeddings. The authors prove that this robustness arises because the representational geometry is a *collective phenomenon*: a continuous latent variable (e.g., "seasonality" or "geography") affects the co-occurrence of *many* words in the vocabulary. This widespread influence creates large, stable eigenvalues in the PMI matrix, whose eigenvectors are insensitive to local noise or perturbations (Section 4, Figure 4). This is further supported by showing that "seasonal" helper words enable reconstruction, whereas non-seasonal words do not (Figures 14, 15, 16).

5. **Universality Across Model Architectures:** Crucially, these predicted geometric structures, their underlying statistical symmetries, and their robustness are consistently observed not only in simple word embedding models but also in the internal, contextualized representations of sophisticated deep transformer models like Gemma 2 2B and EmbeddingGemma (Figures 1, 3, 12). This suggests a fundamental and universal principle at play. The paper also notes that context can resolve polysemantic ambiguity in LLMs, allowing "May" to integrate into the month circle more effectively with contextualized prompts (Figure 13).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a profound contribution to the understanding of how language models learn and represent semantic information, with significant implications across multiple domains:

1. **Unifying Theoretical Framework for LLM Interpretability:** This work provides a much-needed organizing principle for the diverse and complex geometric structures observed in LLM representations. By demonstrating that translation symmetry in low-order token correlations (pairwise co-occurrence statistics) is the universal driver, it moves beyond anecdotal observations to a fundamental, mathematically grounded explanation. This is a critical step towards mechanistic interpretability, offering a causal understanding of why these patterns emerge.

2. **Bridging Simple and Complex Models:** The finding that principles derived from simple word embedding models can accurately predict and explain phenomena in complex, deep transformer architectures is highly significant. It suggests that while LLMs learn rich contextual representations, their foundational structure for continuous concepts may still be heavily influenced by these basic statistical regularities, making the behavior of opaque models more accessible.

3. **Implications for Neuroscience:** The paper draws an intriguing parallel between the Fourier-like representations observed in LLMs and the firing patterns of grid cells in the mammalian entorhinal cortex, which encode two-dimensional space through the interference of Fourier modes. This suggests that the same computational principles related to processing spatial or temporal sequences might be at play in both artificial and biological neural systems, potentially opening new avenues for understanding brain function from a computational perspective. This connection could inspire cross-disciplinary research on representation learning.

4. **Insights into Robustness and Generalization:** The discovery that representational geometry is a "collective phenomenon" and therefore robust to local perturbations has important implications for understanding the robustness and generalization capabilities of models. It suggests that models develop strong, stable representations of latent variables because many words in the vocabulary are indirectly correlated with these variables, creating a highly constrained and resilient semantic space. This could inform the design of more robust and reliable AI systems.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:**
 - **Contextualization in LLMs:** While the paper shows general patterns, it also notes that context allows LLMs to resolve ambiguities (e.g., "May" as a month vs. a modal verb). Further theoretical work could focus on how context-dependent mechanisms build upon or modulate these fundamental, symmetry-driven representations.
 - **Hierarchical Attributes:** The authors acknowledge that their work, along with previous research on binary attributes (Korchinski et al., 2025), is part of a larger quest to provide a global framework for all observed geometric properties, including those for hierarchical attributes (Park et al., 2025), which remain unexplained. This work lays a strong foundation for such a comprehensive theory.
 - **Beyond Low-Order Statistics:** Exploring how higher-order statistics and more complex linguistic phenomena further refine or interact with these basic, symmetry-driven geometries could be a fruitful direction.

In summary, this research provides a powerful, parsimonious explanation for a ubiquitous phenomenon in language model representations. By revealing the deep connection between fundamental data symmetries and learned geometric structures, it significantly advances our understanding of how language models organize knowledge, contributing to both the theoretical foundations of machine learning and its mechanistic interpretability.
