# World Models for Robotic Manipulation: A Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey organizes world models for robotic manipulation along three axes: what future representation is predicted, how prediction connects to action, and when it is used in the learning pipeline. It defines a world model as an action-conditioned predictive system, sorts work into five representation families, and reviews 34 datasets, concluding that world models are becoming predictive infrastructure for robot learning.

## Problem

- The term world model now spans latent dynamics, video generators, 3D/4D predictors, simulators, and VLA modules, fragmenting the literature and obscuring which design choices actually matter for manipulation.
- Prior surveys cover manipulation only peripherally, focus on driving or video generation, restrict to VLA agents, or avoid a strict definition, so RL and imitation traditions stay disconnected.
- Predictive fidelity and action utility diverge: visually plausible futures can violate contact, object permanence, or action feasibility, while compact latent models hide physical structure needed for inspection and safety.

## Method

- Operational definition of a world model as an ==action-conditioned predictive system==, explicitly separated from perception modules, inverse models, policies, reward models, and value functions.
- Five representation families: ==image and video prediction==, ==latent dynamics==, ==motion fields and scene flow==, ==3D/4D scene structure==, and ==physics-informed dynamics==, compared on fidelity, horizon, cost, and robustness.
- ==Functional taxonomy== splitting ==integrated prediction-action models== from ==explicit predictive planners==, with infrastructure roles (data engine, candidate filtering, search, learned environment, verifier) mapped across pretraining, post-training, and inference adaptation.

## Results

- Reviews **34** principal manipulation datasets and synthesizes evaluation protocols across **three** levels: predictive fidelity, downstream task performance, and simulator reliability.
- Characterizes **five** infrastructure roles for world models, namely synthetic experience generation, candidate filtering, search-based evaluation, learned environments, and outcome verification, across **three** pipeline stages.
- Finds world models evolving from task-specific dynamics predictors into predictive infrastructure, while contact modeling, hallucination control, action alignment, and closed-loop benchmarking remain open; no quantitative benchmark comparison is reported.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Classify world models by function (what is predicted, how it feeds action, when it is used) rather than architecture, so RL, imitation, and VLA work become comparable.
> - Visual plausibility is not action utility: evaluate world models on contact, object permanence, and feasibility, not only video realism, since fidelity and control value can diverge.
> - Using world models as data engines, simulators, and verifiers shifts the burden to data, verification, and closed-loop evaluation, making benchmarking under closed-loop use the bottleneck.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The concept of a "world model" has expanded significantly within artificial intelligence and robotics, moving beyond its origins in classical motor control and model-based reinforcement learning. This expansion, particularly with the advent of large-scale, language-conditioned, generalist systems, has introduced considerable ambiguity into the term. World models now encompass diverse predictive systems such as latent dynamics models, action-conditioned video generators, three- and four-dimensional scene predictors, physics-informed simulators, and integrated predictive modules within vision-language-action (VLA) systems. This breadth has led to fragmentation of the literature, making it challenging for researchers to identify effective design choices for robotic manipulation.

Existing survey efforts have addressed world models from various angles but have not fully resolved this manipulation-specific design space. General world-model surveys often emphasize video generation or autonomous driving, treating manipulation peripherally. Surveys of embodied intelligence tend to discuss world models as enabling technologies rather than providing a unified predictive framework. Reviews of three- and four-dimensional modeling focus primarily on geometric representations, omitting broader model-based reinforcement learning and imitation learning traditions. Some manipulation-specific surveys either use broad definitions or restrict their focus, for instance, to VLA agents, thus excluding significant parts of the literature where the modern world-model concept originated.

This survey addresses these gaps by providing a manipulation-centered perspective that integrates insights from reinforcement learning, imitation learning, video generation, geometry, physics, and VLA systems. It aims to offer a unified account of predictive modeling relevant to robotic manipulation, clarifying the design choices and functional roles that are critical for this domain. The paper seeks to move beyond architectural categories to a function-based understanding, thereby providing a more coherent framework for analyzing and advancing world models in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this survey is to address the ambiguity and fragmentation surrounding the term "world model" within the context of robotic manipulation. The rapid expansion of predictive systems, from compact latent dynamics to complex VLA components, has made it difficult to discern how different world model approaches contribute to manipulation tasks and which design choices are most impactful. This ambiguity is particularly problematic for manipulation, where predictive fidelity does not always equate to action utility; for example, a visually plausible prediction might still fail to account for critical physical interactions like contact or force closure.

To resolve this issue, the survey sets forth several key objectives:
1. **Operational Definition:** To establish an operational definition of a world model that is broad enough to cover contemporary predictive systems in robot learning but narrow enough to distinguish it from related components like perception modules, inverse models, policies, and reward functions. This definition centers on a system's ability to estimate task-relevant future world evolution under robot intervention.
2. **Taxonomic Organization:** To organize the diverse literature on world models for robotic manipulation along three complementary axes:
 - **What future representation is predicted?** This objective seeks to categorize models based on their output, such as image/video, latent state, motion fields, 3D/4D scene structure, or physics-informed dynamics.
 - **How prediction is connected to action?** This aims to distinguish between integrated prediction-action models (where prediction is fused with action generation) and explicit predictive planners (where prediction provides intermediate targets like subgoals or trajectories).
 - **When prediction is used in the robot-learning pipeline?** This objective categorizes world models by their role across different stages: pretraining, post-training, and inference adaptation.
3. **Functional Characterization:** To develop a functional taxonomy that separates the roles of world models as reusable learning and decision infrastructure, including synthetic experience generation, candidate filtering, search-based evaluation, learned environments, and outcome verification.
4. **Resource Review:** To review widely used manipulation datasets and benchmarks, analyzing their suitability for training, evaluating, and stress-testing world models.
5. **Evaluation Protocol Synthesis:** To synthesize existing evaluation protocols, distinguishing between predictive fidelity, downstream task performance, and simulator reliability, and to highlight their limitations for manipulation.

By achieving these objectives, the survey aims to provide a structured understanding of world models, clarify critical design trade-offs, identify open challenges, and guide future research towards more reliable and effective predictive systems for robotic manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this survey paper involves a structured analysis and categorization of the existing literature on world models for robotic manipulation. Rather than presenting new experimental results, the paper constructs a comprehensive framework for understanding the field.

The approach is primarily characterized by the following steps:

1. **Operational Definition:** The authors begin by providing a precise operational definition of a "world model" for robotic manipulation. This definition distinguishes world models from related concepts like perception modules, inverse models, policies, and reward/value functions by emphasizing their role as learned predictive systems that estimate future world evolution, typically conditioned on robot actions. This functional definition serves as the boundary for inclusion in the survey.

2. **Multi-Axis Taxonomy Development:** The core of the survey's approach is the development of a multi-axis taxonomy, allowing for a comprehensive and cross-cutting analysis of world models:
 - **Representation (Section III):** The paper categorizes world models based on *what* they predict. Five families are identified: Image and Video Representations (e.g., raw frames, video generators), Learned Latent Representations (e.g., compact latent dynamics), Motion Fields and Scene Flow (e.g., object displacement), Geometric and Spatiotemporal Representations (e.g., 3D point clouds, 4D scene structure), and Physics-Informed Dynamics (e.g., contact mechanics, material behavior). Each family's trade-offs in fidelity, planning horizon, computational cost, and robustness are discussed.
 - **Prediction-Action Interface (Section IV):** This axis addresses *how* the prediction is connected to action generation. A key distinction is made between "Integrated Prediction–Action Models" (where prediction is fused within the controller and may not be explicitly exposed) and "Explicit Predictive Planners" (where prediction generates an intermediate target like a subgoal, trajectory, or latent plan for a separate executor). Subcategories include joint world-action generative modeling, prediction-augmented policy learning, subgoal prediction, trajectory prediction, waypoint/latent-plan prediction, and hierarchical predictive planning.
 - **Learning Lifecycle (Section VI):** This axis analyzes *when* the world model is utilized within the robot learning pipeline. Three stages are defined: "Pretraining" (where the model learns reusable predictive priors), "Post-Training" (where it refines an existing policy, e.g., through synthetic data or learned environments), and "Inference Adaptation" (where it aids online reasoning, adaptation, and correction).

3. **Functional Roles as Infrastructure (Section V):** Complementing the prediction-action interface, the survey elaborates on how world models serve as reusable "Learning and Decision Infrastructure." Five functional roles are identified: Synthetic Experience Generation, Candidate-Action Filtering and Refinement, Search-Based Action Evaluation, Learned Environments for Policy Evaluation and Improvement, and Outcome Scoring and Feasibility Verification. This section emphasizes the systematic reuse of predictive capabilities to address bottlenecks in robot learning.

4. **Dataset and Benchmark Review (Sections VII & VIII):** The methodology includes a systematic review of 34 principal manipulation datasets, categorized by their functional role in world-model learning (e.g., video prediction pioneers, simulation benchmarks, large-scale real-robot corpora, multimodal data, autonomous data paradigms). This is followed by a synthesis of evaluation protocols, distinguishing between direct predictive fidelity metrics (e.g., PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS), downstream task performance metrics (e.g., success rate, sample efficiency), and simulator reliability metrics (e.g., policy-rank correlation, hallucination rate, rollout divergence).

The authors adopt a neutral, factual tone throughout, avoiding promotional language or superlatives. This systematic organization and analysis of the literature, guided by a clear functional definition and multi-dimensional taxonomies, forms the methodological core of the survey, aiming to provide clarity and expose critical challenges in the evolving field of world models for robotic manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey's main findings and results are presented through its comprehensive organization of the literature, the proposed taxonomies, the identification of key trends, and the articulation of open challenges.

1. **Clarified Operational Definition:** The paper establishes an operational definition of a world model as a learned predictive system that estimates task-relevant future world evolution under robot intervention. This definition serves to delineate the scope of "world model" from other related modules (e.g., perception, inverse models, policies in isolation), thus addressing conceptual ambiguity in the field.

2. **Representation Spectrum:** The survey identifies and analyzes five families of world model representations, ordered by increasing structured inductive bias:
 - **Image and Video Representations:** Predict directly in the visual domain, offering interpretability but prone to visual plausibility not equating to action validity. Trend: Used for data engines, learned simulators, and visual planners.
 - **Learned Latent Representations:** Compress observations into a compact state space for efficient prediction and planning, beneficial for model-based RL but with reduced auditability. Trend: Emphasized for imagined policy learning and efficient planning.
 - **Motion Fields and Scene Flow:** Focus on predicting displacement, aligning with short-horizon physical change relevant for manipulation but potentially incomplete without semantic or geometric context.
 - **Geometric and Spatiotemporal Representations:** Represent scenes through depth, point clouds, or 4D volumes, crucial for spatial consistency and handling occlusion in tasks like grasping and assembly, but computationally expensive. Trend: Used for view-invariant prediction and spatial reasoning.
 - **Physics-Informed Dynamics:** Incorporate physical constraints (e.g., rigid-body, contact mechanics) for extrapolation and robustness, particularly for contact-rich tasks, but sensitive to accurate parameter identification.
 A key finding is a trend towards **hybrid representations** that combine aspects from multiple families to balance fidelity, efficiency, and physical plausibility.

3. **Prediction–Action Interface Taxonomy:** The survey proposes a functional taxonomy based on how prediction connects to action:
 - **Integrated Prediction–Action Models:** Prediction is fused within the controller (e.g., joint world-action generative models, prediction-augmented policies). These avoid brittle handoffs but can lack interpretability.
 - **Explicit Predictive Planners:** Prediction exposes intermediate targets (subgoals, trajectories, waypoints, latent plans) for a separate executor. These are more inspectable and modular but face executability and error propagation challenges.
 The work notes that current systems increasingly blur this boundary, integrating predictive reasoning into policies while explicit planners add learned inverse dynamics for better execution.

4. **World Models as Infrastructure:** A significant finding is the increasing role of world models as reusable infrastructure components beyond direct action generation. Five key roles are identified:
 - **Synthetic Experience Generation:** Expanding data coverage for policy learning.
 - **Candidate-Action Filtering and Refinement:** Improving existing policies by evaluating proposed actions.
 - **Search-Based Action Evaluation:** Guiding online decision-making through counterfactual comparison.
 - **Learned Environments for Policy Evaluation and Improvement:** Providing virtual settings for policy refinement and stress-testing.
 - **Outcome Scoring and Feasibility Verification:** Providing feedback (rewards, progress estimates) and assessing the trustworthiness of imagined transitions.
 This highlights a shift from world models as mere dynamics predictors to foundational substrates for broader robot learning processes.

5. **Dataset Landscape and Limitations:** The survey reviews 34 datasets, tracing the evolution from early video-prediction corpora to large-scale real-robot pretraining, multimodal, and autonomous data paradigms. Key findings regarding datasets include:
 - The transition from narrow task-specific data to large, diverse corpora for foundation model pretraining.
 - The growing inclusion of non-visual modalities (e.g., force, audio, tactile) to capture critical physical interactions.
 - The emergence of autonomous data collection paradigms (e.g., robot play) to expand coverage beyond human demonstrations.
 However, open problems persist, such as inconsistent task granularity, heterogeneity of action and embodiment, underutilization of non-visual modalities, and undersampling of rare failures and contact-rich transitions.

6. **Evaluation Tiers and Challenges:** The paper synthesizes evaluation protocols into three tiers:
 - **Predictive Fidelity Metrics:** Assess how well predictions match ground truth (e.g., PSNR, LPIPS for vision; 3D/4D metrics for geometry; latent consistency for latent models).
 - **Downstream Task Performance:** Measure the impact of the world model on policy success, generalization, and sample efficiency on specific tasks (e.g., success rate, sample efficiency on Meta-World, CALVIN).
 - **Simulator Reliability Metrics:** Evaluate the trustworthiness of imagined interactions for policy ranking, improvement, and verification (e.g., policy-rank correlation, hallucination rate, rollout divergence).
 The survey concludes that no single metric is sufficient. Major challenges include the lack of specific metrics for contact-rich manipulation, fragmentation across metric types, inadequate action-conditioned calibration, and the need for benchmarks that expose and test against closed-loop policy exploitation of model artifacts.

Overall, the survey concludes that world models are evolving into crucial predictive infrastructure, but their utility for manipulation hinges on addressing challenges related to action alignment, physical credibility, calibration, and robustness under closed-loop policy use.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey paper holds significant importance for the field of robotic manipulation and broader embodied AI research. Its impact stems from several key contributions:

1. **Clarifying a Fragmented Field:** By providing an operational definition and a multi-axial taxonomy, the survey brings clarity to the increasingly ambiguous and fragmented literature on world models. This structured organization helps researchers understand the diverse approaches, their underlying principles, and their specific roles in the robot learning pipeline. This clarity can accelerate research by enabling more precise communication, comparison, and integration of different methods.

2. **Identifying Key Design Choices and Trade-offs:** The detailed analysis of representation families and prediction-action interfaces illuminates the critical design choices faced by researchers. It systematically outlines the trade-offs between visual fidelity, computational efficiency, physical plausibility, and interpretability. This guidance can help practitioners select or design world models that are best suited for specific manipulation tasks and learning scenarios, moving beyond a "one-size-fits-all" approach.

3. **Highlighting the Infrastructural Role of World Models:** The paper emphasizes the evolution of world models from mere dynamics predictors to foundational infrastructure for robot learning. By categorizing their roles in synthetic experience generation, candidate filtering, search-based evaluation, learned environments, and outcome verification, the survey positions world models as central to addressing fundamental bottlenecks like data scarcity, expensive real-world interaction, and policy refinement. This perspective underscores their potential to enable more scalable, robust, and autonomous robot learning.

4. **Pinpointing Open Challenges and Future Directions:** The survey meticulously outlines critical open problems across representations, action interfaces, datasets, and evaluation protocols. These include:
 - Developing hybrid representations that balance various aspects of prediction.
 - Ensuring action alignment and physical credibility in predicted futures.
 - Addressing the heterogeneity and non-visual modalities in datasets.
 - Creating more robust evaluation metrics for contact-rich tasks, action-conditioned calibration, and resistance to closed-loop exploitation.
 By articulating these challenges, the survey provides a clear roadmap for future research, directing efforts towards areas that promise the greatest advancements in reliable robotic manipulation.

5. **Guiding Benchmark and Dataset Development:** The comprehensive review of datasets and evaluation metrics, coupled with an analysis of their limitations, serves as a crucial guide for the development of future benchmarks. It advocates for metrics that move beyond simple predictive fidelity to encompass downstream utility, simulator reliability, and real-world trustworthiness. This emphasis can lead to more meaningful evaluations that genuinely assess whether world models improve real robot performance.

In conclusion, this survey acts as a foundational reference for understanding, designing, and evaluating world models for robotic manipulation. Its potential impact lies in its ability to systematize knowledge, foster interdisciplinary collaboration, guide methodological choices, and steer the research community towards developing world models that are not only capable of imagining plausible futures but also of reliably supporting complex, real-world robot decision-making and learning.
