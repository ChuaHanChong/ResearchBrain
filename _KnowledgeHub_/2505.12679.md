# Dribble Master: Learning Agile Humanoid Dribbling through Legged Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Tsinghua University developed NVDS+^{+}+, a neural framework that stabilizes flickering depth maps from single-image models, achieving hundreds of times faster inference than test-time methods while improving accuracy and temporal consistency. This framework is supported by VDW, a large-scale and diverse video depth dataset for natural scenes.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots face significant challenges in achieving agile, continuous, and robust dribbling due to the need for dexterous ball manipulation, dynamic balance, and complex robot-ball interactions.
- Real-world ball perception for humanoids is often discontinuous and inaccurate due to camera limitations and occlusions, making continuous visual tracking difficult.
- Existing methods, including rule-based and prior learning-based approaches, struggle with adapting to dynamic ball behavior, generalizing across terrains, and effective sim-to-real transfer for full-sized humanoids.

## Method

- A two-stage curriculum reinforcement learning framework, built on PPO, was developed to progressively acquire skills, starting with basic locomotion and ball chasing before moving to fine-grained ball manipulation.
- A virtual camera model was implemented in simulation with active sensing mechanisms, encouraging the robot to tactically move its head to maintain ball visibility and reacquire it if lost.
- Extensive sim-to-real transfer techniques, including domain randomization of physical parameters, training on rough terrains, and simulating motor delays and perception inaccuracies, were employed to enhance robustness.

## Results

- The method achieved an **86.7%** success rate in the "Dribbling to Target" task and a **93.3%** success rate in the "Obstacle Avoidance" task on a physical Booster T1 humanoid robot, significantly outperforming ablated baselines and tele-operation.
- In simulation, the system demonstrated precise ball velocity tracking, with average direction change errors of 2-**3%** for 45 and 90-degree turns and a speed tracking error of approximately **10%** for a 1 m/s target.
- The learned policy showcased strong generalization, successfully deploying on different bipedal robot platforms in simulation and performing robustly on a physical robot across diverse real-world terrains including artificial grass, potholed ground, and asphalt.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A progressive two-stage curriculum effectively enables reinforcement learning to acquire complex skills like humanoid dribbling by addressing conflicting objectives of stable locomotion and agile manipulation separately.
> - Integrating a realistic virtual camera model and active sensing within the learning framework is crucial for overcoming challenges posed by discontinuous visual perception in dynamic real-world tasks.
> - Robust sim-to-real transfer, achieved through comprehensive domain randomization and simulation of real-world constraints, allows policies to generalize effectively across diverse terrains and different humanoid hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "Dribble Master" contributes to the fields of humanoid robotics, reinforcement learning (RL) for dynamic locomotion, and legged loco-manipulation, particularly in the context of robot soccer.

Current research in humanoid robotics has made progress in dynamic locomotion, enabling robots to perform tasks such as running, crawling, and dancing through learning-based methods. However, the ability of humanoids to interact dexterously with dynamic objects using their legs, a skill termed "legged loco-manipulation," remains less developed compared to arm-based mobile manipulation. Previous efforts in robot-object interaction primarily focused on manipulating objects with arms, with recent advancements allowing humanoids to complete complex tasks like folding clothes.

Within legged loco-manipulation, much of the prior work has focused on quadruped platforms, utilizing either dedicated arms or repurposing legs for tasks like button pressing or circus ball manipulation. While whole-body motion control for humanoids is an emerging area, existing studies often emphasize precise hand-object interaction, expressive upper-body gestures, or agile whole-body maneuvers like basketball shots or stand-up motions. Research specifically addressing humanoid loco-manipulation where feet interact with dynamic objects is scarce.

Robot soccer, particularly the RoboCup competition series, has long served as a benchmark for legged locomotion and coordination. Historically, approaches relied on rule-based or scripted control. More recent years have seen an increase in learning-based methods, but these were often implemented on smaller robots (e.g., Nao) with limitations in achieving continuous ball touching and dribbling. While quadruped robots have shown impressive dribbling and goalkeeping skills using RL, bipedal approaches have largely been confined to discrete kicking, lacking continuous ball control. Humanoid robots present unique challenges due to their smaller support polygon and slower balance recovery, making continuous dribbling more difficult than for quadrupeds. Existing end-to-end learning methods for humanoid soccer skills have achieved agile control in simulation but often depend on specific, structured environments.

This work addresses the gap in achieving agile, continuous, and terrain-generalized dribbling for full-sized humanoids. It aims to develop a system that enables bipedal humanoids to perform refined, multi-touch dribbling in diverse and unstructured environments, thereby advancing the capabilities of humanoid loco-manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a humanoid robot to acquire agile and robust soccer dribbling skills in diverse environments. This overarching goal is driven by several challenges inherent in humanoid soccer dribbling and limitations of existing approaches:

1. **Dexterous Ball Manipulation and Dynamic Balance:** Humanoid dribbling is a highly challenging task because it requires subtle manipulation of a dynamic object (the ball) with the feet while simultaneously maintaining the robot's dynamic balance. This involves complex interactions between the robot's feet and the ball.
2. **Complex Dynamics Modeling:** Accurately modeling the dynamics of a humanoid robot and the interactions between the robot's feet and a rolling ball is difficult. Furthermore, the rolling dynamics of the ball can vary significantly across diverse terrains (e.g., artificial grass, potholes, varying friction and compliance), which complicates the design of traditional model-based controllers.
3. **Discontinuous Visual Perception:** Real-world ball perception is challenging due to factors such as occlusions, image distortions from wide-angle cameras (like fisheye lenses), and a narrow field of view (FOV) of rectangular cameras, which can cause the ball to frequently leave the robot's vision during agile maneuvers.
4. **Limitations of Traditional and Prior Learning-Based Methods:**
 - **Traditional Rule-Based Methods:** These methods often rely on fixed walking patterns and incidental foot-ball contacts, which limits the robot's ability to actively control the ball's direction and results in poor adaptability to real-time ball dynamics.
 - **End-to-End Learning:** While some end-to-end learning approaches have been explored, they often struggle with generalization across different environments.
 - **Hierarchical Methods:** These methods have been attempted but face difficulties with reliable ball tracking and continuous control.
 - **Quadruped vs. Humanoid Challenges:** Existing impressive results in ball manipulation are often for quadrupeds or kid-sized humanoids. Full-sized humanoids face greater challenges due to a smaller support polygon and slower balance recovery.

To address these challenges, the research aims to:
- Develop a learning framework that enables dribbling without explicit dynamics models or predefined trajectories.
- Introduce a method to achieve realistic ball perception during training, considering real robot perception constraints.
- Design mechanisms to encourage active sensing, allowing the robot to broaden its visual range for continuous ball perception.
- Demonstrate successful transfer of the learned policy from simulation to a physical humanoid robot.
- Achieve flexible, visually appealing, and robust dribbling behaviors in varied environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose "Dribble Master," a system built upon a two-stage curriculum reinforcement learning framework, integrated with a virtual camera model for realistic perception and active sensing. The policy is trained in simulation and deployed on a physical humanoid robot.

#### 3.1. A. Reinforcement Learning Problem Formulation

The humanoid dribbling task is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is employed with an asymmetric actor-critic architecture, utilizing privileged information during training and partial observations during deployment.

1. **Observation Space:** The observation space comprises three components:
 - **Commands:** Instead of robot velocity, the x and y ball velocities (v_cmd) in the global frame are used as commands, assuming the ball stays on the ground. The robot learns to control ball velocity directly, facilitating precise modulation for tasks like fast dribbling or shooting.
 - **Proprioception:** Includes robot states such as joint positions (q), joint velocities (q̇), body orientation (yaw, roll, pitch), and ball position (b). An indicator of ball visibility is also included to support active sensing.
 - **Clock Signal:** A sinusoidal clock signal is incorporated to encourage rhythmic leg motion and promote natural gait patterns, serving as a periodic phase variable for hip pitch joints.

2. **Action Space:** The action space consists of 14 dimensions, controlling target positions for the 2-DOF head and 12-DOF legs. These targets are tracked by low-level PD controllers that generate motor torques.

3. **Reward Functions:** A two-stage training pipeline utilizes different reward functions and weights to progressively acquire skills. The discount factor (γ) is 0.994.

#### 3.2. B. Two-Stage Curriculum Learning

To manage the conflicting objectives of stable locomotion and agile dribbling, a two-stage curriculum learning framework is implemented.

1. **Stage 1: Basic Locomotion and Ball Chasing:** The robot learns fundamental locomotion skills, including stable walking and efficient ball chasing. Higher weights are assigned to locomotion rewards (e.g., reference tracking, penalties for torso tilt and stepping cycle mismatches) and ball-chasing rewards (e.g., penalty for distance to the ball). Ball velocity tracking rewards are omitted. The ball is initialized far from the robot to focus learning on upright stability and active approach, providing dense reward signals.
2. **Stage 2: Fine-Grained Ball Manipulation:** Once stable locomotion and pursuit are established, the training transitions to fine-grained ball manipulation. Dribbling rewards (e.g., penalty for mismatched projected ball velocity) are increased in weight, and the ball is initialized closer to the robot. Reference motion rewards are removed to encourage exploration of diverse dribbling behaviors. This curriculum design aims to prevent early training failures due to poor ball approach and facilitates the development of nuanced ball control.

#### 3.3. C. Virtual Camera Model in Simulation

To address the challenges of discontinuous ball perception in the real world, a virtual camera model is implemented in simulation, encouraging the robot to learn active sensing by tactically moving its head to seek the ball.

- The virtual camera simulates RealSense D455 parameters, including Horizontal Field of View (HFOV) and Vertical Field of View (VFOV).
- The system determines if the ball is visible and extracts its position in the image plane. If the ball moves out of view, its position updates in the policy's observation cease.
- A rule-based reward is provided when the ball is within view, encouraging visual contact. The robot learns to align its vision with the ball when visible and rotate its head/body to search when not.
- A 0.3-second history of the ball's location is maintained; if the ball is lost beyond this period, the robot learns to rotate towards the last known direction to reacquire visual contact.
- For improved learning efficiency, the camera's FOV is artificially enlarged (twice the D455's actual FOV) in Stage 1 to provide denser rewards, then reduced to match the actual D455 FOV in Stage 2 to promote precise visual tracking aligned with real-world deployment.

#### 3.4. D. Training Environment in Simulation

1. **Simulation Setup:** The policy is trained using the Humanoid Gym RL framework in Isaac Gym, leveraging parallelization with 4,096 agents. The simulated robot is based on the URDF model of the Booster T1 humanoid (23 DOFs, controlling 12 leg and 2 head joints, with the upper body fixed). The ball is initialized 10 meters away in Stage 1 and within 2 meters in Stage 2, with target velocity updates every 4 seconds.
2. **Policy Architecture:** Both the actor and critic networks are Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). The actor has hidden layer sizes of, while the critic uses and receives additional privileged state information for more accurate value estimation.
3. **Measures to Bridge the Sim-Real Gap:** Several techniques are employed to enhance real-world performance:
 - **Simulating Motor Delay:** Random actuation delays (0-20 milliseconds) are applied to simulate hardware latency.
 - **Training on Rough Terrains:** The robot is trained on terrains with various slopes, friction, and elevation noises to improve stability.
 - **Simulating Ball Perception Inaccuracy:** Additional noise is injected into observed ball positions to account for real-world visual perception inaccuracies.
 - **Domain Randomization:** Physical parameters (e.g., action scale, terrain friction, base mass, CoM position, joint Kp/Kd/Torque scales, joint positions) are sampled from uniform distributions (Table I) to enhance policy robustness and generalization.

#### 3.5. E. Real-World Deployment

1. **Hardware System:** The policy is deployed on a physical Booster T1 humanoid robot (1.18 meters tall, 23 DOFs, IMU, joint encoders, RealSense D455 Depth Camera). Onboard computation is handled by an NVIDIA AGX Orin GPU and a 14-core CPU, running the policy at 50 Hz.
2. **Object Detection:** YOLOv8 is integrated into the vision pipeline for real-time ball detection from RGB images. Bounding box coordinates are transformed into the robot's base frame via extrinsic calibration. The detection module operates at 30 Hz, accelerated by TensorRT.
3. **Joystick Command:** A human operator provides target ball velocity commands (v_x, v_y) in the global frame via a joystick, allowing real-time control over the ball's desired direction and magnitude.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed method in achieving agile and robust humanoid dribbling, both in simulation and on a physical robot.

#### 4.1. A. Velocity Tracking Accuracy in Simulation

Experiments conducted in MuJoCo assessed the system's ability to track target ball velocities during directional changes. The ball was commanded to move straight and then make 45-degree or 90-degree turns (left or right).

- **Direction Tracking Error:** The method showed consistent and accurate alignment with target directions. For a 45-degree target left turn, the average direction change was 43.58 degrees (3.16% relative error). For a 90-degree target left turn, it was 88.06 degrees (2.16% relative error). Similarly, for right turns, the 45-degree target yielded 46.44 degrees (3.20% error) and the 90-degree target yielded 87.98 degrees (2.24% error). These results indicate the system's capability to respond effectively to abrupt directional changes while maintaining precise control.
- **Speed Tracking Error:** The system exhibited accurate speed tracking. For left-turn dribbling, the average ball speed across trials was 0.898 m/s against a 1 m/s target, resulting in a 10.3% relative error. For right-turn dribbling, the average speed was 0.896 m/s, with a 10.4% relative error.

#### 4.2. B. Ablation Study in Real World

An ablation study was conducted on a physical robot to evaluate the contributions of active sensing and two-stage training across two tasks: "Dribbling to Target" and "Obstacle Avoidance." Baselines included direct joystick tele-operation (default controller), the proposed method without active sensing, and single-stage training.

- **Dribbling to Target Task:**
 - **Our Method:** Achieved an 86.7% success rate with an average completion time of 22.0 seconds.
 - **Direct Tele-Operation:** Achieved a 66.7% success rate but required a significantly longer average time of 42.3 seconds. This was attributed to indirect ball velocity control and difficulty in adjusting the robot and kicking force.
 - **Without Active Sensing:** Achieved a 13.3% success rate, indicating its crucial role. Despite a similar completion time (22.0 seconds), its low success rate suggested failures due to losing track of the ball.
 - **Single-Stage Training:** Achieved a 33.3% success rate with an average time of 29.7 seconds, performing worse than the full method.
- **Obstacle Avoidance Task:**
 - **Our Method:** Achieved a 93.3% success rate with an average completion time of 31.4 seconds.
 - **Direct Tele-Operation:** Achieved a 60.0% success rate with an average time of 23.0 seconds. The higher failure rate was linked to unpredictable ball trajectories and uncontrolled contact forces under manual control.
 - **Without Active Sensing:** Achieved a 0% success rate. The task's increased difficulty highlighted the necessity of active sensing to reacquire the ball when it leaves the field of view.
 - **Single-Stage Training:** Achieved a 0% success rate. This baseline struggled to jointly learn stable walking and ball control, confirming the benefits of the staged curriculum.

These results underscore the necessity of both active sensing and multi-stage training for robust and reliable dribbling in challenging real-world scenarios.

#### 4.3. C. Real-World Generalization Study

The generalization capabilities of the method were evaluated across different humanoid hardware in simulation and diverse real-world terrains.

- **Generalization to Various Humanoid Hardware:** In simulation, the trained policy was deployed on the High Torque Mini Pi bipedal robot and the Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Both platforms demonstrated successful dribbling with visual appeal, indicating adaptability across different bipedal systems.
- **Generalization to Diverse Terrains:** Dribbling experiments were conducted on a physical robot across artificial grass, potholed ground, and asphalt pavement. The robot successfully performed the dribbling task on all tested terrains. While some deviations from the reference trajectory were observed, particularly on uneven ground, the system maintained stability and completed tasks without external intervention. The method demonstrated stronger real-world generalization compared to prior works reliant on environment-specific setups, attributed to isolating motion control from the visual front-end and robustness to disturbances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work demonstrates a significant advancement in the field of humanoid legged loco-manipulation, specifically in the challenging domain of soccer dribbling. By introducing a two-stage curriculum reinforcement learning framework and a virtual camera model for active sensing, the research effectively addresses core challenges related to dynamic balance, complex ball-robot interactions, and discontinuous perception that have historically hindered agile humanoid dribbling.

The successful transfer of the learned policy from simulation to a physical full-sized humanoid robot (Booster T1) and its robust performance across diverse real-world terrains and even different robot platforms highlights the practical applicability and strong generalization capabilities of the proposed method. This achievement contrasts with many prior learning-based approaches that often struggle with sim-to-real transfer or require environment-specific tuning.

The contributions of "Dribble Master" have several potential impacts:
- **Advancing Humanoid Robotics:** It pushes the boundaries of what humanoid robots can achieve in terms of dexterous interaction with dynamic objects using their legs, moving beyond basic locomotion to complex, agile manipulation tasks.
- **Enhancing Robot Soccer:** This work provides a foundation for developing more capable and autonomous humanoid soccer robots, potentially leading to more dynamic and strategic gameplay in competitions like RoboCup. The continuous and precise ball control achieved can be a game-changer for bipedal soccer agents.
- **Facilitating Complex Loco-Manipulation:** The methodology, particularly the two-stage curriculum and active sensing, could be adapted to other complex loco-manipulation tasks requiring agile whole-body control and dynamic object interaction in unstructured environments.
- **Generalization and Robustness:** The emphasis on bridging the sim-to-real gap through techniques like domain randomization and motor delay simulation, coupled with training on varied terrains, leads to policies that are more robust and generalizable, reducing the need for extensive real-world training or fine-tuning.

Future directions, such as integrating autonomous obstacle avoidance and exploring multi-agent coordination in adversarial environments, suggest a pathway towards increasingly sophisticated and intelligent humanoid behaviors, contributing to the broader goal of developing athletic intelligence in robots. This work establishes a robust framework for further research in learning-based control for dynamic humanoid interaction.
