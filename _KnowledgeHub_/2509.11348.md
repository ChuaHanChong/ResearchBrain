# On Linear Mode Connectivity of Mixture-of-Experts Architectures

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers extended the concept of Linear Mode Connectivity to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, formally characterizing their functional equivalence through group actions and developing a two-stage weight matching algorithm. Experiments reveal widespread LMC across diverse MoE configurations and tasks, showing that independently trained models can be connected by low-loss linear paths once aligned.

## Problem

- The phenomenon of Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC), observed in simpler neural networks, had not been systematically explored in the architecturally distinct Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models.
- A formal understanding of functional equivalence and inherent symmetries within MoE architectures was missing, hindering the analysis of their loss landscapes.
- There was no established algorithm to align independently trained MoE models to discover potential low-loss linear paths in their parameter space.

## Method

- Researchers formally defined the ==functional equivalence== of MoE models by characterizing their unique symmetries through a novel group action G(n), encompassing expert permutations and gating parameter translations.
- A two-stage Weight Matching Algorithm was developed to align independently trained MoE models, first by matching the optimal order of experts and then aligning internal weights within each expert.
- The approach involved extensive empirical validation across dense, sparse, and shared-expert MoE configurations, utilizing various datasets and tasks in both image classification and language modeling.

## Results

- Extensive experiments empirically confirmed widespread Linear Mode Connectivity across dense, sparse, and shared-expert MoE variants on both image classification and language modeling tasks.
- The developed two-stage Weight Matching algorithm successfully aligns independently trained MoE models, consistently reducing the loss barrier for linear interpolation paths to near-zero.
- Both proposed methods for matching expert order (gating weights and internal weights matching) proved effective and comparable in performance, consistently achieving near-optimal alignments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The functional equivalence of Mixture-of-Experts models is precisely characterized by a group action involving permutations of experts and translations of gating parameters.
> - The translational component of MoE symmetries does not influence the LMC loss barrier, indicating that only permutation alignment is necessary to discover low-loss paths.
> - Linear Mode Connectivity is a consistent feature of MoE architectures across diverse settings, provided their specific permutation symmetries are correctly handled during alignment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper contributes to the growing body of research on the geometry of neural network loss landscapes, specifically extending the concept of Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC) to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures. This work is situated at the intersection of several key research areas:

- **Mode Connectivity and Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC):** The introduction highlights the surprising phenomenon of mode connectivity, where independently trained neural networks often find solutions (local minima) that can be connected by continuous low-loss paths in parameter space. LMC is a more restrictive but analytically convenient variant, where these paths are linear interpolations. This phenomenon challenges traditional views of highly non-convex loss landscapes and has implications for understanding why stochastic optimization methods succeed and how models generalize. Previous work, extensively cited, has explored LMC in standard feedforward and convolutional neural networks, showing its relevance for techniques like Stochastic Weight Averaging and understanding adversarial robustness and generalization.
- **Permutation Invariance in Neural Networks:** A central challenge in observing LMC is the permutation invariance of neural networks, where permuting neurons within a hidden layer does not change the network's function. This means functionally equivalent models can appear disconnected in parameter space. Prior research has addressed this by proposing alignment algorithms (e.g., using Optimal Transport or weight matching) to find permutations that minimize the loss barrier between models. This paper builds on this understanding by adapting and extending alignment strategies for the unique symmetries of MoE.
- **Functional Equivalence in Complex Architectures:** While permutation symmetries are well-characterized in standard networks, they are less explored in modern architectures like Transformers and, crucially, Mixture-of-Experts. The paper explicitly addresses this gap, focusing on characterizing functional equivalence in MoE. A rigorous understanding of these symmetries is essential for enabling LMC analysis in more complex, structured models.
- **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Architectures:** MoE models are gaining prominence due to their scalability and computational efficiency, particularly in large language models. They combine multiple "expert" subnetworks via a learnable gating mechanism, which can be dense (all experts contribute with soft weights) or sparse (only top-k experts contribute). Before this work, the LMC phenomenon had not been systematically investigated in MoE architectures, leaving a gap in understanding their loss landscape geometry and optimization dynamics compared to standard networks.

This paper bridges these areas by providing a theoretical and empirical framework for analyzing LMC in MoE models. By characterizing the unique symmetries of MoE (which involve permutations of both experts and their gating mechanisms), it extends the existing LMC literature to a highly relevant and increasingly popular class of models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations of this research are:

- **Investigate Linear Mode Connectivity in MoE Architectures:** The core objective is to systematically explore whether the LMC phenomenon, previously observed in standard neural networks, also exists in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, which are fundamentally different due to their modular structure and gating mechanisms.
- **Characterize MoE Symmetries for Functional Equivalence:** To enable LMC analysis, a critical step is to formally define and characterize the symmetries inherent to MoE architectures. This includes understanding how permutations act on expert components and the gating function, leading to functionally equivalent models. This theoretical foundation is crucial for aligning independently trained MoE models.
- **Develop a Permutation Alignment Algorithm for MoE:** Based on the characterized symmetries, the paper aims to propose a practical algorithm capable of aligning independently trained MoE models. This algorithm would identify the optimal permutation of experts and their internal parameters to reveal low-loss linear paths in the parameter space.
- **Empirically Validate LMC Across Diverse MoE Configurations:** The research seeks to provide strong empirical evidence for the presence of LMC in various MoE settings, including dense, sparse, and shared-expert variants (like DeepSeekMoE). This empirical validation spans a wide range of model settings, datasets, scales, and modalities (image classification, language modeling) to demonstrate the generality of the findings.
- **Gain Fundamental Insights into MoE Loss Landscapes:** Ultimately, the motivation is to offer fundamental insights into the functional landscape and optimization dynamics of MoE architectures. Understanding LMC can inform model ensembling strategies, generalization theory, and the design of more robust and efficient MoE models.

In essence, the authors are motivated by the need to extend the theoretical understanding of neural network loss landscapes to the architecturally distinct and increasingly important domain of Mixture-of-Experts models, providing both rigorous theoretical underpinnings and practical tools for analysis.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper combines theoretical analysis with extensive empirical validation.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Approach: Characterizing MoE Symmetries and Functional Equivalence

- **Defining MoE Weight Space and Group Action:** The authors first formally define the weight space Φ(n) for a Mixture-of-n-Experts, which comprises the parameters for each expert (e.g., a ReLU feedforward network) and the gating mechanism (weights W and biases b). They then introduce a novel group action G(n) = (R^d × R) × S_n on this weight space. This group action consists of two components:
 - **Translation (c_W, c_b):** A vector `c_W ∈ R^d` added to all gating weights `W_i` and a scalar `c_b ∈ R` added to all gating biases `b_i`.
 - **Permutation (τ):** A permutation `τ ∈ S_n` that reorders both the experts (`θ_i`) and their corresponding gating parameters (`W_i, b_i`).
- **Proving Functional Invariance:** They prove that this group action preserves the functional behavior of MoE models (Proposition 3.1 for dense gating, Proposition 3.2 for sparse gating). For sparse gating (SMoE), additional technical conditions are required due to the discontinuity of the Top-k operator, restricting the invariance to an open and dense subset of the input space where gating scores are distinct.
- **Functional Equivalence Theorems (Theorems 4.1 and 4.2):** The core theoretical contribution lies in formally characterizing when two distinct parameter sets define the same MoE function.
 - **Dense Gating (Theorem 4.1):** Under assumptions that experts are pairwise distinct functions and differences between gating weights are distinct, if two dense MoE models are functionally equivalent, then they must have the same number of experts, and their parameters must be related by an element of the `G(n)` group action (i.e., a permutation of experts and a translation of gating parameters). The proof leverages the local affine structure of ReLU networks and a technical lemma (Lemma B.3) on the linear independence of exponential functions of polynomials, which helps equate coefficients in a functional identity.
 - **Sparse Gating (Theorem 4.2):** This is significantly more complex due to the discontinuous Top-k operator. The theorem for SMoE (with k > 1) requires stronger assumptions, notably that expert functions are *pairwise strongly distinct* (meaning they differ on a dense set) and that differences between gating weights are linearly independent. The conclusion is similar: functional equivalence implies parameters are related by an element of `G(n)`. The k=1 case is explicitly excluded due to additional, continuous symmetries (scaling invariance of argmax).
- **Sufficiency of Permutation Invariance for LMC:** Proposition D.1 demonstrates that the translation component (c_W, c_b) of the group action does *not* affect the LMC loss barrier. This implies that for LMC, only permutation alignment (reordering of experts and their internal units) needs to be considered, simplifying the problem.

#### 3.2. Algorithmic Approach: Permutation Alignment for MoEs

The authors propose a two-stage **Weight Matching Algorithm (Algorithm 1)** for aligning independently trained MoEs to discover LMC:

1. **Matching Order of Experts (Step 1):** This step aims to find the optimal permutation `τ` that aligns the overall role of experts between two MoE models `ϕ` and `ϕ'`. Two methods are proposed, both formulated as Linear Assignment Problems (LAPs) solvable by the Hungarian algorithm:
 - **Gating Weights Matching:** Compares centered gating weights `W` and biases `b` of experts using `L2` norm. Centering handles the translation invariance of the softmax.
 - **Internal Weights Matching:** Compares the internal parameter structure of experts using Gram matrices. Gram matrices of augmented weight matrices (`eA` and `eB`) are intrinsically permutation-invariant with respect to the hidden units *within* each expert (Proposition D.2), making them suitable for comparing expert functionality without prior internal neuron alignment.
2. **Aligning Expert Internal Weights (Step 2):** After identifying the best expert permutation `τ`, for each matched expert pair (`i` and `τ(i)`), their internal hidden units are aligned. This is achieved by applying existing Weight Matching algorithms (e.g., from or), which are also LAP-based, to each individual expert's MLP layers.

The overall algorithm has a time complexity of `O(n^3 + nh^3)`, where `n` is the number of experts and `h` is the hidden dimension of experts, making it scalable for large MoE models.

#### 3.3. Empirical Approach: Extensive Validation

- **Experimental Setup:**
 - **MoE Variants:** Dense MoE, Sparse MoE (SMoE with top-2 routing), and DeepSeekMoE (top-2 routed + one shared expert).
 - **Architectures:** Vision Transformers (ViT) for image tasks, GPT-2 for language tasks.
 - **Datasets:** MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-21k -> CIFAR-10/100 (transfer learning), ImageNet-1k, WikiText103, One Billion Word.
 - **Training:** Pretrained models are used, with only the MoE layer (replacing a FFN in a Transformer block) fine-tuned from multiple random seeds.
 - **LMC Evaluation:** Linear interpolation between checkpoint pairs is performed at 25 evenly spaced points, measuring test loss/accuracy.
- **Ablation Studies:** Investigate the impact of MoE layer placement (first, last, all layers) and number of Transformer layers on LMC. The effectiveness of the two expert order matching methods is also compared.

This robust methodological framework, combining deep mathematical analysis with scalable algorithmic design and comprehensive empirical testing, allows for a thorough investigation of LMC in MoE architectures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields significant theoretical and empirical findings regarding Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC) in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Characterization of MoE Symmetries:** The paper formally characterizes the functional equivalence of MoE architectures, demonstrating that their inherent symmetries are fully captured by a group action `G(n)` consisting of permutations of experts and translations of gating parameters. This is a foundational theoretical result that extends prior work on permutation invariance to MoE models.
- **Dense MoE Functional Equivalence (Theorem 4.1):** For dense MoE, under mild assumptions (pairwise distinct experts and distinct differences in gating weights), two models are functionally equivalent if and only if their parameters are related by an element of `G(n)`.
- **Sparse MoE Functional Equivalence (Theorem 4.2):** For sparse MoE (with `k > 1` experts), a similar equivalence holds, but under stronger assumptions (pairwise strongly distinct experts and linearly independent differences in gating weights). This highlights the increased complexity introduced by the discontinuous nature of sparse routing.
- **Translation Invariance of Loss Barrier:** A key theoretical insight (Proposition D.1) is that the translational component of the `G(n)` group action (adding `c_W` to gating weights and `c_b` to biases) does not impact the LMC loss barrier. This simplifies the LMC problem by indicating that only permutation symmetries need to be accounted for when searching for low-loss paths.

#### 4.2. Empirical Findings

- **Widespread Linear Mode Connectivity in MoE:** The experiments consistently demonstrate the presence of LMC across a wide array of MoE configurations, datasets, and model scales. This includes dense MoE, Sparse MoE (SMoE), and DeepSeekMoE (SMoE with shared experts), evaluated on image classification (MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet) and language modeling (WikiText103, One Billion Word) tasks (summarized in Table 5 and Figures 5-77). This confirms that MoE models, despite their complex dynamic routing, tend to converge to connected optima in the parameter space, similar to standard neural networks.
- **Effectiveness of the Proposed Alignment Algorithm:** The two-stage Weight Matching algorithm (Algorithm 1) successfully aligns independently trained MoE models, revealing the low-loss linear paths. The empirical results show that the algorithm significantly reduces the loss barrier compared to naive interpolation, often achieving near-zero barriers.
- **Comparable Performance of Expert Order Matching Methods:** Both proposed methods for matching expert order – Gating Weights Matching and Internal Weights Matching – demonstrate comparable effectiveness in identifying optimal permutations. They consistently achieve low ranks among all possible permutations and result in near-optimal loss barriers (Table 2 and 8). This suggests flexibility in how expert roles can be aligned.
- **Crucial Role of Expert Alignment:** The importance of correctly aligning expert order is strongly validated. Interpolation paths with suboptimal expert orderings often yield high loss barriers, approaching the "naive" interpolation (Figure 2). This indicates that MoE models do not exhibit trivial connectivity without accounting for their specific permutation symmetries.
- **Depth-Dependent LMC Sensitivity (Ablation Study):** Ablation studies on replacing FFNs with MoE modules at different Transformer layers show a depth-dependent effect (Table 3). LMC is generally more pronounced and robust when the MoE is placed in earlier layers, where models tend to be more sensitive to changes. MoEs in deeper layers, particularly the last one, exhibit less significant LMC behavior, possibly due to their reduced influence on overall convergence or the model's greater stability at later stages. This aligns with prior observations regarding layer sensitivity in standard Transformers (Appendix E).

These findings collectively expand the understanding of deep learning loss landscapes by demonstrating that LMC is not limited to simpler architectures but is a fundamental property that extends to complex, modular MoE models, provided their unique symmetries are properly handled.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant theoretical and practical implications for the field of deep learning:

- **Extended Understanding of Loss Landscape Geometry:** The most significant contribution is the extension of Linear Mode Connectivity (LMC) analysis to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures. This deepens our fundamental understanding of how complex, modular neural networks learn and optimize. The consistent observation of LMC in MoE suggests that even with dynamic, input-dependent routing, MoE models still inhabit "flat" and connected regions of the loss landscape, challenging intuitive notions of extremely rugged, non-convex optimization.
- **Principled Framework for MoE Analysis:** By rigorously characterizing the symmetries of MoE (through the group action and functional equivalence theorems) and developing a dedicated alignment algorithm, the paper provides a principled framework for studying and manipulating MoE models in their parameter space. This framework is essential for tasks beyond LMC, such as model fusion, knowledge transfer, and understanding architectural variations.
- **Implications for Model Ensembling and Generalization:** The existence of LMC suggests that different MoE solutions, even when independently trained, reside in similar or connected basins of attraction. This could inform and improve ensembling techniques for MoE models, such as Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA), leading to better generalization and more robust performance. If models can be linearly connected with low loss, then averaging their weights after alignment (a form of weight-space ensembling) is more likely to yield a better-performing, more generalizable model.
- **Foundation for Future Architecture-Specific LMC Studies:** The methodology developed for MoE, particularly the focus on characterizing architecture-specific symmetries and adapting alignment algorithms, sets a precedent for analyzing LMC in other complex, modern neural network architectures. The authors explicitly mention Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs) as future directions, underscoring the generalizability of their approach.
- **Improved Model Interpretability and Debugging:** Understanding how different MoE models are functionally equivalent and how their parameter spaces connect can offer insights into the learned representations and expert specializations. This could aid in interpreting why MoE models make certain predictions or fail in specific scenarios, potentially leading to better debugging and diagnostic tools.
- **Practical Tool for MoE Alignment:** The proposed Weight Matching algorithm offers a practical and scalable solution for aligning MoE models. Its efficiency (O(n^3 + nh^3)) makes it suitable for real-world applications involving large MoE models, facilitating various research and development tasks that require model comparison or manipulation in parameter space.

While the paper acknowledges the limitation of not providing theoretical bounds on the loss barrier, its comprehensive empirical validation across diverse settings lends strong credibility to its claims. Overall, this work significantly advances the theoretical and practical understanding of Mixture-of-Experts, a crucial step in developing more efficient, robust, and interpretable large-scale AI systems.
