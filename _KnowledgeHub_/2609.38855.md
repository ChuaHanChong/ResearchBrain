# Online Evolution Strategy for Flow-Matching VLA Policies via Self-Supervised Trajectory Distribution Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Online-ES adapts flow-matching VLA policies online by running an evolution strategy in action-trajectory space instead of parameter space. It samples diverse trajectories, perturbs them with Gaussian noise, executes them, and turns success-weighted perturbations into a self-supervised MSE loss with failure-aware repulsion. On LIBERO it lifts GR00T N1.5 from 94.8% to 97.2% average, close to reinforcement fine-tuning, without a value model.

## Problem

- Flow-matching VLAs learn ill-formed action distributions where successful and failed trajectories coexist for the same scene, so failures often come from unlucky noise samples.
- Pruning the latent noise space is task-specific and cannot disentangle per-chunk noise effects across long-horizon closed-loop rollouts with Markovian observation dependencies.
- RL fine-tuning methods for VLAs rely on learned value models or complex advantage estimation, while parameter-space ES is inefficient and produces unsafe actions for huge models.

## Method

- ==Trajectory-space Evolution Strategy==: samples N latent noises per resettable scene, then applies ==Gaussian perturbations== to each generated action chunk to explore locally around the pretrained behavior.
- Self-supervised ==feedback-weighted MSE loss== between detached perturbed and raw trajectories; gradients pass through the ==model Jacobian==, proven to give an unbiased estimate of the ES ascent direction.
- ==Failure-aware regularization== sets failed-rollout feedback to -1, adding a repulsive gradient away from failure regions, with success-to-failure buffer ratio kept above 2:1 for stability.

## Results

- Multi-task LIBERO adaptation raises the GR00T N1.5 baseline from **0.948 to 0.972** average success, beating **VLA-RL (0.810)** and **VLA-RFT (0.911)** but trailing **piRL (0.983)**.
- Single-task adaptation reaches nearly 100% success within **300 steps** with fixed scenes; with 50 random placements, e.g. a LIBERO-Long task rises from **0.784 to 0.956**.
- On a real AgiBot G1 bowl grasp, 150 online steps raise success from **32/100 to 54/100** and cut outright failures from **27 to 12**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generative policy stochasticity is free exploration: a VLA that sometimes succeeds needs its distribution reshaped, not new capabilities or extra human demonstrations.
> - Exploring in output space and projecting through the Jacobian sidesteps both parameter-space ES inefficiency and critic learning, a recipe portable to other generative policies.
> - Broad early exploration with annealed noise and negative feedback from failures both speed convergence, but real robots need trajectory smoothing to keep perturbations safe.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Many robot manipulation tasks can be completed along more than one workable path. A Flow Matching VLA is a policy that generates actions by sampling different behaviors from latent noise. In the same task setting, it can produce both successful and failed trajectories. Under the same task scenario, different noise draws can lead to different outcomes. The authors read this as a possibility: a failure may reflect a poor draw from the policy's learned trajectory distribution, rather than an inability to perform the task. Their response is to nudge candidate action trajectories, execute them, and use the outcomes to update the action-generating part of the policy. They call the method Online-ES.

Trajectories from different latent-noise seeds in one task scenario. Solid green curves are successful seeds and dashed red curves are failed seeds. The paper presents this as a representative example.

### 2. Learning from tested paths

One alternative would be to choose or prune bad noise draws. The authors argue this is difficult. A long action sequence consists of interdependent chunks, and the unfavorable noise regions may differ across tasks. This is their rationale for preferring online adaptation. It is not a measured head-to-head result against a pruning system.

Online-ES shifts exploration to trajectories instead, because execution feedback is available there. For a resettable scenario, it samples multiple trajectories, perturbs them with Gaussian noise, executes the perturbed trajectories, and records the outcomes. The training objective is a feedback-weighted mean-squared error between each original trajectory and its perturbed counterpart. The perturbed target is detached, so gradients flow through the original policy-generated trajectory. Only the action expert is fine-tuned, and other model modules remain frozen.

The paper's derivation gives an unbiased estimate of the gradient of a Gaussian-smoothed execution-feedback objective, transferred through the model Jacobian. "Unbiased" describes the estimator. It is not proof that the policy finds a globally optimal behavior.

Feedback is failure-aware: successful trajectories receive +1 and failures −1. In the authors' interpretation, the successful term provides an attractive signal, while failures push the distribution away from previously explored failure regions. The procedure caps the failure share so that successful guidance dominates.

This design differs from [[2510.25889|πRL's paper]], which adds a value critic and computes advantages. Online-ES uses neither a learned value model nor an advantage calculation. It still backpropagates through policy outputs.

### 3. What improves

The main simulation evidence comes from multi-task, random-scene LIBERO. The reported average is the mean of success rates across four suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long. Each suite includes 10 tasks and 50 object placements per task.

On this average, Online-ES scores **0.972**, compared with **0.948** for its baseline. It improves over that baseline in all four suites, but πRL reports a higher average of **0.983**. The two methods start from different models. Online-ES builds on GR00T N1.5 augmented with GeoVLA depth features, while πRL's result is based on π0.5. The table therefore shows competitive reported performance. It is not a controlled comparison of adaptation methods.

LIBERO success-rate scores by suite and average. πRL (based on π0.5) has the highest average at 0.983. Online-ES (GR00T N1.5 with GeoVLA depth features) scores 0.972, compared with 0.948 for its baseline. Upward arrows mark gains over its own baseline.

On a real robot, the method was tested on a single grasping subtask with a pink bowl. After 150 Online-ES adaptation steps, the policy succeeded in **54 of 100** trials, compared with **32/100** for the baseline. The baseline was trained from 300 teleoperation demonstrations. During adaptation, perturbations were smoothed to avoid high-frequency joint oscillations.

### 4. A distribution, not a single verdict

When the policy produces both workable and failing paths, Online-ES uses execution feedback to shift what it generates. It does not treat one failure as evidence that the task is unsolvable. The improvements hold in these tested settings.

The method's limits are also clear. It requires repeated environment interaction and success/failure feedback. "Self-supervised" here means learning from the policy's own executed trajectories and outcomes. The authors also note that their use of interaction experience could be more efficient.
