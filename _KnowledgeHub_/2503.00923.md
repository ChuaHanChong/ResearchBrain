# HWC-Loco: A Hierarchical Whole-Body Control Approach to Robust Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> HWC-Loco introduces a hierarchical whole-body control framework for robust humanoid locomotion, explicitly learning to recover from safety-critical situations while dynamically balancing task performance and safety. It achieves superior success rates on challenging terrains and under disturbances in both simulation and real-world deployment on Unitree H1 and G1 robots.

## Problem

- Achieving robust and adaptable locomotion for humanoid robots in complex, unpredictable real-world environments due to their high degrees of freedom and complex dynamics.
- Bridging the 'Sim-to-Real' gap, where policies trained in simplified simulations often fail to perform robustly when deployed on physical robots.
- Balancing task performance with safety guarantees, as traditional robust optimization methods often lead to overly conservative behaviors that compromise a robot's ability to complete tasks efficiently.

## Method

- A hierarchical whole-body control framework with a Goal-Tracking Policy (π1) for efficient task completion, a Safety Recovery Policy (π2) for emergency handling, and a High-Level Planning Policy (π0) to dynamically select between them.
- Policy learning is formulated as a robust constrained Reinforcement Learning problem, where the goal-tracking policy focuses on task rewards and mimic learning, while the safety recovery policy is trained under an 'extreme-case uncertainty set' using ZMP constraints.
- Utilizes Isaac Gym for massively parallel simulation training, incorporates human expert motion data via Wasserstein-1 distance for naturalness, and deploys lightweight policies on physical Unitree H1 and G1 humanoid robots.

## Results

- HWC-Loco achieved high success rates on challenging terrains in simulation (e.g., 99.98% on stairs at low speeds, 84.34% at high speeds) and demonstrated effective navigation on real-world stairs and slopes.
- It exhibited superior robustness under various simulated disturbances (e.g., 95.88% success under low-frequency external forces, 81.27% under high impulse disturbances) and recovered stability during real-world pushes and kicks.
- The framework demonstrated natural, adaptable locomotion styles, transitioning from stationary to walking (1.0 m/s with arm swings) to running (2.5 m/s with bent elbows), and successfully scaled to a different robot (Unitree G1) and complex expressive motion tracking tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating policy learning as a robust constrained Reinforcement Learning problem allows the system to explicitly learn recovery from safety-critical scenarios under extreme-case uncertainties, enhancing real-world robustness.
> - A hierarchical control structure, composed of goal-tracking, safety recovery, and high-level planning policies, dynamically resolves the trade-off between task performance and stability, adapting behavior based on perceived risk.
> - Integrating human motion imitation via Wasserstein distance and dynamic constraints like Zero Moment Point (ZMP) guidance ensures both natural, human-like movements and inherent stability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are increasingly recognized as crucial embodiments of artificial intelligence, with the potential to integrate seamlessly into human workspaces and perform diverse tasks. The development of effective control models for these complex physical structures is paramount, yet inherently challenging, particularly when dealing with discrepancies between simulated training environments and real-world deployment scenarios (the "Sim-to-Real" gap).

Historically, humanoid control has relied on **classic model-based optimization methods**. While providing precise control, these approaches demand exact and comprehensive modeling of the robot's structure, kinematics, and dynamics, which is computationally intensive and often impractical in varied, unpredictable environments. Their lack of scalability limits their real-world utility.

More recently, **learning-based approaches, particularly Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, have gained prominence. These methods aim to learn end-to-end control policies from data, offering promising generalizability. However, their primary limitation is the Sim-to-Real gap, as policies trained in simplified simulations often fail to perform robustly in the complex real world. Prior attempts to bridge this gap include introducing additional regularization (which can compromise control efficiency) or employing domain randomization (which may not capture specific safety-critical patterns).

Another area of active research is **whole-body control (WBC)** for humanoid robots. While impressive strides have been made in simulating avatars or enabling robots to imitate complex human-like motions (e.g., boxing, dancing), these often depend on intricate reward shaping or the availability of extensive motion priors. Such dependencies hinder generalization across different humanoid robots or scenarios where motion priors are unavailable.

HWC-Loco directly addresses these critical challenges. It positions itself as an advancement in robust control for humanoid locomotion by:
- **Reconceptualizing policy learning as a robust optimization problem** specifically under misspecified environmental dynamics, aiming to explicitly learn recovery from safety-critical scenarios. This moves beyond traditional robust optimization's tendency for overly conservative behavior, which can impede task completion.
- **Introducing a hierarchical control structure** to dynamically manage the inherent trade-off between aggressive goal-tracking (task performance) and conservative safety recovery. This is a significant departure from monolithic control policies.
- **Leveraging human behavior norms (mimic learning)** and dynamic constraints like the Zero Moment Point (ZMP) to guide policy learning, enhancing both naturalness and stability without rigid reliance on motion priors for *all* aspects of generalization.
- **Demonstrating strong Sim-to-Real transferability** and scalability across different humanoid platforms and tasks, showcasing its potential as a foundational solution for real-world humanoid deployment.

In essence, HWC-Loco aims to push the boundaries of humanoid locomotion by developing a control framework that is not only robust to unforeseen disturbances and environmental variations but also maintains high task performance and human-like naturalness, a critical combination for practical embodied AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a **robust whole-body control algorithm, HWC-Loco, specifically tailored for humanoid locomotion tasks**, that can operate reliably and effectively across diverse and challenging environments, particularly when faced with discrepancies between simulated training and real-world deployment.

The motivation stems from several critical challenges in current humanoid robotics:

- **Complexity of Humanoid Robots and Control Learning:** Humanoid robots possess highly complex physical structures with numerous degrees of freedom. Learning a control model that can function robustly across a wide array of environments is intrinsically difficult.
- **The Simulation-to-Real (Sim2Real) Gap:** Policies are typically trained in simulated environments, which often fail to perfectly replicate the nuances and unpredictable dynamics of real-world deployment. Existing solutions to bridge this gap, such as aggressive regularization or unstructured domain randomization, frequently lead to suboptimal control efficiency or fail to specifically address safety-critical scenarios.
- **Limitations of Traditional Robust Optimization:** While robust optimization aims to guarantee performance under worst-case scenarios, applying it directly to control problems often results in overly conservative behaviors. Such conservatism, while ensuring safety, can severely compromise the robot's ability to efficiently complete its assigned tasks, creating a problematic trade-off between safety and performance.
- **Need for Dynamic Trade-off Resolution:** There is a crucial need for a control policy that can dynamically balance the objectives of task performance (e.g., tracking velocity commands) and maintaining safety (e.g., preventing falls) in real-time, adapting its behavior based on the current situation and perceived risk.

To address these motivations, HWC-Loco sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

- **Explicitly learn to recover from safety-critical scenarios:** By formulating policy learning as a robust optimization problem, the system is designed to proactively handle unexpected disturbances and challenging situations.
- **Dynamically resolve the trade-off between goal-tracking and safety recovery:** Introduce a hierarchical policy that can intelligently switch or blend behaviors to prioritize task completion when safe, and transition to recovery strategies when stability is threatened.
- **Incorporate human behavior norms and dynamic constraints:** Utilize expert human motion data and established stability criteria (like ZMP) to guide the learning process, ensuring natural and inherently stable locomotion.
- **Demonstrate superior performance:** Validate the proposed HWC-Loco against state-of-the-art methods across various terrains, robot structures, and locomotion tasks, under both simulated and real-world conditions, proving its effectiveness, robustness, naturalness, and scalability.

By tackling these challenges, HWC-Loco aims to deliver a more reliable and versatile humanoid control solution, pushing towards the practical deployment of humanoids in real-world, safety-critical environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HWC-Loco employs a **hierarchical whole-body control approach** to achieve robust humanoid locomotion. The core idea is to decouple the complex problem of robust control into more manageable sub-problems, handled by specialized low-level policies, coordinated by a high-level planning policy.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Robust Optimization

The research formulates the humanoid locomotion task within a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The objective is to maximize task rewards while adhering to specific constraints, notably replacing traditional penalty rewards and manual regularizations with:
- **Mimic Learning Objective:** A constraint that ensures the robot's behavior distribution aligns closely with human expert motions (using Wasserstein-1 distance). This promotes naturalness.
- **Feasibility Constraint:** A constraint based on a feasibility function $\phi(\tau)$ that captures the safety and stability of a trajectory.
Crucially, to address the Sim2Real gap and achieve robustness, the problem is framed as a **robust constrained RL objective (Equation 4)**. This involves:
- Maximizing task rewards and mimic learning performance based on the *learning environment's dynamics* ($P^L_T$).
- Ensuring the *feasibility constraint* is satisfied under a *set of mismatched dynamics* ($P^L_\alpha$), effectively guaranteeing worst-case safety without making the task-oriented behavior overly conservative.

#### 3.2. Hierarchical Policy Structure

HWC-Loco is composed of three key policies:

**a) Goal-Tracking Policy ($\pi_1$):**
- **Objective:** To efficiently and naturally track given velocity commands within the simulated training environment.
- **Learning:** This policy is trained to maximize task rewards (e.g., tracking linear and angular velocities) and simultaneously minimize the Wasserstein-1 distance between its generated motion and expert human motion data (e.g., from CMU MoCap). This mimic learning is implemented using an adversarial imitation learning framework, where a discriminator ($f_d$) is trained to distinguish between robot and expert motions, and the policy is trained to "fool" the discriminator. The constrained RL problem is converted into an unconstrained objective using Lagrange duality.
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for training.

**b) Safety Recovery Policy ($\pi_2$):**
- **Objective:** To handle emergency events, recover from safety-critical situations, and prevent task failures (e.g., falls).
- **Learning:** This policy explicitly considers the robust optimization aspect. It is trained under an "extreme-case uncertainty set" ($P^L_\alpha$) designed to simulate real-world disturbances. This set includes:
 - Application of multi-scale external forces and torques.
 - Introduction of random, high-intensity noise to proprioceptive information and PD gains (simulating hardware malfunctions).
 - Resampling of velocity commands to simulate "malicious" or unexpected inputs.
 - Standard domain randomization.
- **Key Constraint:** The Zero-Moment Point (ZMP) constraint (Equation 11) is used as the primary feasibility indicator ($\phi(s,a)$). ZMP determines the point where ground reaction force has no horizontal moment; maintaining it within the support polygon is critical for bipedal stability. The policy learns to coordinate whole-body movements to satisfy this constraint under disturbances.
- **Reward:** While it shares a similar task reward structure with $\pi_1$, it uses larger tolerance values for velocity tracking errors, acting more as a "back-tracking" reward to guide the robot back to a stable state. An additional "stand reward" promotes stable posture.

**c) High-Level Planning Policy ($\pi_0$):**
- **Objective:** To dynamically coordinate and select between the goal-tracking ($\pi_1$) and safety recovery ($\pi_2$) low-level policies based on the robot's current state and historical observations.
- **Learning:** This policy operates on a discrete action space (selecting either $\pi_1$ or $\pi_2$). It is trained to maximize task rewards while minimizing penalties for over-frequent policy switching and task termination/failure. A hyperparameter $\alpha$ allows practitioners to adjust the trade-off between safety guarantees and task completion.
- **Algorithm:** Double-DQN (Algorithm 1 in Appendix) is utilized for training.
- **Practical Deployment:** To handle the absence of privileged information (like external disturbances, terrain dynamics) in real-world settings, a VAE-based estimator is employed to infer such information from partial observations. Frequency encoding and a learnable prior are also used to enhance robustness.

#### 3.3. Training and Deployment Details

- **Simulated Environment:** Isaac Gym, utilizing GPU-accelerated physics simulation and massively parallel environments.
- **Robot Platforms:** Unitree H1 (19 DoF) and Unitree G1 (23 DoF) for real-world deployment.
- **Control Frequency:** 100 Hz.
- **Training Schedule:** Terrain curriculum method increases difficulty as performance improves.
- **System Design:** Policies are optimized for lightweight designs (under 3MB) and exported in JIT format to ensure real-time inference on onboard computers.

This hierarchical and robust optimization-based approach allows HWC-Loco to achieve a sophisticated balance between high-performance task execution and guaranteed safety, leading to superior and more reliable humanoid locomotion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HWC-Loco spans four fundamental perspectives: effectiveness, robustness, naturalness, and scalability, with extensive comparisons against state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods and ablation studies, conducted in both simulated and real-world environments.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness: Locomotion Across Diverse Terrains

- **Simulated Environments:** HWC-Loco consistently achieved the highest success rates across challenging terrains like slopes and stairs (Table 1).
 - Under low-speed commands, HWC-Loco achieved near-perfect success rates (100% on slopes, 99.98% on stairs), significantly outperforming baselines like DreamWaQ (74.32% on stairs).
 - Under high-speed commands, the advantage was even more pronounced, particularly on stairs, where HWC-Loco achieved an 84.34% success rate compared to DreamWaQ's 60.58%. This highlights HWC-Loco's ability to prioritize stability in safety-critical situations, dynamically adapting its gait rather than rigidly maintaining high velocity, even if it slightly decreases goal-tracking performance.
- **Realistic Environments:** Qualitative results (Figures 10 and 11 in Appendix B.1) demonstrate the robot's ability to climb 15 cm stairs and 20° slopes, and navigate outdoor terrains including flat ground, grass, and varying slopes. The system dynamically activates the recovery policy when facing challenges, then smoothly transitions back to goal-tracking.

#### 4.2. Robustness: Stable Control Under Disturbances

- **Simulated Disturbances:** HWC-Loco exhibited superior robustness across various disturbance types (Table 2).
 - **External Force/Torque:** Achieved 95.88% success under low-frequency and 75.95% under constant disturbances, significantly outperforming DreamWaQ (85.92% and 51.31% respectively), validating the recovery mechanism's efficacy against strong and persistent forces.
 - **Impulse Disturbances on CoM:** Maintained high success rates (94.84% low-impulse, 81.27% high-impulse), showing effective mitigation of sudden impacts by quickly adjusting body posture (e.g., lowering body, waving arms).
 - **Payload Disturbances:** Performed best (87.43% low payload, 69.86% high payload), demonstrating robustness even to unseen variations in robot mass distribution.
- **Realistic Deployment:** Qualitative tests (Figures 12 and 13 in Appendix B.1) showed the robot recovering stability under continuous pushes, pulls, and kicks. The controller dynamically switches between goal-tracking and recovery modes, adapting action distributions to environmental changes.

#### 4.3. Naturalness: Imitating Human-like Movements

- HWC-Loco effectively adapted its locomotion style to commanded velocities (Figure 4). At 0 m/s, the robot remained stationary. At 1.0 m/s, it exhibited natural walking with coordinated arm swings and straight elbows. At 2.5 m/s, it transitioned to running with longer strides and bent elbows, closely resembling human running. This dynamic adaptation of stride length and step frequency contrasts with prior approaches that often maintain fixed step frequencies or show unnatural in-place stepping at zero velocity.

#### 4.4. Scalability: Extending HWC-Loco to New Tasks

- **Cross-Embodiment Validation:** HWC-Loco was successfully deployed on the Unitree G1 robot, which is smaller, lighter, and has more DoFs than the Unitree H1 (Table 3). The G1 outperformed the H1 across all metrics (success rate, goal tracking, human-likeness), largely due to its increased agility and whole-body coordination, enabling behaviors like straight-knee walking (Figure 15) which is challenging for the H1.
- **Expressive Motion Tracking:** HWC-Loco demonstrated strong performance in tracking complex expressive motions like punching and dancing under impulse disturbances (Table 4). During a punching motion with an impulse, the robot dynamically activated the recovery policy, prioritizing ZMP stability by lowering its body and adjusting its gait, rather than strictly adhering to the original motion. This "backtracking" for stability (Figure 14) showcases its robust adaptive capabilities beyond simple locomotion.

Overall, the results strongly support HWC-Loco's claims of superior performance across diverse scenarios, highlighting its effectiveness, robustness to disturbances, human-like motion generation, and generalizability to different robot embodiments and complex tasks. The hierarchical structure and robust optimization formulation are key contributors to these advancements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HWC-Loco represents a significant advancement in the field of humanoid robot control, particularly for reliable and robust operation in real-world, dynamic environments. Its core contribution lies in the **hierarchical control framework with an embedded safety recovery mechanism** that intelligently navigates the crucial trade-off between task performance and safety guarantees.

The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Safety:** By explicitly formulating policy learning as a robust optimization problem and introducing a dedicated safety recovery policy, HWC-Loco addresses one of the most critical challenges in humanoid robotics: stable operation under unexpected disturbances and unmodeled dynamics. This moves beyond overly conservative traditional robust control, enabling a more practical balance between safety and performance. The dynamic ZMP constraint and training under "extreme-case uncertainty" are key to this robust behavior.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The methodology directly tackles the Sim-to-Real gap by focusing on robust policy learning under misspecified dynamics and validating performance extensively on physical robots. This brings humanoid robots closer to real-world deployment beyond controlled laboratory settings.
- **Natural and Adaptive Locomotion:** The integration of human motion imitation via Wasserstein distance ensures that the robot's movements are not just functional but also natural and human-like. Furthermore, the ability to dynamically adapt gait and posture based on velocity commands and terrain variations (e.g., transitioning from stationary to walking to running) is crucial for intuitive human-robot interaction and seamless integration into human environments.
- **Scalability and Generalizability:** The successful deployment on two different Unitree humanoid platforms (H1 and G1) and generalization to complex motion tracking tasks demonstrates the framework's scalability and adaptability to diverse robot embodiments and control objectives. This suggests that the principles of HWC-Loco could be applied to a wider range of humanoid applications.
- **Foundational for Safety-Critical Applications:** The paper explicitly positions HWC-Loco as a "foundational solution for safety-critical applications" such as industrial automation and assistive robotics. In environments where human safety is paramount, reliable and predictable robot behavior under unforeseen circumstances is indispensable. HWC-Loco's ability to robustly recover from disturbances and maintain stability makes it highly relevant for such scenarios.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its significant contributions, the authors acknowledge several limitations:
1. **Discrete Policy Transitions:** The current high-level policy makes discrete decisions between low-level controllers. A jointly optimized hierarchical framework could enable smoother and more adaptive transitions, potentially leading to even more fluid and efficient behavior.
2. **Degrees of Freedom (DoFs):** The Unitree H1 robot, used for real-world deployment, has 19 DoFs, which is relatively limited for complex whole-body coordination and expressive recovery behaviors. Utilizing humanoids with more DoFs could unlock richer and more effective recovery strategies.
3. **Simulated Disturbances:** The recovery policy is trained on simulated dynamic scenarios that may not fully capture the vast diversity and complexity of real-world disturbances. Incorporating more realistic or adversarial perturbations (e.g., generated by other adversarial policies) could further enhance the recovery ability.

A promising direction for future research highlighted by the authors is the **integration of HWC-Loco with loco-manipulation skills**. This would enable safety-critical control across a broader range of tasks involving interaction with objects, further expanding the utility and impact of robust humanoid robots in practical applications.
