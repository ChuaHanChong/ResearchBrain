# RoboCoach: World Models as Active Coaches for Compositional Robot Skills

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboCoach is a world-model-guided coaching framework for long-horizon robot manipulation. Its Route-Imagine-Diagnose-Improve loop runs reusable LoRA skill experts inside CoachWorld, a shared action-conditioned video world model. A progress judge finds the first subtask that times out. RoboCoach then requests targeted subtask demonstrations and updates only the matching experts. With 150 extra demonstrations, real-robot success rises from 13.3% to 75.0% on Franka and from 40.0% to 83.8% on AgileX.

## Problem

- Covering the long tail of skill compositions with extra end-to-end demonstrations is costly, especially on physical robots where every trajectory consumes hardware time, environment resets, and safety oversight.
- Action-conditioned world models are used to simulate experience, evaluate policies, or optimize them in imagination, but not to decide where scarce real demonstrations would be most valuable.
- Active and corrective imitation learning choose which supervision to collect, but existing pipelines do not send it to the specific reusable policy component responsible for a long-horizon failure.

## Method

- **Route-Imagine-Diagnose-Improve (RIDI) loop**: a router sends the next unfinished subtask to a ==LoRA== skill expert on a shared VLA backbone (pi0.5 or MolmoAct2), which is then rolled out in closed loop.
- **CoachWorld**: a ==Wan-DiT== video world model trained by ==conditional flow matching== on mixed-domain data. A two-slot end-effector action interface, camera-calibrated trajectory projection and ==PRoPE== let it condition across embodiments.
- First-timeout diagnosis and scorecard: a ==RoboMeter== progress judge with frozen thresholds records the first timed-out subtask-expert pair when at least two of three seeds agree. Task-balanced timeout mass picks the top pairs for new demonstrations and adapter updates.

## Results

- Imagined and deployed success correlate strongly across 22 task-policy pairs (Spearman **0.840**, Pearson **0.820**). Mixed-domain CoachWorld reaches FVD **51.71** on DROID-180, versus **102.97** for Ctrl-World.
- After 150 subtask demonstrations, real-robot success reaches **75.0%** on Franka and **83.8%** on AgileX, versus **30.0%** and **47.5%** for uniform acquisition with a shared adapter.
- Sending the same targeted demonstrations to skill experts instead of a shared adapter adds **3.4 pp** on LIBERO and **13.2 pp** on RoboTwin. Coached experts average **35.0%** on four held-out compositions, versus **0%** for the baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Where supervision lands matters as much as what is collected. Targeted data put into a shared adapter barely helps or even hurts, while the same data put into the matching experts helps clearly.
> - World models can be useful as diagnostic coaches that decide where scarce real data goes. This role needs rank-level agreement with deployment, not pixel-perfect simulation.
> - Modular skill experts make long-horizon failures traceable to one skill and let improvements recombine, so unseen compositions and reorderings succeed where a single fine-tuned policy scores zero.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Long-horizon robot tasks, like making tea or setting a table, are made of reusable skills. A mistake early in the sequence can spoil everything that follows. Teaching every possible sequence with new end-to-end demonstrations is costly, especially on physical robots, where each trajectory requires collection effort, hardware time, resets, and safety oversight.

The authors' approach is to imagine a robot executing a task and use a progress judge to mark the first subtask that remains unresolved. The system then requests demonstrations for that subtask and updates its corresponding reusable skill expert. They call the system RoboCoach. On a Franka robot, 150 additional subtask demonstrations raised its complete-task success rate from 13.3% to 75.0%.

### 2. From failure to a teachable skill

The loop has four stages: Route, Imagine, Diagnose, Improve.

**Route.** A router breaks a long instruction into the next unfinished subtask and dispatches its reusable expert. Different object-specific subtasks can share one skill expert. The experts are LoRA adapters, which are small, skill-specific updates to a shared vision-language-action (VLA) backbone.

**Imagine.** The active expert acts inside CoachWorld, an action-conditioned model that predicts video observations from the robot's actions. Each generated chunk is fed back as the next observation, so the rollout is closed-loop.

**Diagnose.** After each chunk, a progress judge estimates whether the active subtask is complete. If its calibrated threshold is not reached before its time limit, the trial ends and records that subtask–expert pair as the first timeout. This identifies the first judged subtask to time out, not necessarily the sole physical cause of failure.

**Improve.** A scorecard ranks pairs by how often each is the first timeout within a task, averaged so each task has equal weight. Each imagined trial uses three world-model seeds. Only a modal diagnosis supported by at least two seeds is retained. The top two pairs each receive half the round's accepted demonstrations, which human operators check for safety and collect. Only the selected expert adapters change; the backbone and unselected adapters remain fixed.

System overview: route an unfinished subtask to its expert, predict its closed-loop rollout, identify the first missed deadline, and update that expert with targeted demonstrations.

### 3. What the matched comparisons show

The metric is complete-task success, averaged equally across tasks. A task counts as successful only when the complete instructed sequence succeeds. All conditions freeze the VLA backbone and train LoRA adapters.

**On hardware**, the comparison method acquired demonstrations uniformly and updated a shared adapter. On Franka, it reached 30.0% on the same task suite and budget, against RoboCoach's 75.0%. Success is averaged across three tasks, each evaluated over 20 trials. This is evidence of higher task success under a fixed demonstration budget. It does not show fewer attempts, faster elapsed time, or reduced computation.

**In simulation** (LIBERO and RoboTwin, 50 trials per task, three rounds of 100 accepted subtask demonstrations), one comparison isolates where the update goes. RoboCoach and "Single VLA + WM-targeted" receive the same scorecard-selected demonstrations. RoboCoach updates the corresponding skill experts, while the baseline updates one shared adapter. At the final budget, RoboCoach leads 71.2% to 67.8% on LIBERO and 68.0% to 54.8% on RoboTwin.

Targeted acquisition alone is not uniformly helpful. With a shared adapter, targeting changes final success by +0.8 points on LIBERO and −4.8 on RoboTwin, relative to uniform acquisition. The benefit in this comparison comes from routing the same targeted supervision to experts.

### 4. Boundaries

RoboCoach shows that an imagined failure can guide both what to teach and where to apply that teaching. Under matched demonstration budgets, this produced higher complete-task success. The diagnosis has limits. Agreement across world-model seeds reduces sensitivity to random variation but cannot rule out systematic model bias. Occluded objects and out-of-view contact remain difficult, and humans still check safety and collect the requested demonstrations.
