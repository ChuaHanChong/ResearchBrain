# Suppression Sticks, Locality Is Fragile: A Closed-Loop Target-and-Control Audit of Task-Vector Negation in VLA Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> This research conducts a closed-loop audit of task-vector subtraction in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, revealing that while efficient for suppressing a target skill, its behavioral locality is fragile and context-dependent. The study found that suppressed skills are often masked rather than permanently erased, exhibiting rapid relearnability.

## Problem

- Efficiently modifying or removing specific learned behaviors in complex multitask VLA policies is challenging due to the need for safety, deprecation, or user-driven deletion requests.
- Existing model editing methods like Task Arithmetic (TA) primarily focused on static models or additive operations, leaving the behavioral implications of skill *subtraction* for *closed-loop robot control* largely unexplored.
- Current Machine Unlearning (MU) techniques are often computationally intensive or require significant data/gradient access at edit time, making them infeasible for large VLA models and real-time application.

## Method

- The study designed a **closed-loop target-and-control audit** to evaluate per-skill ==task-vector subtraction== in multitask VLA policies using the MergeVLA framework.
- The edited model was constructed by subtracting a scaled per-skill task vector from the multitask goal model, a ==closed-form, data- and gradient-free operation== at edit time.
- Behavioral locality was assessed using closed-loop success rates on the LIBERO benchmark, measuring both target skill suppression and ==collateral damage== to nominally unrelated control skills across various VLA architectures.

## Results

- On LIBERO-Goal, subtraction yielded Target–Control Separation for **5** skills (**57.8%** mean control retention), Resistance for **3** skills, and Global Collapse for **2** skills.
- Locality proved fragile on held-out states; while target suppression remained robust, mean control retention dropped to **52%**, and in **4 out of 5** cases, at least one control skill was driven to **0%** success.
- Cross-architecture consistency was observed qualitatively, but selectivity largely broke down on other LIBERO suites (Spatial, Object, Long10), where target suppression consistently resulted in control skill collapse (e.g., **5.8%** mean SR on Object suite).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Closed-loop, target-and-control evaluation is critical for assessing model editing in embodied AI, as static-loss diagnostics fail to capture the fragile and heterogeneous behavioral locality observed in dynamic systems.
> - Task-vector subtraction effectively suppresses target skills in specific VLA contexts, but its behavioral locality is fragile and highly dependent on the task's separability; multi-skill negation often leads to global collapse.
> - Suppressed skills are shown to be quickly re-learnable from negated models, suggesting that task-vector subtraction induces behavioral masking rather than permanent knowledge deletion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development and deployment of increasingly sophisticated multitask Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies necessitate methods for modifying or removing specific learned behaviors. Such modifications may be required for safety, deprecation, or user-driven deletion requests. The existing landscape for model editing and machine unlearning presents several approaches, each with limitations concerning VLA policies.

**Task Arithmetic (TA)**, introduced by Ilharco et al. (2023), offers a closed-form, data- and gradient-free method for editing models by adding or subtracting "task vectors." These vectors represent the difference between an expert model (fine-tuned on a specific task) and a base model. Prior applications of TA primarily focused on model *merging* (addition of task vectors) or observed *negation* as a side effect in *static classification or generation models*. The behavioral implications of task-vector *subtraction* for *closed-loop robot control* remained largely unexplored.

**Machine Unlearning (MU)** research aims to erase specific knowledge from models without requiring a full retraining cycle. Existing MU techniques often fall into two categories:
1. **Exact and Certified Methods**: These methods offer strong guarantees of knowledge removal but are computationally intensive, typically requiring access to the "retain set" and compute resources comparable to full retraining. For large VLA models (e.g., 500M+ parameters), such approaches are often infeasible.
2. **Gradient-Based Methods**: Approaches like gradient ascent (NegGrad) or those incorporating retain terms (NegGrad+, Gradient-Difference) can perform targeted removal. However, they generally require access to specific "forget" and "retain" datasets and involve backpropagation during the removal process, making them data- and/or gradient-dependent at edit time.

This paper identifies a critical gap: while task arithmetic provides an efficient, closed-form intervention (data- and gradient-free at edit time given precomputed task vectors), its behavioral *locality* in *closed-loop robot control* has not been systematically audited. Prior work on static models cannot capture the cumulative effects of weight perturbations in dynamic, embodied systems, where a seemingly minor change might lead to unintended cascading failures in unrelated skills over a full rollout. The safety-relevant question—whether removing one skill silently degrades others—lacked empirical evaluation for this specific, efficient editing paradigm in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to conduct a detailed, closed-loop audit of per-skill task-vector subtraction in multitask VLA policies. This audit aimed to thoroughly characterize the behavioral locality of such edits, specifically whether a targeted skill could be suppressed without negatively impacting other, nominally unrelated skills.

The motivation for this investigation was multi-faceted:

- **Efficiency of Intervention**: Task-vector arithmetic offers a potentially highly efficient method for model modification. It is a closed-form operation that can be executed in under a second on a CPU, assuming expert task vectors are precomputed. This efficiency is a significant advantage over retraining or computationally intensive gradient-based unlearning methods, which can be prohibitive for large-scale VLA models.
- **Need for Targeted Behavioral Control**: As VLA policies are deployed in real-world scenarios, the ability to selectively remove or modify specific learned behaviors becomes crucial. This could be driven by safety concerns (e.g., an unsafe learned maneuver), obsolescence (e.g., a deprecated interaction), or user privacy requests (e.g., deleting knowledge associated with specific data).
- **Assessing Behavioral Locality in Dynamic Systems**: A core concern is the *locality* of an edit—its ability to affect only the intended target while preserving other functionalities. While task arithmetic showed promise for static models, its implications for dynamic, embodied robot control were unclear. In robotics, a small weight perturbation might accumulate over a closed-loop rollout, leading to unexpected and potentially severe degradation of unrelated skills. Static-loss diagnostics, commonly used in previous model editing studies, are insufficient to capture these behavioral consequences.
- **Addressing Limitations of Prior Work**: Existing research on task arithmetic primarily focused on additive operations for model merging or treated negation as a secondary observation on static tasks. Furthermore, most machine unlearning methods required significant computational resources or data access at removal time. This work sought to specifically evaluate the most efficient form of task-vector negation for skill removal in a complex, dynamic robotic context, thereby filling a critical gap in the understanding of its practical utility and safety implications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research designed a closed-loop audit to evaluate task-vector subtraction on VLA policies.

- **Task-Vector Formulation**: The study built upon the MergeVLA framework, which employs a multitask goal model ($\theta_{Goal}$) trained on multiple skills and per-task experts ($\theta_{exp,i}$) fine-tuned from a shared base initialization ($\theta_{base}$). Following prior work, a per-skill task vector $\tau_i$ was defined as the weight delta: $\tau_i = \theta_{exp,i} - \theta_{base}$. These vectors were computed across the VLM backbone, action head (AH), and proprio projector (PP) components.
- **Negation Mechanism**: The edited model ($\theta_{edit}$) was constructed by subtracting the scaled task vector $\tau_i$ from the multitask goal model: $\theta_{edit} = \theta_{Goal} - \alpha \cdot \tau_i$, where $\alpha > 0$ controls the suppression strength. This edit is performed as a single CPU tensor operation, making it data-free and gradient-free at edit time, assuming precomputed expert deltas.
- **Closed-Loop Target-and-Control Audit**:
 - **Models**: The primary model used was MergeVLA-LIBERO-Goal, a 1.4B parameter policy (Qwen2.5-0.5B language backbone + dinosiglip-224px vision encoder + 0.16B action expert). Additional evaluations were conducted on OpenVLA-7B (a discrete-token, ~10x larger backbone) and $\pi_{0.5}$ (a 3B flow-matching model) to assess cross-architecture generality. Per-task experts for MergeVLA were LoRA fine-tuned.
 - **Benchmarks**: Experiments primarily used the LIBERO-Goal benchmark (10 tasks), with LIBERO-Spatial, -Object, and -Long10 suites utilized for cross-suite generalization tests.
 - **Evaluation Metric**: Closed-loop success rate (SR) was the primary metric, obtained from 20 rollout trials per (task, condition) cell. This behavioral metric is crucial for capturing the cumulative effects of actions in dynamic environments.
 - **Audit Protocol**: For each target skill, its corresponding task vector was subtracted. Subsequently, the closed-loop success rates of both the target skill and all other nominally unrelated skills (controls) were measured.
 - **Alpha ($\alpha$) Selection**: For each suppressible skill, a task-specific $\alpha^*$ (constrained operating point) was selected. This involved identifying the smallest $\alpha$ that resulted in a 0% target SR, while concurrently maximizing the mean control SR on a designated development panel.
- **Taxonomy of Outcomes**: Based on the observed success rates, three qualitative regimes were defined:
 1. **Target–Control Separation**: The target skill's SR falls to $\leq 20\%$, and its mean off-diagonal control SR remains $\geq 40\%$.
 2. **Resistant**: The target skill's SR remains $> 20\%$.
 3. **Global Collapse**: The target skill's SR falls to $\leq 20\%$, but its mean off-diagonal control SR also falls below $40\%$.
- **Held-Out Validation**: To assess the generalizability of the findings, the five skills identified as suppressible were re-evaluated at their chosen $\alpha^*$ on a disjoint set of initial states (indices 20-39, compared to 0-19 used for selection). This assessed whether target suppression persisted and how control retention performed under novel conditions.
- **Multi-Skill Negation**: The study investigated simultaneously suppressing a set of skills by subtracting the sum of their task vectors, with the sum scaled by $1/\sqrt{|S|}$ to maintain perturbation norm.
- **Comparative Analysis**: Task-vector negation was benchmarked against established data-dependent gradient-based unlearning methods (NegGrad, NegGrad+, Gradient-Difference) and a leave-one-task-out retraining oracle on a matched panel for a specific target skill (t$_0$).
- **Diagnostic Probes**:
 - **Loss Landscape Sharpness**: Sampled perturbation sensitivity was calculated for expert, goal, and negated models to characterize the geometry of the loss landscape.
 - **Task-Vector Geometry**: Cosine similarity between task vectors and their energy distribution across VLM, AH, and PP components were analyzed.
 - **Localization Ablations**: The impact of restricting the negation edit to specific depth bands of the VLM or individual components (VLM, AH, PP) was examined.
 - **Relearning Probe**: The speed at which a suppressed skill could be re-acquired via fine-tuning from a negated model was compared to relearning from a task-naive base model, providing insight into whether knowledge was truly erased or merely masked.
 - **Alignment Score ($AS_i$)**: A forward-only, data-dependent diagnostic was developed to predict a skill's susceptibility to target suppression based on the relative shift in its hidden state representations when perturbed by its own task vector.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The audit produced a detailed characterization of task-vector subtraction in VLA policies.

- **Three Regimes of Subtraction on LIBERO-Goal**: Across the ten LIBERO-Goal skills, subtraction yielded three distinct outcomes:
 - **Target–Control Separation (5 skills)**: For skills t$_0$, t$_2$, t$_3$, t$_5$, and t$_6$, the target skill was effectively suppressed (success rate (SR) $\leq 15\%$). Concurrently, the mean baseline-normalized control retention across other skills was 57.8%, with individual retention ranging from 44% to 65%.
 - **Resistance (3 skills)**: Skills t$_1$, t$_4$, and t$_7$ resisted suppression, with their target SR remaining high (e.g., t$_1$, t$_7$ at 100% SR; t$_4$ at 30%).
 - **Global Collapse (2 skills)**: For skills t$_8$ and t$_9$, target suppression was achieved (0% SR), but this resulted in a near-total collapse of all control skills (mean control SR $< 40\%$).
- **Fragility of Locality on Held-Out States**: When the five suppressible skills were re-evaluated at their optimized $\alpha^*$ on a disjoint set of held-out initial states, target suppression remained robust (all 0% success). However, the mean baseline-normalized control retention across the nine controls decreased to 52% (from 57.8% on the selection panel). Locality proved heterogeneous and fragile; retention ranged widely from 78% (for t$_0$) down to 4% (for t$_6$), and in four out of five cases, at least one nominally unrelated control skill was driven to 0% success.
- **Cross-Architecture Consistency and Boundaries**: The qualitative target–control pattern recurred across different VLA architectures: MergeVLA (continuous regression), OpenVLA-7B (discrete tokens), and $\pi_{0.5}$ (flow-matching). However, the precise quantitative margins of control retention remained model-dependent. Selectivity was most pronounced on LIBERO-Goal. It largely broke down on other LIBERO suites:
 - **LIBERO-Spatial**: No clean separation was observed; for the one suppressible target (t$_0$), its controls also collapsed.
 - **LIBERO-Object and LIBERO-Long10**: While targets were often suppressed (e.g., 3/4 targets on Object, 2/4 on Long10), control skills consistently collapsed, with off-diagonal mean SRs of 5.8% and 11.7% respectively. This suggested that shared motor primitives or subgoals in these suites led to non-selective effects.
- **Failure of Multi-Skill Subtraction**: Attempting to simultaneously suppress multiple skills by subtracting the sum of their task vectors (even with norm-matched scaling) led to control collapse when combining two or more skills. Neither sequential subtraction nor projection-based multi-negation rules reliably restored selectivity at full suppression strength.
- **Directional Asymmetry and Composition**: At matched perturbation norms, the *sign* of a single task vector was critical. Subtracting $\tau_0$ achieved selective suppression (0% target, 100% control), whereas adding the identical vector left the target partially intact (50% target, 90% control). This demonstrated a direction-specific effect rather than generic norm damage. For multi-skill composition, outcomes depended on the *anchor model* ($\theta_{Goal}$ versus $\theta_{base}$) and *aggregate magnitude*, often leading to collapse.
- **Comparison with Gradient-Based Baselines**: In a matched-panel comparison for target t$_0$:
 - All removal methods (negation, NegGrad, NegGrad+, Gradient-Difference, retraining oracle) successfully suppressed the target to 0%.
 - Plain NegGrad resulted in global collapse (0% mean control SR).
 - Gradient-Difference (data- and gradient-dependent) achieved the highest control retention (88.3%).
 - Negation, despite being data- and gradient-free at edit time, yielded competitive control retention (73.9%), outperforming NegGrad+ (66.1%). The retraining oracle provided the upper bound (95.6%) but required a full retrain.
- **Diagnostics and Permanence**:
 - **Weight-Space Geometry**: Mean VLM task-vector cosine similarity was consistently low (approximately 0.014 across all suites), indicating near-orthogonality. However, this geometric property did not predict the wide variation in control survival across suites.
 - **Depth Localization**: Restricting negation to individual depth bands within the VLM backbone was insufficient for target suppression; the full-depth vector was required.
 - **Relearning Probe**: The negated model re-acquired a suppressed skill (t$_0$) significantly faster than a task-naive base model learning from scratch. This finding is consistent with *behavioral masking* (the skill's substrate is preserved but access is suppressed) rather than permanent, certified unlearning.
 - **Alignment Score ($AS_i$)**: A forward-only diagnostic showed a correlation with target-suppression susceptibility *within* the LIBERO-Goal suite (Spearman $\rho = 0.72$), but its absolute threshold did not generalize effectively across different suites.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a comprehensive empirical audit of task-vector subtraction for skill removal in VLA policies, yielding insights into its capabilities and limitations.

- **Necessity of Closed-Loop Target-and-Control Evaluation**: The study critically demonstrates that traditional static-loss diagnostics are insufficient for evaluating locality in embodied model editing. The observed disparities between target suppression and control retention on held-out states—where a target-only metric would register "success" while collateral damage to controls varies from 78% retention down to 4%—underscore the essential role of closed-loop, target-and-control evaluation. This involves measuring target success, baseline-normalized mean and worst-case control retention, and transparently reporting evaluation splits and resource requirements.
- **Characterization of Task-Vector Subtraction**: Task-vector subtraction is identified as a fast and robust method for *target skill suppression* in certain contexts. However, its behavioral *locality* is shown to be fragile and heterogeneous. Its effectiveness is highly dependent on the nature of the task (e.g., behavioral separability), the specific VLA policy, and whether it's a single-skill or multi-skill edit.
- **Implications for Model Editing and Unlearning**: The findings establish empirical boundaries for the practical application of task arithmetic in VLA skill removal. While efficient, the method does not guarantee safe or selective behavioral removal across all conditions, especially when skills share motor primitives or subgoals. The observation that suppressed skills are rapidly re-learnable suggests that task-vector negation may induce *behavioral masking* rather than irreversible knowledge deletion from the model weights. This is a critical distinction for safety-critical applications, where certified unlearning might be required.
- **Guidance for Future Research**: The work highlights the ongoing need for more robust model editing techniques that can guarantee stronger locality, especially in complex, entangled VLA policies or for multi-skill deletion scenarios. The limitations of current diagnostic tools (e.g., weight-space cosine, Alignment Score) in predicting behavioral locality across diverse contexts also point to areas for further development. Future research could explore norm-matched ablations for deeper localization insights and develop more generalizable diagnostics for assessing selectivity.
- **Standardization of Reporting**: The paper advocates for a standardized reporting framework for model editing interventions, emphasizing the need for comprehensive metrics beyond simple target success rates to truly assess the safety and reliability of such methods in embodied AI.
