# Learning Robot Manipulation from Audio World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A transformer-based flow matching model predicts future audio spectrograms in a latent space, then feeds current plus predicted audio (with image observations) into a robot policy. Tested on water-filling and piano-playing, lookahead audio prediction beats no-lookahead baselines, hitting 100% success on water filling.

## Problem

- Multimodal robot learning treats audio only as an auxiliary signal supplementing vision, ignoring tasks where audio alone carries the decisive physical information.
- Video-based world models need large generative models with heavy compute and latency to predict future visual frames for manipulation planning.
- Existing audio-conditioned policies react only to current audio, without reasoning about how rhythmic or pitch patterns will evolve to inform future actions.

## Method

- ==AudioMAE== audio latent autoencoder pre-trained to encode/decode spectrograms into a smooth, expressive latent space via reconstruction loss.
- ==Transformer-based latent flow matching world model==, decoupling frame-wise conditioning from time-axis attention, regresses a vector field with a ==flow-matching plus velocity-consistency loss== to generate future audio latents.
- ==Flow-matching robot policy== conditioned on current image, current audio, and world-model-predicted future audio spectrograms, trained end-to-end via flow-prediction MSE on end-effector velocity.

## Results

- Real-world Kinova Gen3 water-filling task achieves **100%** success rate over **30** trials using closed-loop future audio prediction at roughly **50 milliseconds** per step.
- Generated spectrograms accurately capture button press/release onset-offset and rising pitch, with predicted release consistently appearing just before the bottle actually fills.
- Piano-playing simulation with SAC policy shows improved F1 score when future MIDI-derived goal states from the world model replace lookahead-free RL baseline observations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Audio can be a primary, not merely auxiliary, decision signal when visual cues are ambiguous or unchanging during contact-rich manipulation.
> - Predicting future sensory states, not just current multimodal fusion, is what lets a policy exploit intrinsic physical rhythmic and pitch dynamics for planning.
> - Decoupled multi-stage training of world model and policy outperformed end-to-end joint training, and modularity let components like MusicVAE or SAC swap in per task.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper operates at the intersection of **robot manipulation, world models, and multimodal machine learning**, with a specific emphasis on the under-explored role of *audio prediction* in robotic policies.

The broader research landscape in robotic learning has seen significant advancements through **world models**, which aim to learn internal representations of the environment and predict future states, thus enabling agents to plan and act effectively. Traditionally, this domain has been dominated by:
1. **Video-based World Models:** These models (e.g., Liang et al., 2025; Assran et al., 2025) focus on predicting future visual frames. While powerful for capturing causal dependencies, they often require large-scale generative models, leading to high computational demands and latency, especially for high-fidelity prediction.
2. **Latent Vision-Language World Models:** To mitigate the computational overhead of pixel-level prediction, recent work (e.g., Zheng et al., 2025) explores learning internal, abstract representations of future states, often combining visual and linguistic inputs, thereby bypassing explicit full-frame reconstruction.

Within multimodal robot learning, audio signals have typically been incorporated as an **auxiliary sensory modality**. Approaches like Liu et al. (2024) for bagel flipping or Mejia et al. (2024) for food scooping demonstrate how audio can enhance performance when combined with visual cues. These methods often treat audio as supplementary information, enriching the perception but not always being critical for the core task understanding or long-term planning.

This paper distinguishes itself by challenging the notion of audio as merely supplementary. It argues that in many scenarios, audio provides **essential and often unique information** that visual cues alone cannot convey, or where visual information is ambiguous or incomplete. Critically, it posits that **predicting future audio states**, rather than just incorporating current audio, is paramount for effective robot action policies in these specific scenarios. This is a significant shift from previous work, which has largely focused on visual prediction or static multimodal fusion. The authors highlight that "the audio cues in these tasks often reflect certain intrinsic physical dynamics, such as pitch and rhythmic patterns," making audio prediction a direct means of anticipating physical consequences and environmental changes that are vital for informed decision-making. By focusing on *generative latent flow matching* for audio, the paper introduces a novel approach to build audio-driven world models, drawing parallels with the success of generative models (like diffusion models and flow matching) in other domains like image and video synthesis, but applying it to the under-explored modality of temporal audio prediction for robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate a novel framework for **robot manipulation learning that leverages the prediction of future audio observations**. This is driven by the motivation that audio is not merely an auxiliary sensory input but can be a *critical and indispensable source of information* for robots, especially in tasks where visual cues are ambiguous, incomplete, or lack the necessary temporal dynamics.

The core motivations can be summarized as follows:

- **Addressing Visual Ambiguity and Incompleteness:** The paper highlights scenarios where visual information alone is insufficient. For instance, when filling a bottle with water, visual observations of the water level might be unclear or change slowly, making it difficult to determine when the bottle is full. Audio, however, provides distinct cues like the rising pitch of the water sound, which directly indicates the filling progress and completion.
- **Leveraging Intrinsic Physical Dynamics:** Many robotic manipulation tasks involve interactions that generate audio reflecting underlying physical properties, such as material properties, contact events, and temporal processes (e.g., liquid pouring, object dropping, musical instrument playing). These dynamics manifest as specific pitch patterns, rhythmic structures, or sound envelopes that are directly perceivable and predictable through audio.
- **Enabling Long-Term Reasoning and Planning:** Unlike static multimodal fusion, predicting *future* audio states allows the robot to anticipate the consequences of its actions over a longer horizon. This foresight is crucial for complex tasks requiring sequential decision-making and precise timing, such as playing a musical instrument or accurately stopping a pouring action.
- **Advancing Audio-Driven World Models:** While video-based and vision-language world models have seen significant development, audio-driven world models, particularly those focusing on prediction for policy learning, remain largely underexplored. The authors aim to fill this gap by proposing a robust generative model for future audio states.
- **Improving Robot Manipulation Performance:** Ultimately, the motivation is to enhance the success rate and efficiency of robot manipulation tasks by providing policies with richer, more predictive sensory information that is otherwise inaccessible or difficult to infer from other modalities.

In essence, the paper argues that for specific classes of manipulation tasks, the ability to accurately foresee how audio will evolve in response to robot actions and environmental dynamics is not just helpful, but *critical* for successful execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method integrates a generative latent flow matching model for audio prediction with a robot action policy in a modular, multi-stage training paradigm. The overall architecture is depicted in Fig. 1 and comprises three main phases:

#### 3.1. Audio Latent Autoencoder

- **Representation:** Audio signals are first transformed into **spectrograms**, which capture time-frequency information effectively. Spectrograms are chosen for their ability to represent temporal and spectral features, such as button pressing/releasing actions and pitch changes during water filling.
- **Encoding/Decoding:** An **AudioMAE** (Audio Masked Autoencoder, Zhu et al., 2025) is used to encode these spectrograms into a latent space and then decode them back. This autoencoder is pre-trained with a reconstruction loss to ensure an expressive and smooth latent representation of the audio. For the piano playing task, a **MusicVAE** (Roberts et al., 2018) is interchanged for encoding and decoding Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) signals, demonstrating the modularity.

#### 3.2. Flow Matching in Latent Space (Audio World Model)

- **Core Mechanism:** The heart of the audio world model is a generative latent **flow matching** model (Lipman et al., 2022; Rouxel et al., 2024). Flow matching is presented as an alternative to diffusion models, offering potentially faster and higher-quality sampling.
- **Objective:** The model aims to predict future audio states by learning to reconstruct a *target vector field* that maps a simple source distribution (e.g., Gaussian noise) to complex data distributions (the target audio latents).
- **Architecture:** It employs a transformer-based vector field predictor, adapted from DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023), specifically modified (Ki et al., 2024) to decouple frame-wise conditioning from time-axis attention for temporally consistent latent generation.
- **Training Details:**
 - The model regresses on a vector field `v_t(x_t, c_t; θ)`, where `x_t` is a sample at timestep `t ∈`, and `c_t` represents driving conditions (current audio latents and time step).
 - To ensure smooth transitions for sequences longer than the prediction window, the last `L'` audio feature latents from the preceding window are incorporated as additional input.
 - A **flow matching objective** (`L_fm`) minimizes the difference between the predicted vector field and the true target vector field.
 - A **velocity loss** (`L_v`) is added to supervise temporal consistency by penalizing frame-wise differences in the predicted and target vector fields.
 - Dropout (0.5 probability) is applied to preceding audio latents during training to enhance smooth transitions.
- **Inference:** During inference, random waypoints from a source distribution are progressively flowed into the target audio latents by iteratively estimating the vector field from `t=0` to `t=1` over multiple steps.

#### 3.3. Robot Policy

- **Input:** The robot policy takes the **current audio, current image observation representation, and the predicted future audios** (decoded from the flow matching model's latent predictions) as input.
- **Policy Architecture:** A flow matching policy, as proposed in Zhang & Gienger (2024), is adopted. In the piano playing task, a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) reinforcement learning policy can be interchangeably used, again highlighting modularity.
- **Training:** The robot policy is trained end-to-end in a supervised manner. It uses an MSE loss on future robot end-effector velocity over a 16-step horizon, with ground truth future audio frames used during training. During evaluation, it uses the *generated* audio from the world model.

#### 3.4. Learning Details

- **Multi-stage Training:** The training process is decoupled into multiple stages: first the audio autoencoder, then the audio flow matching world model, and finally the robot policy. This modular approach, inspired by Liang et al. (2025), is found to yield significantly higher performance than end-to-end training and allows independent adaptation of components.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The entire pipeline (autoencoder, audio flow matching, robot policy) is relatively lightweight, requiring approximately one day of training on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU for 20,000 spectrograms.
- **Closed-loop Operation:** During robot evaluation, the system operates in a closed-loop manner, with the world model predicting future audio and the policy generating actions based on these predictions, costing about 50 milliseconds per step.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper demonstrates the superior capabilities of their audio-driven world model through two distinct manipulation tasks, emphasizing the critical role of future audio prediction.

#### 4.1. Real-World Water Filling Task

- **Setup:** A Kinova Gen3 robotic arm controlled a water dispenser. RGB video and audio (from a MAONO omnidirectional USB lapel microphone) were recorded during teleoperation to collect data. Audio waveforms were converted to log Mel filterbank spectrograms.
- **Task:** The robot had to press a button to start water flow and then release it when the bottle was full, relying on audio cues to determine the filling status. Visual cues were acknowledged as ambiguous.
- **Predictions:** The audio world model was tasked with predicting future spectrograms (2.56 seconds ahead) given current spectrograms (1.28 seconds). The policy then used these predictions along with synchronized image observations to generate 6-DoF Cartesian end-effector velocity commands.
- **Results:**
 - The generated future spectrograms accurately captured key events: the onset and offset of the button press/release, and the gradually increasing pitch of the water filling sound (Fig. 2).
 - Crucially, the predicted button-releasing activity consistently appeared in the generated spectrogram *just before the bottle was actually filled*, demonstrating the model's ability to anticipate the physical dynamics.
 - The proposed method achieved a **100% success rate over 30 trials**, showcasing its capability to precisely predict future audio states based on underlying physical dynamics and pitch patterns, leading to successful task completion. This superior performance is explicitly contrasted with "methods without future lookahead" mentioned in the abstract, implying that such baselines would not achieve similar success.

#### 4.2. Simulated Piano Playing Task

- **Setup:** A simulated piano environment (Zakka et al., 2023) where a robot performs a piano duet, requiring anticipation of musical notes. Music was represented by MIDI signals, converted to piano rolls. A SAC policy was used for robot joint action prediction.
- **Task:** The robot needed to anticipate upcoming, unheard musical notes from auditory input to effectively plan and play its own part, requiring a lookahead horizon of several seconds.
- **Predictions:** The audio world model (using MusicVAE for MIDI encoding/decoding) was used to dynamically generate music (64 time steps, around 8 seconds) autoregressively, which then served as future goal states for the RL agent.
- **Results:**
 - The model successfully generated both ground-truth and autoregressively generated spectrograms and MIDI data for pieces like "Nocturne" (Fig. 2).
 - Evaluation using the **F1 score** (a common metric in audio information retrieval) showed **improved performance when future goal states (generated by the audio world model) were incorporated into the observation** for the SAC policy.
 - This improvement was attributed to the policy's ability to plan more effectively, such as pre-positioning non-finger joints (e.g., the wrist) to reach upcoming notes promptly, which is a direct benefit of future lookahead. The paper directly compares this to a "basic RL baseline without future lookahead," confirming the value of the audio world model.

In both experiments, the qualitative (spectrogram generation fidelity) and quantitative (success rate, F1 score) results strongly support the hypothesis that predicting future audio states—especially those reflecting intrinsic physical and rhythmic patterns—is critical for robot manipulation and leads to superior performance compared to approaches lacking such foresight.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes a significant contribution to the field of robotic learning, particularly in the realm of multimodal perception and action. Its key significance lies in:

- **Challenging the Auxiliary Role of Audio:** The paper compellingly argues and demonstrates that audio is not merely a supplementary modality but can be *essential* for robot perception and decision-making, especially when visual information is ambiguous or incomplete. This shifts the paradigm from simply fusing multimodal inputs to understanding the unique and predictive power of specific modalities.
- **Pioneering Audio-Driven World Models:** By proposing a generative latent flow matching model for anticipating future audio observations, the authors introduce a novel approach to constructing world models that are explicitly driven by audio. This opens up a new avenue for research in developing intelligent robot behaviors that are deeply sensitive to auditory cues.
- **Enabling Enhanced Long-Term Reasoning and Planning:** The ability to predict future audio states allows robot policies to reason about long-term consequences of actions and plan proactively. This foresight is critical for tasks requiring precise timing, sequential actions, and anticipating dynamic environmental changes, moving beyond reactive control.
- **Demonstrating Practical Applicability:** The success in both a real-world, contact-rich task (water filling) and a complex rhythmic task (piano playing) highlights the versatility and robustness of the proposed framework. The 100% success rate in the water filling task is particularly compelling evidence of its real-world effectiveness.
- **Modular and Efficient Architecture:** The multi-stage training paradigm and the use of lightweight, yet powerful, generative models (flow matching) make the proposed system computationally efficient and flexible. This modularity allows for easier adaptation, interchangeability of components, and potentially faster iteration in research and development.
- **Broader Implications for Human-Robot Interaction:** Many human activities are rich in auditory feedback. Robots that can understand and anticipate these sounds could lead to more intuitive, responsive, and safer human-robot collaboration, for instance, in domestic environments or industrial settings where audio cues are paramount for task monitoring and safety.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. It could pave the way for a new generation of robots capable of performing more dexterous, nuanced, and adaptive manipulation tasks in complex, dynamic environments. Future research directions, as suggested by the authors, include extending this framework to more complex tasks requiring fine-grained and dexterous manipulation, which could involve integrating more sophisticated audio feature extraction, enhancing the fidelity of long-term audio prediction, and exploring its application in domains like surgical robotics, assembly lines, or advanced domestic assistance where subtle auditory feedback is crucial for success. This paper lays a strong foundation for future exploration into the predictive power of auditory perception for embodied AI.
