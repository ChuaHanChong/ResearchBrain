# Diffusion Transformer Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Shanghai AI Lab and collaborators developed the Diffusion Transformer Policy (DiT Policy), a generalist robot policy that directly denoises continuous action sequences using a scalable multi-modal diffusion transformer. This approach, leveraging large transformer architectures, achieved state-of-the-art generalization across diverse simulation and real-world robot manipulation tasks, including a 3.61 average sequence length on CALVIN and an 82.4% average success rate on LIBERO.

## Problem

- Existing generalist vision-language-action (VLA) models often rely on small, fixed action heads or action discretization, which limits their ability to effectively handle diverse continuous action spaces.
- Previous diffusion policies in robotics were typically designed for single-task learning or used smaller, simpler networks (e.g., MLPs) for denoising, restricting their scalability and direct conditioning on detailed multi-modal observations.
- Developing robot policies that can generalize across varied tasks, environments, and robot embodiments with minimal task-specific training data remains a core challenge in robot learning.

## Method

- The **DiT Policy** introduces an ==in-context conditional diffusion transformer== architecture, using a large causal transformer as the core backbone for denoising.
- It directly denoises continuous 7D robot action *chunks* (sequences of actions) rather than individual actions, conditioned on multimodal inputs like language instructions (CLIP) and visual observations (jointly optimized **DINOv2** features selected by a ==Q-Former==).
- The model is trained with a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (==DDPM==) objective to predict noise added to action chunks, allowing end-to-end optimization on large datasets like Open X-Embodiment.

## Results

- Achieved state-of-the-art performance on the CALVIN benchmark, completing an average of 3.61 tasks in a row using only a single static RGB camera, and attained a **50.0%** success rate for 5 consecutive instructions.
- Outperformed state-of-the-art baselines like Octo and OpenVLA on the LIBERO benchmark with an **82.4%** average success rate and showed strong real-to-sim and novel camera view generalization on SimplerEnv and Maniskill2 (**65.8%** success).
- Demonstrated consistent improvements in 10-shot finetuning and exhibited zero-shot generalization capabilities on a real Franka arm, successfully grasping novel objects and executing complex multi-step tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging a large transformer directly for action sequence denoising enables a more scalable and robust modeling of complex, high-dimensional continuous action distributions compared to traditional small action heads.
> - The 'in-context conditional' design allows the diffusion transformer to directly perceive and condition on detailed image patches and language tokens, fostering a stronger perception-action coupling.
> - Denoising entire *action chunks* offers a more holistic and temporally consistent prediction of future robot behavior, contributing to improved performance in long-horizon tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of generalist robot policies, vision-language-action (VLA) models, and diffusion models in imitation learning. The broader research landscape for robot learning has traditionally relied on task-specific data collection and policy training, which is time-consuming and expensive. However, inspired by the success of large foundational models in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Computer Vision (CV), the robotics community is increasingly moving towards developing **generalist robot policies** capable of performing diverse tasks in varied environments with minimal specific training data.

Current efforts in this direction often involve pretraining large VLA models on extensive, diverse robot datasets (e.g., Open X-Embodiment Dataset). Notable examples include:
- **Robot Transformers (RT-1, RT-2)**: These models leverage transformer architectures for robust generalization by training on large-scale datasets, often discretizing action spaces.
- **Octo**: This approach employs an autoregressive transformer with a small MLP-based diffusion action head to denoise individual continuous actions.
- **OpenVLA**: This model discretizes the action space and uses pretrained vision-language models to build VLAs from large datasets.

The authors identify a critical limitation in these existing VLA models: they often predict individual discretized or continuous actions using a relatively small "action head." This design choice restricts their ability to handle diverse action spaces effectively and can limit generalization, especially when actions are discretized, leading to potential internal deviations during robot execution.

Concurrently, **diffusion models** have emerged as a powerful paradigm, initially for image generation and more recently for robot policy learning (Diffusion Policy). These models excel at modeling complex, high-dimensional data distributions. However, many existing diffusion policies are designed for single-task imitation learning, relying on architectures like UNets or shallow cross-attention networks. While Octo incorporates a diffusion head, it still uses a small MLP network for denoising, conditioned on a single embedding from a multi-modal transformer, rather than leveraging the full scalability of transformers for the diffusion process itself.

The "Diffusion Transformer Policy" (DiT Policy) addresses these gaps by proposing a **scalable multi-modal diffusion transformer** that directly denoises *action chunks* (sequences of actions) rather than individual actions, and crucially, utilizes a *large transformer model* as its diffusion backbone. This represents a significant shift from using small, separate action heads or simple MLPs for action prediction/denoising, aligning robot learning more closely with the scaling capabilities observed in large transformer models for other AI domains. By modeling continuous action sequences directly with a large diffusion transformer, the work aims to overcome the limitations of previous VLA models in handling diverse and complex action spaces, thereby achieving superior generalization performance across varied robot embodiments and tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the Diffusion Transformer Policy are:

1. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing VLA Models in Diverse Action Spaces:** Existing large vision-language-action (VLA) models, while showing promise in generalization, often rely on small action heads or action discretization for predicting actions. This approach limits their ability to effectively handle the vast diversity of continuous action spaces found in large, cross-embodiment robot datasets. The authors are motivated to design a policy that can natively model these complex, continuous actions without such compromises.
2. **Leveraging Transformer Scalability for Action Denoising:** Inspired by the scaling capabilities of transformer architectures in multimodal image generation (e.g., Diffusion Transformers in CV), the authors seek to apply this architectural strength to the problem of robot action prediction. Instead of a small network, the goal is to use a large, scalable transformer directly for the denoising process of action sequences, anticipating better generalization and performance due to the transformer's inherent ability to model complex dependencies.
3. **Facilitating Generalization Across Diverse Robot Datasets:** The ultimate goal is to develop a generalist robot policy that can effectively learn from and generalize across large and diverse robot datasets (like Open X-Embodiment Dataset). By modeling continuous action *chunks* with a large multi-modal diffusion transformer, the authors hypothesize that the proposed approach will capture more nuanced and generalizable action representations, leading to better transferability to new environments, tasks, and robot embodiments (real-to-sim, long-horizon tasks, novel objects/views).
4. **Enabling Direct Conditioning on Detailed Observations:** Previous diffusion policies often fuse historical image observations and instructions into a single embedding before the denoising process. The authors are motivated to enable the action denoising learning to directly condition on detailed historical visual observations (e.g., image patches), allowing the model to perceive subtle nuances necessary for precise action anticipation.

In essence, the motivation is to create a more robust, scalable, and generalizable robot policy by integrating the strengths of large transformer architectures and continuous action diffusion models, specifically designed to address the challenges posed by the diverse action spaces and rich visual information in large robot learning datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Diffusion Transformer Policy (DiT Policy) introduces an **in-context conditional diffusion transformer architecture** for generalist robot policy learning. The core idea is to directly denoise continuous action *chunks* using a large transformer, conditioned on multimodal inputs like language instructions and visual observations.

The methodology involves several key components and design choices:

1. **Multimodal Input Processing:**
 - **Instruction Tokenization:** Language instructions are processed using a frozen **CLIP** (Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training) model's text encoder. This provides high-quality, semantically rich text embeddings.
 - **Image Observation Representations:** Raw image observations are fed into **DINOv2**, a powerful vision transformer pretrained on web data. Unlike typical frozen encoders, DINOv2 parameters are *jointly optimized* with the rest of the transformer, allowing fine-tuning to robot-specific visual nuances.
 - **Q-Former for Feature Selection:** To manage computational cost and focus on relevant visual information, a **Q-Former** (Querying Transformer) with FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation) conditioning is employed. This module selects pertinent image features from DINOv2's patch features, guided by the instruction context. The query length of image features is reduced (e.g., to 32 tokens).

2. **Action Preprocessing:**
 - **Continuous 7D Action Space:** The policy operates on end-effector actions, represented as a 7-dimensional vector: 3D translation, 3D rotation (Euler angles), and 1D gripper position (open/close).
 - **Action Representation:** To align with image and language tokens, the 7D continuous action vector is padded with zeros to create action representations. Noise is only added to the actual 7D action components during denoising.
 - **Action Chunks:** Crucially, the model denoises *action chunks* (sequences of actions, e.g., 32 action chunks) rather than individual actions, allowing for a more holistic prediction of future behavior.

3. **Diffusion Transformer Architecture (Core Design):**
 - **In-context Conditional Backbone:** The central component is a large **Causal Transformer**, akin to a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) structure. This transformer serves as the denoising network, processing a concatenated sequence of image features, language tokens, timestep embedding, and *noised action tokens*.
 - **Direct Conditioning:** Unlike prior methods that might aggregate observations into a single embedding, this "in-context conditional" design allows the transformer to directly condition action denoising on *individual image observation patches* and language tokens. This enables the model to perceive subtle visual cues and capture detailed action changes.
 - **Scalability:** By using a large transformer as the diffusion backbone, the architecture inherently retains the scaling properties of transformers, aiming for improved generalization from diverse and large datasets. The model itself has 334M parameters, with 221M being trained.

4. **Training Objective:**
 - **DDPM Objective:** The model is trained using a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) objective. During training, a Gaussian noise vector `xt` is sampled at a random timestep `t` and added to the ground truth action `a` to create a noised action `â`.
 - **Noise Prediction:** The causal transformer `ϵθ(ât, c_obs, c_instru, t)` is trained to predict this noise vector `xt`. The objective is to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between the predicted noise and the actual added noise.
 - **Inference:** During inference, actions are generated by iteratively denoising a sampled Gaussian noise vector over `T` steps (e.g., 100 for inference) using a noise scheduler (DDIM for acceleration).

5. **Pretraining and Finetuning:**
 - **Open X-Embodiment Dataset:** The model is extensively pretrained on the Open X-Embodiment dataset, a large collection of diverse robot data, following established practices. Actions are normalized and outliers filtered.
 - **End-to-End Optimization:** The training process is end-to-end, optimizing DINOv2 parameters alongside the transformer and Q-Former, with only the CLIP text encoder frozen.

This comprehensive approach combines robust multimodal understanding with a scalable diffusion transformer architecture, specifically tailored to model continuous action sequences, thereby offering a powerful framework for generalist robot policy learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness and strong generalization capabilities of the Diffusion Transformer Policy (DiT Policy) across various simulation and real-world benchmarks, consistently outperforming several state-of-the-art baselines like Octo, OpenVLA, and RT-1-X.

Key findings and results include:

1. **SimplerEnv (Real-to-Sim Generalization):**
 - DiT Policy achieved strong generalization performance on SimplerEnv, a real-to-sim platform for Google Robot simulation, across different variances (background, texture, objects, spatial positions).
 - It significantly outperformed RT-1-X, Octo-Base, and OpenVLA-7B in tasks like "pick up coke can," "move an object near to others," and "open/close drawer," achieving higher success rates across matching and variant scenarios (e.g., 72.7% vs. 16.3% for "coke can/matching_avg" against OpenVLA).

2. **CALVIN (Long-Horizon Language-Conditioned Tasks):**
 - On the challenging CALVIN (ABC→D) benchmark, evaluating novel task generalization, DiT Policy achieved state-of-the-art results using only a single static RGB camera stream.
 - It improved the average number of tasks completed in a row from 2.47 (RoboFlamingo) and 3.27 (3D Diffuser) to **3.61**, and achieved a 50.0% success rate for completing 5 instructions in a row, significantly higher than competitors.
 - Pretraining on the Open X-Embodiment Dataset provided a substantial boost, improving success sequence length by over 1.2 compared to training from scratch, demonstrating strong transferability.
 - DiT Policy also significantly outperformed its Diffusion Action Head baseline (3.61 vs. 3.16 average length), highlighting the architectural advantages.

3. **LIBERO (Multitask and Lifelong Robot Learning):**
 - DiT Policy achieved the best overall performance on the LIBERO benchmark, which tests knowledge transfer abilities.
 - It showed a nearly **6% increase** in average success rate (82.4%) compared to OpenVLA (76.5%), Octo (75.1%), and Diffusion Policy (72.4%).
 - Notably, it made significant progress on LIBERO-LONG (63.8%), indicating a strong potential for long-horizon tasks.

4. **Maniskill2 (Novel Camera View Generalization):**
 - DiT Policy demonstrated superior performance on Maniskill2, a benchmark for generalizable manipulation skills, especially under large-scale novel camera view generalization.
 - It achieved an overall success rate of 65.8%, outperforming Discretization Action Head (30.2%) and Diffusion Action Head (58.6%) baselines.
 - The model showed particular strength in complex tasks like PickSingleYCB (62.0% vs. 37.0%) and PickClutterYCB (36.0% vs. 24.0%), tasks requiring precise grasping in cluttered environments.

5. **Real Franka Arm (Zero-shot and Few-shot Generalization):**
 - **10-shot Finetuning Generalization:** DiT Policy achieved consistent improvements over Octo and OpenVLA on the real Franka Arm across various pick-and-place and complex long-horizon manipulation tasks. For instance, in "Pick & Pouring," DiT Policy achieved 40% (Step-1) and 10% (Step-2) success, while competitors struggled. It demonstrated better robustness and understanding of multi-step, long-horizon tasks.
 - **In-domain Finetuning Generalization:** DiT Policy achieved a 46.9% overall success rate on few-shot in-domain tasks, outperforming Discretized (19.3%) and Diffusion (34.8%) action heads. It also showed some ability to pick up novel, unseen objects, unlike baselines.
 - **Zero-shot Generalization:** Visualizations showed DiT Policy successfully grasping objects in zero-shot scenarios where OpenVLA and Octo failed to approach the correct grasp position, implying a more robust policy learning during pretraining.

6. **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Trajectory Length (Action Chunks):** Performance generally increased with longer trajectory lengths (number of action chunks predicted). For complex tasks like PickClutterYCB, longer horizons (e.g., 32 chunks) significantly improved performance (from 9.0% to 36.0%), suggesting better object localization and understanding of future actions.
 - **Observation Length:** Using two historical observation images yielded better performance for long prediction horizons (e.g., 32-chunk trajectory) compared to one or three. Two observations likely provide sufficient visual difference for action inference, while more observations can make convergence difficult.
 - **Execution Steps:** Shorter execution steps (1 or 2 steps per inference) were slightly better than longer steps (4, 8, 16 steps), indicating that prediction quality degrades farther from the current frame.
 - **Convergence:** DiT Policy demonstrated faster convergence on the OXE dataset compared to a diffusion action head strategy with a similar number of parameters.

In summary, the DiT Policy consistently delivered state-of-the-art or superior performance across diverse robot learning scenarios, highlighting the benefits of its large diffusion transformer architecture for modeling continuous action sequences and generalizing from large multimodal datasets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Diffusion Transformer Policy" represents a significant advancement in the field of generalist robot learning, with several important implications and potential impacts:

1. **Advancing Generalist Robot Policies:** The work pushes the boundaries of creating truly generalist robot policies. By demonstrating superior generalization across diverse simulation environments (SimplerEnv, Maniskill2, CALVIN, LIBERO) and real-world robot systems (Franka arm), DiT Policy brings the vision of robots capable of performing a wide array of tasks in various settings closer to reality.

2. **Scalable and Unified Architecture for Continuous Actions:** The most significant contribution is the successful integration of large transformer models with diffusion processes for direct, continuous action sequence denoising. Unlike previous VLA models that relied on discretization or small, separate action heads, DiT Policy leverages the full scaling capabilities of transformers to model complex action distributions directly. This provides a unified and highly scalable architecture that can handle the inherent diversity of continuous action spaces in large, cross-embodiment datasets.

3. **Improved Performance in Complex and Long-Horizon Tasks:** The consistent outperformance on challenging benchmarks, especially in tasks requiring long-horizon planning (CALVIN, LIBERO-LONG) and fine-grained manipulation (Maniskill2 PickClutterYCB), highlights the policy's ability to capture subtle visual nuances and anticipate extended sequences of actions. This is crucial for real-world robotic applications that often involve multi-step, sequential operations.

4. **Enhanced Real-World Robot Learning:** The impressive results on the real Franka arm, particularly in few-shot and zero-shot generalization settings, underscore the practical utility of DiT Policy. The ability to quickly adapt to new tasks or operate in novel real-world environments with minimal new data collection significantly reduces the cost and time associated with deploying robots. The observation that DiT Policy handles complex tasks better than baselines in the real world suggests a more robust and reliable policy for practical deployment.

5. **Direct Conditioning for Better Perception-Action Coupling:** The "in-context conditional" design, allowing the diffusion transformer to directly condition on detailed image patches and language tokens, strengthens the perception-action loop. This enables the model to make more informed action predictions based on granular visual information, which is critical for precision and adaptability in dynamic environments.

6. **Paving the Way for Future Robot Foundation Models:** By effectively combining large vision-language models (CLIP, DINOv2, Q-Former) with a scalable diffusion transformer for action generation, DiT Policy offers a promising architectural template for developing future robot foundation models. These models could serve as powerful pre-trained bases for various robotic tasks, much like large language models have revolutionized NLP.

A recognized limitation, however, is the inference speed. The requirement for multiple denoising steps during inference can impede real-time application. Future work would likely focus on techniques like diffusion distillation or fewer-step inference methods to accelerate action generation without significant performance degradation. Nevertheless, this work establishes a new state-of-the-art and provides a robust framework for building more intelligent and adaptable robot agents.
