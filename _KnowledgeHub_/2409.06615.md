# One-Shot Imitation under Mismatched Execution

> [!summary] Summary
> RHyME trains one-shot imitation policies for robots to follow long-horizon human demonstration videos despite mismatched execution styles. It retrieves and composes short human play clips into imagined paired demonstrations using sequence-level optimal transport distance, avoiding paired human-robot data, achieving over 50% higher task success than prior methods.

## Problem

- Prior human-robot translation methods need paired demonstration data, which is impractical to collect at scale for long-horizon manipulation tasks.
- Zero-shot transfer methods assume matched execution style between human and robot, breaking down when humans move faster, use both hands, or interleave tasks.
- Frame-level visual similarity metrics used for aligning human-robot embeddings fail under execution mismatch since individual frames diverge visually across embodiments.

## Method

- **RHyME** imagines paired demonstrations by retrieving short-horizon human play clips matching robot trajectory segments via ==sequence-level distance functions==, no paired data needed.
- ==Optimal Transport distance== (==Sinkhorn-Knopp==) computes sequence-level cost between robot and human embedding distributions, replacing brittle frame-level or ==TCC== correspondence matching.
- ==Hybrid policy training== alternates conditioning on real robot video embeddings and imagined human embeddings, letting the policy generalize across mismatched embodiments at inference.

## Results

- On SPHERE-HARD bimanual mismatch dataset, RHyME reaches **53%** task recall versus XSkill's **1%**, with OraclePairing as upper bound.
- In real-world unseen task compositions, RHyME achieves **83%** task attempt rate and **67%** task completion rate versus XSkill's **50%** and **33%**.
- RHyME-OT outperforms RHyME-TCC across all three simulated datasets, confirming optimal transport retrieval beats temporal cyclic consistency under mismatch.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sequence-level alignment tolerates execution mismatch far better than frame-level correspondence, since matching distributions is more robust than matching individual timesteps.
> - Retrieval-based synthetic pairing can substitute for costly paired cross-embodiment datasets, turning an unpaired-data problem into a train-time search problem.
> - Frame-level cyclic consistency degrades when one embodiment's action maps to multiple frames of another (unimanual versus bimanual), a caution for one-to-one alignment assumptions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of programming robots for long-horizon manipulation tasks using human demonstrations, a field often referred to as imitation learning. Human demonstrations offer rich, grounded cues for task execution, object interaction, and sequencing, distinguishing them from language-based instructions. However, translating these demonstrations into robot-executable actions faces significant hurdles.

A primary challenge lies in the data requirements. Many existing imitation learning methods necessitate paired human-robot demonstrations, where a human and a robot perform the exact same task in a visually and temporally aligned manner. Collecting such paired data for complex, long-horizon tasks is impractical and not scalable. While large, unpaired datasets of human videos (e.g., YouTube) and robot demonstrations exist, they cannot be directly used for learning a translation policy due to the lack of correspondence.

Prior research has attempted to leverage unpaired human and robot demonstrations by learning shared visual representations. These methods typically map both human and robot images into a common embedding space, allowing a robot policy trained on robot video embeddings to be transferred to human prompt videos at test time. A critical assumption in many of these approaches, such as XSkill, is that human and robot execution styles are visually matched or sufficiently similar. However, this assumption often breaks down in real-world scenarios where humans exhibit diverse, dynamic, and often bimanual movements, while robots may operate more slowly, unimanually, or with different kinematic constraints. This disparity, termed "mismatched execution," leads to misalignment in the shared embedding space, hindering the effectiveness of direct policy transfer.

This paper positions itself within the "one-shot visual imitation from demonstration videos" setting, where a robot aims to replicate demonstrated actions after viewing a prompt video once. It specifically tackles the problem of imitation under mismatched execution, going beyond methods that assume matched execution or rely on explicit motion tracking. Unlike methods that learn reward functions from videos (which require reinforcement learning and can be challenging for complex tasks) or those that strictly align representations through video translation or masking (which might still struggle with severe execution mismatches), this work proposes a novel approach. It focuses on establishing a "sequence-level equivalence" between human and robot actions, even when frame-level visual similarities are lacking. This distinction is crucial, as it suggests that an overall understanding of the task sequence can bridge individual visual disparities without requiring fine-tuning of representations on paired data. The proposed framework, RHyME, aims to synthesize paired datasets from unpaired human and robot data by retrieving and composing human video snippets that are semantically equivalent to robot trajectories at a sequence level, thereby enabling more robust one-shot imitation learning in conditions of significant execution mismatch.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable robots to perform long-horizon manipulation tasks by imitating human demonstrations, specifically addressing scenarios where there is a significant "mismatched execution" between the human demonstrator and the robot. This encompasses differences in movement styles, physical capabilities, and even the number of manipulators used (e.g., bimanual human actions versus unimanual robot actions).

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in the existing landscape of robot imitation learning:

1. **Scalability of Paired Data:** Traditional imitation learning methods often require paired human-robot demonstration datasets, meaning that for every robot action sequence, there must be a corresponding human demonstration of the exact same task. Collecting such synchronized and perfectly matched data for a wide variety of long-horizon tasks is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and impractical at scale. The motivation is to move beyond this reliance on paired data to leverage abundant, unpaired human video data (e.g., from online sources) and robot datasets.

2. **Fragility of Frame-Level Visual Similarity:** Many state-of-the-art methods that work with unpaired data attempt to learn shared visual representations by aligning human and robot images based on frame-level visual similarities. This approach assumes that visually similar frames correspond to similar actions or states across embodiments. However, human execution is inherently different from robot execution. Humans might act more swiftly, use different kinematic chains, employ both hands for a task a robot does with one, or even perform multiple sub-tasks simultaneously. These natural differences result in significant visual and kinematic discrepancies, causing frame-level visual alignment methods to fail and leading to poor generalization when a robot policy, trained on robot-centric data, is prompted by a human video.

3. **Need for Robust Cross-Embodiment Translation:** The core motivation is to develop a method that can robustly translate a human demonstration video into a sequence of robot actions despite these pronounced execution mismatches. The authors propose that while individual frames may look different, a high-level, sequence-based equivalence can be established. This insight forms the basis for their approach, which seeks to find similarity and alignment not at the granular frame level, but by reasoning over entire sequences of image embeddings. This allows the system to establish a "semantic match" between tasks performed by different embodiments, rather than a "visual match."

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to achieve the following specific objectives:
- To develop a retrieval-based algorithm, RHyME, that can automatically create a synthetic paired dataset of human and robot trajectories from unpaired data, specifically designed to handle mismatched execution.
- To define and evaluate sequence-level similarity functions, particularly focusing on optimal transport, which can effectively measure alignment between human and robot video embeddings despite variations in execution.
- To demonstrate that training a robot policy on this synthetically paired dataset, which incorporates both real robot data and imagined human sequences, can significantly improve one-shot imitation performance compared to existing methods that rely on zero-shot transfer under mismatched conditions.
- To validate the proposed framework's effectiveness in both simulated environments with progressively increasing execution mismatch and in real-world robotic manipulation tasks.
- To introduce novel datasets in the Franka Kitchen simulator that systematically model increasing levels of execution mismatch, enabling controlled evaluation of cross-embodiment learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RHyME framework aims to train a robot policy capable of translating a human demonstration video into robot actions, specifically under conditions of mismatched execution, without requiring paired human-robot data for training. The methodology consists of two main stages: training a vision encoder and training the robot policy using a retrieval-based method.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The inference goal is for a policy $\pi(a_R|s_R, v_H)$ to translate a human demonstration video $v_H$ into robot actions $a_R$ at robot state $s_R$. For training, the system has access to two unpaired datasets: a robot dataset ($D_{robot}$) containing state-action trajectories and corresponding robot videos, and a human play dataset ($D_{play}$) consisting of short-horizon human video clips. The objective is to train a vision encoder $\phi(v)$ that maps both human and robot videos into a shared embedding space, and a policy $\pi(a_R|s_R, z_H)$ that conditions on these embeddings $z_H = \phi(v_H)$ to predict robot actions.

**B. Training the Vision Encoder ($\phi$)**
Before policy training, a video encoder $\phi$ is trained to align human and robot video embeddings. This alignment is achieved through a combination of unsupervised losses, without requiring trajectory-level correspondences:
- **Visual Alignment ($L_{vis}(\phi)$):** Utilizes SwAV, a self-supervised method that clusters images based on shared visual features. This encourages the encoder to map visually similar features from both human and robot videos to common prototypes.
- **Temporal Alignment ($L_{temp}(\phi)$):** Employs Time Contrastive Loss, which promotes similarity between embeddings of frames that are temporally close within a video, capturing continuity in both human and robot sequences.
- **Task Alignment ($L_{task}(\phi)$):** An optional loss applied when a small set of short-horizon paired human and robot snippets is available. This loss uses an optimal transport distance to measure similarity between sequences of video embeddings and then applies a contrastive learning objective (INFO-NCE) to pull matched task embeddings closer and push different task embeddings apart. By default, this loss is not used unless specific paired data is provided.

The final loss function for training the vision encoder is a weighted sum of these components. Once trained, this encoder is frozen for the subsequent policy training phase.

**C. Training the Robot Policy ($\pi_\theta$)**
The robot policy training proceeds in two stages, outlined in Algorithm 1 (RHyME):

1. **Stage 1: Create a Paired Dataset (Imagining Human Demonstrations):**
 For each robot trajectory and its corresponding video $v_R$ from $D_{robot}$:
 - The robot video $v_R$ is encoded into embeddings $z_R = \phi(v_R)$ using the frozen vision encoder.
 - An "imagined" human demonstration embedding sequence, $\hat{z}_H$, is retrieved using the `Imagine-Demo` function (Algorithm 2). This function works as follows:
 - The long-horizon robot embedding sequence $z_R$ is divided into short-horizon segments.
 - For each robot segment, the function searches the human play dataset $D_{play}$ to find the human video snippet whose embeddings are "closest" to the robot segment's embeddings. This retrieval is guided by a sequence-level distance function.
 - The retrieved human snippets are concatenated to form the long-horizon imagined human demonstration $\hat{z}_H$.
 - This process generates a synthetic paired dataset $D_{paired}$ consisting of $(\hat{z}_H, z_R, \xi_R)$, where $\xi_R$ is the robot's state-action trajectory.

 **Distance Functions for Retrieval (in `Imagine-Demo`):** Two primary methods are proposed for computing the sequence-level distance $d(z_R, z_H)$ between human and robot video embeddings:
 - **Optimal Transport (OT) Distance:** This method calculates the Wasserstein distance between the distributions of human and robot video embeddings. It determines the minimum "cost" to transform one sequence of embeddings into another, effectively finding the optimal assignment (matching) between frames of two sequences, even if they have different lengths or varying execution speeds. The cost matrix for transport is based on the cosine distance between individual frame embeddings. An entropy-regularized version (Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm) is used for efficient computation.
 - **Temporal Cyclic Consistency (TCC) Distance:** This method, based on prior work, computes cycle consistency. For each robot frame embedding, it finds a soft-nearest neighbor in the human video embeddings, and then cycles back to find a soft-nearest neighbor in the robot video embeddings. The distance is the mean squared error between the original robot frame and its cycled-back counterpart. The authors hypothesize that TCC may be inaccurate when execution mismatches are severe, leading to poor frame alignment or one-to-many correspondences.

2. **Stage 2: Train Policy on Paired Dataset:**
 The robot policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained in a hybrid fashion on the synthetically generated $D_{paired}$. For each element in $D_{paired}$:
 - **Mode 1:** The policy is conditioned on the robot video embeddings $z_R$ to predict actions $\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t, z_R)$.
 - **Mode 2:** The policy is conditioned on the imagined human demonstration embeddings $\hat{z}_H$ to predict actions $\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t, \hat{z}_H)$.
 By alternating between these two modes, the policy learns to generalize from both robot-specific visual cues and the semantically equivalent, imagined human demonstrations, thereby improving its ability to handle diverse human prompts at inference time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated RHyME's performance in both simulation and real-world settings, comparing it against baselines, particularly XSkill, and an OraclePairing baseline representing an upper bound.

**A. Experimental Setup**
- **Simulation:** The Franka Kitchen simulator was used with a 7-DOF Franka arm performing 7 tasks. Three novel cross-embodiment datasets were created with increasing levels of mismatch:
 - **SPHERE-EASY:** Introduces a visual gap by replacing the robot's rendering with a sphere.
 - **SPHERE-MEDIUM:** Adds manipulation style mismatches (e.g., robot drags, human lifts).
 - **SPHERE-HARD:** Features significant divergence, with the demonstrator performing two tasks simultaneously (bimanual), contrasting with the robot's unimanual operation.
 The simulation training dataset included 580 long-horizon robot trajectories and over 3 hours of cross-embodiment demonstrator play data.
- **Real-world:** A 7-DOF Franka arm performed 4 tasks. The training involved 40 long-horizon robot trajectories and approximately 15 minutes of human play data. Testing included unseen task compositions.
- **Baselines:** XSkill (zero-shot transfer from robot-trained policy to human prompts), OraclePairing (gold-standard requiring paired data), RHyME-TCC (using TCC for retrieval), and RHyME-OT (using Optimal Transport for retrieval).
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Task Recall (percentage of successfully completed tasks), Task Imprecision (percentage of incorrectly attempted tasks). Real-world evaluation further distinguished Task Attempts and Task Completions.

**B. Performance Across Different Levels of Execution Mismatch (Simulation)**
- RHyME consistently outperformed XSkill across all three simulated datasets (SPHERE-EASY, SPHERE-MEDIUM, SPHERE-HARD) in terms of Task Recall and Imprecision.
- The performance gap was most significant in the SPHERE-HARD dataset, where RHyME achieved a Task Recall of 53% compared to XSkill's 1%. This indicates RHyME's robustness to severe execution mismatches (e.g., bimanual human actions vs. unimanual robot).
- The OraclePairing baseline established an upper bound on performance.
- Analysis of t-SNE plots of visual representations revealed that as execution mismatch increased, robot and demonstrator embeddings became less clustered by task (Fig. 4 Left), which explains XSkill's reduced ability to transfer zero-shot. RHyME's sequence-level reasoning addressed this issue.

**C. Real-world Performance with Human Videos**
- In real-world kitchen tasks, RHyME-OT demonstrated consistent outperformance compared to XSkill when prompted with human videos, for both seen and unseen task compositions.
- For unseen task compositions, RHyME showed significant improvements in Task Attempts (83% vs. 50% for XSkill) and Task Completions (67% vs. 33% for XSkill).
- Visualization of real-world task embeddings indicated task clustering, but deviations were observed during tasks like "Light Switch." XSkill frequently failed to attempt this task when prompted by human embeddings, whereas RHyME consistently attempted and largely completed it.

**D. Impact of Optimal Transport vs. TCC for Video Retrieval**
- RHyME-OT consistently outperformed RHyME-TCC across all simulated datasets, indicating that Optimal Transport led to more accurate train-time retrievals.
- Inaccuracies in TCC retrievals were attributed to its attempts to establish one-to-one correspondences between frames, which is challenging when human and robot executions differ in speed, style, or bimanual vs. unimanual operation (Fig. 4 Right). For example, a single bimanual human frame might not have a direct unimanual robot frame equivalent, leading to high TCC losses and incorrect cycle-back mappings.
- Optimal Transport, by reasoning over the distributions of embeddings across a sequence and finding optimal global assignments, was shown to be more effective in matching video clips at a task level, even with varying lengths and significant execution mismatches (Fig. 5).

**E. Effect of Fine-tuning Visual Representations with Task-Equivalent Pairs**
- Introducing a small set of short-horizon paired task snippets to fine-tune the vision encoder using the $L_{task}(\phi)$ loss improved the performance of both XSkill and RHyME-OT in the SPHERE-HARD setting.
- However, RHyME-OT, even without this fine-tuning (i.e., with $L_{task}(\phi)=0$), still significantly outperformed a fine-tuned XSkill baseline. This finding underscores the robustness and effectiveness of RHyME's retrieval-based approach and the benefits of optimal transport for handling execution mismatches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, RHyME, that addresses a notable challenge in robot learning: robustly imitating human demonstrations under conditions of mismatched execution, without requiring extensive paired human-robot data. The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Enabling Scalable Imitation Learning:** By largely eliminating the need for paired human-robot demonstration datasets, RHyME offers a pathway to leverage vast, readily available, and unpaired human video data (e.g., from online platforms like YouTube) for training robots. This could significantly reduce the cost and labor associated with data collection, making robot programming more accessible and scalable for complex, long-horizon tasks.

2. **Increased Robustness to Execution Mismatches:** The framework’s ability to handle substantial differences in movement styles, speed, and physical capabilities between human demonstrators and robots represents a step toward more adaptable and versatile robotic systems. This is critical for real-world deployment, where human demonstrations are naturally diverse and often differ from a robot's operational kinematics.

3. **Advancement in Cross-Embodiment Representation Learning:** The explicit focus on sequence-level similarity functions, particularly Optimal Transport, is a significant methodological contribution. It demonstrates that a holistic, distributional comparison of video embeddings can overcome the limitations of frame-level visual alignment, which often fails under severe mismatches. This approach provides a more robust mechanism for establishing semantic equivalence between diverse visual streams from different embodiments.

4. **Improved One-Shot Imitation Capabilities:** The demonstrated performance gains in both simulation and real-world tasks highlight RHyME's effectiveness in one-shot imitation settings. A robot that can learn a new, complex task from a single, unconstrained human video greatly enhances its flexibility and ease of programming for end-users, moving towards more intuitive human-robot collaboration.

5. **New Tools for Research:** The introduction of novel simulated datasets (SPHERE-EASY, -MEDIUM, -HARD) with progressively increasing execution mismatches provides a valuable benchmark for future research in cross-embodiment learning and robust imitation.

**Limitations:** The paper acknowledges that while the method generalizes well to new compositions of known tasks, it relies on transitions between task pairs already present in the robot dataset. This implies that the current framework may not be capable of learning entirely novel task sequences whose constituent transitions are completely unseen during training, representing a current boundary for its generalization capabilities.

Despite this limitation, the RHyME framework offers a practical and effective solution to a fundamental problem in robot learning, paving the way for robots that can more readily learn from human observation in dynamic and unstructured environments.
