# CoIRL-AD: Collaborative-Competitive Imitation-Reinforcement Learning in Latent World Models for Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called CoIRL-AD integrates imitation learning and reinforcement learning using a competitive dual-policy approach within a latent world model for offline end-to-end autonomous driving, enhancing generalization in challenging scenarios and reducing L2 error and collision rates on the nuScenes benchmark without an external simulator.

## Problem

- End-to-end autonomous driving models, primarily based on imitation learning (IL), often generalize poorly to out-of-distribution or "long-tail" scenarios due to fixed data distributions.
- Integrating reinforcement learning (RL) into autonomous driving is challenging, especially in offline settings with expert-dominated real-world datasets lacking diverse behavioral exploration.
- Existing IL-RL integration methods suffer from training instability, objective conflicts, and biased world model predictions when policies deviate from expert data.

## Method

- **CoIRL-AD** proposes a competitive dual-policy framework with distinct IL and RL actors, both utilizing a shared latent ==world model== to predict future states for offline training.
- The RL actor employs a step-aware group sampling mechanism for efficient exploration and a rule-based reward function (imitation and collision avoidance) for long-horizon advantage estimation.
- A competitive mechanism adaptively transfers knowledge between the IL and RL actors based on their reward scores, mitigating objective conflicts and stabilizing training by preventing policy divergence and value overestimation.

## Results

- CoIRL-AD achieved lower L2 errors and collision rates on the nuScenes benchmark across 1s, 2s, and 3s horizons, resulting in the lowest combined L2 7 Col metric compared to the LAW baseline.
- The framework demonstrated substantially improved cross-city generalization and enhanced performance in challenging long-tail scenarios, which are typically difficult for IL-only models.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the competitive dual-policy learning and the latent world model were crucial for stabilizing RL training, suppressing value overestimation, and preventing policy divergence, leading to better overall robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A learned latent world model can effectively enable model-based reinforcement learning in an offline setting, providing imagined future rollouts for long-horizon reward estimation without an interactive simulator.
> - Decoupling IL and RL policies and introducing a competitive interaction mechanism significantly mitigates gradient conflicts and stabilizes RL training on expert-dominated datasets.
> - Backward planning (inverse causal mask) in the planning head, combined with competitive learning, enhances the RL actor's exploration capabilities and improves overall performance in autonomous driving.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of end-to-end (E2E) autonomous driving, which aims to develop unified models that directly map sensory inputs to driving actions, encompassing perception, prediction, and planning. Most existing E2E autonomous driving systems primarily rely on imitation learning (IL), where models are trained to mimic expert demonstrations. However, a known limitation of IL is its tendency to generalize poorly, particularly in out-of-distribution states or "long-tail" scenarios where expert demonstrations are sparse. This limitation arises because IL models are optimized on a fixed data distribution and can suffer from compounding errors when deployed in real-world environments that present novel situations.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is recognized as a complementary approach that can address these shortcomings by providing task-level supervision through reward signals, allowing agents to learn desired behaviors even in scenarios lacking explicit human demonstrations. However, integrating RL into autonomous driving presents its own set of challenges. Many RL-based driving methods rely on interactive simulators (e.g., CARLA) for online data collection. This paper specifically focuses on the more constrained and practical setting of *offline* RL, where training is conducted solely on existing real-world datasets (such as nuScenes) without interaction with an external simulator.

Within this offline context, a significant challenge is that real-world driving datasets are typically dominated by near-optimal expert actions, leading to limited behavioral diversity. This lack of diverse, suboptimal trajectories makes it difficult for standard offline RL algorithms to learn robust value functions and differentiate between alternative actions. Furthermore, existing approaches for combining IL and RL, such as two-stage methods (pretraining with IL, then fine-tuning with RL) or one-stage methods (jointly optimizing IL and RL), face issues like training instability due to biased world model predictions for out-of-distribution actions, or gradient conflicts between the behavior cloning and reward maximization objectives.

This work integrates learned world models, which have been explored in recent E2E driving research (e.g., LAW, World4Drive), to provide imagined future rollouts. CoIRL-AD distinguishes itself by proposing a framework designed to stably integrate IL and RL in an offline, expert-data-dominated environment, without relying on an external simulator, thereby aiming to improve generalization and robustness in real-world autonomous driving applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the generalization capabilities and performance of end-to-end autonomous driving models, particularly in challenging long-tail scenarios, by integrating reinforcement learning with imitation learning in an offline training setup without an external simulator.

The motivations driving this objective are multifaceted:

1. **Addressing Imitation Learning's Generalization Deficiencies:** Current end-to-end autonomous driving systems, predominantly built upon imitation learning, exhibit limitations in real-world deployment. These models are optimized for a fixed distribution of expert demonstrations. During deployment, small prediction errors can lead the agent into novel, unseen states (distribution shift), causing performance degradation and poor generalization. This issue is particularly pronounced in rare or "long-tail" scenarios where expert data is scarce or non-existent, making it difficult for IL-only models to react appropriately.

2. **Leveraging Reinforcement Learning for Robustness:** Reinforcement learning offers a principled way to learn from task-level reward signals. This capability is valuable for autonomous driving as it allows the agent to learn desirable behaviors and recover from errors, potentially improving robustness and adaptability to diverse and complex situations beyond what is explicitly demonstrated by experts.

3. **Overcoming Challenges of Offline RL in Autonomous Driving:**
 - **Absence of Online Simulators:** Many successful RL applications in driving rely on interactive simulators. The objective here is to apply RL directly to static, real-world datasets, which eliminates the possibility of active exploration and introduces the "offline" constraint.
 - **Data Distribution Bias:** Real-world driving datasets are typically dominated by expert or near-optimal demonstrations. This homogenous data distribution provides limited "exploratory" data or suboptimal trajectories, which are crucial for traditional offline RL algorithms to learn effective value functions and discover optimal policies by evaluating diverse behaviors. Learning value differences becomes difficult when only good actions are observed.
 - **Long-Horizon Reward Estimation:** Autonomous driving tasks inherently involve long-term consequences. Estimating long-horizon cumulative rewards is challenging in offline settings, requiring mechanisms to predict future states and evaluate long-term outcomes without direct interaction.
 - **Instability in IL-RL Integration:** Previous attempts to combine IL and RL in a single framework have encountered issues. Jointly optimizing behavior cloning and reward maximization objectives can lead to conflicting gradients. Furthermore, if a learned world model is used for RL, its predictions can be biased for out-of-distribution actions, potentially leading to over-optimistic value estimates and unstable RL training, especially when policies deviate from the expert data distribution.

To address these motivations, the specific goals of the CoIRL-AD framework include:
- Designing an RL framework tailored for offline E2E driving that can operate effectively with expert-dominated datasets.
- Developing a method to estimate long-horizon rewards using a latent world model, thereby avoiding reliance on external simulators.
- Proposing an integration strategy for IL and RL that mitigates objective conflicts and stabilizes training, particularly by preventing RL from exploiting world model inaccuracies.
- Introducing a mechanism for stable interaction and knowledge transfer between the IL and RL components to selectively transfer beneficial behaviors while maintaining adherence to expert-like driving.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CoIRL-AD proposes a competitive dual-policy framework to integrate imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) within an offline training regime for end-to-end autonomous driving. The framework consists of a shared latent world model, a perception module, an IL actor, an RL actor, and a critic.

#### 3.1. Actor Modeling and Latent World Model

The system begins with a perception module that encodes current observations (e.g., camera images) into a latent state `s`. A planning head then extracts waypoint features from `s` via cross-attention and decodes them into an action sequence `τ_a` (representing future waypoints).
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** The IL actor is trained using an L1 loss (`L_imi`) that supervises its output action sequence `τ_a` against expert demonstrations `τ_e_a`.
- **Latent World Model:** A latent world model is trained in a self-supervised manner to predict future latent states `ŝ'` given the current state `s` and a planned action sequence `τ_a`. This prediction is supervised by a mean squared error (MSE) loss (`L_wm`) against ground-truth future states `s'`.
- **Total IL Loss:** The overall imitation learning loss (`L_IL`) combines the imitation loss and the world model loss: `L_IL = L_imi + α · L_wm`, where `α` is a hyperparameter.

#### 3.2. Backward Planning

To provide richer contextual information for earlier actions in the predicted trajectory, the planning head employs an inverse causal mask in its self-attention layer. This "backward planning" mechanism conditions the `i`-th action on current and future waypoint features (`s_w,j≥i`), rather than only past and current features. This design is motivated by human goal-oriented driving and is shown to benefit the RL actor by supporting goal-oriented reward maximization.

#### 3.3. Reinforcement Learning (RL)

The RL component aims to optimize long-horizon cumulative rewards.
- **Reward Function:** A rule-based reward function `r_i` is defined for each step `i` of a generated trajectory. It combines two components:
 - `r_imi`: An imitation reward based on the exponential negative squared L2 distance between the predicted action `a_i` and the expert action `a_e_i`, encouraging expert-like behavior.
 - `r_col`: A collision reward, which is 1 if no collision is detected for the predicted trajectory portion and 0 otherwise. Collision detection uses static map information and dynamic agent trajectories from the dataset, acting as a non-reactive simulation.
 - The final reward `r_i = r_col · r_imi`.
- **Stochastic Policy Parameterization:** To enable exploration, the RL actor's planning head outputs both the mean (`μ_i`) and standard deviation (`σ_i`) for each action `a_i`, modeling it as a Gaussian distribution.
- **Step-aware Group Sampling:** Inspired by GRPO, `G` action sequences are sampled from the stochastic policy for exploration. To ensure that these sampled trajectories are kinematically plausible and smooth (rather than random and unrealistic), a "step-aware" mechanism is introduced. Within each sampled sequence, only one action `a_i` is sampled stochastically, while the remaining actions are set to the mode (mean) of their respective policies. This approach focuses exploration on individual action decisions while maintaining the overall trajectory's coherence.
- **Long-Term Advantage Estimation:** A critic model `V` is trained to estimate the value of states. Since offline datasets do not provide next states for sampled trajectories, the latent world model is utilized to predict future states `ŝ'^(g)` for each sampled trajectory `τ^g_a`. This allows for the computation of a long-term advantage `A^g_long` using the sum of immediate rewards and the discounted value of the imagined future state (`Στ^g_r + γV(ŝ'^(g))) - V(s)`). These advantages are then Z-score normalized within each group (`A^g_cri`).
- **RL Objectives:** The RL actor is optimized using a policy gradient-like objective (`L_act`) that maximizes `A^g_cri · log π(a^g_i | s_w,j≥i)`. The critic is optimized with an MSE loss (`L_cri`) against the target value. A small behavior cloning term (`β · L_bc`) is added to the total RL loss (`L_RL = L_act + L_cri + β · L_bc`) as a regularizer, providing weak expert guidance and improving training stability. A two-critic trick is employed for stable critic learning.

#### 3.4. Dual-Policy Learning Framework

To manage the potential gradient conflicts between IL and RL objectives, the framework decouples the planning module into distinct IL and RL actors, which are optimized separately by `L_IL` and `L_RL`, respectively.
- **Competitive Learning Mechanism:** To foster interaction and knowledge transfer between the two decoupled actors, a competitive mechanism is introduced. Periodically, the accumulated reward scores of the IL and RL actors are compared. Based on the score difference (`Δr_acc`) and predefined thresholds (`λ_min`, `λ_max`), the parameters of the "loser" actor are adaptively updated:
 - If performance is similar, both actors remain unchanged.
 - If the performance gap is moderate, soft merging is applied, gradually transferring knowledge from the winner to the loser (e.g., `loser.weight := loser.weight · p + winner.weight · (1 − p)`).
 - If the gap is large, the loser's parameters are directly replaced with the winner's.
This adaptive mechanism promotes stable cooperation, preventing premature dominance by one actor and allowing the RL actor to recover from potentially degraded updates caused by over-optimistic value estimates or world model errors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the nuScenes benchmark evaluated CoIRL-AD against existing methods, focusing on L2 displacement error, collision rate, cross-city generalization, and long-tail scenario performance.

- **Overall Performance on nuScenes:** CoIRL-AD consistently demonstrated improvements over the LAW baseline (a strong IL-based method) across various metrics. Without temporal augmentation, CoIRL-AD achieved lower L2 errors and collision rates at 1s, 2s, and 3s prediction horizons. When temporal augmentation was enabled, CoIRL-AD further reduced the L2 error and maintained a low collision rate, resulting in the lowest combined L2 · Col metric. The method operates with no additional inference latency compared to the baseline.

- **Cross-city Generalization Ability:** A key finding was CoIRL-AD's significantly improved robustness in cross-city generalization. When trained on nuScenes-Singapore and tested on nuScenes-Boston, the LAW baseline exhibited notable performance degradation. In contrast, CoIRL-AD showed markedly better performance in terms of both L2 error and collision rate, which the authors attribute to the exploration capabilities introduced by the RL actor.

- **Performance in Long-Tail Scenarios:** The research specifically evaluated performance on subsets of scenarios identified as challenging (high L2 error or high collision rate) for the baseline model. CoIRL-AD demonstrated substantial improvements over the baseline in both long-tail subsets, indicating that the reinforcement learning component is particularly beneficial in scenarios where expert demonstrations are sparse or insufficient.

- **Ablation Study: Effect of Causality:**
 - The inverse causal mask (backward planning) was found to primarily benefit the RL actor's exploration. While applying it to the pure IL baseline degraded performance, its use within CoIRL-AD improved both L2 error and collision rate.
 - Mismatched causal masks between the IL and RL actors (e.g., IL using forward AR, RL using inverse AR) resulted in weaker knowledge transfer and reduced performance, indicating the importance of aligned representations for competitive learning.
 - Removing the competitive mechanism when using inverse causal masking led to degraded performance, suggesting that RL exploration requires stabilization.
 - The combination of inverse causal masking and the competitive mechanism yielded the most effective performance, indicating a synergistic relationship.

- **Ablation Study: Effect of the Latent World Model:** CoIRL-AD's performance was superior to a variant without the latent world model, particularly in reducing the 3-second horizon collision rate. This underscores the importance and effectiveness of using the learned world model as a reactive simulator for reinforcement learning.

- **Ablation Study: Integration of IL and RL Strategies:** Various IL-RL integration strategies were compared: loss merging, alternating optimization (IL-RL interval), two-stage pretraining/fine-tuning, and decoupled actors (with and without competition).
 - Pure RL and two-stage IL-RL transfer exhibited significant performance degradation, often worse than pure IL. The authors attributed this to the offline setting where world model predictions can be biased for out-of-distribution actions, leading to over-optimistic value estimates and unstable training.
 - Only the decoupled actors with competitive interaction strategy consistently improved both L2 error and collision rate over the IL baseline, highlighting its effectiveness in mitigating objective conflicts and stabilizing RL.

- **Competition Analysis:**
 - During training, the IL actor initially performed better, leading the learning process. As the latent world model improved and the RL actor learned fundamental driving behaviors through exploration, the RL actor progressively achieved higher scores and became dominant in later training stages.
 - The competitive mechanism was observed to suppress value overestimation in the critic's predictions for imagined future states. Without competition, value estimates grew rapidly, but with competition, they plateaued.
 - Similarly, competition prevented the RL actor's policy from diverging significantly from the expert policy (measured by L2 distance between modal trajectories). Unconstrained RL without competition led to larger policy divergence, likely due to the pursuit of hallucinated rewards from overestimated values. The competitive mechanism helped anchor the RL actor to a safer trust region near the expert policy.

- **Qualitative Analysis:** Visualizations of planned trajectories demonstrated that CoIRL-AD proactively recognized hazardous situations and avoided potential collisions with other vehicles and pedestrians more effectively than the baseline LAW model. Examples included successful collision avoidance with fast-approaching vehicles, yielding to crossing pedestrians, and adapting to incorrect high-level commands.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents CoIRL-AD as a contribution to the field of autonomous driving, particularly concerning the challenges of deploying robust end-to-end systems trained on offline real-world datasets.

The significance of this work stems from several points:

1. **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** By effectively integrating RL with IL, CoIRL-AD addresses a fundamental limitation of imitation-learning-based autonomous driving models: their poor generalization and fragility in novel or under-represented "long-tail" scenarios. The demonstrated improvements in cross-city generalization and performance in challenging situations suggest a step towards more robust and adaptable autonomous driving systems.

2. **Novel Offline IL-RL Integration Paradigm:** The proposed competitive dual-policy framework offers a distinct approach to combining IL and RL in an offline setting. It directly tackles issues such as objective conflicts between IL and RL, the instability of RL training on expert-dominated datasets, and biases in learned world models. This framework demonstrates that effective integration of IL and RL can be achieved without relying on computationally expensive interactive simulators, which is a practical constraint for many real-world applications.

3. **Stable Reinforcement Learning from Expert Data:** The research provides insights into how to stabilize model-based reinforcement learning when training on offline datasets that are primarily composed of expert demonstrations. The use of a latent world model for imagined future rollouts, coupled with the step-aware group sampling mechanism, allows for effective exploration and value estimation in a data-constrained environment where traditional offline RL methods might fail.

4. **Mitigation of Value Overestimation and Policy Divergence:** The competitive learning mechanism is a key innovation that actively manages the interaction between the IL and RL actors. It helps in suppressing value overestimation in RL and prevents the RL policy from drifting too far from expert-like behavior, thereby maintaining safety and stability during exploration. This is crucial for real-world applications where erratic or unsafe actions are unacceptable.

5. **Practical Applicability:** The CoIRL-AD framework introduces no additional inference latency, maintaining the real-time performance required for autonomous driving systems. By improving safety and reliability in critical situations, the work contributes to building more trustworthy and deployable autonomous vehicles.

The techniques developed in CoIRL-AD, particularly the competitive dual-policy learning and the strategies for stabilizing model-based RL in offline, expert-dominated settings, may extend beyond autonomous driving. They could find application in broader embodied AI tasks and other domains where learning from limited expert demonstrations and improving generalization in complex, sequential decision-making environments are critical. This work suggests a promising direction for future research in combining different learning paradigms for enhanced AI agent capabilities.
