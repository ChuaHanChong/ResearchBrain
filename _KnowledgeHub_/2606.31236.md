# TactX: Learning Shared Tactile Representations Across Diverse Sensors

> [!summary] Summary
> The TACTX framework unifies tactile observations from fundamentally different sensor modalities (vision-based, magnetic, resistive) into a shared latent space. This enables zero-shot policy transfer for robotic manipulation tasks, improving average success rates from 27.5% with vision-only transfer to 45.9% across diverse sensor types.

## Problem

- Tactile representations and manipulation policies are typically hardware-dependent, requiring extensive data collection and retraining when sensors are changed.
- The inherent diversity in physical transduction principles among tactile sensors limits policy transferability across different robotic setups and hardware platforms.
- Existing cross-sensor learning methods primarily focus on similar sensing substrates, not bridging fundamentally different physical modalities like vision, magnetic, and resistive sensing.

## Method

- **TACTX** employs modality-specific encoders (==ResNet-18== for vision, ==MLP== for magnetic, ==CNN== for resistive) to map heterogeneous tactile signals into a shared 16-dimensional latent space.
- The framework is trained using paired quasi-static contact data from co-located sensors and a multi-objective loss combining ==self-reconstruction==, ==cross-reconstruction==, ==contrastive alignment== (NT-Xent), and ==KL divergence== to a shared prior.
- For downstream tasks, deterministic latent representations from **TACTX** encoders replace raw tactile inputs, enabling pre-trained manipulation policies (==Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT)==) to be deployed zero-shot with different tactile sensors.

## Results

- TACTX improved average zero-shot cross-sensor policy transfer success rates from **27.5%** (vision-only baseline) to **45.9%** across four contact-rich robotic manipulation tasks.
- The framework achieved strong cross-sensor alignment, with a transitive alignment cosine similarity of **0.928** between sensors that were never directly paired during training.
- The shared latent space demonstrated content preservation through accurate self- and cross-reconstructions and enabled object classification with **60.8%** (self-sensor) and **56.2%** (cross-sensor) accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pairwise supervision is sufficient to establish a globally consistent and transitively aligned latent space across multiple heterogeneous tactile sensors, even for non-directly observed pairs.
> - The learned shared latent space effectively preserves task-relevant object-level contact information, crucial for robust manipulation, while reducing sensor-specific details.
> - A combination of reconstruction, contrastive alignment, and KL divergence regularizes the latent space, forcing sensor-agnostic representations that retain core tactile content.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Tactile sensing is recognized as providing critical information for contact-rich manipulation tasks that vision-only systems may not adequately address. This includes tasks requiring sub-millimeter precision, operating in occluded environments, or estimating contact states. The field of robotic manipulation has seen numerous demonstrations of tactile feedback improving robustness in scenarios such as peg-in-hole insertion, in-hand reorientation, and grasping under clutter.

However, a significant challenge in tactile robotics is the diversity of sensor hardware. Modern tactile sensors utilize various transduction principles, including vision-based deformation, magnetic field changes, and resistive pressure measurements. This diversity, while enabling specialized capabilities for different applications, leads to hardware-dependent tactile representations and policies. A policy trained with data from one specific tactile sensor is typically coupled to that sensor's unique observation space, often necessitating extensive data collection and retraining when the sensor hardware is changed.

Existing research on cross-sensor tactile learning primarily focuses on transferring representations between sensors within a similar sensing substrate, most commonly within the family of vision-based tactile sensors, where signals share an image-like format. While some efforts have begun to address sensors without a common input format, these often involve mapping signals to a unified input structure or aligning distributions without paired data.

This paper addresses a gap in the literature by focusing on learning shared tactile representations across sensors that rely on *fundamentally different* physical transduction modalities, rather than just similar types of sensors. The TACTX framework aims to develop a sensor-agnostic approach that can align observations from vision-based, magnetic, and resistive tactile sensors into a single, consistent latent space without requiring explicit per-sensor input transformations, thereby enabling broader transferability of learned manipulation policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, TACTX, for learning a transferable tactile representation that can be shared across heterogeneous tactile sensors. This aims to overcome the limitation where tactile representations and manipulation policies are tightly coupled to specific sensor hardware, which hinders transferability across different robots and hardware platforms.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges in current tactile manipulation:
- **Hardware Dependence:** Current tactile representations and downstream policies are typically specific to the particular sensor used for training. This means that if a robot's tactile sensor is replaced with a different model or modality, the existing policy often becomes incompatible, requiring a complete retraining process. This is costly in terms of data collection, computational resources, and development time.
- **Limited Transferability:** The lack of a shared representation across diverse tactile sensors limits the transferability of learned manipulation skills. Policies developed for one robotic setup cannot be directly applied to another with different tactile hardware, impeding the scalability and generalization of robotic systems.
- **Heterogeneous Sensing Modalities:** Tactile sensors vary significantly in their underlying physical principles, leading to distinct observation spaces (e.g., image-like data from vision-based sensors, vector fields from magnetic sensors, pressure grids from resistive sensors). Bridging these fundamentally different modalities into a common representation is a complex problem that existing cross-sensor methods have largely not addressed in a comprehensive, multi-modality manner. Prior work often focused on variations within a single modality, such as different vision-based sensors.

To address these challenges, the key objectives of TACTX are:
1. **Develop a Framework for Cross-Modal Alignment:** To create a framework capable of mapping heterogeneous tactile observations, specifically from resistive, magnetic, and vision-based sensors, into a shared latent space.
2. **Enable Zero-Shot Policy Transfer:** To demonstrate that policies trained with one tactile sensor can be transferred directly, without retraining, to physically distinct sensors through the learned shared latent representation.
3. **Preserve Task-Relevant Information:** To ensure that while achieving sensor-agnosticism, the shared latent space retains sufficient object-level contact information necessary for robust manipulation tasks.
4. **Establish a Scalable Training Strategy:** To introduce a training approach that leverages pairwise contact data to align multiple sensor modalities into a globally consistent latent space, allowing for the natural incorporation of additional sensors in the future.

Ultimately, the motivation is to take a step toward sensor-agnostic tactile manipulation, thereby reducing the dependency on specific hardware and facilitating the development of more adaptable and generalizable robotic manipulation capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TACTX framework is designed to align tactile observations from diverse sensing modalities into a shared latent representation. This is achieved through a combination of modality-specific encoders, contrastive learning for alignment, and reconstruction objectives for content preservation.

**Data Collection:**
To facilitate the learning of shared representations, TACTX relies on paired contact data. Data is collected using a parallel-jaw gripper where two different tactile sensors are mounted on opposing fingers. This setup allows for the simultaneous measurement of the same physical contact event by two distinct sensors. The data collection process involves:
- **Paired Observations:** For each sensor pair (S_i, S_j), quasi-static grasps of rigid, symmetric objects are recorded. Each grasp produces a temporally aligned pair of observations (x_i^(t), x_j^(t)), corresponding to the same contact point.
- **Sensor Modalities:** The study uses three distinct tactile sensor modalities:
 - **Daimon:** A vision-based tactile sensor.
 - **eFlesh:** A magnetic tactile sensor.
 - **FlexiTac:** A resistive tactile sensor.
- **Dataset Composition:** Paired datasets are collected for all combinations of sensor pairs (Daimon-eFlesh, eFlesh-FlexiTac, FlexiTac-Daimon). The data includes interactions with 10 different 3D-printed objects designed to provide diverse contact geometries (point, edge, area contacts). An automated protocol ensures consistent contact conditions across numerous repetitions. Preprocessing steps include contact gating based on sensor readings to filter non-contact frames.

**Architecture:**
The TACTX architecture consists of modality-specific encoders and decoders:
- **Encoders (f_i):** For each sensor 'i', a dedicated encoder maps its native signal x_i into a 16-dimensional shared latent space Z. This mapping is probabilistic, outputting the parameters (mean μ_i(x_i) and variance diag(σ^2_i(x_i))) of a Gaussian posterior q_i(z | x_i). The low dimensionality of the latent space encourages the encoders to extract shared, compact contact features. The encoders are trained from scratch without pre-trained weights.
 - Daimon (vision-based): Uses a ResNet-18 backbone.
 - eFlesh (magnetic): Employs a multi-layer perceptron (MLP).
 - FlexiTac (resistive): Utilizes a residual CNN backbone.
- **Decoders (g_i):** Each sensor 'i' also has its own decoder, g_i: Z → X_i. These decoders are responsible for reconstructing the native sensor signal from the latent representation. They are used for both self-reconstruction (decoding z_i back into x_i) and cross-reconstruction (decoding z_j from a paired sensor into x_i).

**Training Objective:**
The training objective, L_TACTX, is optimized jointly across all available sensor pairs (i, j) at each step. It comprises three main terms:
1. **Reconstruction Loss (L_recon):** This term uses an L1 norm and combines both self-reconstruction and cross-reconstruction.
 - *Self-reconstruction:* Aims for the latent representation from a sensor (z_i) to reconstruct its own ground-truth observation (x_i).
 - *Cross-reconstruction:* Requires the latent representation from one sensor (z_i) to reconstruct the ground-truth observation of its paired sensor (x_j). This component is crucial for forcing shared content into the latent space and establishing ties between modalities.
2. **Alignment Loss (L_align):** A symmetric NT-Xent loss is applied to the L2-normalized posterior means of paired contacts (˜μ_i, ˜μ_j). This contrastive loss pulls the latent representations of positively paired contacts (from the same physical interaction) closer together in the latent space, while pushing away representations of negative pairs (other embeddings in the batch).
3. **KL Divergence Loss (L_KL):** This term regularizes each sensor's posterior distribution toward a shared prior (standard normal distribution N(0, I)). This helps ensure that all modalities converge to a common, consistent region in the latent space.

**Pairwise Joint Training:**
During training, each step samples from all available pair datasets (D_DE, D_EF, D_FD). The model optimizes the objective using these paired batches simultaneously. This setup exposes every sensor encoder to paired supervision at each step. The shared latent space and the common prior facilitate the global consistency of the pairwise alignments across all three modalities.

**Inference for Downstream Tasks:**
For downstream manipulation policies, the deterministic posterior mean z = μ_i(x_i) is used as the stable 16-dimensional latent representation of tactile input. These precomputed latents replace raw tactile inputs in policy datasets. A lightweight MLP adapter maps these latents into a single tactile token for an Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policy. This approach enables policies trained with one sensor to be deployed zero-shot with another, as only the sensor-specific encoder branch for obtaining the latent changes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TACTX focused on assessing the alignment quality, content preservation within the shared latent space, and its practical utility for zero-shot policy transfer in robotic manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Cross-Sensor Alignment

- **Quantitative Alignment:** TACTX demonstrated consistent alignment of paired contacts across all sensor pairs. When comparing cosine similarity between positive contact pairs, TACTX achieved performance comparable to a contrastive-only model, which is optimized solely for alignment, while also incorporating reconstruction.
- **Qualitative Alignment:** t-SNE visualizations showed that after training with TACTX, samples from the three distinct sensor modalities (Daimon, eFlesh, FlexiTac) became substantially more mixed in the latent space, indicating a reduction in sensor-specific clustering compared to pre-training.
- **Sensor Invariance:** A linear sensor-identity probe, trained on frozen latent features, showed that TACTX reduced sensor prediction accuracy from 67.5% (for a reconstruction-only variant) to 47.5%. This value is closer to the 33.3% chance level for three sensors, suggesting that TACTX effectively reduces sensor-specific information in the latent space, thus increasing sensor invariance.

#### 4.2. Three-Way Alignment from Pairwise Data

- The study investigated whether pairwise supervision is sufficient to establish a globally consistent latent space for multiple sensors that are never simultaneously observed. TACTX was trained on Daimon-eFlesh (D-E) and eFlesh-FlexiTac (E-F) pairs.
- **Transitive Alignment:** TACTX achieved a transitive alignment cosine similarity of 0.928 between Daimon and FlexiTac via eFlesh, significantly outperforming a reconstruction-only model (0.626) and an L2-alignment model (0.679). This result indicates that TACTX does not merely learn independent pairwise mappings but induces a globally consistent shared latent space across all three modalities through the transitive property of the pairwise alignments.

#### 4.3. Tactile Content Preservation

- **Reconstruction Quality:** Self-reconstructions (decoding a sensor's latent back to its own signal) and cross-reconstructions (decoding one sensor's latent to another's signal) from the shared latent space preserved dominant contact patterns across all three sensing modalities. Visualizations showed that the reconstructed outputs were visually consistent with the ground truth, suggesting that the latent space retained object- and contact-level structural information.
- **Object Classification:** Object classifiers trained on frozen TACTX latent features achieved high accuracy (60.8% for self-sensor evaluation and 56.2% for cross-sensor evaluation) in classifying 10 different object classes. This indicates that the shared latent representation preserves object-level tactile structure, even when information is transferred and decoded across different sensor types.

#### 4.4. Zero-Shot Policy Transfer for Robotic Manipulation

- **In-Domain Policy Performance:** Policies (ACT) trained with tactile input (either raw or TACTX latents) generally outperformed vision-only policies on the four contact-rich manipulation tasks (pick-and-place, plug insertion, board wiping, object reorientation). TACTX-based policies retained most of the benefits observed with raw tactile inputs, indicating that the shared latent space captures task-relevant information effectively for same-sensor performance.
- **Cross-Sensor Policy Transfer:**
 - TACTX achieved improved zero-shot cross-sensor policy transfer compared to vision-only and binary contact transfer baselines.
 - Averaged over all transfer directions and tasks, TACTX improved the success rate from 27.5% for vision-only transfer to 45.9%.
 - The largest improvements were observed in contact-rich tasks like board wiping and object reorientation, where the shared latent representation provided more nuanced contact geometry information than a simple binary contact signal.
 - Transfer performance was noted to be asymmetric: transfer from eFlesh (lower-dimensional magnetic signal) to FlexiTac (richer resistive signal) was weaker than the reverse. This suggests that policies trained with a richer tactile representation may generalize more effectively when deployed with a lower-bandwidth sensor.
- These results collectively indicate that TACTX not only improves latent space metrics but also provides a practical and effective shared representation for enabling zero-shot tactile policy transfer across heterogeneous sensors in real-world manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TACTX framework represents an advancement in robotic tactile sensing by addressing a fundamental challenge: the hardware dependence of tactile representations and manipulation policies. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Enabling Sensor-Agnostic Manipulation:** TACTX offers a tangible step toward sensor-agnostic tactile manipulation. By learning a shared latent representation that unifies data from fundamentally different transduction modalities—vision-based, magnetic, and resistive sensors—it allows a single learned policy to be deployed across diverse tactile hardware without re-training. This capability is critical for increasing the flexibility and adaptability of robotic systems.

- **Reduced Development Costs and Time:** The ability to transfer policies zero-shot across sensors significantly reduces the need for extensive data collection and retraining when new tactile sensors are introduced or hardware configurations change. This can lead to substantial savings in development time, computational resources, and human effort in robotic application development.

- **Broader Applicability of Tactile Learning:** Previous cross-sensor tactile learning efforts largely focused on similar types of sensors (e.g., within vision-based modalities). TACTX expands the scope to fundamentally disparate physical sensing mechanisms, opening new avenues for integrating and leveraging the strengths of various tactile sensor technologies in a unified manner.

- **Enhanced Robot Robustness and Generalization:** By providing policies with access to rich, modality-independent contact information, TACTX can lead to more robust and generalizable manipulation capabilities. The demonstrated improvements in success rates for contact-rich tasks, particularly over vision-only and binary contact baselines, underscore the value of the shared tactile representation in resolving manipulation failures that visual input alone cannot address.

- **Scalable Framework for Future Sensors:** The pairwise joint training strategy is designed to be extensible. New tactile sensor modalities can be integrated into the shared latent space by collecting paired data with existing aligned sensors, facilitating the continuous expansion of the framework to accommodate emerging tactile technologies.

While the work has limitations, such as its reliance on paired quasi-static contact data and current limitations with highly dynamic interactions, TACTX offers a foundational contribution towards building more adaptable, efficient, and versatile robotic systems capable of leveraging a wide spectrum of tactile sensing technologies. This paves the way for a more unified and scalable approach to tactile intelligence in robotics.
