# Bridging Reinforcement Learning and Optimal Control via Feasible Action Mapping

> [!summary] Summary
> Feasible Action for Optimal Control (FAOC) is a framework integrating Reinforcement Learning (RL) with Optimal Control (OC) to enable RL agents to operate within a simple abstract action space while strictly guaranteeing OCP feasibility. This method improves RL sample efficiency and final performance, achieving superior metrics in robot table tennis such as return rate and generated topspin, particularly excelling under lower RL decision frequencies.

## Problem

- Pure Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies lack strict guarantees for constraint satisfaction, hindering their use in safety-critical real-world applications.
- Optimal Control (OC) methods often face computational intractability for complex, non-convex, or long-horizon problems and can struggle with exploration or sparse reward signals.
- Existing hybrid RL-OC approaches frequently suffer from RL actions leading to infeasible Optimal Control Problems (OCPs) or use static, simplified RL action spaces that misrepresent the complex, state-dependent feasible parameter sets of the OCP.

## Method

- **FAOC** develops a hierarchical control framework that maps abstract RL actions, derived from a high-level policy, into state-dependent, guaranteed-feasible parameters for a low-level ==Optimal Control Problem (OCP)== using a novel optimization-based algorithm.
- The approach provides a topological characterization of OCP feasibility, ensuring the target parameter set is compact, solid, and convex, and implements ==invertible mapping== algorithms (2D area-matching or linear transformations) to transform abstract actions.
- It ensures computational tractability for real-time control by developing methods to compute interior points and transformation matrices for implicitly defined, state-dependent ==feasible sets== without requiring explicit geometric representations online.

## Results

- FAOC demonstrated superior sample efficiency and achieved the highest normalized reward in robot table tennis simulations, outperforming baselines in return rate, generated topspin, and generated velocity.
- The framework maintained its performance advantage even when tested with stronger RL algorithms (SimBaV2) and showed greater robustness at lower RL decision frequencies (**7.8125 Hz** vs **31.25 Hz**) compared to other controllers.
- Two-dimensional controllers, including FAOC, consistently surpassed one-dimensional variants, and FAOC's strict feasibility guarantee for RL actions led to more effective learning compared to baselines that either used unconstrained action spaces or penalized infeasibility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The state-dependent feasible parameter set of a generic OCP can be characterized as compact, solid, and convex under specific conditions, which is crucial for constructing robust and invertible mappings.
> - An invertible mapping between a static abstract action space and the state-dependent feasible OCP parameter space is essential to avoid "action aliasing" and ensure full exploration, which can be efficiently implemented through boundary scaling and directional transformations.
> - Geometric distortion between the abstract and feasible action spaces can be mitigated through specific transformations (e.g., 2D area-matching or linear transformations based on inscribed ellipsoids), preserving sampling properties and improving learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The control of constrained dynamical systems is an active area of research, with two primary paradigms: Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Optimal Control (OC), particularly Model Predictive Control (MPC). Both approaches offer distinct advantages and disadvantages.

Reinforcement Learning excels at solving complex, long-horizon control problems, often without requiring explicit system models, as evidenced by its application in areas such as robotic manipulation, drone racing, and game playing. However, pure RL-based controllers typically lack strict guarantees for constraint satisfaction, which is a critical requirement for safety-critical real-world applications. They can also suffer from poor sample efficiency and difficulties with exploration.

Optimal Control, and specifically MPC, addresses the safety concern by systematically incorporating system dynamics and explicitly enforcing constraints on inputs, states, and outputs. This ensures predictable and safe operation, even in novel environments. Nevertheless, OC methods often face computational intractability when applied to large-scale, nonconvex, or long-horizon problems, particularly under strict real-time constraints. They can also be sensitive to initialization and may struggle with sparse reward signals, which are common in tasks requiring extensive exploration.

The integration of RL and OC is a recognized strategy to combine their complementary strengths. Prior research in this hybrid domain has explored several approaches. One common method involves using RL to learn the terminal cost of an OCP, approximating the infinite-horizon cost-to-go, though this often renders the OCP nonconvex, impacting real-time solvability. Another prevalent strategy is to combine RL with a parameterized OCP, where the RL agent selects OCP parameters to maximize long-term rewards.

However, existing parameterized OCP approaches often encounter two significant issues:
1. **Feasibility Coupling:** When RL actions directly influence OCP feasibility, some actions may lead to an infeasible OCP. Current solutions include introducing slack variables, reformulating the OCP, or penalizing infeasible configurations via the reward function, which forces the RL agent to implicitly learn complex feasibility boundaries, hindering learning efficiency.
2. **Action Space Design:** For tractability, many methods employ simple, static, continuous RL action spaces. However, the actual feasible parameter sets of the underlying OCP are typically state-dependent and geometrically complex. This mismatch can result in static action spaces containing infeasible or redundant actions while omitting optimal feasible ones, thus degrading performance and exploration. The definition of meaningful action space bounds often relies on heuristics that require careful tuning.

This work, Feasible Action for Optimal Control (FAOC), positions itself within this landscape by addressing these specific challenges. It aims to develop a framework that enables RL agents to operate within a static, simple abstract action space while strictly guaranteeing OCP feasibility. This is achieved through a computationally efficient, optimization-based mapping algorithm that transforms the RL agent's abstract action into a state-dependent, feasible parameter for the OCP. Unlike prior methods, FAOC's abstract action space does not require expert design or heuristic tuning, and the OCP formulation is not compromised by the RL agent's inability to intrinsically guarantee feasibility. The paper differentiates its approach from related work, such as methods limited to one-dimensional mappings or those requiring restrictive assumptions about the action and feasible sets, by offering a generalized, robust, and scalable solution for multi-dimensional, implicitly defined convex sets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel control framework, Feasible Action for Optimal Control (FAOC), that effectively integrates Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Optimal Control (OC) to control constrained dynamical systems. This integration aims to harness the strategic decision-making capabilities of RL while ensuring the strict safety and constraint satisfaction inherent to OC.

The motivation for FAOC stems from the persistent shortcomings observed when applying RL or OC in isolation, or through existing hybrid approaches, particularly in real-world scenarios:

1. **Ensuring Safety in RL:** Pure RL policies demonstrate significant proficiency in solving complex tasks but fundamentally lack mechanisms to strictly guarantee constraint satisfaction (e.g., physical limits, safety protocols). This absence of formal safety guarantees impedes the adoption of RL in critical real-world applications where system failures can have severe consequences.
2. **Overcoming OC Limitations:** While OC, especially Model Predictive Control (MPC), excels at enforcing constraints and utilizing system dynamics knowledge, it often faces computational intractability for nonconvex problems, long prediction horizons, or high-dimensional systems, especially under real-time deadlines. Its effectiveness is also limited in tasks requiring extensive exploration or dealing with sparse reward signals, as it primarily focuses on local optimization.
3. **Addressing Feasibility in Hybrid RL-OC Approaches:** Prior attempts to combine RL and OC frequently encounter challenges at their interface. When an RL agent's action directly influences the feasibility of the underlying OCP, there is a risk of generating infeasible control problems. Existing solutions, such as employing slack variables or penalizing infeasibility, often require the RL agent to implicitly learn complex, state-dependent feasibility boundaries. This process can significantly degrade learning efficiency and overall performance.
4. **Improving RL Action Space Design:** Many hybrid methods use static, simplified RL action spaces, which often fail to accurately represent the complex, state-dependent feasible parameter sets of the OCP. Such static designs can lead to "action aliasing" (different actions mapping to the same parameter) or an action space that includes infeasible parameters while excluding optimal feasible ones. This necessitates heuristic tuning of action space bounds, which can be suboptimal and problem-specific.

The core motivation behind FAOC is to resolve these feasibility and exploration challenges by introducing a mechanism that transparently manages the interface between RL and OC. Specifically, the framework aims to:
- Allow the RL policy to sample actions from a geometrically simple, static abstract set.
- Guarantee that these abstract actions are always transformed into parameters that render the underlying OCP feasible.
- Ensure that all valid and reachable parameters of the OCP remain accessible to the RL agent.
- Relieve the RL agent from the burden of learning physical constraints, allowing it to focus its learning capacity solely on the overarching task objectives and reward maximization.
- Maintain computational efficiency suitable for real-time control, even for implicitly defined and state-dependent feasible parameter sets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FAOC is a hierarchical control framework that integrates a high-level Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy with a low-level Optimal Control Problem (OCP) through a novel, computationally efficient mapping algorithm.

#### 3.1. Framework Overview

The FAOC framework operates in three primary steps (illustrated in Figure 1):
1. An RL agent, observing the system state, outputs parameters (mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$) of a multivariate Gaussian distribution. A raw action is sampled (or taken as the mean during deployment) and then transformed by a nonlinear function $b$ (e.g., $\tanh$) into an abstract action $\bar{a}$ confined within a static, compact abstract set $\bar{A}$. The abstract set $\bar{A}$ is assumed to be compact, solid, and convex.
2. The abstract action $\bar{a}$ is then mapped by a Feasible Action Mapping algorithm ($M$) into a parameter $p$ belonging to the state-dependent feasible parameter set $P(x)$ of the OCP. This mapping guarantees that $p$ is always a feasible input for the OCP, strictly satisfying the dynamical system’s constraints. The set $P(x)$ is assumed to be compact, solid, and convex (Lemma 1 and Corollary 1).
3. The mapped parameter $p$ fixes a component of the OCP, which is subsequently solved to yield an optimal control input trajectory.
4. This trajectory is applied to the system, and the resulting next state $x^+$ is observed, becoming the initial condition for the subsequent control cycle.

#### 3.2. Topological Characterization of OCP Feasibility

The theoretical foundation of FAOC relies on establishing that the state-dependent parameter set $P(x)$ of a generic OCP (formulated as $\min_{z \in Z} f(z, p, x)$ s.t. $g(z, p, x) \le 0, H_z z + H_p p + H_x x + h = 0$) possesses specific topological properties. Lemma 1 details conditions under which $P(x)$ is a compact, solid, and convex subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. These conditions include:
- The set of decision variables $Z$ is nonempty, compact, and convex.
- The cost function $f$ is continuous, and constraint function $g$ is jointly convex and continuous.
- The joint feasible set $S(x)$ has no directions of recession in $p$.
- There exist strictly feasible points $(\tilde{z}, \tilde{p})$ satisfying certain rank conditions for the affine equality constraints.
Corollary 1 applies these general conditions to a Linear MPC problem with a terminal constraint, showing that $P(x)$ is compact, solid, and convex if the terminal constraint matrix $C$ has full row rank.

#### 3.3. Invertible Mapping Between Convex Sets

The core of FAOC is Algorithm 1, which provides an invertible mapping $M: \bar{A} \to P(x)$. The algorithm operates by:
1. Identifying an interior point $x_c \in \text{int } \bar{A}$ and $y_c \in \text{int } P(x)$.
2. For a given abstract action $x \in \bar{A}$, defining a direction $d = x - x_c$.
3. Computing a scaling factor $\alpha$ such that $x_c + \alpha d$ lies on the boundary of $\bar{A}$.
4. Applying a directional transformation $\phi$ to $d$, yielding $d' = \phi(d)$. The transformation $\phi$ must be continuous, invertible, and positively homogeneous.
5. Computing a scaling factor $\beta$ such that $y_c + \beta d'$ lies on the boundary of $P(x)$.
6. The mapped feasible parameter $y$ is then calculated as $y_c + (\beta/\alpha)d'$.
Proposition 1 formally proves the bijectivity (invertibility) of this mapping, which is crucial to avoid "action aliasing" and to allow for techniques like symmetric data augmentation. The boundary scaling factors $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are determined via convex line-search problems, which simplify to computationally trivial ratio tests for polytopes defined by H-representations.

#### 3.4. Mitigation of Geometric Distortion

To prevent point accumulation (where uniformly sampled points in $\bar{A}$ map unevenly into $P(x)$ due to differing geometries), specific directional transformations $\phi$ are developed:
- **2D Area-Matching Transformation (for $n=2$):** Definition 2 introduces a $\phi$ that matches the marginal angular distributions of the sets' areas. This transformation ensures that if points are uniformly distributed angularly in $\bar{A}$, they will also be uniformly distributed angularly in $P(x)$ (Proposition 3). Computationally, for convex polygons, the normalized area functions and their inverses can be calculated analytically using sequential signed triangular area calculations, avoiding expensive trigonometric operations.
- **Linear Transformation in Arbitrary Dimensions (for $n>2$):** For higher dimensions where exact area-matching is intractable, the paper proposes an invertible linear transformation $\phi(d) = \Phi d$. This matrix $\Phi$ is designed to approximate the geometric distortion between $\bar{A}$ and $P(x)$ by using affinely related ellipsoidal surrogate sets (Assumption 1). Proposition 4 shows that $\Phi = Q_{\tilde{Y}} Q_{\tilde{X}}^{-1}$, where $Q_{\tilde{X}}$ and $Q_{\tilde{Y}}$ are the shape matrices of the Maximum Volume Inscribed Ellipsoids (MVIEs) of $\bar{A}$ and $P(x)$, respectively.

#### 3.5. Tractability for Implicitly Defined Target Sets

A significant challenge is that $P(x)$ is often implicitly defined by the OCP constraints and is state-dependent, making explicit geometric representations (e.g., vertices for MVIE computation) computationally prohibitive in real-time. To address this, the methodology extends the linear transformation for implicitly defined target sets $Y = EW + e$, where $W$ is a higher-dimensional convex set of OCP decision variables and $E$ is a projection matrix.
- **Computing an Interior Point $y_c$:** Proposition 5 states that projecting a relative interior point $w_c \in \text{relint } W$ yields an interior point $y_c = Ew_c + e \in \text{int } Y$. Methods to find a robust "deep" interior point in $Y$ are presented:
 - Proposition 6 uses a vertex-based approach for polyhedral norms to maximize an inscribed ball (suitable for small $n$).
 - Proposition 7 proposes a lifting-based method that finds a lower bound to the maximum inscribed ball radius, scaling better to higher dimensions by restricting the number of constraints to the complexity of $W$.
- **Computing the Linear Transformation Matrix $\Phi$:** Proposition 8 outlines how to construct the shape matrix $Q_{\tilde{Y}}$ for $Y$ by mapping a solid ellipsoidal surrogate from a reduced, higher-dimensional space of OCP decision variables (derived by parameterizing the affine hull of $W$) forward into the target space. Example 3 further shows how a *state-independent* $\Phi$ can be computed offline for a parameterized MPC by lifting the initial state into the input space and effectively slicing the augmented surrogate ellipsoid. This avoids expensive online optimization.
- **Computing the Boundary Scaling Factor $\beta$:** Instead of performing a line search on an explicit representation of $Y$, $\beta$ is found by solving a convex optimization problem that lifts the search into the higher-dimensional space of $W$ (equation 8). This avoids online projection operations.

By combining these techniques, FAOC executes the mapping without requiring an explicit geometric representation of the state-dependent target set $P(x)$ during real-time control, ensuring computational tractability.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup (Robot Table Tennis)

The framework is evaluated on a real-time motion planning problem for an 8-DoF robot arm in table tennis.
- **Motion Planning:** An OCP is formulated for each joint to generate 32ms-horizon trajectories, ensuring smooth, safe motions within physical limits (position, velocity, acceleration, jerk). The 32ms horizon is divided into $N=4$ intervals of $T=8$ms, assuming constant jerk, leading to a cubic spline trajectory. A key technique for recursive feasibility involves slightly shrinking the joint limits, enabling a computationally efficient MCIS for the terminal state. The OCP solves for jerk inputs and targets terminal position and velocity (p = Cx_N).
- **Comparisons:** FAOC is compared against:
 - **1D controllers (1Dpos, 1Dvel, 1Dacc):** These use the method from, where the RL agent specifies a single 1D waypoint (position, velocity, or acceleration), incorporating reachability awareness.
 - **2Dsoft:** A baseline where the RL policy outputs a 2D position-velocity waypoint over the full kinematic range. This action space may contain infeasible targets, so the OCP uses a soft terminal cost (penalizing deviations from unreachable targets) instead of a hard constraint, requiring tuning of cost weights.
- **RL Formulation:** A Universal Value Function Approximator (UVFA) agent learns three table-tennis skills (ball placement, topspin/backspin, smashes) from sparse rewards.
- **Metrics:** Fraction Balls Returned, Generated Topspin, Generated Velocity, Generated Backspin, and Goal Distance. Experiments include a baseline (SAC + BRONets), testing the effect of stronger RL algorithms and architectures (SimBaV2), and evaluating performance at lower RL decision frequencies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of FAOC focused on real-time motion planning for robot table tennis, comparing its performance against various baselines in simulated environments.

#### 4.1. Baseline Experiment (SAC + BRONets)

- **Sample Efficiency and Performance:** FAOC demonstrated superior sample efficiency and achieved the highest final normalized reward among all compared controllers (FAOC, 2Dsoft, 1Dpos, 1Dvel, 1Dacc).
- **Dimensionality Impact:** The two-dimensional controllers (FAOC and 2Dsoft) consistently outperformed the one-dimensional variants (1Dpos, 1Dvel, 1Dacc). The 1D controllers also exhibited considerably greater variability across different random seeds, with one (1Dacc) failing to achieve a 50% return rate in three of five seeds.
- **Specific Metric Advantages:** FAOC significantly outperformed other methods in return rate, generated topspin, and generated velocity, contributing to its overall higher reward. No statistically significant differences were observed in generated backspin or goal distance across methods, potentially due to the inherent difficulty of these sub-tasks and the conservative reward design.
- **Explanation of Performance Differences:** The inferior performance of 1D controllers was attributed to reduced controllability, as the RL agent could only directly specify one waypoint variable, leaving other degrees of freedom to the optimizer. This limited the range of achievable behaviors. While 2Dsoft offered similar controllability to FAOC, its static, state-independent action space contained unreachable waypoints. Exploring these infeasible actions reduced its sample efficiency and made it harder for the RL agent to learn optimal policies compared to FAOC, which guarantees feasible waypoints.

#### 4.2. Effect of Algorithm and Architectures (SimBaV2)

- **Robustness to Stronger RL Methods:** The comparison was repeated using SimBaV2, which incorporates categorical distributional critics and larger actor/critic networks, representing a stronger RL baseline. While all controllers showed substantial performance improvements over the baseline SAC setup, FAOC maintained its advantage.
- **Consistent Superiority:** FAOC remained among the best-performing methods and retained statistical superiority in eight pairwise comparisons across the evaluation metrics. These results indicate that FAOC's action space formulation provides a performance advantage irrespective of the underlying RL algorithm's strength or network architecture.

#### 4.3. Effect of Low RL Frequency (SimBaV2 & Low RL Frequency)

- **Preservation of Performance:** In this experiment, the RL decision frequency was reduced from 31.25 Hz (32 ms horizon) to 7.8125 Hz (128 ms horizon), simulating scenarios with lower control frequency due to latency or other constraints. FAOC largely preserved the performance achieved at the higher decision frequency.
- **Degradation of Baselines:** In contrast, the other controllers experienced more substantial performance degradation under the lower RL frequency.
- **Enhanced Controllability:** The statistical analysis showed that FAOC was significantly better than competing methods in 13 pairwise comparisons at the lower frequency, an increase from 8 at the higher frequency. This finding supports the hypothesis that FAOC's design, which combines 2D waypoints with reachability awareness through its state-dependent action space, provides superior trajectory segment controllability, making it more robust to reduced replanning rates.

In summary, FAOC consistently demonstrated higher sample efficiency and better final closed-loop performance across various experimental conditions, particularly excelling in return rate, topspin generation, and return velocity. Its fundamental advantage lies in strictly guaranteeing the feasibility of RL actions while maintaining effective exploration, a benefit that becomes more pronounced in complex, multi-dimensional control tasks and under lower decision frequencies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FAOC framework offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of control engineering and reinforcement learning:

1. **Bridging the RL-OC Gap with Feasibility Guarantees:** FAOC directly addresses a fundamental challenge in integrating Reinforcement Learning and Optimal Control: providing strict guarantees of constraint satisfaction while allowing RL to operate effectively. By mapping abstract RL actions to guaranteed-feasible OCP parameters, FAOC enables the benefits of RL (learning complex tasks, adapting to sparse rewards, operating in long horizons) to be leveraged in safety-critical applications typically dominated by OC.
2. **Enhanced Safety and Reliability:** The framework's core contribution is its ability to enforce recursive feasibility and safety constraints through an optimization-based mapping algorithm. This is critical for real-world deployment of autonomous systems, where adherence to physical limits and safety protocols is paramount. By ensuring the underlying OCP is always feasible, FAOC mitigates risks of system failure due to out-of-bounds or unsafe actions.
3. **Improved RL Learning Efficiency and Performance:** FAOC eliminates the need for RL agents to implicitly learn complex, state-dependent feasibility boundaries. This simplification allows the RL policy to dedicate its learning capacity solely to maximizing task-specific rewards, leading to improved sample efficiency and higher final performance compared to baselines that either use heuristic bounds or penalize infeasible actions.
4. **Generalized and Computationally Tractable Mapping:** The research provides a topological characterization of OCP feasibility and develops a family of invertible mapping algorithms. This includes a 2D area-matching transformation for precise geometric preservation and a scalable linear transformation for arbitrary dimensions. Crucially, the methodology enables the computation of necessary mapping components (interior points, transformation matrices, boundary scaling factors) for implicitly defined, state-dependent feasible sets without requiring computationally prohibitive explicit geometric representations online. This ensures FAOC's applicability and efficiency for real-time control.
5. **Validation in a Complex Robotic Task:** The successful application of FAOC to real-time motion planning for robot table tennis demonstrates its efficacy in a challenging, high-dimensional, and dynamic environment. The reported achievement of professional human-level performance on a physical robot underscores the practical utility and robustness of the framework.
6. **Open-Source Contribution:** The open-sourcing of the mapping algorithm and OCP implementation contributes to the broader research community by providing tools that can facilitate further research and application of hybrid RL-OC methods.
7. **Potential for Broader Impact:** By offering a robust and efficient solution for integrating RL and OC, FAOC has the potential to accelerate the adoption of RL in a wider range of safety-critical domains, including autonomous driving, industrial robotics, aerospace control, and other areas where both task complexity and stringent safety requirements coexist. The framework's ability to maintain performance even at lower control frequencies suggests its applicability to systems with inherent latency or slower decision-making cycles.

In conclusion, FAOC presents a principled approach to integrating learning-based and model-based control strategies, offering a pathway for developing autonomous systems that are both highly capable and rigorously safe.
