# Safe Online Learning via Smooth Safety-Structured Policy Composition

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework named AutoSafe is introduced that integrates safety mechanisms directly into a deep reinforcement learning agent's action generation, enabling smooth online learning under hard safety constraints. This approach achieves higher task returns with minimal safety violations in complex simulated and real-world control tasks, outperforming conventional hard-intervention methods while maintaining computational efficiency.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning agents struggle to operate safely online while adhering to hard safety constraints, which are crucial for real-world safety-critical applications.
- Traditional hard safety filters introduce discontinuities in the action space, disrupting gradient flow and destabilizing online learning by blocking informative signals to the policy and biasing value estimates.
- Soft safety constraint methods, while maintaining smooth gradients, inherently permit temporary safety violations, making them unsuitable for safety-critical systems.

## Method

- **AutoSafe** introduces a ==structural policy composition==, blending a stochastic action from the learning policy with a deterministic safe action from a prior using a dynamic, state-dependent mixing weight λ(s).
- A risk-calibrated, ==learnable intervention mechanism== determines λ(s) via an ==exponential ramp function==, ensuring minimal intervention while guaranteeing the executed policy places negligible probability mass in unsafe regions.
- The framework integrates with the ==Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)== algorithm and utilizes a model-based ==Simplex architecture== as a robust safety prior for real-time applications.

## Results

- AutoSafe achieved higher task returns with significantly fewer safety violations across complex simulated control tasks like **3D Quadrotor Goal Reaching** and **Quadruped Navigation**, outperforming hard-intervention methods.
- The framework maintained low computational overhead for action inference and policy optimization, comparable to vanilla SAC, by avoiding per-step online optimization.
- Successfully deployed on a physical cart-pole system running at **50 Hz** on a Raspberry Pi, demonstrating safe interaction, continuous performance improvement, and adaptability to dynamically changing constraints in real-time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The convex combination of learning policy and safe prior actions maintains differentiability, ensuring continuous gradient flow for stable policy optimization even during safety interventions.
> - The composite policy inherently exhibits a mean shift towards the safe action and a quadratic reduction in exploratory variance as risk increases, acting as an intrinsic, first-principles safety mechanism.
> - A learnable sharpness parameter allows adaptive control over intervention strictness, enabling the policy to reduce reliance on potentially suboptimal safe priors as it learns safer behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central challenge in the field of deep reinforcement learning (DRL): enabling agents to interact safely with their environment while simultaneously maintaining stable and continuous policy improvement, particularly in real-world, safety-critical applications. This domain requires safety requirements to be enforced as hard constraints, which complicates the process of online learning.

Existing safe reinforcement learning approaches generally fall into two categories, each with inherent trade-offs:
- **Soft Safety Constraints:** Methods like constrained policy optimization and safety-aware reward shaping typically formulate safety as soft constraints. This approach allows for temporary violations during learning, preserving smooth gradient propagation during policy optimization. However, it implicitly assumes that learning can occur through experiencing safety violations, which is unacceptable in environments where even transient unsafe behavior poses significant risks.
- **Hard Safety Enforcement (Safety Filters):** These methods enforce safety through explicit intervention mechanisms that modify or project unsafe actions. While offering stronger formal safety guarantees, they often introduce abrupt action corrections. Such discontinuities can disrupt the flow of gradients during policy optimization, destabilizing online and continual learning, especially when interventions are frequent.
- **Differentiable Safety Filters:** Recent advancements have explored differentiable safety filters to mitigate the gradient flow issue. However, many of these approaches still rely on computationally expensive online optimization or are primarily evaluated in simplified or offline settings. This limits their scalability and applicability in higher-dimensional systems and real-time online learning scenarios, where efficient and timely safety intervention is critical.

This research aims to bridge the gap identified in these existing approaches by proposing a method that combines strong real-time safety assurance with smooth learning dynamics, thereby resolving the fundamental trade-off between strict safety enforcement and stable policy optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a deep reinforcement learning framework that can operate safely online, adhering to hard safety constraints, while preserving smooth optimization dynamics and enabling stable policy improvement. The motivation for this objective stems from critical limitations observed in current safe reinforcement learning paradigms:

- **Disruption of Learning Dynamics by Hard Interventions:**
 - Traditional safety filters, which employ binary intervention logic (e.g., action replacement or projection), cause the executed action to become independent of the learning policy (π_θ) whenever an intervention is triggered. This prevents the agent from receiving informative learning signals that could guide the policy to avoid future interventions.
 - Mathematically, non-differentiable intervention rules (η) block the backpropagation of gradients to the actor (∇_θ J(θ) = E[∇_θ η(s, π_θ(s)) ∇_˜a C_ψ_˜π_θ(s, ˜a)]). This hinders effective policy updates and stabilizes learning.
 - The "many-to-one" mapping problem arises when distinct intended unsafe actions are modified into the same executed filtered action. This ambiguity in observed state-action transitions can bias the critic's value estimates and weaken its ability to capture action-dependent value differences, consequently slowing down policy convergence.
 - Abrupt switching between the learning policy and a fallback controller introduces non-smooth transition dynamics near safety boundaries. These discontinuities can inflate gradient variance and further bias value estimation in actor-critic methods, negatively impacting learning stability.

- **Inadequate Safety Assurance from Soft Constraints:**
 - Methods relying on soft constraints, while maintaining smooth gradient flow, inherently permit temporary safety violations during training. This is deemed unacceptable in safety-critical applications where any violation could lead to severe consequences.

To address these issues, the authors propose AutoSafe with the following specific objectives:
- To enable smooth transitions between regimes of inactive and active safety intervention, ensuring that informative gradients are preserved and learning signals remain stable for policy optimization.
- To facilitate the learning of naturally safe behavior, where the policy progressively biases its actions towards safety and reduces exploratory variance as the safety margin tightens, thereby proactively mitigating accidental safety violations.
- To integrate safety monitoring and intervention directly into the action generation process as structural inductive biases, rather than treating them as external post-processing steps. This design aims to inherently reshape the policy's parameterization and its learning dynamics.
- To embed a safe policy prior that is defined over the entire state space. This prior serves as a reliable fallback behavior, guiding precautionary interventions before the system reaches a critical safety boundary, which is expected to result in smoother state trajectories and more stable online learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AutoSafe introduces a safety-aware policy architecture designed to integrate safety mechanisms directly into the action generation process, fostering smooth online learning under hard safety constraints. The methodology is structured around two core innovations: a structural policy composition and a risk-calibrated learnable intervention mechanism.

#### 3.1. Structural Policy Composition

The central concept involves synthesizing the executed action (˜a) by interpolating between a stochastic action proposed by a learning-based policy (a_θ) and a deterministic safe action provided by a safety prior (a_safe). This composition is represented by the equation:
˜a = (1 − λ(s)) a_θ + λ(s) a_safe, where λ(s) ∈.
Here, λ(s) functions as a dynamic mixing weight that balances between maximizing task performance and mitigating risk.
- **Convexity and Differentiability:** This formulation leverages the convexity of continuous action spaces, ensuring that any convex combination of valid actions remains valid. Crucially, the composition is differentiable with respect to λ(s), preserving gradient flow to the learning policy even during intervention.
- **Expressiveness:** The architecture allows for flexible behavior: in safe regions (λ → 0), the learning policy retains full autonomy. As risk increases (λ → 1), the policy smoothly transitions towards the safe prior, ensuring safety.

#### 3.2. Distributional Shaping and Variance Control

For continuous control tasks often utilizing Gaussian policies (π_θ(· | s) = N(μ_θ(s), Σ_θ(s))), AutoSafe demonstrates that the composite policy (˜π) also follows a Gaussian distribution. The moments of this composite policy are modified as:
˜μ(s) = (1 − λ)μ_θ(s) + λa_safe
˜Σ(s) = (1 − λ)²Σ_θ(s)
This structure reveals two key effects:
- **Mean Bias:** The expected action (˜μ) shifts towards the safe prior (a_safe) as λ increases, acting as a corrective mechanism.
- **Variance Damping:** The exploratory noise (variance) of the policy is quadratically reduced by a factor of (1 − λ)². This inherent reduction in uncertainty as risk increases is a first-principles mechanism to prevent accidental boundary violations and stabilize learning.

#### 3.3. Risk-Calibrated Learnable Intervention

The selection of the intervention weight λ(s) is guided by a principle of minimal intervention, aiming to preserve as much control authority for the learning policy as possible, while ensuring the executed policy places negligible probability mass in unsafe regions.
- **Safety Margin Definition:** A relative safety margin is defined as ˆΔ(s, a) := Δ(s, a) − Δ_min, where ˆΔ(s, a) ≥ 0 indicates satisfaction of the safety condition.
- **Chance Constraint:** The executed policy is required to satisfy a state-wise chance constraint: Pr(ˆΔ(s, ˜a_λ) < 0 | s) ≤ δ(s), where δ(s) is a state-dependent risk tolerance.
- **Closed-Form Intervention Template:** Under the assumption of a locally affine approximation of the safety margin along the interpolation segment, a closed-form expression for the smallest feasible λ*(s) is derived (Eq. 9). This expression indicates that λ should increase as the safety margin decreases, uncertainty increases, or the desired risk tolerance becomes stricter.
- **Learnable Sharpness Parameter:** Instead of relying on online estimation of complex terms from the closed-form solution, AutoSafe implements a structural, learnable exponential ramp function for λ(s):
 λ(˜Δ(s), p(s)) = (exp(p(s)(1 − ˜Δ(s))) − 1) / (exp(p(s)) − 1)
 Here, ˜Δ(s) is a normalized safety margin (1 for safe interior, 0 for safety boundary), and p(s) is a learnable sharpness parameter. This parameter controls how quickly intervention rises as the system approaches the boundary. p(s) is parameterized by a small prediction head (p(s; ϕ) = p_min + (p_max − p_min) sigmoid(h_ϕ(s))) and optimized jointly with the actor.
- **Precautionary and Endpoint Safety:** This learnable rate shapes precautionary behavior before the safety boundary is reached. When the monitor detects an imminent safety violation (˜Δ(s) = 0), AutoSafe sets λ = 1, ensuring full execution of the certified safe action (a_safe) and thus preserving endpoint safety.

#### 3.4. Backbone Algorithm and Safety Prior Design

- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** The Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm is adopted for policy learning. AutoSafe integrates with SAC by modifying only how the actor generates executable actions (˜a), leaving the critic and the overall learning objective unchanged.
- **Safety Prior:** The Simplex architecture (Sha, 2001) is used as the baseline for the safety prior. This involves:
 - A robust, certified-safe fallback policy (π_safe: a_safe := Fs), obtained by solving an offline model-based optimization problem (Semidefinite Program - SDP) that accounts for closed-loop stability under safety constraints.
 - A safety monitor using a state-dependent Lyapunov-like function (Δ(s) := 1 − sᵀPs), where P is a positive definite Lyapunov matrix derived from the SDP.
 - This approach provides a deterministic safe action and avoids expensive runtime optimization, making it suitable for real-time applications.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of AutoSafe, conducted across various continuous-control benchmarks and a physical system, yielded several key findings regarding its performance, safety assurance, and learning dynamics.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Safety Assurance

- **Superiority in Benchmarks:** AutoSafe consistently demonstrated strong performance across four simulated continuous-control tasks: Cartpole Balancing, Glucose Regulation, 3D Quadrotor Goal Reaching, and Quadruped Navigation. It achieved higher returns while maintaining strong safety assurance, evidenced by minimal or zero safety violations in most tasks (Table 1).
- **Stability Across Dimensions:** In low-dimensional tasks like Cartpole and Glucose, AutoSafe's performance was comparable to hard-intervention safety filters (SimplexRL, CBF). However, in higher-dimensional and dynamically complex tasks (Quadrotor, Quadruped), AutoSafe significantly outperformed these baselines. Hard-intervention methods experienced substantial performance degradation due to frequent interventions introducing abrupt distribution shifts, leading to unstable learning and occasional divergence.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Other Safe RL Approaches

- **Safety Filters:** Conventional safety filters often struggled in complex environments, exhibiting unstable learning. AutoSafe's differentiable policy composition and smooth intervention mechanism circumvented these issues.
- **Policy Prior-Aided Methods (Residual, AdaLam):** While Residual learning performed well in simpler tasks, its effectiveness was sensitive to the quality and alignment of the safe prior. AdaLam, although adaptively blending actions, did not explicitly enforce safety, leading to increased safety violations as its blending parameter decreased, which impeded further learning progress.
- **Constrained RL (Lyapunov, Lagrangian):** These methods typically learn safety from constraint violations, resulting in more safety violations during initial training phases and requiring considerably more interaction to converge compared to AutoSafe.

#### 4.3. Computational Efficiency

- AutoSafe maintained a low computational overhead for both action inference and policy optimization, comparable to vanilla SAC. This efficiency is attributed to its design which avoids per-step online optimization, a requirement for methods like CBF (Table 5).

#### 4.4. Influence of Hard Intervention on Learning Dynamics

- **Goal Conflict Experiment (Cartpole):** Experiments on Cartpole with varying goal conflicts demonstrated AutoSafe's robustness. In contrast, hard-intervention mechanisms became increasingly sensitive with intensified goal conflicts. Frequent interventions resulted in unstable critic estimation (manifested by rapidly increasing critic loss) and stagnated policy optimization, significantly degrading task performance (Fig. 4a).
- **Learning to Be Safe:** AutoSafe's smooth policy composition allowed the agent to gradually reduce its reliance on the safe policy (decreasing λ) as it learned safer, more performant behaviors. For hard-intervention methods, the intervention rate often plateaued under strong goal conflicts, suggesting that persistent interventions induced extrapolation errors in the critic, preventing informative learning signals (Fig. 4b).

#### 4.5. Adaptation of Learnable Sharpness Parameter `p`

- The learnable sharpness parameter `p` in AutoSafe's intervention function enabled the agent to adaptively adjust its conservatism. As the suboptimality of the safe prior increased (e.g., due to higher goal mismatch), the value of `p` increased, reducing the blending weight `λ` and allowing the agent to rely less on the suboptimal safe policy. This adaptive learning of `p` across different tasks highlights its benefit over fixed heuristic schedules (Fig. 5, Fig. 12, Fig. 13).

#### 4.6. Precautionary Safety Intervention

- AutoSafe's architecture facilitates precautionary safety intervention. As the system approached a safety boundary, the intervention weight `λ` smoothly increased, guiding the agent towards safer actions before a hard violation occurred. This contrasts with last-moment triggering of traditional safety filters. Throughout much of the operation, `λ` remained small, preserving performance-driven behavior (Fig. 6).

#### 4.7. Real-World Applicability

- **Physical Cart-Pole System:** AutoSafe was successfully validated on a real-world cart-pole system, with the policy deployed on an embedded device (Raspberry Pi) operating at 50 Hz. The agent demonstrated safe interaction while continuously improving task performance, and the `λ` parameter decreased over time, indicating reduced reliance on the safe prior (Fig. 7).
- **Dynamic Constraints:** AutoSafe also showed adaptability to dynamically changing obstacle constraints in both Cartpole and Quadrotor environments. This was achieved by efficiently adapting the offline-certified safe recoverable region in a closed-form manner, without requiring online optimization (Section D.5, Fig. 15, Fig. 16).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of safe reinforcement learning by proposing a novel policy architecture that addresses a long-standing challenge: balancing strict safety enforcement with stable and continuous learning dynamics.

The **significance** of AutoSafe lies in its ability to:
- **Resolve the trade-off:** It effectively resolves the fundamental trade-off between guaranteeing hard safety constraints and preserving smooth policy optimization, which is a critical requirement for deploying DRL in real-world systems.
- **Enhance learning stability:** By embedding safety monitoring and intervention as structural inductive biases and utilizing a differentiable policy composition, AutoSafe ensures uninterrupted gradient flow. This leads to more stable and efficient online learning dynamics compared to conventional hard-intervention safety filters that introduce discontinuities.
- **Improve performance in complex environments:** The method demonstrates superior performance in high-dimensional and dynamically complex tasks where existing hard-intervention techniques often fail due to learning instability.
- **Enable precautionary safety:** The architecture allows for precautionary safety interventions by smoothly modulating behavior based on risk proximity, rather than relying on abrupt, last-moment action modifications. This contributes to smoother state trajectories and a more predictable system behavior.
- **Facilitate real-world deployment:** Its computational efficiency, achieved by avoiding expensive online optimization, makes it suitable for real-time, high-frequency control applications on embedded systems. The demonstrated adaptability to dynamic constraints further enhances its practical value.

The **potential impact** of AutoSafe is substantial, particularly for safety-critical applications:
- **Safer Autonomous Systems:** It provides a more robust and reliable framework for training autonomous systems (e.g., robotics, aerospace, medical devices) to operate safely, reducing the risk of accidents or failures during online adaptation.
- **Accelerated Development and Deployment:** By offering a method for stable and efficient safe online learning, AutoSafe could accelerate the development and deployment of advanced DRL agents in various industries, overcoming a significant hurdle to their adoption.
- **Advancement in Trustworthy AI:** The work contributes to the broader goal of trustworthy AI by creating systems that are not only high-performing but also inherently safe and predictable, fostering greater confidence in AI-driven autonomy.
- **New Research Directions:** The demonstrated effectiveness and identified limitations (e.g., reliance on model quality, complexity of dynamic constraints) open up avenues for future research, including adaptive safety mechanisms under uncertainty and in highly dynamic environments.

**Limitations:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations:
1. **Reliance on Model-Based Safety Design:** AutoSafe, like many provably safe learning architectures, relies on a model-based safety design using prior knowledge of the system dynamics. The quality of this derived safe policy or safety monitor directly influences the overall system performance. Inaccurate or overly conservative models can limit exploration and achievable task performance, even with the adaptive mixing parameter `λ`.
2. **Constraint Complexity:** The current framework is primarily evaluated with safety constraints defined in relatively local and structured state spaces. More complex, dynamic environments featuring time-varying constraints, disturbances, or moving obstacles may necessitate more advanced online adaptation of the safe policy or safe set, which is an area for future research.
