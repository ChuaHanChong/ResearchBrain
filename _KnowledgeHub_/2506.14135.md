# GAF: Gaussian Action Field as a 4D Representation for Dynamic World Modeling in Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University and Beijing Normal University developed Gaussian Action Field (GAF), a 4D representation leveraging motion-augmented 3D Gaussian Splatting, for dynamic scene modeling and action generation in robotic manipulation. GAF achieved an average success rate of 60.4% across 9 simulated RLBench tasks, outperforming the Diffusion Policy baseline by 15.7% and Act3D by 7.3%, and demonstrated successful deployment on a real-world robot.

## Problem

- Existing vision-based robotic manipulation methods (V-A and V-3D-A) struggle with dynamic scenes, treating environments as static entities despite inherent object and robot movement.
- V-A paradigms rely on implicit scene understanding, which limits precise 3D spatial reasoning essential for high-precision manipulation tasks.
- V-3D-A methods, while using 3D representations, often neglect temporal evolution and face challenges in explicitly encoding dynamic information or acquiring sufficient 3D datasets, leading to actions lacking physical feasibility.

## Method

- Introduced **GAF** (Gaussian Action Field), a unified ==spatiotemporal representation== that extends ==3D Gaussian Splatting== by associating each Gaussian primitive with ==learnable motion dynamics (∆μ)== for high-fidelity 4D scene modeling.
- Developed a ==geometry-agnostic and pose-free== GAF architecture that reconstructs motion-augmented Gaussians directly from sparse, unposed multi-view RGB inputs using a ==Vision Transformer== and decoupled prediction heads.
- Implemented an ==action-vision-aligned denoising== framework, utilizing a ==diffusion model== conditioned on "Actionable Multiview RGB Guidance," to refine initial vision-driven actions from GAF, ensuring physical feasibility and accurate execution.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **60.4%** across **9** RLBench tasks, outperforming the Diffusion Policy baseline by **15.7%**, Act3D by **7.3%**, and ManiGaussian by **10.3%**.
- Demonstrated superior scene reconstruction and future state prediction, with GAF showing average improvements of **+11.5385 dB** PSNR for current states and **+10.5311 dB** PSNR for future states compared to ManiGaussian.
- Successfully deployed on a Franka Emika Panda robot for **5** real-world tasks, completing tasks like "Push Button" with **10/10** success, and exhibiting robust spatial generalization and multi-tasking capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling scene dynamics with learnable motion vectors (∆μ) directly within a 3D Gaussian representation enables high-fidelity 4D world modeling and more accurate understanding of evolving manipulation environments.
> - Deriving initial actions by matching gripper-related Gaussians between current and predicted future states provides a strong, physically grounded, and vision-driven prior for robotic manipulation.
> - Refining these initial, purely vision-driven actions through an action-vision-aligned diffusion model is critical to ensure physical feasibility and precision, bridging the gap between perceived dynamics and executable robotic movements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of vision-based robotic manipulation relies on accurate scene perception to enable robots to interact with their environments. Current approaches generally follow one of two paradigms: Vision-to-Action (V-A) or Vision-to-3D-to-Action (V-3D-A).

The V-A paradigm, exemplified by methods such as Diffusion Policy and GENIMA, directly maps RGB observations to action sequences. While these methods benefit from end-to-end learning and can leverage extensive image datasets and powerful foundation models, their reliance on implicit scene understanding can limit their ability to model 3D spatial relationships precisely.

The V-3D-A paradigm, which includes methods like Act3D, PerAct, and GNFactor, incorporates intermediate 3D representations such as point clouds or voxel grids. These representations enable explicit geometric reasoning, capturing accurate spatial relationships and often leading to higher success rates in manipulation tasks. However, V-3D-A methods typically require 3D datasets, which are less readily available than 2D image datasets, and they often struggle with the dynamic nature of manipulation scenes, as they primarily model static geometry.

A common limitation shared by both V-A and V-3D-A paradigms is their failure to explicitly capture the temporal evolution of scene geometry. Robotic manipulation inherently involves dynamic interactions where both the robot and objects in the scene change over time. Existing methods often infer actions from a static understanding of the scene, which can lead to inaccuracies and an inherent mismatch with dynamic action generation.

In response to this challenge, the V-4D-A (vision-to-4D-to-action) paradigm has emerged. This paradigm augments 3D representations with motion information to model the spatiotemporal evolution of scenes. Early efforts in V-4D-A, such as those discussed in and, have explored 4D representations but often model dynamics implicitly, relying on the sequential nature of video frames without explicitly embedding dynamic information into the scene representation itself. More recent Gaussian-based world models like ManiGaussian and GWM deform current Gaussians to achieve future scene consistency, using future states as volumetric priors to supervise action prediction. However, these methods have been noted for their implicit use of Gaussian representations and lower fidelity, which can limit their effectiveness.

This work, by introducing the Gaussian Action Field (GAF), positions itself within the V-4D-A paradigm. It aims to address the limitations of prior V-4D-A methods by constructing a more explicit and high-fidelity 4D representation. GAF directly models the temporal transformation between current and future states using motion-augmented 3D Gaussian Splatting, enabling direct and reliable action inference grounded in an accurate dynamic scene understanding. The paper states that its approach aims to connect dynamic visual perception with action generation within a unified framework, moving towards a world model concept that predicts future scene dynamics to support decision-making.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and implement a V-4D-A (Vision-to-4D-to-Action) framework capable of accurate scene perception and action reasoning for robotic manipulation tasks in dynamic environments. This framework is instantiated through the Gaussian Action Field (GAF), which aims to leverage motion-aware 4D representations to directly infer actions.

The motivation behind this work stems from several limitations observed in existing vision-based robotic manipulation approaches:

1. **Inadequate Handling of Dynamic Scenes:** Conventional V-A (Vision-to-Action) and V-3D-A (Vision-to-3D-to-Action) paradigms typically treat scenes as static entities. However, robotic manipulation inherently involves dynamic interactions, where objects and the robot itself move and deform. This static understanding creates a mismatch with the dynamic nature of action generation, leading to action inaccuracies and reduced effectiveness in complex, real-world scenarios.
2. **Implicit Scene Understanding in V-A Methods:** V-A methods, while benefiting from end-to-end learning, often rely on implicit scene understanding from 2D visual inputs. This can hinder their ability to capture precise 3D spatial relationships, which are critical for high-precision manipulation tasks.
3. **Limitations of 3D Representations in V-3D-A Methods:** While V-3D-A methods leverage 3D representations (e.g., point clouds, voxel grids) to provide explicit geometric reasoning, they frequently neglect the temporal evolution of these geometries. Furthermore, acquiring high-quality 3D datasets for training can be challenging compared to the abundance of 2D image data.
4. **Need for Explicit Motion Encoding in 4D Representations:** Although the V-4D-A paradigm has emerged to address temporal dynamics, prior efforts often model 4D representations by primarily leveraging the sequential nature of video frames, without explicitly embedding dynamic information directly into the scene representation. Some Gaussian-based world models have used future states as volumetric priors, but their implicit use and potentially low fidelity limit their effectiveness in guiding precise actions.
5. **Bridging Perception and Action with High Fidelity:** There is a motivation to create a more explicit 4D representation that can directly model the temporal transformation between high-fidelity current and future states. Such a representation could enable more reliable action inference by inherently containing movement trend information, allowing for a direct connection between dynamic visual perception and action generation.
6. **Ensuring Physical Feasibility of Actions:** Initial actions derived from purely visual models might be visually plausible but lack physical feasibility for real-world execution. Therefore, a motivation exists to refine these initial actions using an action-vision-aligned framework that incorporates real action data and visual guidance to ensure physically consistent and accurate execution.
7. **Real-Time and Resource-Efficient Operation:** The objective includes developing a system that is fully feed-forward and capable of real-time execution on typical hardware (e.g., a single GPU), making it practical for online robotic manipulation.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond static or implicitly dynamic scene representations to develop a high-fidelity, explicit 4D representation (GAF) that directly informs and refines robotic actions, thereby enhancing perception accuracy and manipulation success rates in dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the Gaussian Action Field (GAF) as a V-4D-A framework, unifying dynamic scene perception and action generation. The overall pipeline comprises two main stages: 4D GAF scene reconstruction and action refinement via a diffusion model.

#### 3.1. GAF Representation

GAF is defined as a unified spatiotemporal representation extending 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). Each Gaussian primitive *g(x)* at time *t* is associated with geometric attributes and motion dynamics. Formally, GAF is parameterized by a continuous function F_Θ that maps *{g(x), t}* to *{μ, ∆μ, f}*:
- **μ ∈ ℝ³**: 3D position of the Gaussian.
- **∆μ ∈ ℝ³**: Displacement vector indicating temporal motion over a future interval ∆t.
- **f = {c, σ, r, s}**: Appearance parameters, representing color (c), opacity (σ), rotation (r), and scale (s).

GAF generates three types of outputs from these parameters:
1. **Current Gaussian**: Constructed from *{μ, f}*, representing the scene at the present time.
2. **Future Gaussian**: Obtained by applying the predicted motion to the position parameters, resulting in *{μ + ∆μ, f}*, representing the scene at time *t + ∆t*.
3. **Init Action**: Estimated through point cloud matching (specifically ICP) between the gripper-related Gaussians of the current and future states. This yields a rigid transformation *T_{t→t+∆t} ∈ SE(3)*, which is then interpolated into a sequence of transformations to form the initial action *a_init*.

#### 3.2. GAF Architecture (Dynamic Gaussian Reconstruction)

The GAF architecture is designed to reconstruct motion-augmented Gaussians directly from sparse, unposed RGB inputs. This approach is geometry-agnostic and pose-free, contrasting with methods requiring dense camera poses or strong geometric priors.

- **Input**: Two unposed H × W RGB images and their corresponding intrinsics *{I^t_v, k^t_v}* for *V* views at timestep *t*.
- **Feature Extraction**: Images are tokenized into patch sequences and concatenated. These tokens are then fed into a shared-weight Vision Transformer (ViT) with cross-view attention to extract hybrid scene features. The ViT is initialized with weights from MASt3R.
- **Decoupled Heads**: The extracted features are processed by three specialized heads, all based on a DPT architecture:
 - **Gaussian Center Head (h_center)**: Predicts Gaussian point positions *μ^t_j* for each point *j*.
 - **Gaussian Param Head (h_feature)**: Estimates the appearance parameters *f^t_j = {c^t_j, σ^t_j, r^t_j, s^t_j}*.
 - **Motion Prediction Head (h_motion)**: Predicts the per-point displacement *∆μ^(t→t+∆t)_j*, representing the motion of each Gaussian over the future interval *∆t*.
- **Future State Generation**: The predicted displacements *∆μ^(t→t+∆t)_j* are added to *μ^t_j* to obtain the future Gaussian positions *μ^(t+∆t)_j*. These displaced centers are then combined with the appearance parameters *f^t_j* to form the future Gaussian.
- **Rendering and Supervision**: From the current and future Gaussians, novel view images *{Î^t_v}* and *{Î^(t+∆t)_v}* are rendered using alpha-blend rendering. The GAF is trained by minimizing a unified objective function *L_GAF*, which combines LPIPS and MSE losses for both current and future rendered images against their respective ground truths:
 - *L_GAF = L^t_LPIPS + L^t_MSE + L^(t+∆t)_LPIPS + L^(t+∆t)_MSE*
 This facilitates joint optimization of motion-augmented Gaussian reconstruction using direct RGB video frame supervision.

#### 3.3. Manipulation with GAF (Action Refinement)

Since the init actions from GAF are solely vision-driven and trained without real action supervision, they may lack physical feasibility. To address this, an action-vision-aligned denoising framework is introduced for action refinement.

- **Actionable Multiview RGB Guidance**: For each denoising step, the gripper's CAD model is rendered onto the current multi-view RGB images. The gripper's pose is computed by applying a candidate action *a* (a relative transformation) to its current world pose. Using camera extrinsics (obtained via calibration) and intrinsics, an image of the rendered gripper is created, *R_c*. This process generates a unified visual representation that integrates 3D observations with temporally predicted actions.
- **Diffusion Model for Denoising**: A diffusion model takes this "Actionable Multiview RGB Guidance" as a condition, along with the *init action a_init*, to refine the action. The diffusion model minimizes a loss function *L_refine* that includes L1 losses on the denoising direction (*D*) and added noise (*ϵ*), and a BCE loss on the gripper's opening/closing action (*g*) against their ground truth labels.
- **Control Loop**: The entire manipulation pipeline operates in a closed loop: GAF reconstructs the 4D scene and predicts an *init action*; the denoising framework refines this action; the refined action is executed via inverse kinematics; new observations are collected; and the process repeats until the task is complete. This iterative framework allows for continuous adaptation to scene changes.
- **Efficiency**: During inference, only 3 diffusion iterations are used, enabling fast action prediction (less than 0.3 seconds for 8 future actions). Prediction and execution are run in parallel threads for real-time deployment.
- **Training Details**: GAF is trained end-to-end for 80k iterations. The denoising process is trained for 50k iterations, using GT wrist camera data as an auxiliary resource for local observations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides experimental validation in both simulated and real-world environments, alongside ablation studies and generalization tests.

#### 4.1. Comparison on Simulated Environments (RLBench)

- **Setup**: Evaluated on 9 tasks in RLBench, covering various manipulation challenges (e.g., fine-grained placement, occlusion, articulated objects). 20 demonstrations per task were used for training, and 100 unseen poses for testing.
- **Baselines**: Compared against Diffusion Policy (DP) (V-A paradigm), Act3D (V-3D-A paradigm), and ManiGaussian (Gaussian-based world model). Hyper-parameters were adjusted for fair comparison.
- **Results**: GAF achieved an average success rate of 60.4% across the 9 tasks, outperforming all baselines.
 - **Vs. Diffusion Policy (V-A)**: GAF showed a 15.7% average improvement (e.g., "Toilet Seat Down": 71% vs 39%). This difference was attributed to GAF's 3D perception capabilities, especially in occlusion-heavy tasks.
 - **Vs. Act3D (V-3D-A)**: GAF achieved a 7.3% average improvement (e.g., "Lift Lid Up": 100% vs 91%). This indicated the benefit of incorporating future-aware 4D dynamic scene representations.
 - **Vs. ManiGaussian**: GAF yielded a 10.3% average increase in success rate (e.g., "Close Microwave": 85% vs 67%). This was attributed to GAF's explicit action generation and denoising strategy.

#### 4.2. Comparison on Scene Reconstruction and Prediction

- **Scope**: GAF was compared against ManiGaussian for current frame reconstruction and future frame prediction, both of which generate Gaussian point clouds.
- **Qualitative Analysis**: GAF demonstrated superior reconstruction fidelity and novel-view synthesis. Its renders preserved fine geometric structures (e.g., gripper joints, object surfaces) even under partial observations, in contrast to ManiGaussian's renders, which exhibited blurred textures and incomplete geometric details.
- **Quantitative Metrics**: Using PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS, GAF consistently outperformed ManiGaussian.
 - **Current Scene Reconstruction**: GAF achieved an average improvement of +11.5385 dB PSNR, +0.3864 SSIM, and -0.5574 LPIPS.
 - **Future State Prediction**: GAF achieved an average improvement of +10.5311 dB PSNR, +0.3856 SSIM, and -0.5757 LPIPS.
 These metrics support the claim of high geometric accuracy and temporal coherence in GAF's dynamic rendering framework.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

- **Ablation on GAF**: A version "W/o GAF" directly predicted actions from input images using a diffusion model without GAF's multi-view rendering or init action priors. This resulted in a 9.1% drop in average success rate compared to the full GAF method, indicating GAF's contribution to action prediction accuracy.
- **Ablation on Denoising Framework**: Directly executing the *init actions* without the action refinement module led to task failures, such as misaligned contacts, even when GAF's initial reconstruction was visually plausible. This confirmed the critical role of the denoising framework in ensuring physically feasible and successful task completion.

#### 4.4. Spatial Generalization

- **Setup**: Tested on 2 RLBench tasks with objects placed across the entire workspace, including boundaries and corners.
- **Results**: GAF demonstrated superior spatial generalization compared to the Diffusion Policy baseline. While DP encountered challenges at workspace boundaries and corners, GAF maintained higher success rates in these varied object placements.

#### 4.5. Multi-task Evaluation

- **Setup**: A single network was trained jointly on data from 4 RLBench tasks (20 demonstrations each).
- **Results**: GAF's average success rate declined by 11.25% compared to single-task training. This was considered robust performance, especially when compared to the 21% decline observed in the Diffusion Policy baseline. The largest decline for GAF was noted in the "Lift Lid Up" task, which was distinct from the other three. This suggests GAF possesses robust multi-tasking capabilities.

#### 4.6. Real-World Deployment

- **Setup**: Evaluated on a Franka Emika Panda robot with a Panda Hand on 5 real-world tasks (e.g., Push Button, Place Apple). Visual input was provided by three RealSense D435i cameras (two external, one wrist-mounted). GAF used only intrinsics for 4D reconstruction, with extrinsics for action refinement. 20 demonstrations were collected for each task.
- **Results**: GAF demonstrated the ability to complete tasks in the real world, navigating various noise sources such as imperfect camera calibration. Success rates ranged from 6/10 for "Place Apple" to 10/10 for "Push Button." Failures were primarily observed in grasping-related tasks, possibly due to the absence of force feedback.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Gaussian Action Field (GAF) introduces a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing the challenge of dynamic scene understanding and action generation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Advancement of 4D Dynamic Scene Modeling**: GAF establishes a concrete implementation of the V-4D-A paradigm, providing a novel and explicit 4D spatiotemporal representation. By extending 3D Gaussian Splatting with learnable motion attributes, it enables high-fidelity modeling of dynamic scenes, which surpasses the capabilities of prior static 3D representations and implicit 4D approaches. This allows for a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of evolving manipulation environments.

2. **Enhanced Robotic Manipulation Performance**: The work demonstrates state-of-the-art performance in robotic manipulation tasks across both simulated and real-world environments. GAF's ability to unify scene reconstruction, future prediction, and action generation within a single framework leads to higher success rates compared to existing V-A and V-3D-A paradigms, as well as previous Gaussian-based world models. This improvement is critical for robust robot operation in complex, unstructured settings.

3. **Improved Action Precision and Reliability**: The integration of an action-vision-aligned denoising framework is crucial. While GAF's direct visual-based action inference provides an *init action*, the refinement process ensures these actions are not only visually plausible but also physically feasible and accurate for real-world interaction. This reduces the gap between perceived dynamics and executable movements, which is a common challenge in robotic control.

4. **Practical Feasibility and Efficiency**: GAF operates in a fully feed-forward manner, requiring only sparse-view RGB images and their intrinsics for 4D reconstruction. Its real-time execution capability, achieved through optimized inference (e.g., reduced diffusion iterations and parallel processing), makes it suitable for online deployment in practical robotic systems. This efficiency mitigates computational bottlenecks often associated with detailed 3D/4D modeling.

5. **Robustness and Generalization**: The experiments indicate GAF's robustness to spatial variations, demonstrating superior generalization to unseen object positions at workspace boundaries and corners. Furthermore, its performance in multi-task learning, with a comparatively smaller decline in success rate than baselines, suggests its potential for broader applicability and more versatile robot intelligence. Successful deployment on a real-world robot further validates its practical utility in handling real-world noise and complexities.

6. **Foundation for Future Research**: GAF provides a strong foundation for future research in several directions. The paper identifies the potential for incorporating higher-level semantic or task-level understanding, possibly through language modeling, to enable more context-aware manipulation. This integration could lead to robots that not only perceive and predict dynamics but also understand the intent and purpose of their actions within a broader task context.

In essence, this research significantly advances the state-of-the-art in dynamic world modeling for robotics, offering a practical and effective solution for developing more capable and adaptable robotic manipulation systems.
