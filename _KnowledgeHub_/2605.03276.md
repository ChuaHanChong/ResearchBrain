# VEBench:Benchmarking Large Multimodal Models for Real-World Video Editing

> [!summary] Summary
> A new benchmark, VEBENCH, evaluates Large Multimodal Models on real-world video editing, revealing a substantial gap between current models and human-level performance in tasks requiring both editing knowledge understanding and multi-video operational reasoning. It highlights models' limitations in audiovisual integration and precise temporal localization.

## Problem

- Real-world video editing demands both expert knowledge of cinematic techniques and operational ability to manipulate multiple footage sources into coherent narratives.
- Existing video understanding benchmarks often overlook crucial aspects like audiovisual synchronization, long-range temporal context, and the active, multi-video operational dimension of editing.
- Large Multimodal Models lacked a comprehensive evaluation framework for these complex, human-like video editing capabilities.

## Method

- Introduces **VEBENCH**, a benchmark designed with two subtasks: ==Video Editing Technique Recognition== (TechRec) for knowledge understanding and ==Video Editing Operation Simulation== (OpSim) for operational reasoning.
- The benchmark utilizes 3.9K high-quality, long-form edited videos from YouTube, totaling over 257 hours, and 3,080 human-verified multimodal QA pairs.
- A rigorous ==human-AI collaborative pipeline== was employed for annotation to ensure accurate and fine-grained labeling across diverse editing scenarios.

## Results

- Evaluations demonstrated a substantial performance gap between current state-of-the-art LMMs (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-4o) and human capabilities in real-world video editing tasks.
- Models struggled significantly with fine-grained temporal localization, achieving near-zero tIoU_cond scores, and showed weak audiovisual integration, failing on techniques reliant on sound continuity like J-Cuts and L-Cuts.
- Open-source LMMs generally performed lower than proprietary models and exhibited a consistent bias towards predicting timestamps at the very beginning of video segments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human video editing involves a two-stage cognitive process—perceiving techniques and actively constructing narratives—which VEBENCH's dual subtasks are designed to mimic.
> - Subtitles consistently improve LMM performance across tasks, indicating they provide a crucial semantic bridge that aids models in inferring narrative flow and temporal alignment.
> - Models prefer continuous visual cues over abrupt frame separators for maintaining temporal coherence, suggesting a reliance on explicit markers for contextual reasoning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of computer vision and multimodal intelligence has seen significant advancements in video understanding, traditionally focusing on tasks such as recognizing actions, events, and objects within single video clips. Recent research has extended these capabilities to temporal, causal, and cross-modal reasoning. However, the domain of video editing presents a more complex challenge, demanding both specialized knowledge of cinematic techniques and the capacity to manipulate and combine disparate video footage into cohesive narratives.

Prior benchmarks have explored aspects related to video editing and filmmaking. For instance, VEU-Bench investigates various editing techniques, ShotBench focuses on shot-level analysis and cinematic attributes, Edit3K evaluates visual effects, and EditVid-QA assesses understanding of meme-style edits. While these contributions have advanced video understanding in editing-related contexts, the authors identify two primary limitations in the existing landscape:
1. **Limited Context and Modality:** Current benchmarks primarily evaluate knowledge-based comprehension on very short clips (e.g., VEU-Bench averages 6.22 seconds) and predominantly rely on visual cues. They often neglect crucial elements for real-world editing, such as audiovisual synchronization and long-range temporal context, which are indispensable for understanding editing intent and continuity.
2. **Absence of Operational Reasoning:** Existing benchmarks largely overlook the practical, operational dimension of video editing. This dimension involves the active process of selecting, aligning, and combining footage from multiple sources to construct coherent narratives, requiring multi-video reasoning and intent-aware decision-making. These capabilities have remained largely unexplored in current Large Multimodal Models (LMMs).

VEBENCH positions itself as the first comprehensive benchmark designed to unify both editing knowledge understanding and operational reasoning within a single framework. By addressing the aforementioned limitations, VEBENCH aims to bridge the gap between passive video comprehension and active, human-like video editing intelligence, thereby establishing a more realistic and demanding testbed for evaluating advanced LMMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation behind VEBENCH stems from the recognition that real-world video editing is a sophisticated cognitive process that combines expert knowledge of cinematic principles with goal-directed operational reasoning. Current LMMs, despite their progress in general video understanding, lack a comprehensive evaluation framework that assesses these dual aspects of video editing intelligence.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **To Formulate a Human-Inspired Video Editing Task:** The primary objective is to define a new task that accurately reflects how human editors work. This involves two complementary paradigms:
 - **Evaluating Editing Knowledge Understanding:** This sub-objective focuses on assessing a model’s ability to perceive and interpret specific editing techniques. Professional editors first recognize and understand various cuts (e.g., L-Cut, Jump Cut, Match Cut) and their effects on narrative and visual flow. Therefore, the benchmark aims to test if LMMs can identify these techniques, not just based on visual changes but also incorporating auditory cues and broader narrative context in long-form videos. This moves beyond simplistic recognition to a more nuanced comprehension of cinematic language.
 - **Evaluating Operational Reasoning:** This sub-objective addresses the practical decision-making involved in editing. Human editors actively select, trim, and arrange footage from multiple sources to achieve specific narrative goals. The benchmark seeks to simulate this workflow by requiring models to select the most suitable footage from a pool of candidates and precisely localize the relevant segment to fit a given context, thereby mirroring real-world intent-aware decision-making and multi-video reasoning.

2. **To Introduce VEBENCH, a Professionally Annotated Benchmark:** A significant objective is the creation of a high-quality, diverse, and reasoning-intensive dataset. The authors highlight the challenges in constructing such a benchmark due to its multi-video, multimodal, and complex reasoning nature. The motivation is to overcome these challenges by employing a rigorous, three-round human-AI collaborative annotation pipeline involving professional annotators. This ensures fine-grained temporal accuracy, multimodal synchronization, and semantic consistency across a wide spectrum of cinematic and vlog-style content. The aim is to provide a robust dataset that features longer videos and richer context than prior efforts, which is crucial for evaluating complex temporal and narrative understanding.

3. **To Conduct Comprehensive Evaluations of Leading LMMs:** The research aims to evaluate both proprietary (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-4o) and open-source LMMs against the proposed benchmark. The motivation here is to establish a baseline for current state-of-the-art model performance in video editing and, crucially, to diagnose the limitations and gaps that exist between current model capabilities and human-level video editing cognition. By revealing these gaps, the authors intend for VEBENCH to serve as a catalyst for future research and development in multimodal reasoning, particularly in bridging video understanding with creative operational intelligence.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to advance the field of intelligent video editing by providing a comprehensive, realistic, and challenging benchmark that encourages the development of next-generation LMMs capable of not just understanding video content, but actively participating in and reasoning through the complex process of video creation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VEBENCH is designed with a dual-task structure that collectively approximates human editing cognition, integrating both knowledge understanding and operational reasoning. The benchmark comprises two subtasks: Video Editing Technique Recognition (TechRec) and Video Editing Operation Simulation (OpSim).

#### 3.1. Task Definition

##### 3.1.1. Video Editing Technique Recognition (TechRec)

This subtask focuses on evaluating a model's ability to identify seven fundamental video editing techniques: L-Cut, J-Cut, Jump Cut, Smash Cut, Cutaway, Invisible Cut, and Match Cut. These techniques span both temporal and semantic transitions, requiring multimodal reasoning (visual, auditory, narrative). Unlike previous benchmarks, TechRec emphasizes fine-grained multimodal perception in long-form videos (averaging 213 seconds), where success depends on recognizing subtle cues like cross-scene audio continuity, rhythmic pacing, and visual composition. Models must determine the specific technique used at each transition point within a finely edited video.

##### 3.1.2. Video Editing Operation Simulation (OpSim)

This subtask models real-world editing workflows. Given a reference video clip, the model must select the most appropriate footage from a list of candidate videos and then temporally localize the precise segment to be inserted. The task is formulated as a multiple-choice QA, simplifying the workflow while retaining the core reasoning challenge. The model outputs the selected video index and its start-end timestamps. This task introduces multi-video reasoning, requiring models to infer editing intentions and construct narrative continuity across independent sources. Questions are framed to either append footage at the beginning or the end of the reference video.

#### 3.2. Video Collection

All videos for VEBENCH were sourced from a public video platform.
- **For TechRec:** Videos were chosen to contain numerous editing points. GPT-4o was used to generate thousands of topics and corresponding YouTube search queries (e.g., "LeBron James highlight moments") to enable large-scale retrieval across diverse contexts and genres.
- **For OpSim:** Interview videos featuring film actors were deliberately selected. These videos often alternate between main interviews (A-Roll) and complementary B-roll footage (e.g., movie clips), naturally exhibiting cross-references and narrative alignment. Samples were curated from high-quality YouTube channels like "60 Minutes" and "GQ Iconic Characters."
- **Filtering:** All collected videos underwent automatic screening using Gemini-2.5-Pro to filter out samples with poor editing quality or unstable composition, ensuring high visual integrity and narrative coherence.

#### 3.3. Annotation Process

A rigorous three-round human-AI collaborative annotation and verification process was designed for each subtask to ensure accuracy and consistency.

##### 3.3.1. TechRec Annotation Pipeline

1. **Initial Manual Pass:** Annotators carefully inspect each video, locate all transition points, and label them with one or more of the seven defined editing techniques.
2. **Cross-Validation:** An independent annotator performs a second review for verification.
3. **Final Consistency Review:** Automated checks are applied, particularly for audiovisual alignment for techniques like L-Cut and J-Cut.
4. **QA Generation:** High-quality metadata is used to automatically generate 5-option multi-choice QA pairs using predefined templates (e.g., temporal localization, count-based, or identifying technique over an interval).

##### 3.3.2. OpSim Annotation Pipeline

This multi-stage process emulates real-world editing workflows and also involves three iterative annotation-review rounds.
1. **Stage 1 (LMM-assisted Metadata Generation):** Gemini-2.5-Pro processes videos to extract shot-level metadata and scene boundaries, including editing quality assessment, interviewee identification, and a timestamp-based scene breakdown with descriptions, source movies, and scene types (A-Roll, B-Roll, Intro Graphic).
2. **Stage 2 (A/B Roll Pairing):** Human annotators, given a target B-Roll, identify A-Rolls with strong narrative/semantic connections. They specify the rationale for each A/B-Roll pair using eight predefined relationship types (e.g., Cause-and-Effect, Illustration, Contrast, Emotional Reinforcement) and indicate modality grounding (audio, visual, or both).
3. **Stage 3 (Option Videos Selection):** Annotators locate the correct source video and its timestamps, then design three semantically plausible distractor clips (e.g., a similar scene, another film by the same actor).
4. **Stage 4 (QA Generation):** Questions are automatically generated based on the annotated data, using templates to ask for the best fit to add "at the end" or "at the beginning" of a reference video.
5. **Stage 5 (QA Validation):** Annotators review all candidate clips, re-select the best-fitting option, and provide timestamp-level justifications for ambiguous cases.

#### 3.4. Statistics

VEBENCH comprises 3.9K edited videos, totaling over 257 hours, with 3,080 human-verified QA instances.
- **TechRec:** 885 long-form videos with corresponding QA pairs. Videos average 213.6 seconds, with 3.3 annotated editing cuts per video.
- **OpSim:** 783 reference videos and 2,217 unique candidate videos. Reference videos average 51.6 seconds, while candidate videos are substantially longer (approx. 228.5 seconds), providing rich temporal context for cross-video reasoning.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Settings and Metrics

Experiments were conducted on proprietary (Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-4o) and open-source (Qwen-VL, InternVL families) LMMs.
- **TechRec Metric:** Accuracy for multi-choice VideoQA.
- **OpSim Metrics:**
 - **OpSim-FS (Footage Selection Accuracy):** Accuracy of selecting the correct video option.
 - **Full-task performance:** Conditioned temporal Intersection-over-Union (tIoU_cond), calculated as tIoU between predicted and ground-truth segments if the correct video choice is made, otherwise 0. This penalizes incorrect video source selection.
- **Implementation Details:** For OpSim, open-source LMMs lacking native multi-video input support were evaluated by concatenating candidate videos into a single sequence with on-screen text prompts to delineate segments. The impact of subtitles and different visual prompts (on-screen text vs. frame separators) was also analyzed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of state-of-the-art LMMs on VEBENCH revealed significant performance discrepancies, indicating a substantial gap between current model capabilities and human-level video editing reasoning.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Gaps

Current LMMs, including proprietary models like Gemini-2.5-Pro and GPT-4o, and open-source models from the Qwen-VL and InternVL families, exhibited limited performance across both subtasks. While Gemini-2.5-Pro generally outperformed other models, its performance remained considerably below the level required for practical editing applications. The overall scores (average of TechRec and OpSim-FS) ranged from 27.99% (InternVL3-8B) to 39.55% (Gemini-2.5-Pro), with human performance not explicitly stated but implied to be near perfect for these tasks.

#### 4.2. Video Editing Technique Recognition (TechRec) Results

- **Difficulty with Fine-Grained Recognition:** Models struggled with accurately identifying editing techniques. Gemini-2.5-Pro achieved the highest accuracy at 34.65%, while open-source models ranged from 25.21% (InternVL3-8B) to 28.99% (Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct). This indicates difficulty with fine-grained boundary detection and interpreting complex transitions within long videos.
- **Importance of Audio Cues:** The presence of audio cues was found to be critical for this task, particularly for techniques like J-Cuts and L-Cuts, which rely on cross-scene sound continuity. Models without robust audio understanding performed poorly, suggesting a lack of multimodal synchronization awareness.
- **Accuracy Breakdown by Cut Type:** Analysis across different cut types (Table 6) showed consistently low accuracy, even for frequently occurring techniques like L-Cut (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Pro at 31.24%). This suggests that the challenge lies in the intrinsic difficulty of recognizing subtle editing semantics rather than merely data imbalance.

#### 4.3. Video Editing Operation Simulation (OpSim) Results

- **Footage Selection (OpSim-FS):** In this component, which measures the ability to select the correct video option from candidates, models performed moderately better. Gemini-2.5-Pro achieved 44.44%, while GPT-4o, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct also showed comparable performances around 40%. This indicates that LMMs possess some capacity for reasoning across multiple candidate clips to identify semantically relevant content.
- **Temporal Localization (Full-task tIoU_cond):** This proved to be a significant challenge. Nearly all models achieved tIoU_cond scores close to zero (0.00 or 0.01 for open-source models). Only Gemini-2.5-Pro produced a non-trivial result of 0.11. This outcome highlights a profound inability of current LMMs to perform precise temporal grounding of selected footage within the specified editing context. Visualizations further confirmed that open-source models frequently predicted timestamps near the beginning of videos, regardless of the actual correct segment.

#### 4.4. Impact of Subtitles

Subtitles consistently led to moderate performance improvements across both TechRec and OpSim-FS. For example, Gemini-2.5-Pro's OpSim-FS score improved from 41.25% (without subtitles) to 44.44% (with subtitles), and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct improved from 38.52% to 39.08%. This indicates that subtitles provide supplementary semantic grounding, explicitly linking dialogue or narration to visual context and reinforcing temporal alignment, thereby compensating for LMMs' limited ability to infer narrative flow solely from raw audiovisual cues.

#### 4.5. Impact of Visual Prompts

The choice of visual prompting strategy in OpSim also influenced performance. Using on-screen text (OT) to delineate video segments resulted in better performance compared to using a blank frame separator (SP). For instance, Gemini-2.5-Pro's OpSim-FS accuracy dropped from 44.44% with OT to 40.23% with SP, and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct saw a drop from 39.08% to 30.01%. This suggests that continuous visual continuity cues, like on-screen text, are important for maintaining temporal coherence and contextual reasoning, while abrupt separators can disrupt a model's understanding of narrative flow.

#### 4.6. Limitations of Existing LMMs on Video Editing

The evaluations revealed three major factors limiting current LMMs:
- **Weak Audiovisual Integration:** Most models tend to process visual and audio modalities independently, failing to capture synchronized cues that are essential for many editing techniques and for reasoning about temporal or emotional continuity.
- **Limited Temporal Capacity:** Many LMMs are constrained to short input sequences (e.g., 64 frames), preventing them from grasping fine-grained motion, transitions, and stylistic changes across the hundreds of frames typically found in real-world edited videos.
- **Inaccurate Temporal Localization:** LMMs demonstrated a systematic inability to predict precise start and end timestamps for relevant segments, as evidenced by near-zero tIoU scores and a tendency for open-source models to predict timestamps at the beginning of videos.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

VEBENCH contributes to the field of multimodal AI and computer vision by introducing a benchmark designed to assess advanced video understanding and reasoning capabilities in the context of real-world video editing.

The significance of this work is multifaceted:
- **Comprehensive Evaluation Framework:** VEBENCH addresses limitations of previous benchmarks by integrating both the theoretical understanding of editing techniques and the practical, operational reasoning required to execute edits across multiple, long-form videos. This dual nature provides a more holistic and challenging evaluation of LMMs' cognitive abilities beyond simple content recognition.
- **Diagnostic Tool for LMMs:** The benchmark effectively identifies and quantifies specific weaknesses in current state-of-the-art LMMs, including proprietary and open-source models. The significant performance gap observed between models and human-level competence, particularly in fine-grained temporal localization and multi-video reasoning, highlights critical areas for future research and development.
- **Emphasis on Multimodal and Long-Context Reasoning:** By incorporating audio cues and requiring reasoning over long-duration videos and multiple candidate sources, VEBENCH underscores the necessity for LMMs to move beyond unimodal or short-context processing. It challenges models to develop more robust audiovisual integration and extended temporal reasoning capacities.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** VEBENCH is positioned as a foundational resource for guiding the development of next-generation multimodal systems. It provides a practical testbed for advancing research into areas such as causal reasoning in video, intent understanding in narrative construction, and the development of models that can perform precise temporal grounding.
- **Driving Intelligent Video Editing Systems:** Ultimately, the benchmark aims to accelerate progress towards intelligent video editing systems that can assist or even automate complex editing workflows. By pushing models to emulate human-like editing intelligence and creative reasoning, VEBENCH could lead to the development of more sophisticated tools for filmmakers, content creators, and media professionals.
