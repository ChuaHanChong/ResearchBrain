# VLA Knows Its Limits: Adaptive Execution Horizons for Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> AutoHorizon is a test-time method for flow-based VLA policies that dynamically sets the execution horizon per action chunk by reading action self-attention weights as a proxy for the model's predictive confidence, via a bidirectional soft-pointer that locates attention plateaus. Across LIBERO, RoboTwin, and real-world Franka trials it beats fixed-horizon and random baselines with negligible overhead.

## Problem

- Action chunking policies fix the execution horizon by human heuristic or brute-force sweep, ignoring that optimal horizon should vary with task phase and environment dynamics.
- Prior chunking work analyzes the consistency-reactivity trade-off only qualitatively, without a mechanism to detect where a predicted chunk's forecast becomes unreliable.
- Existing chunk-transition or rejection-sampling methods treat the whole chunk uniformly, giving no per-timestep signal for when to stop trusting later actions in the chunk.

## Method

- **AutoHorizon**: attention-guided per-chunk execution-horizon estimator that treats ==action self-attention weights== as an implicit proxy for the VLA's predictive limit at test time, needing no retraining.
- ==Bidirectional soft-pointer mechanism== that scans forward and backward from chunk boundaries to find the first turning point where accumulated attention mass stops advancing and plateaus, using low-entropy row filtering and an incremental-change threshold.
- ==Radial-action-sink== and intra-chunk attention-invariance analysis of cross- and self-attention in flow-based VLAs, providing the theoretical/empirical basis (including a proof of a unique error-minimizing horizon) that motivates the estimator design.

## Results

- On LIBERO with pi-0.5 (p=50), AutoHorizon reaches **96.5**/**98.0**/**94.4**/**92.1** percent across Spatial/Object/Goal/10, matching or beating the brute-force Static Oracle+ baseline that needs p rollouts per task.
- With GR00T N1.5 (p=16) AutoHorizon scores **96.7**/**98.7**/**96.0**/**92.7** percent on the four LIBERO suites, outperforming both fixed Static Oracle settings and Static Oracle+.
- On real-world Franka pick-and-place tasks AutoHorizon achieves **98.0**/**92.0**/**99.0** percent stage-solve rate versus fixed horizons that drop as low as **0** percent (cube-plate, e=5) or **50** percent (e=50).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Attention weights already encode a model's own confidence about how far ahead its predictions remain valid, so they can be repurposed as a free test-time uncertainty signal instead of training a separate confidence head.
> - A single global hyperparameter (fixed execution horizon) is a poor proxy for a quantity that should track task phase; per-step adaptivity recovers most of the gap to an expensive oracle sweep at near-zero cost.
> - Boundary tokens acting as attention anchors ('radial sinks') is a structural bias worth checking for in any chunked sequence-prediction model, not just VLAs, when diagnosing where the model overcommits to stale context.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research operates within the field of embodied artificial intelligence (AI), which focuses on enabling agents to interact with the physical world. A significant direction in this field involves Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models aim to translate visual perception and linguistic instructions into executable actions, demonstrating capabilities in various language-conditioned robot manipulation tasks.

A common practice in VLA models, particularly those trained via imitation learning, is "action chunking." Instead of predicting a single action at each time step, these policies output a sequence of actions, or an "action chunk." During policy execution, the robot typically executes only an initial portion of this predicted chunk—referred to as the "execution horizon"—before re-planning. This closed-loop prediction mechanism aims to balance long-term consistency with short-term reactivity.

However, the choice and effect of this "execution horizon" have remained largely underexplored. Prior research often sets the execution horizon heuristically or through exhaustive empirical tuning, which can be computationally intensive and may not yield optimal performance across varied task phases. Previous studies have indicated that short horizons enhance reactivity but can lead to instability, while longer horizons promote temporal smoothness at the cost of responsiveness. The present work addresses this gap by proposing a method for dynamic and adaptive determination of the execution horizon, thereby aiming to improve the performance and adaptability of VLA policies. The paper leverages insights from attention mechanisms in Transformer-based models, drawing parallels with "attention sink" phenomena observed in large language models (LLMs) and multi-modal architectures, to infer the predictive limits of VLA policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an automatic and efficient method for determining the execution horizon for each action chunk within flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This objective is motivated by several observations and underlying challenges:

1. **Underexplored Problem Space:** The research highlights that despite the widespread adoption of action chunking in VLA models, the specific effect and the optimal choice of the execution horizon—the number of actions executed from each predicted chunk—have not been thoroughly investigated.

2. **Empirical Performance Fluctuations:** Initial empirical observations demonstrated that varying the execution horizon leads to substantial performance deviations in robotic tasks. Specifically, performance typically exhibits a "peaked pattern," initially improving as the horizon increases, then declining. This phenomenon underscores that a fixed, sub-optimal execution horizon can significantly limit policy effectiveness, yet the reasons for this pattern and how to consistently achieve peak performance are not well understood.

3. **Limitations of Fixed Execution Horizons:** Current practices often rely on human heuristics or exhaustive, brute-force evaluation to set a fixed execution horizon. The authors argue that a fixed horizon is inherently suboptimal because the ideal balance between temporal consistency and reactivity varies throughout a task. For instance, tasks requiring smooth, continuous motion (e.g., reaching for an object) might benefit from longer horizons, while those demanding precise, responsive adjustments (e.g., grasping or placing an object) necessitate shorter horizons. A fixed horizon cannot dynamically adapt to these changing requirements, leading to sub-optimal performance in different phases of policy rollout.

4. **Need for Adaptive Solutions:** The insights regarding the varying optimal balance between consistency and reactivity across different task phases motivate the development of an adaptive, per-chunk method for determining the execution horizon. Such a method would allow the robot to dynamically adjust its planning granularity based on the immediate perceptual context and task demands.

To achieve this, the authors aim to analyze the internal mechanisms of VLA models, specifically their cross- and self-attention weights. By understanding how actions attend to vision, language, and other action tokens, the research seeks to uncover the intrinsic predictive limits of these models. This understanding is intended to form the basis for a data-adaptive strategy that leverages the model's internal confidence cues to infer a suitable execution horizon dynamically.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research proceeds in three main stages: a theoretical and empirical analysis of execution horizon effects, an in-depth examination of VLA attention mechanisms to explain observed phenomena, and the development of AutoHorizon, a dynamic execution horizon estimation algorithm based on these insights.

### 4. Preliminary Concepts

The paper defines a flow-based VLA model π(A_t | o_t, c), where o_t represents visual observations, c is the language command, and A_t = [a_t..., a_{t+p-1}] is an action chunk of length p, the "prediction horizon." During execution, the robot executes the first e actions from A_t, where e is the "execution horizon." Attention weights, S_ij, quantify the similarity between an action query token (from Q) and key tokens (from vision, language, and actions in K), indicating the policy's focus.

### 5. Analysis of Optimal Execution Horizon

The researchers first provide a theoretical justification for the observed peaked performance curve. They model the total accumulated error, L(e), over a policy rollout of L low-level actions. This error consists of two components: a loss incurred at each chunk transition (δ_c) and a divergence loss within each executed chunk (δ_d_j(e)). Assuming δ_d_j(e) is a monotonically increasing function with respect to e (specifically, modeled as k * e * log e), they prove that a unique minimizer, e\* = clamp(δ_c / k, 1, p), exists for L(e). This proposition indicates that an optimal execution horizon exists, representing a trade-off: longer horizons are favored when chunk transition costs are high (promoting consistency), while shorter horizons are better when intra-chunk divergence is high (enhancing reactivity). Empirically, they confirm this peaked performance pattern in various LIBERO tasks (Figure 1).

### 6. VLA Knows Its Limits: Attention Weight Analysis

To understand the underlying reasons for the performance pattern, the paper analyzes the attention weights within flow-based VLAs, particularly the state-of-the-art π_0.5 model. This analysis reveals two key phenomena:

1. **Invariance of Intra-chunk Vision-Language Attention:** It was observed that actions within the same predicted chunk consistently attend to the same vision and language tokens with nearly identical importance (Figure 3). This "invariance" suggests that later actions in a chunk do not adaptively adjust to environmental changes. Instead, they rely on static perceptual contexts, which may become outdated or misleading as the environment evolves, limiting the adaptability of later actions within a chunk. The analysis also noted an "attention sink" in the first language token, which appeared largely redundant; masking language tokens resulted in only marginal performance changes, suggesting linguistic semantics are primarily embedded in visual representations due to strong pretraining.

2. **Emergence of Radial Action Sinks:** Analysis of action self-attention maps revealed that attention is strongly concentrated on both the initial and terminal action tokens within a chunk (Figure 4). These "radial action sinks" maintain high correspondence strength initially, then rapidly decay, stabilizing into a low-attention plateau. The authors infer two reasons: (i) the initial action, having the lowest cumulative error, acts as a stable anchor for subsequent actions, promoting smooth trajectories; (ii) both initial and terminal actions contribute to preserving inter-chunk continuity, reflecting the model's implicit objective for smooth transitions. This attention structure implies that intermediate actions are organized around these boundary tokens, revealing a bias in the model's temporal reasoning.

Based on these observations, the researchers interpret action self-attention weights as implicit indicators of the model's predictive limit. High attention to radial action sinks suggests confidence in alignment with anchors, while declining attention indicates a shift towards self-referential dependence, amplifying errors and degrading reactivity.

### 7. AutoHorizon Algorithm

Motivated by the predictive limit interpretation of attention weights, AutoHorizon is proposed as a data-adaptive strategy to dynamically estimate the execution horizon for each action chunk. The algorithm works as follows:

1. **Attention Map Processing:** For a given sampling step t, the action self-attention maps are extracted, averaged across all Transformer blocks and attention heads, and then normalized row-wise to form S_t ∈ R^(p×p).
2. **Low-Entropy Row Filtering:** Rows (query actions) in S_t with low entropy (H_t[i] ≤ Q_q(H_t)) are retained (R_t). This step identifies actions with sharper, more confident attention patterns, providing reliable structural cues, and filters out actions with diffused attention.
3. **Bidirectional Soft-Pointer Mechanism:**
 - **Forward Pointer:** An expected predictive horizon (μ_t[i]) is computed for each row i, enforced to be non-decreasing. The incremental change (Δμ_t[i]) is tracked. A "plateau" (P_t) is identified where Δμ_t[i] falls below a threshold τ, signifying where the attention mass ceases to advance. The forward execution horizon (N_f) is then determined from the minimum index in R_t ∩ P_t.
 - **Backward Pointer:** The same procedure is applied to a reversed attention matrix (S_t) to derive a backward horizon (N_b).
4. **Horizon Determination:** If N_f + N_b ≥ p, the full prediction horizon (p) is adopted (N = p). Otherwise, the forward prefix length is used (N = N_f).

AutoHorizon is designed to incur negligible computational overhead because it reuses existing attention weights from the VLA model. It typically operates on the first or third sampling step and uses fixed hyperparameters (q=0.9, τ=0.3) without additional tuning.

### 8. Main Findings and Results

The research provides extensive empirical validation of AutoHorizon in both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks, comparing it against several baselines.

### 9. Simulation Results

- **LIBERO Benchmark (π_0.5 and GR00T N1.5):**
 - **Performance Trend:** The empirical results consistently demonstrated the peaked performance pattern with varying execution horizons, validating the theoretical premise that an optimal horizon exists and that performance degrades when deviating from it. This was observed across both π_0.5 (with p=10 and p=50) and GR00T N1.5 (p=16).
 - **AutoHorizon Superiority:** AutoHorizon consistently outperformed all baselines: Static Oracle (fixed horizon), Static Oracle+ (brute-force optimal fixed horizon), and Random (randomly sampled horizon). For instance, with π_0.5 (p=50), AutoHorizon achieved success rates of 96.5% (LIB-Spatial), 98.0% (LIB-Object), 94.4% (LIB-Goal), and 92.1% (LIB-10), surpassing Static Oracle+ which reached 96.4%, 97.6%, 93.9%, and 91.9% respectively.
 - **Generalizability:** The method's consistent performance across different flow-based architectures (π_0.5 and GR00T N1.5) and varying prediction horizons (p=10, p=50, p=16) indicates its robustness and generalizability.
 - **Adaptive Horizon Distribution:** Visualization of AutoHorizon's estimated execution horizons showed a broad distribution, favoring moderate values for reactivity but occasionally selecting larger horizons for smooth motions. This dynamic adaptation was shown to be superior to fixed horizons, even those tuned to be optimally static.

- **RoboTwin Benchmark (π_0.5):**
 - On bimanual coordination tasks with varying difficulties, AutoHorizon achieved comparable or superior performance compared to baselines. For "Adjust Bottle," it reached 100.0% success (vs. Static Oracle+ 98.7%), and for "Pick Bottles," it achieved 68.0% (vs. Static Oracle+ 67.0%). This further demonstrated its adaptability across diverse task dynamics.

- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Nearest Static Oracle:** AutoHorizon achieved higher or comparable success rates compared to fixed-horizon policies whose horizons were closest to AutoHorizon's mean estimated values, highlighting the benefit of dynamic adjustment.
 - **Language Token Effect:** Masking language tokens' attention weights resulted in only marginal performance changes, and sometimes improvements when partially redistributing language attention to other modalities. This suggests redundancy in explicit language attention, as semantic information might be largely embedded in visual representations from pretraining.
 - **Smoothness Analysis:** AutoHorizon produced smoother joint velocity transitions over time compared to fixed-horizon policies, indicating more stable and fluid actions.
 - **Hyper-parameter Sensitivity:** AutoHorizon's performance remained stable across different combinations of its hyperparameters (attention choice L, entropy quantile q, and threshold τ), demonstrating robustness to these settings.

### 10. Real-World Results

- **Setup:** Experiments were conducted on a Franka Research 3 robot for three single-arm pick-and-place tasks: "put cucumber on plate," "put Rubik's cube on plate," and "put Rubik's cube into bowl."
- **Performance:** AutoHorizon consistently achieved higher success rates than all baselines. For example, on "put Rubik's cube into bowl," AutoHorizon achieved 99.0% success, compared to 97.5% for Static Oracle+.
- **Qualitative Observations:**
 - **Short Fixed Horizons (e.g., e=1-5):** Led to robot hesitation or stalling due to prediction of subtle, low-amplitude movements.
 - **Moderate Fixed Horizons (e.g., e=20-40):** Caused overreaching or collisions, reflecting a loss of reactivity.
 - **Long Fixed Horizons (e.g., e>40):** Resulted in struggles with object localization, frequent drops, and diminished error correction.
 - **AutoHorizon Adaptation:** AutoHorizon dynamically adjusted its horizon: increasing during stable conditions (reaching, transporting) to accelerate progress, and shortening during physical interaction (grasping, placing) to enhance reactivity. This adaptability contributed to its higher success rates and more effective task completion.

### 11. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a fundamental and previously underexplored challenge in robot policy learning: the determination of the execution horizon in action chunking flow policies. By proposing AutoHorizon, the authors introduce the first test-time method that dynamically estimates the execution horizon for each predicted action chunk.

The significance of this work lies in several aspects:
1. **Improved Robot Performance and Adaptability:** AutoHorizon consistently demonstrates superior performance over conventional fixed-horizon strategies across a diverse range of simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks. This improvement stems from its ability to adapt the execution horizon dynamically to changing perceptual conditions and task phases, effectively balancing temporal consistency and short-term reactivity. This leads to more reliable and efficient task execution.
2. **Enhanced Understanding of VLA Models:** The detailed analysis of attention weights in flow-based VLAs, revealing "intra-chunk action invariance" and "radial action sinks," provides a deeper understanding of how these models internally represent and process temporal action sequences. Interpreting action self-attention weights as indicators of predictive confidence offers a novel perspective on VLA limitations and capabilities.
3. **Computational Efficiency:** AutoHorizon operates with negligible computational overhead, as it leverages the attention weights already computed by the VLA model. This makes it practical for real-world deployment where computational resources and latency are critical considerations.
4. **Generalizability:** The method's effectiveness across different flow-based VLA architectures (π_0.5 and GR00T N1.5), prediction horizons, and diverse task suites (LIBERO, RoboTwin, and real-world scenarios) demonstrates its broad applicability.
5. **Advancement in Embodied AI:** By enabling robots to autonomously determine how far ahead to plan their actions, this work contributes to the development of more intelligent and autonomous embodied AI systems. It moves beyond brittle, heuristically tuned control parameters towards self-adaptive policy execution, which is crucial for robots operating in complex and unstructured environments.

The potential impact of AutoHorizon is that it could facilitate the deployment of VLA-controlled robots in more dynamic and sensitive real-world applications by making their actions more robust, adaptive, and performant without requiring extensive manual tuning or computationally expensive search strategies. This could accelerate progress towards more capable and general-purpose robotic systems.
