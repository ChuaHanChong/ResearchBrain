# RL makes MLLMs see better than SFT

> [!summary] Summary
> Research from KAIST and NAVER AI Lab demonstrates that Reinforcement Learning (RL) with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) fundamentally enhances vision encoders within Multimodal Language Models (MLLMs) more effectively than Supervised Finetuning (SFT). This approach yields stronger visual representations and improves MLLM performance on vision-centric tasks, leading to the development of PIVOT, an efficient method for evolving existing vision models.

## Problem

- A prevailing assumption that MLLM performance primarily inherits from the Large Language Model (LLM) backbone, leading to an oversight of the vision encoder's independent role.
- A lack of systematic understanding of how different MLLM post-training strategies (SFT vs. RL) specifically impact the underlying visual representations of the vision encoder.
- The absence of an efficient framework to specifically optimize existing vision encoders for optimal performance within MLLMs, beyond traditional vision pre-training methods.

## Method

- Conducted a systematic comparison of Supervised Finetuning (SFT) and ==Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)== as post-training strategies on MLLMs across varying vision encoder and LLM scales.
- Analyzed the impact of these strategies on vision encoder capabilities through vision-only benchmarks (ImageNet, semantic segmentation) and gradient visualizations (==Grad-CAM==).
- Developed ==PIVOT (Preference-Instructed Vision OpTimization)==, an RL-driven auxiliary training process to efficiently enhance existing vision encoders for MLLM applications.

## Results

- DPO consistently outperformed SFT on MLLM performance, particularly on vision-centric tasks like OCR & Chart VQA (**+**4.2%**p**) and Vision-Centric VQA (**+**2.4%**p**), while showing marginal gains on weakly vision-related tasks.
- DPO-trained vision encoders demonstrated superior performance on vision-only benchmarks, including a **+**1.83%**p** improvement in ImageNet Top-1 accuracy and **+**1.08%**p** increase in patch-level recall for semantic segmentation, over SFT-trained encoders.
- PIVOT-enhanced vision encoders achieved significant performance boosts within MLLMs, with a PIVOT-enhanced SigLIP1-So/14 surpassing a more recent SigLIP2-So/16 (**5**3.2%**p** vs. **5**2.4%**p** average VQA score) at less than **1%** of standard vision pre-training computational cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MLLM post-training, particularly with DPO, actively reshapes and significantly enhances the intrinsic visual representations within the vision encoder, rather than merely aligning an LLM with a frozen vision component.
> - DPO's contrastive nature generates more precise, fine-grained gradient signals that focus on semantically relevant regions, leading to improved object localization and stronger vision-language alignment compared to SFT.
> - Larger LLM backbones provide more informative optimization signals during MLLM training, resulting in vision encoders that achieve higher performance on vision-only tasks after post-training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal Language Models (MLLMs) integrate large language models (LLMs) with visual understanding capabilities. A prevalent assumption in MLLM research is that the model's overall performance largely stems from the LLM backbone, given its scale and advanced linguistic abilities. Consequently, the vision encoder, which dictates how MLLMs process images, has received comparatively less in-depth analysis. Initial MLLM development primarily focused on architectural combinations of pre-trained LLMs and vision models, followed by supervised finetuning (SFT) to align these modalities and foster instruction-following.

Recent advancements in LLM training have seen a shift from SFT to reinforcement learning (RL), particularly methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), for aligning models with human preferences and enhancing conversational abilities. This shift is now extending to MLLMs, with several studies demonstrating that incorporating human preference data via RL improves MLLM performance and mitigates issues like hallucination. However, these works have largely focused on the overall MLLM performance gains without comprehensively analyzing the differential effects of SFT and RL, especially on the intrinsic visual representations learned by the vision encoder. Prior investigations into the vision encoder mostly involved architectural modifications or comparing frozen versus fine-tuned encoders, rather than a systematic examination of how post-training strategies (SFT vs. RL) specifically reshape its visual capabilities. This paper aims to address this analytical gap by providing a detailed comparison of SFT and RL's impact on both the MLLM's downstream performance and the fundamental nature of its vision encoder.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the differential impacts of two dominant MLLM post-training strategies, Supervised Finetuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), on both the overall MLLM performance and, critically, on the underlying visual representations learned by the vision encoder. The authors specifically focus on Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) as the representative RL algorithm due to its practical advantages in controlled comparisons.

The motivation for this study stems from several observations and existing research gaps:

1. **Under-exploration of the Vision Encoder:** Despite the rapid progress in MLLMs, there has been a limited understanding of "how MLLMs perceive images." The field has largely operated under an implicit, LLM-centric assumption that MLLM capabilities are primarily inherited from the language backbone, leading to a void in the analysis of the vision encoder's role and its transformation during MLLM training.
2. **Lack of Systematic Comparison of Training Paradigms:** While RL has demonstrated efficacy in enhancing MLLM performance and addressing issues like hallucination, a comprehensive and controlled comparison between SFT (for instruction-following) and RL (for preference alignment) within MLLMs has been absent. This includes a systematic analysis of how these strategies compare across diverse benchmarks and with model scaling.
3. **Impact of Post-Training on Visual Representations:** Existing research has progressed little beyond the finding that fine-tuning the vision encoder yields better outcomes than keeping it frozen. There is a notable absence of understanding how post-training strategies like SFT and RL specifically reshape the visual representations within the vision encoder. The authors hypothesize that preference alignment, beyond affecting the language module, may also fundamentally alter the model's visual processing capabilities.
4. **Developing Stronger Vision Backbones:** By understanding how different training strategies influence visual representations, the authors aim to identify effective and efficient methods for building more capable vision encoders for MLLMs. This includes exploring whether RL-trained models can surpass existing state-of-the-art vision models for MLLM integration.

To address these motivations, the study seeks to answer specific questions such as: How do SFT and DPO affect MLLM performance on diverse VQA tasks? Is DPO superior to SFT, and does this trend persist with model scaling? How does MLLM training influence visual representations, including aspects like localization ability? And finally, can the observed effects be formalized into a practical recipe for advancing vision backbones for MLLMs?

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic approach involving model scaling, a two-stage training procedure, diverse evaluation benchmarks for MLLMs, and in-depth analyses of vision encoder properties through vision-only tasks.

**Model Architecture and Scaling:**
The core MLLM architecture integrates an LLM with a vision encoder via a two-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) as a multimodal projector. The authors investigated model scaling by varying:
- **LLM Backbone:** Qwen2.5 (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B parameters), with the vision encoder fixed to SigLIP2-So/16.
- **Vision Encoder:** SigLIP2 (B/16 (86M), L/16 (303M), So/16 (400M), g/16 (1B) parameters), with the LLM fixed to Qwen2.5-3B.

**Training Procedure:**
The MLLM development process comprised two stages:
1. **Stage 1 Pre-training:**
 - **Projector-only training:** The visual and language embedding spaces were initially aligned by training only the multimodal projector on the LAION/CC/SBU-558K dataset.
 - **Base MLLM training:** All model parameters (vision encoder, projector, and LLM) were then trained end-to-end on diverse vision-language (VL) datasets, including Visual Question Answering (VQA), vision-grounded dialogue, and image captioning, using the LLaVA-OneVision-3.2M dataset.
2. **Stage 2 Post-training:**
 - This stage involved a full-parameter update of the base MLLM from Stage 1, employing either SFT or DPO.
 - **Dataset:** A post-training dataset `X_PT` was constructed by randomly sampling 20K instances from the MPO (Mixed Preference Optimization) dataset. Each instance `x_i` contained an image `I_i`, a query `q_i`, and corresponding chosen (`y_c_i`) and rejected (`y_r_i`) responses.
 - **SFT Objective:** The model `π_θ` was optimized to maximize the log-probability of generating the chosen response `y_c_i` for a given `I_i` and `q_i`.
 - **DPO Objective:** The model `π_θ` was optimized to increase the likelihood of the chosen response and decrease the likelihood of the rejected response, relative to a reference model `π_ref`, using the DPO loss function.
 - **Controlled Comparison:** Both SFT and DPO were trained with the same number of 'image-query-response' pairs (20K samples) to ensure a controlled comparison.
 - **Hyperparameters:** Specific learning rates (e.g., 1e-6 for DPO, 5e-6 for SFT) and a batch size of 256 were used, with a more detailed description in the supplementary materials.

**Evaluation of MLLMs:**
Performance was assessed using the Cambrian benchmark suite, which includes 16 tasks across four categories of VQA: General, Knowledge, OCR & Chart, and Vision-Centric. This suite provides a broad comparison across various vision-language capabilities.

**Analysis of Visual Representations (Vision Encoder Focused):**
To understand the impact on the vision encoder itself, several vision-only evaluations were conducted:
- **ImageNet Classification:** After Stage 2 training, the vision components (encoder and projector, or just encoder) were detached from the LLM. Their standalone performance was evaluated via linear-probe classification on ImageNet, using extracted visual features.
- **Gradient Visualization (Grad-CAM):** To visualize how optimization signals propagate, Grad-CAM was used to map gradients of the MLLM loss (SFT or DPO) back to the feature activations of the vision encoder for specific image regions and queries.
- **Image Segmentation:** Segmentation probing was performed on the ADE20K dataset. The MLLM-tuned vision encoder was frozen, and a two-layer MLP was trained as a patch-level classifier for segmentation, with performance measured by patch-level macro recall. This aimed to assess object localization capabilities.
- **Vision & Language Alignment:** The representational similarity between the vision encoder and various reference LLMs was evaluated using a kernel alignment metric to gauge how well visual features align with linguistic understanding.

**Preference-Instructed Vision OpTimization (PIVOT):**
The findings from the SFT vs. DPO analysis were formalized into PIVOT, a recipe for evolving vision models.
- **Process:** An existing vision encoder (e.g., CLIP, SigLIP) was integrated with an LLM, and the entire MLLM was optimized through pre-training and DPO post-training.
- **Evaluation:** The resulting PIVOT-enhanced vision encoder was then detached and frozen. This enhanced encoder was coupled with a *new* Qwen2.5-1.5B LLM. The projector-only was then pre-trained, followed by instruction finetuning of the projector and LLM on a 737K dataset. This setup aimed to isolate and assess the intrinsic quality and usefulness of the PIVOT-enhanced visual representations within an MLLM context.
- **Generalization:** Experiments were also extended to other RL algorithms like GRPO and PPO, and a DPO variant (MPO), to confirm the generalizability of the findings beyond DPO.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the impact of SFT and RL on MLLMs and their vision encoders:

**1. Importance of Vision Encoder for Fine-grained Visual Understanding (Finding 1):**
- MLLM performance consistently improved with increased vision encoder size (from SigLIP2-B/16 (86M) to SigLIP2-g/16 (1B)), confirming the importance of visual representation capacity.
- These gains were particularly pronounced in "strongly vision-related tasks" (e.g., OCR & Chart VQA, Vision-Centric VQA), with DPO-tuned MLLMs showing increases of +10.6%p and +4.5%p respectively when scaling the vision encoder from B/16 to g/16. In contrast, "weakly vision-related tasks" (e.g., Knowledge VQA) saw comparatively minor improvements (+1.9%p).

**2. Superiority of DPO (RL) over SFT for MLLM Performance (Finding 2):**
- DPO consistently outperformed SFT on MLLM benchmarks, especially in tasks requiring detailed visual comprehension.
- For instance, with a SigLIP2-L/16 vision encoder, DPO achieved +4.2%p higher performance on OCR & Chart VQA and +2.4%p on Vision-Centric VQA, while showing only marginal gains (+0.3%p) on Knowledge VQA.
- This trend held across different scales of the language model (Qwen2.5 from 0.5B to 7B), suggesting that DPO's preference alignment impacts visual processing capabilities beyond just the language module.
- **Data Scaling:** DPO demonstrated higher data efficiency, achieving strong performance even with smaller datasets. For example, DPO with 3K samples surpassed SFT trained on 40K samples.

**3. MLLM Post-training Reshapes Visual Representations (Finding 3):**
- The post-training strategy (SFT or DPO) fundamentally reshaped the visual representations within the vision encoder.
- When evaluated on vision-only ImageNet classification via linear probing (detaching the vision encoder from the LLM), DPO consistently produced stronger visual representations than SFT. For a SigLIP2-So/16 encoder, DPO yielded a +1.83%p increase in ImageNet Top-1 accuracy compared to SFT.
- Larger LLM backbones (e.g., 7B vs. 0.5B) led to higher-performing vision encoders when trained with DPO, suggesting that larger LLMs provide more informative optimization signals to the vision encoder during backpropagation.
- Only DPO-trained models benefited from increased data scaling in terms of the intrinsic quality of visual representation, unlike SFT.

**4. DPO Enhances Localization Capabilities (Finding 4):**
- **Gradient Visualization:** Grad-CAM analysis revealed that DPO-induced gradient signals propagated to the vision encoder were more precisely focused on question-relevant image regions compared to the more scattered signals from SFT. This suggested a more fine-grained understanding.
- **Image Segmentation:** On the ADE20K dataset, DPO-tuned vision encoders consistently outperformed SFT-tuned ones in segmentation probing, with, for example, a +1.08%p increase in patch-level recall for a CLIP-L/14 336px encoder. This provided quantitative evidence that DPO improves object localization abilities.

**5. Vision Encoder Benefits from Larger LLM in Alignment (Finding 5):**
- Representation alignment metrics showed that vision encoders trained with DPO exhibited stronger alignment scores with various reference LLMs compared to SFT-trained counterparts.
- Consistently higher alignment scores were observed when the vision encoder was paired with a larger LLM, further supporting the idea that larger LLMs contribute more valuable backward signals to the vision encoder.

**6. Generalizability of RL Advantage:**
- Experiments with other RL algorithms like GRPO (reward-model-free) and PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), as well as a DPO variant (MPO), corroborated the findings. GRPO, for instance, achieved a +3.1%p higher average MLLM score and also yielded stronger visual representations on ImageNet (+1.93%p) and segmentation (+1.83%p) compared to SFT.

**7. PIVOT Unlocks Vision Model Potential (Finding 6):**
- Reframing RL training as "Preference-Instructed Vision OpTimization" (PIVOT) demonstrated its efficacy in enhancing existing vision models for MLLM applications.
- A PIVOT-enhanced SigLIP1-So/14 vision encoder achieved an average VQA score of 53.2%p, surpassing even a more recent and larger original SigLIP2-So/16 encoder (52.4%p).
- A PIVOT-enhanced SigLIP2-So/16 (400M parameters) outperformed the larger SigLIP2-g/16 (1B parameters), achieving 55.6%p vs. 53.9%p.
- PIVOT (using DPO) consistently provided advantages over SFT-enhanced vision encoders (e.g., +1.3%p for SigLIP2-g/16).
- PIVOT successfully improved performance across diverse pre-trained vision encoders, including CLIP, DINOv2, MAE, MOCO, and ImageNet-supervised models.
- The enhancement was achieved with comparatively low computational cost: approximately 18 hours on 8 H100 GPUs, estimated to be less than 1% of the cost of standard large-scale vision pre-training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of multimodal AI:

1. **Re-evaluating the Role of Vision Encoders in MLLMs:** The work challenges the prevalent LLM-centric view by demonstrating that MLLM post-training, particularly through Reinforcement Learning (RL), fundamentally reshapes the vision encoder's underlying visual representations. This shifts the understanding from MLLM capabilities being primarily inherited from the LLM to recognizing the critical and transformable role of the vision encoder in determining "how MLLMs perceive images."

2. **Establishing RL's Superiority in Visual Representation Learning:** The consistent finding that RL (DPO, GRPO, PPO) produces stronger and more localized visual representations compared to Supervised Finetuning (SFT) is a key insight. This suggests that preference alignment, through its contrastive objective, provides more fine-grained and semantically relevant gradient signals to the vision encoder, leading to improved visual comprehension and object localization capabilities. This extends the known benefits of RL beyond language alignment to include visual processing.

3. **Introducing PIVOT for Efficient Vision Model Advancement:** The development of "Preference-Instructed Vision OpTimization" (PIVOT) provides a practical and computationally efficient recipe for enhancing existing vision models for MLLM integration. PIVOT demonstrates that even state-of-the-art vision encoders, despite extensive pre-training, possess substantial untapped potential for improvement within MLLM contexts. The ability to achieve significant performance gains with less than 1% of the computational cost of standard vision pre-training presents an economically viable pathway for improving MLLM vision backbones.

4. **Enabling Stronger MLLMs for Vision-Centric Tasks:** By improving the visual representations, this work directly contributes to building MLLMs with superior performance on tasks requiring detailed visual understanding, such as OCR & Chart VQA, and general Vision-Centric VQA. This can lead to more reliable and accurate MLLM applications in domains where precise visual reasoning is crucial.

5. **Opening New Avenues for Research:** The findings motivate further exploration into the design of diverse RL algorithms tailored for vision encoder development and visual representation learning. Additionally, it encourages investigation into novel dataset formats specifically engineered to leverage the strengths of DPO for visual learning, extending beyond the current focus on language alignment. The study also opens questions about the generalization of these findings to other MLLM architectures and LLM backbones, and the interplay between preference data characteristics and task domains.

In summary, this research reframes the understanding of MLLM training by highlighting the significant, and previously under-appreciated, impact of post-training strategies on the vision encoder. It offers a practical method for leveraging RL to unlock the full potential of vision models within MLLMs, paving the way for more capable and efficient multimodal AI systems.
