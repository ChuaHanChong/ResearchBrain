# Group Sequence Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) introduces a sequence-level approach to importance sampling in reinforcement learning, stabilizing the training of large language models and enabling more efficient policy updates. This method resolves the instability and model collapse observed in prior token-level algorithms, particularly benefiting Mixture-of-Experts architectures by simplifying their training infrastructure, and has been applied to Qwen3 models.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning algorithms like GRPO face instability and catastrophic model collapse when applied to very large language models, particularly with long generated responses.
- The token-level importance sampling used in prior methods introduces high-variance noise, which accumulates and is amplified by clipping, leading to unreliable gradient estimates.
- Training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models with reinforcement learning is challenging due to "expert-activation volatility," causing token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically and hindering convergence.

## Method

- ==Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)== redefines importance ratios based on the likelihood of the entire generated sequence, applying a ==length normalization== for variance reduction.
- The algorithm performs clipping and optimization at the sequence level, ensuring the unit of optimization aligns with the sequence-level reward and objective.
- GSPO's gradient derivation ensures all tokens within a response are weighted equally by the ==sequence-level importance ratio==, thereby eliminating instability from token-specific weighting.

## Results

- GSPO achieved superior training stability and continuous performance improvement across mathematical reasoning **(AIME’24)** and programming **(LiveCodeBench, CodeForces)** benchmarks, outperforming GRPO.
- The algorithm inherently resolved expert-activation volatility in Mixture-of-Experts models, eliminating the need for complex stabilization techniques like "Routing Replay."
- Successfully applied to the **Qwen3** models, GSPO led to notable performance enhancements, validating its effectiveness in practical, state-of-the-art large language model development.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The instability of prior RL algorithms like GRPO is rooted in the misapplication of importance sampling at the token level, creating high-variance noise in gradient estimates.
> - Aligning importance sampling and policy optimization with the entire sequence unit provides a more stable and effective learning signal, addressing challenges unique to Mixture-of-Experts models.
> - High-quality, sequence-level gradient signals can lead to superior training efficiency even when fewer individual tokens contribute to gradient estimation, demonstrating the importance of principled importance sampling.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has seen explosive growth, with reinforcement learning (RL) becoming a cornerstone for enhancing their capabilities, particularly in areas requiring complex reasoning such as mathematics and programming (e.g., OpenAI, 2024; DeepSeek-AI, 2025). The most common paradigm is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or its automated variants. However, scaling RL to gigantic models, especially those with Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, introduces significant challenges, predominantly related to training stability and efficiency.

Historically, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) has been a dominant algorithm in RL. PPO, while effective, requires a separate value model, which introduces substantial memory and computational overhead and whose reliability is critical for algorithmic effectiveness. As LLMs grow in size and complexity, these limitations become more pronounced.

To circumvent the need for a value model, recent advancements have led to algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024). GRPO bypasses the value model by calculating relative advantages within a group of responses to a given query. While innovative, GRPO has exhibited its own set of critical shortcomings: severe stability issues, often leading to catastrophic model collapse, particularly when applied to colossal language models and long-response tasks. This instability prevents researchers and developers from fully leveraging RL to push the boundaries of LLM capabilities.

GSPO enters this landscape as a direct response to these limitations, especially those observed in GRPO. It acknowledges the benefits of GRPO's value-model-free approach but fundamentally re-evaluates its core mechanism: the application of importance sampling weights. The paper argues that GRPO's token-level importance ratios are a misapplication of importance sampling, leading to high-variance noise that accumulates and causes instability. GSPO aims to rectify this by aligning the unit of importance sampling and optimization with the unit of reward – the entire sequence. By proposing a sequence-level approach, GSPO positions itself as an algorithmic improvement designed to unlock the next phase of RL scaling for LLMs, particularly addressing the unique challenges posed by MoE architectures and simplifying the complex infrastructure typically required. It represents an evolution in RL algorithms specifically engineered for the demands of modern, extremely large language models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a stable, efficient, and performant reinforcement learning algorithm for training large language models (LLMs). The authors are driven by the ambition to scale LLMs to tackle increasingly sophisticated problems, which necessitates robust and scalable RL training.

The primary motivation for introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) stems from critical shortcomings identified in existing state-of-the-art RL algorithms, particularly Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The authors highlight two major issues:

1. **Instability and Model Collapse in GRPO:** Current algorithms like GRPO suffer from severe stability problems, frequently leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse during the training of gigantic LLMs. This instability is a significant barrier to continuously pushing the capability boundaries of LLMs through further RL training.
2. **Misapplication of Importance Sampling Weights:** The authors diagnose the root cause of GRPO's instability as a fundamental misapplication of importance sampling weights. They argue that GRPO's token-level importance ratios, derived from individual token probabilities, introduce high-variance training noise. This noise accumulates over long response lengths and is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism, ultimately precipitating model collapse. The core principle of importance sampling, which relies on averaging over multiple samples for effective distribution correction, is violated when weights are applied at the token level for a single sample.
3. **Challenges with Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Models:** The instability of GRPO is particularly acute when training MoE models. The sparse activation nature of MoE models leads to "expert-activation volatility," where the set of activated experts for a given response can change significantly after gradient updates. This phenomenon causes token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically, further invalidating them and hindering convergence.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of GSPO are:
- To define importance ratios based on **sequence likelihood** rather than token likelihood.
- To perform **sequence-level clipping, rewarding, and optimization**, ensuring alignment between the unit of optimization and the unit of reward.
- To inherently resolve the **stability issues** observed in GRPO, especially for MoE models, without requiring complex stabilization strategies.
- To achieve **superior training efficiency and performance** compared to existing methods.
- To simplify the design and implementation of **RL infrastructure**.

By addressing these points, the authors aim to provide a robust and scalable algorithmic foundation that will facilitate the continued advancement of large-scale RL training for future generations of LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GSPO’s methodology centers on a principled re-evaluation of importance sampling and policy optimization within the context of RL for LLMs, shifting from token-level to sequence-level operations.

#### 3.1. Foundation and Critique of Prior Work

The paper begins by establishing context through a review of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).
- **PPO:** Acknowledged for its policy update objective with a clipping mechanism, but criticized for its heavy reliance on a value model, leading to considerable memory/computational burden and scalability challenges for long responses and complex tasks.
- **GRPO:** Praised for bypassing the value model by using group-based relative advantages. However, it's identified as suffering from a "fundamental misapplication and invalidation of importance sampling weights." The token-level importance ratio `w_i,t(θ) = π_θ(y_i,t|x,y_i,<t) / π_θ_old(y_i,t|x,y_i,<t)` (ratio of new policy token probability to old policy token probability) is deemed problematic because it's based on a single token sample, leading to high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences and is amplified by clipping. This noise, in turn, causes model collapse.

#### 3.2. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) – Core Algorithm

GSPO introduces a novel objective that addresses the identified issues in GRPO.
- **Sequence-level Importance Ratio (`s_i(θ)`):** The core innovation is to define the importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire sequence, `π_θ(y_i|x) / π_θ_old(y_i|x)`. This sequence likelihood ratio is then raised to the power `1/|y_i|` (length normalization) to reduce variance and standardize the numerical range of importance ratios across different response lengths. Mathematically, `s_i(θ) = (π_θ(y_i|x) / π_θ_old(y_i|x))^(1/|y_i|)`. This normalization ensures that the clipping range can be unified, regardless of sequence length.
- **Group-based Advantage Estimation (`b_A_i`):** GSPO retains GRPO's effective approach of estimating advantage. The advantage for an entire response `y_i` is computed as the normalized reward `(r(x, y_i) - mean({r(x, y_i)}) ) / std({r(x, y_i)})`, where the mean and standard deviation are taken over all responses in a group for a given query `x`.
- **Sequence-level Clipping and Optimization:** Unlike GRPO, which clips individual token importance ratios, GSPO applies clipping to the *entire sequence-level importance ratio `s_i(θ)`*. This ensures that "overly off-policy" samples are excluded at the granularity of a full response, aligning the clipping mechanism with the sequence-level reward and optimization objective.
- **Gradient Analysis:** The paper provides a mathematical derivation of GSPO's gradient and contrasts it with GRPO's. This analysis reveals that GSPO weights all tokens within a response equally using the sequence-level importance ratio, thereby eliminating the instability introduced by GRPO's unequal, token-specific weighting based on individual token likelihood ratios.

#### 3.3. GSPO-token: A Token-level Objective Variant

For scenarios requiring finer-grained advantage adjustments (e.g., multi-turn RL), GSPO introduces a variant called GSPO-token.
- **Flexible Advantage:** This variant allows for token-wise advantage customization (`b_A_i,t`).
- **Preserved Sequence-level Influence:** Crucially, `s_i,t(θ)` (the effective importance ratio for each token) is defined such that it numerically equals `s_i(θ)` (the sequence-level importance ratio from GSPO) while still allowing token-level gradients to be weighted by token-specific advantages. This is achieved by multiplying a numerically fixed `sg[s_i(θ)]` (stop-gradient sequence ratio) with a `π_θ(y_i,t|x, y_i,<t) / sg[π_θ(y_i,t|x, y_i,<t)]` term, ensuring the overall sequence-level importance ratio is maintained for the gradient calculations, even with token-level advantage adjustments.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Model:** A cold-start model fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, an MoE architecture.
- **Benchmarks:** AIME’24 (mathematical reasoning), LiveCodeBench (programming), and CodeForces (competitive programming Elo Rating).
- **Comparison:** GRPO served as the baseline, with its hyperparameters (clipping ranges) carefully tuned for a fair comparison.
- **Key Differentiator in MoE:** The experiments also highlighted GRPO’s dependency on a "Routing Replay" strategy for MoE models to converge, a dependency that GSPO aims to eliminate.

In essence, GSPO provides a theoretically sound and empirically validated framework for stabilizing and enhancing RL training of large language models by re-conceptualizing importance sampling at the sequence level, making it particularly suited for the complexities of modern LLM architectures like MoE.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) against Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) yielded several significant findings across stability, efficiency, performance, and specific architectural benefits.

#### 4.1. Superior Training Stability and Efficiency

- **Continuous Improvement:** GSPO demonstrated remarkably stable training dynamics throughout the entire process. Unlike GRPO, which is prone to model collapse, GSPO consistently delivered continuous performance improvements with increased computational investment, regular updates to the query set, and extended generation lengths.
- **Higher Efficiency:** Even under the same training compute and consumed queries, GSPO exhibited superior training efficiency. It achieved better training accuracy and significantly higher benchmark performance on AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, and CodeForces compared to GRPO. The training curves presented in Figure 1 clearly illustrate GSPO's sustained upward trend in rewards and benchmark scores, while GRPO's progress is less pronounced or plateaus earlier.
- **Real-world Validation:** The authors successfully applied GSPO to the RL training of their "latest Qwen3 models," directly attributing "exceptional performance improvements" to GSPO's efficacy. This serves as strong validation of the algorithm's practical utility and scalability for state-of-the-art LLMs.

#### 4.2. Curious Observation on Clipping Fractions

- **Counter-intuitive Clipping Behavior:** A striking observation was the significant difference in clipped token fractions. Despite GSPO's sequence-level clipping mechanism, it resulted in two orders of magnitude *more* clipped tokens than GRPO (as shown in Figure 2). This means GSPO effectively uses a much smaller proportion of tokens for gradient estimation.
- **Implication for Gradient Quality:** Counter-intuitively, this higher clipping fraction in GSPO did *not* hinder performance; instead, GSPO still achieved superior training efficiency. This finding strongly supports the authors' hypothesis that GRPO's token-level gradient estimates are inherently noisy and inefficient. Even with fewer "valid" token data points, GSPO's sequence-level approach provides a more reliable and effective learning signal, leading to better overall training outcomes.

#### 4.3. Significant Benefit for MoE Training

- **Resolution of Expert-Activation Volatility:** GSPO inherently resolved the notorious expert-activation volatility issue that plagues RL training of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models when using token-level importance ratios (like GRPO). This volatility, where activated experts for the same response change significantly after policy updates, causes token-level importance ratios to fluctuate wildly, hindering GRPO's convergence.
- **Elimination of Routing Replay:** GRPO required a complex and resource-intensive "Routing Replay" strategy (caching and replaying old policy's activated experts) to stabilize MoE training. GSPO, by focusing on sequence likelihoods (`π_θ(y_i|x)`), proved immune to this volatility, as the overall sequence likelihood remains relatively stable even if individual expert activations shift. Consequently, GSPO entirely obviated the need for "Routing Replay," simplifying the training process and allowing MoE models to leverage their full capacity without artificial constraints or additional overhead (Figure 3 demonstrates GRPO's failure without Routing Replay).

#### 4.4. Simplification of RL Infrastructure

- **Tolerance to Precision Discrepancies:** GSPO's reliance on sequence-level likelihoods makes it significantly more tolerant to precision discrepancies between training engines and inference engines (e.g., Megatron vs. SGLang/vLLM).
- **Direct Use of Inference Likelihoods:** This tolerance potentially allows for direct use of likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization, eliminating the need for costly recomputation of old policy likelihoods using the training engine. This simplification is particularly beneficial in complex RL setups such as partial rollout, multi-turn RL, and training-inference disaggregated frameworks, leading to reduced computational overhead and engineering complexity.

In summary, GSPO not only outperformed GRPO in terms of stability and efficiency but also provided fundamental architectural benefits for MoE models and promises significant simplification for RL infrastructure, all validated by its successful application to the Qwen3 models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) represents a significant advancement in the field of Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models (LLMs), with profound implications for the development and scaling of next-generation AI.

#### 5.1. Algorithmic Advancement and Foundational Stability

GSPO offers a theoretically grounded and empirically superior alternative to prior policy optimization algorithms like GRPO, especially for gigantic LLMs. By correctly applying importance sampling at the sequence level and aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward, it addresses a fundamental flaw that led to instability and model collapse in previous methods. This stability is not merely an incremental improvement; it is a prerequisite for pushing the boundaries of LLM capabilities through continued, large-scale RL training. As such, GSPO provides a more robust and reliable foundation for RL-driven LLM development.

#### 5.2. Enabling Advanced LLM Architectures (MoE Models)

The most striking impact of GSPO is its ability to inherently resolve the stability challenges associated with training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models using RL. MoE models are crucial for scaling LLMs to unprecedented sizes and achieving higher computational efficiency, but their sparse activation nature posed unique difficulties for existing RL algorithms. By eliminating the need for complex workarounds like "Routing Replay," GSPO not only simplifies the training process but also allows MoE models to fully leverage their architectural advantages without artificial constraints. This capability is critical for the continued scaling of models like Qwen3, enabling the development of more powerful, efficient, and specialized LLMs.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Training Efficiency and Performance

Beyond stability, GSPO demonstrably improves training efficiency, achieving better benchmark performance with the same computational budget. This means that developers can either train more capable models in less time or achieve higher levels of performance within existing training schedules. The counter-intuitive finding that GSPO clips significantly more tokens yet performs better highlights that the *quality* of the learning signal is more important than the sheer *quantity* of samples, reinforcing the value of its sequence-level approach. This efficiency gain is vital in an era where LLM training costs are astronomically high.

#### 5.4. Simplification of RL Infrastructure and Cost Reduction

The potential for GSPO to tolerate precision discrepancies between training and inference engines, and thus allow direct use of likelihoods from inference engines, promises a significant simplification of RL infrastructure. This could lead to a reduction in engineering complexity, computational overhead, and overall operational costs, especially in complex multi-turn or distributed RL scenarios. This practical benefit lowers the barrier to entry for advanced RL-LLM applications and accelerates iterative development cycles.

#### 5.5. Catalyst for Future Research and Development

The authors position GSPO as a "scalable algorithmic cornerstone" for future advances. Its success in the Qwen3 models signals that it is production-ready and effective at the cutting edge of LLM research. This could spur further research into sequence-level RL methodologies, new ways of designing importance ratios, and more efficient integration of RL with complex generative models. By providing a stable and efficient platform, GSPO enables researchers to explore more ambitious RL objectives and more complex tasks for LLMs, ultimately accelerating the path towards more intelligent and versatile AI systems.

In essence, GSPO is not just an incremental improvement; it is a fundamental re-thinking of how RL is applied to LLMs, addressing critical stability and efficiency bottlenecks that were hindering further progress. Its impact will likely be seen in more robust, capable, and economically feasible large language models across various domains.
