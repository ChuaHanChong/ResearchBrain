# FACTR 2: Learning External Force Sensing for Commodity Robot Arms Improves Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces FACTR 2, a system that enables high-quality external force sensing on commodity robot arms without dedicated force-torque sensors and leverages this information to improve policy learning. It presents NEXT, a data-driven method for estimating external joint torques with an 87.6% lower L1 error than baselines during contact, and FIRST, a behavior cloning strategy that uses these estimates to strategically re-weight training data, leading to enhanced performance across various contact-rich manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Many robot manipulation tasks, especially those involving contact, require precise force awareness that traditional vision systems struggle to infer.
- Dedicated force-torque sensors are expensive and typically absent from most commodity robot arms, creating a cost and accessibility barrier for force-aware robotics.
- Existing sensorless force estimation methods are often model-based, requiring extensive manual engineering and susceptible to complex, unmodeled robot dynamics.

## Method

- Developed ==Neural External Torque Estimation== (NEXT), a data-driven neural network that learns free-space inverse dynamics from minimal contact-free data to estimate external joint torques as a residual.
- Integrated NEXT's estimated torques into a bilateral teleoperation system to provide haptic feedback on non-sensorized robot arms, making force-aware control accessible.
- Proposed Force-Informed Re-Sampling Training (FIRST), a behavior cloning method that uses NEXT's torque estimates to identify and up-sample critical pre-contact and contact phases in expert demonstrations.

## Results

- NEXT achieved an average L1 error of **0.547** Nm during external contact (**87.6%** lower than FILIC and **62.8%** lower than a disturbance observer) and less noisy free-space estimates than dedicated sensors.
- User studies showed NEXT-based force feedback improved perceived ease of use for teleoperation on both Franka and AgileX Piper arms, comparable to systems with dedicated sensors.
- FIRST consistently yielded the highest average task progress across five long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation tasks, with up-sampling pre-contact phases proving most beneficial for robust policy learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurate sensorless external torque estimation can be achieved with a data-driven LSTM model trained on just 10 minutes of free-motion data, outperforming model-based baselines.
> - Leveraging estimated external torque for force-feedback teleoperation significantly improves user experience and control on low-cost, non-sensorized robot arms.
> - Strategically re-weighting imitation learning data to emphasize pre-contact and contact phases substantially improves policy robustness and success rates in contact-rich manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Many robotic manipulation tasks, particularly those involving contact with the environment such as assembly, insertion, or handling deformable objects, necessitate precise force awareness. While humans naturally leverage force feedback for such interactions, traditional robot vision systems often struggle to infer subtle contact information reliably. Dedicated force-torque (FT) sensors, which provide this crucial information, are typically integrated only into high-cost robotic platforms (e.g., Franka, KUKA). A significant portion of the robot arms available on the market, especially lower-cost models, lack these built-in sensors. Retrofitting these arms with end-effector FT sensors is often expensive and technically challenging, sometimes costing more than the robot arm itself. Emerging low-cost tactile sensors, such as capacitive or magnetic variants, offer alternatives but are often not robust enough for the larger force magnitudes encountered at the end effector during typical manipulation.

In response to the limitations of dedicated force sensing, the field has explored sensorless external force estimation. Traditional approaches rely on analytical modeling of robot dynamics and system identification to infer external forces from the residuals between predicted and observed motor signals. While effective in some contexts, these methods typically demand substantial manual engineering effort and can be sensitive to complex actuator nonlinearities (e.g., dead zones, torque ripple) and unmodeled effects. Learning-based methods have emerged to address these challenges, including supervised approaches that learn from paired proprioceptive and contact data, and unsupervised methods primarily for contact detection. However, inverse-dynamics learning, while explored for specialized platforms, has not been broadly demonstrated on a wide range of commodity robot arms.

The integration of force information into imitation learning (IL) policies is another active area. Previous research has explored several strategies: using force-related signals (e.g., joint effort, joint torque, end-effector force) as direct policy inputs, employing auxiliary objectives to encourage contact-aware representations, or combining behavior cloning with hybrid force-position or impedance control during inference. The FACTR system previously demonstrated the use of force information during training to enhance policy generalization, but it relied on the availability of dedicated force sensors.

This paper addresses a gap in the research landscape by enabling high-quality external force sensing on commodity robot arms *without* dedicated force-torque sensors. It then leverages this sensorless force information to advance both force-feedback teleoperation and robust policy learning. By extending the capabilities of low-cost robots, this work aims to democratize access to force-aware robotic manipulation, bridging the gap between expensive, sensorized systems and more accessible, non-sensorized platforms. Furthermore, it introduces a novel approach to dataset reweighting in imitation learning, informed by the learned force signals, building upon existing work in policy learning with data reweighting to specifically target critical phases of contact-rich tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the disparity between the demands of contact-rich manipulation tasks and the sensing capabilities of many commercially available robot arms.
- **The Necessity of Force Sensing:** Many practical manipulation tasks, such as inserting a peg into a hole, fastening components, or handling delicate objects, inherently involve physical contact. Humans intuitively rely on force and tactile feedback for these interactions, which are difficult to infer reliably from visual information alone due. The absence of force feedback in robots restricts their ability to perform such tasks robustly and precisely.
- **Cost and Accessibility Barriers:** Dedicated force-torque sensors are typically expensive and bulky, limiting their integration to high-end robotic platforms. This cost barrier precludes their use in a broader range of applications and on more affordable robot systems, thereby limiting the accessibility of force-aware robotics.
- **Limitations of Current Sensorless Approaches:** Existing model-based methods for estimating external forces without dedicated sensors often require extensive, robot-specific system identification and are susceptible to unmodeled complexities in robot dynamics, such as actuator nonlinearities, friction, and hysteresis. This makes them challenging to deploy broadly across diverse robotic hardware, especially low-cost arms where detailed dynamic models might not be readily available or accurate.
- **Policy Learning Challenges in Contact-Rich Scenarios:** In imitation learning, policies often struggle with contact-rich tasks. Failures frequently concentrate around precise alignment stages before contact and during the nuanced, force-sensitive interactions required in the contact phase. Standard behavior cloning methods typically sample data uniformly, which may dilute the learning signal from these critical but often brief segments of a demonstration.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Data-Driven External Torque Estimation Method (NEXT):** Create a neural network-based approach that can accurately estimate external joint torques without requiring dedicated force-torque sensors or complex, manual system identification. This method should be trainable with minimal data (e.g., approximately 10 minutes of free-motion data).
2. **Enable Force-Feedback Teleoperation on Low-Cost Arms:** Utilize the estimated external torque from NEXT to provide haptic feedback for teleoperation systems, making force-aware control accessible to non-sensorized, low-cost robot platforms.
3. **Propose a Force-Informed Policy Learning Strategy (FIRST):** Introduce a behavior cloning technique that leverages the estimated external torque to intelligently re-weight demonstration data. Specifically, FIRST aims to identify and up-sample pre-contact and contact phases within expert demonstrations, thereby focusing policy learning on the most critical and failure-prone segments of manipulation tasks.
4. **Demonstrate Improved Policy Performance:** Empirically evaluate whether FIRST, in conjunction with NEXT, leads to demonstrably higher success rates and more robust behaviors in long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation tasks compared to existing baseline methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces two primary components: Neural External Torque Estimation (NEXT) for sensorless force estimation, and Force-Informed Re-Sampling Training (FIRST) for leveraging these estimates in behavior cloning.

#### 3.1. Neural External Torque Estimation (NEXT)

NEXT is a data-driven approach designed to estimate external joint torques ($\tau_{ext}$) without the need for dedicated force-torque sensors.
- **Core Principle:** Instead of relying on analytical robot dynamic models to compute the torque required for free-space motion ($\tau_f$), NEXT learns this free-space inverse dynamics directly from data. The external torque is then estimated as the residual between the measured motor torque ($\tau_m$) and the learned free-space torque prediction ($\hat{\tau}_f$). Measured motor torque ($\tau_m$) is derived from motor current measurements ($I_m$) using a known torque constant ($K$), i.e., $\tau_m = KI_m$. The estimated external torque is $\hat{\tau}_{ext} = \tau_m - \hat{\tau}_f$.
- **Data Collection for Training:** The model $f_\theta$ (which predicts $\hat{\tau}_f$) is trained on a dataset ($D_{free}$) consisting of approximately 10 minutes of contact-free robot arm trajectories. During free-space motion, the measured motor torque is considered equal to the free-space torque, providing the ground truth for training. This procedure avoids the need for dedicated force sensors, payload calibration, or manual system identification.
- **Input Features:** The input to the neural network ($x_i$) is a temporal history of proprioceptive quantities, specifically joint positions ($q$), joint velocities ($\dot{q}$), and the difference between commanded and current joint positions ($\Delta q_d$). A history length (H) of 50 timesteps is utilized. The inclusion of temporal history allows the model to capture complex, history-dependent effects such as hysteresis, actuator dynamics, and frictional memory, which are difficult to represent with instantaneous rigid-body dynamics.
- **Model Architecture:** The free-space inverse dynamics model ($f_\theta$) is an LSTM-based sequence model. It comprises a 2-layer LSTM with a hidden size of 128, followed by a 2-layer MLP with a hidden size of 256 for the prediction head. A stateless sliding-window formulation is used, where the hidden state is reset for each sequence within the look-back window, capturing local temporal dynamics.
- **Training:** The model is trained using an L2 regression loss between the network's prediction $\hat{\tau}_f(x_i)$ and the measured motor torque $\tau_{m,i}$ from the free-space dataset. Training is computationally lightweight, requiring about 1 minute on an NVIDIA RTX 3090.
- **Deployment:** At runtime, the measured motor current is used to obtain $\tau_m$. The trained LSTM predicts $\hat{\tau}_f$ from the current proprioceptive history. The estimated external joint torque $\hat{\tau}_{ext}$ is then calculated as the residual. The model operates at a rate of 100 Hz, with a forward pass latency of approximately 1.76 ms.

#### 3.2. Force-Feedback Teleoperation

The external joint torque estimates produced by NEXT are integrated into a bilateral teleoperation system. This enables force feedback on non-sensorized robot arms. The force feedback control law used applies a torque to the leader arm ($\tau_{feedback}$) proportional to the estimated external torque ($\hat{\tau}_{ext}$) sensed by the follower arm, scaled by a gain $K_{f,p}$ (i.e., $\tau_{feedback} = K_{f,p}\hat{\tau}_{ext}$). This setup is analogous to the FACTR teleoperation system but operates without dedicated hardware sensors.

#### 3.3. Force-Informed Re-Sampling Training (FIRST)

FIRST is a behavior cloning method designed to enhance policy learning for contact-rich tasks by strategically re-weighting training data based on contact phases identified by NEXT.
- **Policy Inputs and Outputs:** The policy conditions on observations ($o_t$) comprising multi-view camera images ($I_{1:M,t}$), joint position ($q_t$), and the estimated external joint torque ($\hat{\tau}_{ext,t}$) from NEXT. It predicts a chunk of future joint position targets ($\hat{a}_{t:t+k}$). The policy architecture can be a flow-matching policy or an Action Chunking Transformer (ACT), trained with standard behavior cloning objectives.
- **Trajectory Segmentation:** The method first labels each datapoint in the expert demonstration dataset as either Free Motion, Pre-Contact, or Contact.
 - **Contact Identification:** A scalar contact score ($f_t = ||\hat{\tau}_{ext,t}||_1$) is derived from the estimated external joint torque. Hysteresis thresholding ($T_{high}, T_{low}$) is applied to $f_t$ to generate a binary contact indicator ($c_t$), which switches on or off based on these thresholds to reduce noise.
 - **Phase Labeling:** Contact onsets ($T_{onset}$) are identified when $c_t$ switches from non-contact to contact.
 - **Contact (C):** Timesteps where $c_t = 1$.
 - **Pre-Contact (PC):** A one-second window (F timesteps at 100Hz) immediately preceding each contact onset.
 - **Free Space (F):** All remaining timesteps.
- **Up-Sampling during Training:** After phase labeling, FIRST modifies the sampling distribution for minibatch construction. Phase-dependent sampling weights ($w_F, w_{PC}, w_C$) are assigned, typically with $w_{PC}, w_C > w_F$. During training, datapoints are sampled with a probability proportional to their assigned weight ($p_t = w(s_t) / \sum w(s_j)$). This increases the representation of pre-contact and contact segments in each training batch, focusing the learning process on critical interaction phases.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results across external force estimation, force-feedback teleoperation, and imitation learning.

#### 4.1. NEXT External Force Estimation Performance

- **Comparison to Ground Truth and Baselines (Franka Arm):**
 - **Under External Contact:** NEXT demonstrated an average L1 error of 0.547 Nm, which was 87.6% lower than FILIC and 62.8% lower than a disturbance observer (DO). Qualitatively, NEXT's estimates closely tracked the Franka's built-in external joint torque sensor during contact and transitions, whereas baselines exhibited substantial deviations.
 - **In Free Space:** Where true external torque is zero, NEXT achieved an average L1 error of 0.414 Nm. This performance surpassed FILIC (2.460 Nm), the disturbance observer (2.429 Nm), and even the Franka's dedicated external sensor (0.449 Nm). NEXT's free-space estimates were observed to be less noisy and closer to zero than the dedicated sensor.
- **Performance on Low-Cost Arm (AgileX Piper):** On the Piper arm, which lacks dedicated force sensors, NEXT achieved an average joint L1 error of 0.018 Nm in free space (against a ground truth of zero). This was substantially lower than FILIC (0.435 Nm) and the disturbance observer (0.248 Nm), indicating NEXT's effectiveness on non-sensorized platforms.
- **Model Architecture Ablations:** Ablation studies confirmed that an LSTM architecture, using a history of joint positions, velocities, and commanded-to-current position differences ($\Delta q_d$) over 50 timesteps, yielded the most accurate external torque estimates. Recurrent models outperformed MLPs, and $\Delta q_d$ was found to be an informative input.

#### 4.2. Force-Feedback Teleoperation

- **User Study (Franka Arm):** A user study with 20 participants performing a wiping task on a Franka arm indicated that NEXT-based force feedback was rated as easier to use compared to baselines (no feedback, disturbance observer, position-position feedback). It also resulted in reduced applied joint torque, performing comparably to FACTR Teleop which utilizes dedicated joint torque sensors.
- **User Study (AgileX Piper Arm):** On the Piper arm, NEXT-based force feedback similarly outperformed baseline feedback methods (no feedback, DO, PP) in terms of ease of use, demonstrating its utility on low-cost hardware.

#### 4.3. FIRST Policy Learning Performance

- **Overall Task Progress:** FIRST consistently achieved the highest average task progress across five long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation tasks (LEGO Assembly, NIST Belt, NIST Insertion, Tool Clean Up, Cap Screwing). This improvement was observed with both flow-matching policies and Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) architectures. Qualitatively, FIRST policies exhibited more robust recovery behaviors and finer alignment adjustments before key contact events.
- **Phase-wise Validation Loss Analysis:** Analysis of validation loss across different contact phases (free-space, pre-contact, contact) for the NIST Belt task showed that baseline policies (Base Policy + Torque) exhibited higher losses on pre-contact and contact samples. FIRST, by up-sampling these critical phases, successfully reduced their validation losses, bringing them closer to the free-motion loss, indicating focused learning.
- **Upsampling Ablation:**
 - Upsampling only pre-contact (PC) samples yielded the highest average task progress (0.818).
 - Upsampling both pre-contact and contact (PC+C) was also competitive (0.811), proving most beneficial for tasks requiring sustained contact (e.g., Cap Screwing).
 - Upsampling only contact (C) samples did not improve performance significantly. These results suggest that the moments preceding contact, where precise alignment is crucial, are particularly valuable for training.
 - **Upsampling Magnitude:** Moderate up-sampling ratios (e.g., a factor of 5) were found to be optimal, with overly aggressive up-sampling leading to saturation or performance degradation.
- **Input Comparison (Raw Joint Current vs. NEXT $\tau_{ext}$):** Policies conditioned on NEXT-estimated external joint torque consistently outperformed those conditioned on raw joint current for contact-sensitive tasks (NIST Insertion, Cap Screwing). While raw current provided some benefit, NEXT's cleaned external torque signal provided a more effective representation for the policy, particularly in detecting tightened contact states to prevent over-screwing. Attention analysis supported this, showing clearer, event-aligned attention to NEXT's $\tau_{ext}$ during critical contact events.
- **Per-Batch Up-sampling vs. Weighted Regression:** FIRST's per-batch up-sampling strategy for emphasizing contact-relevant samples was found to be more effective than simply re-weighting the loss function. Weighted regression sometimes degraded performance or provided minimal gains, suggesting that directly modifying the training distribution is more stable for policy learning.
- **Benefit Without Force Input to Policy:** Even when NEXT-derived external torque was used *only* for segmenting demonstrations into phases and re-sampling during training (without directly feeding the torque to the policy), it still provided a clear improvement over standard behavior cloning, demonstrating the utility of phase-informed data re-sampling. However, directly conditioning the policy on the estimated external torque provided additional performance gains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in making force-aware robotic manipulation more accessible and robust, particularly for a broader range of robotic platforms.

- **Democratization of Force Sensing:** The most significant impact of NEXT is its ability to provide high-quality external force estimation for low-cost, off-the-shelf robot arms that lack dedicated force sensors. This capability effectively democratizes force sensing, removing a substantial cost and complexity barrier that previously limited advanced manipulation tasks to expensive, specialized hardware. This could enable new applications and research on more affordable robotic systems.
- **Enhanced Robot Capabilities:**
 - **Improved Teleoperation:** By enabling force-feedback teleoperation on previously non-sensorized arms, NEXT enhances human-robot interaction. Operators can gain a more intuitive feel for contact interactions, potentially improving control, precision, and safety in remote manipulation tasks.
 - **More Robust Policy Learning:** FIRST provides a method to significantly improve the performance of policies for contact-rich tasks. By strategically focusing policy learning on critical pre-contact and contact phases, it leads to higher success rates, improved alignment, and more robust interaction behaviors. This is particularly valuable for long-horizon, multi-stage tasks where precise force control is essential.
- **Reduced Development Overhead:** NEXT's data-driven approach requires only 10 minutes of free-motion data and 1 minute of training, significantly reducing the manual engineering effort, system identification, and calibration traditionally associated with sensorless force estimation. This ease of adoption lowers the barrier for researchers and developers to implement force-aware capabilities on new robot platforms.
- **Guidance for Imitation Learning:** The ablation studies in FIRST provide empirical insights into data reweighting for contact-rich tasks, highlighting the particular value of emphasizing pre-contact samples for robust policy learning. This offers practical guidance for designing effective training strategies in imitation learning.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in several areas:
 - **Advanced Control:** The availability of accurate, low-cost external force estimates could enable more sophisticated force-based controllers and adaptive impedance control schemes on a wider variety of robots.
 - **Learning from Demonstration:** The ability to automatically segment demonstrations into contact phases can facilitate richer semantic understanding of manipulation skills, leading to more intelligent and adaptable learning from demonstration frameworks.
 - **Real-World Deployment:** By making force awareness more accessible, FACTR 2 could accelerate the deployment of robots into complex, unstructured real-world environments where delicate contact and precise interaction are common.

In summary, this research contributes to making advanced robotic manipulation more accessible, robust, and easier to implement, potentially broadening the impact of robotics in various domains.
