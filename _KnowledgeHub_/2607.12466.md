# Deployable Human Preference Alignment in Robotics: Learning Representative Rewards from Diverse Human Preferences

> [!summary] Summary
> The research introduces Preference-based REward Clustering (PREC), a robotics framework designed to learn a compact set of representative reward models from diverse, sparse, and noisy human preference feedback. This approach consistently achieves higher social welfare metrics (Utilitarian, Egalitarian, Nash) in D4RL MuJoCo environments compared to single-policy and fully individualized methods, demonstrating robust user clustering and efficacy under real-world data limitations.

## Problem

- Existing human preference alignment methods in robotics are either unscalable (per-user alignment leading to a high validation burden and unstable learning from sparse/noisy data) or undifferentiated (single shared policies neglecting diverse user preferences).
- Robotic systems need to align with heterogeneous human preferences, but collecting dense and reliable data for each individual is resource-intensive and often unfeasible.
- Regulatory requirements for critical robotic systems (e.g., medical devices) make the deployment of a large number of individualized policies impractical due to extensive validation needs.

## Method

- Develops **Preference-based REward Clustering (PREC)**, a framework that learns a limited set of 'K' deployable policies and corresponding ==reward models== from offline ==binary preference labels==.
- Factorizes the reward model into a population-level self-supervised ==trajectory encoder== (pretrained with ==Self-Predictive Representations==) and subpopulation-level decoders to decouple representation learning from noisy preference supervision.
- Utilizes a 'leaky' ==Expectation-Maximization (EM)== algorithm to jointly assign users to preference-coherent clusters and learn a robust, representative reward model for each cluster, ensuring stability and preventing overfitting in small clusters.

## Results

- PREC consistently achieved higher **Silhouette** and **Calinski–Harabasz** scores, indicating superior preference-coherent user clustering compared to baseline methods across varying numbers of clusters (**K=2, 3, 5**) in D4RL MuJoCo environments.
- Policies trained with PREC variants consistently delivered the **highest social welfare** across **Utilitarian**, **Egalitarian (worst-off 30%)**, and **Nash** metrics, outperforming single-pooled and fully individualized policies under sparse and noisy feedback conditions.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the self-supervised SPR encoder and the leaky EM algorithm are essential components, contributing significantly to PREC's overall performance and robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling feature extraction using a self-supervised encoder (SPR) from preference label supervision makes the reward learning process more robust to sparse and noisy human feedback.
> - A compact set of representative reward models (and policies) offers a practical middle ground, balancing effective personalization for diverse users with the scalability required for real-world deployment and regulatory compliance.
> - The 'leaky' M-step in the EM algorithm is critical for stabilizing the joint clustering and reward learning, preventing pathological cases where small clusters might receive insufficient updates or overfit.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems, including robots, into everyday user-facing applications necessitates their behavior to align with human preferences. This alignment has been shown to enhance the performance of systems such as large language models (LLMs) and robotic platforms. However, existing approaches to preference alignment in AI systems face several limitations.

One common strategy involves "per-user alignment," where a system is tailored to individual user preferences. While aiming for high personalization, this approach encounters significant challenges in real-world robotics deployment. Preference feedback from individual users is often sparse and noisy, leading to unstable policy learning. Furthermore, the creation and validation of a growing number of individualized policies pose substantial burdens, particularly due to regulatory requirements (e.g., FDA classification of medical exoskeletons as Class II devices, requiring software validation and risk analysis).

Conversely, adopting a "single shared policy" approach, which aggregates preferences across all users, avoids the high validation cost but fails to capture the heterogeneity of human preferences. This can lead to the neglect of minority preferences and a reduction in overall user satisfaction, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as the "tyranny of the crowd-worker."

Previous research in human alignment in robotics has explored various feedback interfaces, including pairwise comparisons, structured natural language, and free-form natural language, applied in domains like exoskeleton gait personalization and assistive robotics. Similarly, preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL) has advanced methods for learning reward models from binary feedback, improving data efficiency and robustness. However, many PbRL studies primarily focus on preferences from a single labeler distribution or aggregate feedback from multiple users into a single policy, thereby not explicitly modeling heterogeneous user populations or addressing the deployment-specific constraints of robotics. Online PbRL, while effective, can also expose users to unsafe behaviors during exploration, limiting its real-world applicability. Offline PbRL has emerged to mitigate interaction costs but remains sensitive to sparse or noisy preference labels.

This work addresses a gap in the current landscape by focusing on "deployable" human preference alignment from a deployer's perspective. It seeks to bridge the chasm between the unscalable per-user alignment and the undifferentiated single-shared policy by developing a framework that learns a *compact set* of representative policies. This approach is intended to mitigate the regulatory validation burden and the challenges of sparse/noisy data, while preserving a degree of preference diversity for heterogeneous user populations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables deployable human preference alignment in robotics, allowing robotic systems to effectively serve diverse end-users while accommodating the practical constraints of real-world deployment.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in deploying preference-aligned robotic systems at scale:

- **Heterogeneous User Preferences:** As robots interact with a broad spectrum of people, their behavior needs to adapt to diverse individual preferences. Existing solutions either homogenize these preferences into a single policy, which neglects significant portions of the user base, or attempt full individualization, which is often impractical. The objective is to find a middle ground that captures significant preference diversity without infinite customization.

- **Sparse and Noisy Preference Data:** Collecting dense and reliable human preference data for each individual user is resource-intensive and often unfeasible in real-world scenarios. Human feedback is also inherently noisy, making it difficult to learn stable and accurate reward models, especially for per-user alignment. The research aims to develop a method that remains robust and effective even with limited and imperfect human feedback.

- **Regulatory and Validation Burden for Policy Deployment:** For critical robotic systems, such as medical devices (e.g., lower-extremity exoskeletons regulated by the FDA), each deployed policy often requires stringent software validation, risk analysis, and clinical evaluation. A fully individualized alignment approach would generate a vast number of unique policies, leading to an unsustainable validation burden for deployers. The motivation is to reduce this burden by learning a manageable, *compact set* of policies, allowing deployers to control the granularity of alignment based on specific regulatory and operational requirements.

- **Limitations of Existing PbRL Approaches:** Current Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) methods often struggle with data sparsity and noise, especially when dealing with heterogeneous user populations. Many approaches either assume a single preference distribution or aggregate diverse preferences into a single, potentially compromised, reward signal. This work is motivated to move beyond these limitations by explicitly modeling and leveraging user diversity more effectively.

To address these motivations, the key objective is to introduce a novel framework, Preference-based REward Clustering (PREC), which learns a limited number of representative reward models from offline preference feedback. This approach aims to enhance social welfare across all users, including those with minority preferences, by effectively clustering users with similar preferences and training policies that cater to these representative groups.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Preference-based REward Clustering (PREC), is designed to learn a compact set of representative reward models from binary preference labels provided by diverse users. It operates by decoupling representation learning from noisy preference supervision and employing a specialized Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm for user clustering and reward model learning.

**Problem Formulation:**
The environment is a shared Markov environment $E = (S, A, T, T_0, \gamma)$. A population of $N$ users may have different behavioral preferences. Each user $i$ provides a dataset $D_i = \{(\tau_{i,m}, y_{i,m})\}_{m=1}^{M_i}$, where $\tau_{i,m}$ is a trajectory segment and $y_{i,m} \in \{0,1\}$ is a binary preference label (bad/good). The objective is to find a limited set of $K \leq N$ deployable policies $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K)$ and a user-to-policy assignment matrix $\alpha$ that maximizes a social welfare function.

**Reward Model Factorization:**
PREC factorizes the reward model $\hat{r}_{\psi}(s,a)$ into two components:
1. **Population-Level Shared Trajectory Encoder ($f_{\psi_{enc}}$):** This component embeds each state-action pair $(s,a)$ into a latent feature space.
 - **Self-Supervised Pretraining:** The encoder is pretrained in a self-supervised manner using Self-Predictive Representations (SPR) on the union of all offline trajectories collected across all users, without using any preference labels. The goal is to predict the one-step state difference $s_{t+1} - s_t$ from $f_{\psi_{enc}}(s_t, a_t)$. This pretraining step allows the encoder to learn broad state-action structures while avoiding the influence of sparse and noisy human labels.
 - **Freezing:** After pretraining, the encoder $f_{\psi_{enc}^*}$ is fixed.
2. **Subpopulation-Level Decoders ($h_{\psi_{dec_k}}$):** A set of $K$ decoders, one for each cluster, maps the latent features from the frozen encoder to a scalar reward. The cluster-specific reward model is defined as $\hat{r}_{\psi_k}(s,a) = h_{\psi_{dec_k}}(f_{\psi_{enc}^*}(s,a))$.

**Subpopulation-level Decoder Learning with Leaky EM:**
PREC employs an EM framework to jointly assign users to preference-coherent clusters and learn a representative reward model (decoder) for each cluster.
- **Segment Score and Likelihood:** For a labeled segment $(\tau_{i,m}, y_{i,m})$, the predicted segment score for cluster $k$ is $\hat{R}_{\psi_k}(\tau_{i,m}) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \hat{r}_{\psi_k}(s_t, a_t)$. The likelihood of observing $y_{i,m}$ given cluster $k$ is modeled using a sigmoid function: $P(y_{i,m} | \tau_{i,m}, Z_i=k, \psi_k) = \sigma(\hat{R}_{\psi_k}(\tau_{i,m}))^{y_{i,m}} (1 - \sigma(\hat{R}_{\psi_k}(\tau_{i,m})))^{1-y_{i,m}}$. The user-level likelihood $L_{ik}$ aggregates these over all segments for user $i$.
- **E-step (Responsibility Calculation):** Given the current decoders, the E-step calculates the posterior responsibility $\gamma_{ik}$ of each cluster $k$ for explaining user $i$'s preferences: $\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\rho_k L_{ik}}{\sum_{k'=1}^K \rho_{k'} L_{ik'}}$, where $\rho_k$ is a fixed cluster prior. This responsibility is then converted into a hard assignment $\hat{z}_i = \arg\max_k \gamma_{ik}$, assigning user $i$ to the cluster that best explains their preferences.
- **Leaky M-step (Decoder Update):** To address the brittleness of standard hard M-step updates (where clusters with few users might overfit), PREC introduces a "leaky" M-step. Instead of a strictly binary assignment weight, each user $i$ contributes to cluster $k$'s decoder training with a weight $\omega_{ik} = \max(1[\hat{z}_i=k], \nu)$, where $\nu \in [0,1)$ is a small leak coefficient. This ensures that even non-assigned clusters receive a small positive weight, regularizing small clusters and stabilizing learning without sacrificing distinct reward structure. The decoder $\psi_{dec_k}$ is updated by minimizing the weighted Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss over all users: $\sum_{i=1}^N \omega_{ik} \sum_{m=1}^{M_i} \text{BCE}(\sigma(\hat{R}_{\psi_k}(\tau_{i,m})), y_{i,m})$. This leaky EM procedure is theoretically shown to perform coordinate ascent on a restricted-family Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), guaranteeing monotone improvement and convergence.

**Cluster-wise Policy Optimization:**
After the leaky EM converges, a set of $K$ cluster-specific reward models is obtained. For each cluster $k$, a policy $\pi_k^*$ is trained using the learned reward model $\hat{r}_{\psi_k^*}$. The authors relabel a shared offline transition pool for each cluster and use Implicit Q-learning (IQL) to train one policy per cluster.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Environments:** D4RL MuJoCo locomotion environments (HalfCheetah, Ant, Hopper, Walker2d).
- **Trajectory Data:** Offline trajectory pools created by mixing random, medium, and expert datasets from D4RL.
- **User Modeling:** Scripted labelers were used, defining users by a preference weight vector $w_i$ over a three-dimensional trajectory feature vector $\phi_e(\tau)$ (e.g., postural stability, directional consistency, speed tracking). Binary labels $y_{i,m}$ were generated by thresholding $s_i(\tau) = w_i^T \phi_e(\tau)$.
- **Noise Injection:** Noise was injected into labels based on an adaptation of the B-Pref (Lee et al. 2021) scheme for single-segment binary feedback, including stochastic, myopic, mistake, skip, and equal preference noise types, at two intensity levels.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate PREC's ability to cluster users, improve social welfare under sparse and noisy feedback, and validate the contribution of its components.

**Preference-coherent User Clustering (Section 4.3):**
PREC was evaluated on its ability to recover preference-coherent user groups. With 30 users and 50 feedback samples per user across four environments, PREC's clustering quality was measured using the Silhouette (SH) score and Calinski–Harabasz (CH) index in the ground-truth preference-weight space.
- **Performance:** PREC consistently exhibited higher SH and CH scores compared to baseline methods (Random, K-Means, MLP, K-Means (Axis), W-K-Means (Axis)) across varying numbers of clusters (K=2, 3, 5). This indicates that PREC formed more compact and better-separated clusters that aligned with underlying user preferences.
- **Comparative Advantage:** Notably, PREC outperformed baselines like K-Means (Axis) and W-K-Means (Axis), which directly utilized ground-truth preference features. The authors attributed this to PREC's joint optimization of user assignments and cluster-level reward models, which allows for pooling feedback from similar users and more stable clustering under sparse data conditions, unlike baselines that perform individual estimations before clustering.

**Social Welfare under Sparse and Noisy Feedback (Section 4.4):**
The social welfare achieved by policies trained with PREC was compared against existing user alignment paradigms under sparse (50-100 feedback samples/user) and noisy conditions. Evaluated methods included a single pooled policy (Pooled), fully individualized policies (Indiv), and multi-user approaches adapted from other domains (RLDHP, IdEmb, VPL). User utilities were summarized using Utilitarian, Egalitarian (worst-off 30%), and Nash welfare metrics.
- **Overall Superiority:** PREC variants (PREC(2), PREC(3), PREC(5)) generally achieved the highest social welfare across all metrics and environments in the oracle setting. PREC(3) and PREC(5) were particularly consistent in achieving peak performance.
- **Robustness to Noise:** These performance trends largely persisted when noise was injected into the human feedback, confirming PREC's robustness against common human labeling errors.
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - **Individualized Approaches (Indiv, IdEmb, VPL):** These methods struggled under sparse feedback. Individualized models or embeddings were undertrained or underidentified due to insufficient labels, leading to lower social welfare compared to PREC.
 - **Pooled/Aggregated Approaches (Pooled, RLDHP):** While providing broad data support, these methods generally yielded lower social welfare than PREC. The single shared policy (Pooled) struggled to reconcile conflicting preferences from diverse users, particularly impacting egalitarian welfare as feedback diversity increased. RLDHP, while improving over Pooled by latent-space regularization, still performed below PREC, which benefits from explicit user partitioning.
- **Conclusion:** PREC demonstrated an ability to deliver higher social welfare than both single-shared policy and per-user individualized approaches in sparse and noisy data regimes, balancing efficiency and equity.

**Ablation Study (Section 4.4 - Table 2):**
An ablation study investigated the contribution of the SPR encoder and leaky EM by evaluating variants of PREC.
- **Contribution of Components:** Full PREC consistently outperformed variants where the SPR encoder was replaced with an MLP encoder (w/o SPR), where leaky EM was replaced with hard EM (w/o leaky EM), or where both components were disabled. This indicates that both the self-supervised population-level SPR encoder and the leaky EM algorithm contribute to PREC's overall performance.

**Analysis of the Effective Operating Regime (Appendix C.3):**
Further analysis explored how PREC's performance varied with the per-user feedback budget.
- **Sparse Feedback Advantage:** In sparse feedback regimes (10 to 50 labels per user), PREC significantly outperformed both Pooled and Indiv policies across social welfare metrics.
- **High Feedback Scenario:** As the per-user feedback budget substantially increased (e.g., 500 labels per user), the fully individualized (Indiv) approach showed monotonic improvement and eventually surpassed PREC in utilitarian and Nash welfare. This suggests that with abundant data, full individualization can be optimal for aggregate satisfaction.
- **Practical Trade-off:** However, the authors emphasize that even with abundant data, the practical constraints of managing and validating numerous individualized policies in robotics make PREC a viable trade-off, offering competitive social welfare with a limited policy set.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, PREC, that offers a practical and principled solution to a key challenge in the deployment of preference-aligned robotic systems. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Bridging the Gap in User Alignment:** PREC effectively bridges the gap between rigid single-shared policies and unscalable fully individualized policies. By learning a compact set of representative reward models, it enables deployers to choose the desired granularity of user alignment, balancing personalization with deployability and regulatory compliance. This is particularly relevant for applications like assistive mobility devices and exoskeletons, where both safety regulations and individual comfort are critical.

- **Robustness to Real-World Data Challenges:** The framework's design directly addresses the prevalent issues of sparse and noisy human preference data. The decoupled representation learning with a self-supervised SPR encoder insulates the foundational feature extraction from label noise, while the leaky EM algorithm stabilizes clustering and reward learning even when individual users provide limited or imperfect feedback. This robustness makes PREC more suitable for real-world robotics deployment scenarios.

- **Improved Social Welfare and Equity:** The consistent outperformance of PREC across utilitarian, egalitarian, and Nash welfare metrics demonstrates its capacity to not only maximize average user satisfaction but also to improve outcomes for less satisfied users. This emphasis on preserving preference diversity and catering to sub-populations can lead to more inclusive and ethically sound AI systems, mitigating concerns about the "tyranny of the crowd-worker" or the neglect of minority preferences.

- **Methodological Innovation:** The introduction of leaky EM, theoretically grounded as a coordinate ascent on a restricted-family ELBO, provides a novel and stable mechanism for joint clustering and reward learning. This approach can be a valuable contribution to the broader machine learning community dealing with learning from heterogeneous, sparse, and noisy labels.

- **Scalability for Deployment:** By offering a manageable number of policies (K), PREC significantly reduces the validation and management burden for robot deployers. This increased scalability can accelerate the adoption of preference-aligned robots in commercial and medical applications.

- **Foundation for Future Work:** The authors' stated plans to extend PREC to real human preferences and physical robots suggest a direct path towards tangible real-world applications. Successful deployment on physical platforms could demonstrate the practical efficacy of this approach in diverse robotic contexts.

In summary, PREC offers a practical, robust, and theoretically supported approach to human preference alignment in robotics, providing a pathway for the broader and more equitable deployment of AI systems in real-world settings.
