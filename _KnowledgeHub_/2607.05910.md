# PolicyShiftGuard: Benchmarking and Improving Policy-Adaptive Image Guardrails

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces POLICYSHIFTBENCH, a new benchmark to evaluate image guardrails' ability to adapt to dynamic moderation policies, and proposes PolicyShiftGuard, a two-stage trained model for this challenge. PolicyShiftGuard-7B achieved state-of-the-art performance on the benchmark, with an average F1-score of 76.9 and a Policy Shift Score (PSS) of 72.1, while also reducing inference latency by approximately 1.6x.

## Problem

- Existing image guardrail benchmarks treat safety as an intrinsic property, failing to account for policy-dependent content acceptability and dynamic moderation rules.
- Current multimodal models struggle to adapt decisions for the same image when policies change, lacking fine-grained policy interpretation and generalization to novel policy definitions.
- There is a lack of comprehensive policy-conditioned training data and specific metrics to measure a model's capability to revise decisions based on policy shifts.

## Method

- Introduces **POLICYSHIFTBENCH**, a benchmark structured with risk categories, moderation scenarios, and ==compositional policy bundles==, featuring the new ==Policy Shift Score (PSS)== metric.
- Proposes **PolicyShiftGuard**, an end-to-end framework with an ==auditable data curation pipeline== and a ==two-stage training recipe==.
- The training comprises ==Randomized Policy SFT (RP-SFT)== for robust policy instruction following and ==Boundary-Pair Policy Adaptation (BP-Adapt)== — a ==boundary-pair loss== — to explicitly enhance policy-sensitive decision-making.

## Results

- **PolicyShiftGuard-7B** achieved state-of-the-art performance on POLICYSHIFTBENCH with an average **F1-score of 76.9** and an average **Policy Shift Score (PSS) of 72.1**, significantly outperforming its base model.
- The model demonstrated robust transferability to external safety benchmarks (UnSafeBench, SafeEditBench) and improved the latency-performance trade-off, reducing inference time for the **7B** model from **273.3 ms** to **163.9 ms**.
- Ablation studies confirmed the effectiveness of Randomized Policy SFT, the superiority of concise 'no-think' output over explicit reasoning, and the critical role of the Boundary-Pair Loss in achieving policy-adaptive capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generic safe/unsafe content recognition is distinct from policy-shift sensitivity; models can detect risky content but fail to adapt decisions when policies change for the same image.
> - Model scale alone does not fully resolve policy adaptation challenges; explicit training is required to precisely bind visual attributes to actively defined policy boundaries.
> - The difficulty of policy adaptation varies significantly across risk categories, with nuanced categories like regulated goods or IP proving more challenging than visually salient ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of multimodal safety, particularly concerning image guardrails, has been an area of active research due to the increasing deployment of multimodal systems. Traditional approaches to image guardrailing have typically focused on identifying unsafe content based on a fixed safety taxonomy, implicitly treating safety as an inherent property of an image. This perspective is reflected in many existing benchmarks such as UnsafeBench, MM-SafetyBench, VSCBench, and FigStep, which primarily assign a single safety label to each image under a static set of rules. While these benchmarks are valuable for evaluating general unsafe content detection, they do not adequately address the dynamic nature of real-world content moderation.

In practical deployments, the acceptability of an image is often policy-dependent, varying across different products, age groups, regions, advertisers, or regulatory frameworks. For instance, an image might be permissible under one product's guidelines but restricted by another's, or its status might change if policy boundaries evolve. Some recent efforts, such as LLaVA-Guard and SafeEditBench, have begun to explore policy-conditioned evaluations, but these are often limited in scope, lacking comprehensive compositional runtime policies, public policy-conditioned training data, or specific metrics designed to measure a model's ability to adapt to same-image policy shifts.

Existing guardrail methods, including specialized models like Llama Guard, GuardReasoner-VL, SafeGuard-VL-RL, QwenGuard, and ShieldGemma, as well as general-purpose multimodal large language models (MLLMs), demonstrate limitations in policy-adaptive behavior. They may identify risky visual cues but often fail to adjust their decisions when the same cue is allowed under one policy but blocked under another. Furthermore, their performance can degrade substantially when policy definitions shift beyond familiar settings.

This work addresses these limitations by introducing a new benchmark and a corresponding methodology explicitly designed for "policy-adaptive image guardrailing." It aims to bridge the gap between static safety assessments and the dynamic, policy-driven requirements of real-world content moderation by evaluating whether models can accurately interpret and apply changing runtime policies to visual content.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are twofold: to establish a robust evaluation framework for policy-adaptive image guardrailing and to develop a model that effectively navigates policy shifts.

**Motivation for a New Benchmark:**
The motivation for developing a new benchmark, PolicyShiftBench, stems from the observed shortcomings of existing evaluation methods. Current visual safety benchmarks often treat image safety as an intrinsic, fixed property. This approach leads to several problems in real-world applications:
- **Policy Inflexibility:** Models trained and evaluated under fixed policies struggle when deployed in environments with varying or evolving moderation guidelines. The same image might be deemed acceptable in one context (e.g., medical research) but unacceptable in another (e.g., family-friendly social media).
- **Lack of Fine-Grained Policy Following:** Existing evaluations do not sufficiently test a model's ability to precisely interpret and apply fine-grained policy rules. Models may detect general "unsafety" but fail to distinguish between nuanced policy boundaries that permit or block specific content attributes based on context.
- **Absence of Policy Shift Measurement:** There is a lack of benchmarks that specifically measure a model’s capability to *revise* a decision for the same image when the governing policy changes. Existing metrics primarily focus on overall accuracy or F1-score under a single policy, not on the model’s sensitivity to policy alterations.
- **Limited Training Data and Scenarios:** Publicly available policy-conditioned training data that is grounded in realistic moderation scenarios and encompasses compositional policies is scarce.

**Motivation for a New Model (PolicyShiftGuard):**
The motivation for proposing PolicyShiftGuard is to address the limitations of current guardrail models in handling policy adaptivity:
- **Brittleness Under Policy Shift:** Existing MLLMs and specialized guardrails often perform poorly when policy definitions shift beyond their initial training distributions, leading to inconsistent or incorrect decisions.
- **Inefficient Policy Integration:** Current models may not effectively integrate textual policy descriptions with visual evidence, instead relying on learned priors about "safe" or "unsafe" images.
- **Auditable Decisions and Latency:** Real-world guardrails require not only accurate decisions but also concise, auditable outputs with low latency, which many complex reasoning-based models struggle to provide efficiently.

**Key Objectives:**
1. **Introduce POLICYSHIFTBENCH:** To create a comprehensive benchmark that evaluates policy-adaptive image guardrailing by structuring visual safety into distinct risk categories, moderation scenarios, and compositional policy bundles. This benchmark is designed to specifically test a model's ability to adapt to runtime policies and generalize to held-out policy definitions, incorporating a new metric, Policy Shift Score (PSS), to quantify this adaptability.
2. **Propose PolicyShiftGuard:** To develop an end-to-end framework for policy-adaptive image guardrails, featuring an auditable data curation pipeline and a two-stage training recipe (Randomized Policy SFT and Boundary-Pair Policy Adaptation), aimed at improving policy-sensitive performance and optimizing the latency-performance trade-off.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **POLICYSHIFTBENCH** as a new evaluation framework and **PolicyShiftGuard** as a two-stage training methodology to address policy-adaptive image guardrailing.

#### 3.1. POLICYSHIFTBENCH: Benchmark Structure and Data Curation

- **Benchmark Structure:**
 - **Risk Categories and Scenarios:** The benchmark organizes visual safety into seven distinct risk categories (e.g., Nudity, Violence, Regulated Goods, IP, Cultural Sensitivity, Privacy, Text-in-Image Safety). These categories are intersected with five broad moderation scenarios (e.g., Mainstream Social, Child-Safe, Medical Reference, Journalism, Creative Freedom) to instantiate 28 policy variants.
 - **Policy Definition:** A specific policy is defined as the intersection of a risk category and a scenario. For example, a "Medical Nudity" policy might allow exposed nudity in medical contexts, while a "Family-Safe Nudity" policy would block it universally. This structure allows the same image attribute vector to yield different acceptability labels based on the active policy.
 - **Policy Bundles:** For evaluation, a comprehensive "runtime policy bundle" is composed by sampling one policy variant from each of the seven categories. The model's task is to interpret the aggregate constraints of this bundle.
 - **Evaluation Sets:** The benchmark includes two main evaluation sets:
 - **Adaptive Split:** Contains 1,000 instances over 130 images and 16 policies, evaluating policy following under policy families similar to those seen during training.
 - **Shift Split:** Contains 1,000 instances over 152 images and 12 held-out policies, testing generalization to entirely new policy definitions.
 - **Policy-Discriminative Instances:** The benchmark emphasizes instances where the image and target category are fixed, but changing the active policy causes the correct decision to flip from "pass" to "block" or vice versa. This design ensures that models must adapt to the policy rather than relying on generic unsafe content recognition.

- **Data Curation:**
 - **Attribute-Level Annotation:** The data curation separates visual perception from policy judgment. Images are first annotated at an "attribute" level (e.g., `Has_Exposed_Genitalia`, `Is_Medical_Educational`, `Has_Real_Modern_Weapons`). These attributes are atomic facts about the image, not moderation decisions, serving as triggers or exemptions for policy rules.
 - **Multi-Modal Annotators and Majority Vote:** For each image, multiple multimodal annotators predict these category-specific boolean attributes. Predictions are aggregated by majority vote to produce a structured metadata record for each image.
 - **Deterministic Labeling:** Labels are constructed deterministically by applying executable policy rules (f_c,p) to the voted attributes. A final image-level label is assigned as "unsafe" if any active category policy blocks the image, and "safe" only if all active policies pass it. This method ensures auditable labels, where a label flip is directly attributable to a specific change in policy logic.
 - **Training Corpus:** The released training corpus contains 9,816 policy-conditioned instances, including Randomized Policy SFT (RP-SFT) examples and Boundary-Pair Policy Adaptation (BP-Adapt) boundary-pair examples, with training images disjoint from evaluation images.

- **Policy Shift Score (PSS):** A new metric introduced to measure a model's ability to handle same-image policy flips. PSS rewards a model only when it correctly handles both the "pass" and "block" decisions for the same image under different policies within a target risk category. It is a macro-average over valid groups of policy-flip pairs.

#### 3.2. PolicyShiftGuard: Two-Stage Training Method

PolicyShiftGuard is trained using a two-stage recipe:

- **Stage 1: Randomized Policy SFT (RP-SFT):**
 - **Objective:** To teach the guardrail model to follow full policy bundles and produce concise, structured decisions (e.g., "true | c" or "false").
 - **Data:** Uses policy-conditioned SFT data with images disjoint from evaluation splits. Policies follow the same schema as the Adaptive branch.
 - **Methodology:** The model is optimized via supervised next-token prediction. A key element is randomization (ρ) of policy presentation, including policy order, surface identifiers, and category-slot assignment. This randomization prevents the model from relying on superficial cues (e.g., fixed policy positions) and forces it to robustly interpret the supplied policy bundle and bind visual evidence to the active rule.

- **Stage 2: Boundary-Pair Policy Adaptation (BP-Adapt):**
 - **Objective:** To explicitly enhance the model's ability to adapt to new policies by focusing on boundary-crossing examples.
 - **Data:** Uses a separate post-training source of "boundary pairs" where the image and visual evidence are fixed, but the active policy changes the correct label (one policy permits, one blocks). This data is independent of both Adaptive and Shift evaluation branches.
 - **Methodology:** Fine-tunes the RP-SFT checkpoint with a composite loss function:
 - **Cross-Entropy (L_CE):** Trains the exact answer string.
 - **Label Loss (λ_l L_label):** Separates the correct safe/unsafe prefix for each prompt.
 - **Category Loss (λ_w L_cat):** Stabilizes the violated category ID on blocked examples.
 - **Pair Loss (λ_p L_pair):** This is the core component, defined as `max(0, m - (s_θ(true | q+) - s_θ(true | q-)))`. It ensures that the model assigns a higher unsafe score to the blocking policy (`q+`) than to the passing policy (`q-`) for the same visual evidence. This directly optimizes the model's ability to discriminate between nuanced policy boundaries.
 - **Models:** PolicyShiftGuard-3B and PolicyShiftGuard-7B are compact guardrail models, initialized from Qwen2.5-VL backbones.

#### 3.3. Experiment Settings

- **Evaluated Models:** A wide range of models are evaluated, including general-purpose MLLMs (Qwen3.5, Qwen2.5-VL variants), specialized guardrails (Llama Guard-4-12B, GuardReasoner-VL, SafeGuard-VL-RL-7B, QwenGuard-7B, ShieldGemma2-4B), and closed-source MLLMs (Claude-Sonnet-4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini-3-Flash-Preview). PolicyShiftGuard-3B and -7B are compared against these.
- **Benchmarks:** Primary evaluation is on POLICYSHIFTBENCH (Adaptive and Shift Splits), with cross-benchmark generalizability assessed on UnSafeBench and SafeEditBench.
- **Metrics:** Accuracy, F1, and Policy Shift Score (PSS) on POLICYSHIFTBENCH; F1 on external benchmarks; and inference Time (ms) to assess latency.
- **Ablation Studies:** Conducted to analyze the impact of RP-SFT, no-think mode training, the boundary pair loss, and loss-weight configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded several findings regarding policy-adaptive image guardrailing capabilities of various models and the effectiveness of PolicyShiftGuard.

#### 4.1. PolicyShiftGuard Performance

- **Substantial Improvement on POLICYSHIFTBENCH:** PolicyShiftGuard demonstrated significant advancements in policy-adaptive performance. The 7B model achieved an average F1-score of 76.9 and an average Policy Shift Score (PSS) of 72.1, representing the state-of-the-art performance among all evaluated systems on POLICYSHIFTBENCH. This indicates a substantial improvement over its base model, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, which scored 20.6 Avg. F1 and 4.8 Avg. PSS.
- **Strong Transferability:** PolicyShiftGuard also exhibited robust generalizability to external safety benchmarks. The 7B model obtained the best overall score across UnSafeBench, SafeEditBench, and the Adaptive/Shift splits of POLICYSHIFTBENCH. The 3B model was the second-best overall.
- **Improved Latency-Performance Trade-off:** The concise output format adopted by PolicyShiftGuard contributed to a reduced inference latency. The 7B model's latency decreased from 273.3 ms (base Qwen2.5-VL-7B) to 163.9 ms, while simultaneously improving both Avg. F1 and PSS. PolicyShiftGuard occupies a favorable Pareto region, offering faster inference than many closed-source and reasoning-heavy models while achieving higher average performance. Specifically, PolicyShiftGuard-7B surpassed Gemini-3-Flash-Preview in both Avg. F1 and PSS, with significantly lower latency.

#### 4.2. Findings on PolicyShiftBench

- **Finding 1: Distinction Between Generic Recognition and Policy Shift Sensitivity:** The results indicate that ordinary safe/unsafe content recognition and policy-shift sensitivity are distinct capabilities. Several models achieved non-trivial average F1-scores but very low average PSS values (e.g., GuardReasoner-VL-3B with 59.2 Avg. F1 but only 3.2 Avg. PSS). This suggests these models can often detect visually risky content but inconsistently revise their decisions when the governing policy changes for the same image, highlighting the gap PSS is designed to expose.
- **Finding 2: Limitations of Scaling Alone:** While larger general-purpose MLLMs generally performed better, model scale alone did not fully resolve the policy adaptation challenge. For instance, Qwen2.5-VL improved from 20.6 Avg. F1 at 7B to 49.4 Avg. F1 at 72B, but its Avg. PSS only reached 27.4. This suggests that POLICYSHIFTBENCH requires models to bind visual attributes to actively defined policy boundaries, beyond mere generic visual recognition or increased model size.
- **Finding 3: Category-Dependent Difficulty:** The difficulty of policy adaptation varied across risk categories. Visually salient risks like nudity and violence were generally easier for many models, as unsafe evidence is often directly recognizable. Conversely, categories such as regulated goods, IP and brand safety, privacy and PII, and text-in-image safety presented greater challenges, requiring more nuanced attribute extraction and precise adherence to policy exceptions. This supports the approach of constructing labels from explicit image attributes and executable policy rules.
- **Finding 4: Importance of Speed-Performance Pareto:** The evaluation underscores the importance of jointly considering latency and safety performance for guardrails. Closed-source models often demonstrated high accuracy but were slow, while some open models were fast but weak. Reasoning-style guardrails frequently incurred high latency without commensurate policy-shift sensitivity. This finding suggests that for deployment-facing guardrails, both policy sensitivity and response time are critical evaluation criteria.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study Results

- **Randomized Policy SFT (RP-SFT):** This technique consistently improved performance over standard SFT in Stage 1, enhancing Avg. F1 by 1.2 points for the 3B model and 7.2 points for the 7B model. However, randomization alone did not consistently improve policy-adaptive capability, as seen by a decrease in Shift PSS for the 7B model, indicating it reduces reliance on fixed policy order but is insufficient for robust adaptation to held-out policies.
- **Thinking Mode vs. Concise Supervision:** Training with a "thinking mode" (generating explicit reasoning outputs) consistently underperformed the concise "no-think" setting in both training stages. No-think mode yielded substantial improvements in Avg. F1 (+4.3 for 3B and +8.9 for 7B in Stage 1, further increasing to +5.6 for 3B and +12.3 for 7B in Stage 2). This suggests that direct optimization of final decision tokens is more effective for policy-adaptive guardrailing than free-form rationale generation.
- **Boundary-Pair Loss (L_pair):** Removing the L_pair component from the BP-Adapt stage substantially reduced both Avg. F1 and Avg. PSS for both 3B and 7B models. This confirms that the performance gains are not solely from additional post-training data but specifically from explicitly teaching the model to distinguish between matched pass/block decisions for the same image under different policies.
- **Loss Weight Stability:** The BP-Adapt method demonstrated stability across varied loss weights (λ_w, λ_p, λ_l), with Avg. F1 and Avg. PSS fluctuating only mildly. This suggests that the improvement primarily arises from the boundary-pair training formulation itself rather than requiring precise hyperparameter tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of multimodal safety and content moderation, with significant implications for both research and practical deployment.

- **Addresses a Critical Gap in Evaluation:** The introduction of **POLICYSHIFTBENCH** is a significant step towards a more realistic and comprehensive evaluation of image guardrails. By focusing on policy adaptivity rather than fixed safety definitions, the benchmark addresses a critical limitation of previous evaluation frameworks. Its design, incorporating compositional policy bundles, fine-grained attribute-based labeling, and held-out policy definitions, allows for a more rigorous assessment of a model's ability to interpret and apply dynamic moderation rules. The Policy Shift Score (PSS) provides a dedicated metric to quantify this crucial capability.

- **Advances Policy-Adaptive Guardrail Technology:** The proposed **PolicyShiftGuard** model and its two-stage training methodology represent a substantial advancement in building robust and adaptable image guardrails. The combination of Randomized Policy SFT for robust policy instruction following and Boundary-Pair Policy Adaptation for explicit optimization of policy-sensitive decisions enables the model to effectively navigate complex policy shifts. This approach demonstrates a clear performance advantage over existing general-purpose MLLMs and specialized guardrails, particularly in handling the nuances of policy variations.

- **Shifts the Paradigm for Guardrail Assessment:** The findings advocate for a fundamental shift in how multimodal guardrails are evaluated. Instead of solely focusing on generic safe/unsafe content recognition, the research emphasizes the importance of evaluating whether models can accurately ground their decisions in the *specific policy being enforced*. This reorientation highlights the necessity for guardrails to be policy-aware and context-sensitive, mirroring the complexities of real-world content moderation.

- **Enhances Practical Deployability:** PolicyShiftGuard's compact model size, improved latency-performance trade-off, and concise output format (e.g., "true | XX" or "false") are critical for practical deployment. Guardrails often operate on the critical path of content processing, and the ability to make fast, auditable decisions without sacrificing accuracy significantly increases their utility in real-time moderation pipelines. The strong transferability of PolicyShiftGuard to external safety benchmarks further underscores its robustness for diverse applications.

- **Informs Future Research Directions:** This work opens several avenues for future research. These include expanding the policy catalog to encompass more diverse industries, jurisdictions, and cultural norms; developing models capable of understanding multilingual or legally formatted policies; and extending policy adaptivity to more complex modalities such as video, audio, or multi-turn conversational contexts, which may require advanced temporal reasoning and dialogue-state tracking. The study also implicitly encourages further exploration into the cognitive processes that enable humans to make policy-adaptive decisions, potentially informing new model architectures or training paradigms.
