# PAINT: Partner-Agnostic Intent-Aware Cooperative Transport with Legged Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich developed PAINT, a hierarchical learning framework enabling legged robots to infer partner motion intent solely from joint-level proprioceptive signals during cooperative transport. This approach facilitates stable locomotion on uneven terrains and robustly scales to diverse partners, varying payload masses, and multi-robot teams with zero-shot transfer, outperforming baselines in tracking and interaction compliance.

## Problem

- Existing cooperative transport systems often rely on expensive and fragile external Force-Torque (FT) sensors for intent inference, which are prone to noise from locomotion.
- Most prior intent-aware robot transport systems are validated primarily on flat, structured terrains, lacking robustness and stability on uneven ground while under load.
- Scaling cooperative transport to larger, heterogeneous multi-robot teams is challenging due to dependencies on centralized control, motion capture, or predefined configurations.

## Method

- A hierarchical learning framework (**PAINT**) is proposed, decoupling intent understanding (High-Level policy) from terrain-robust locomotion (pre-trained Low-Level backbone).
- The High-Level policy's 'student' network, incorporating an intent estimator, infers partner interaction wrenches purely from arm joint proprioceptive histories, eliminating external sensors.
- A teacher-student training scheme with KL divergence regularization distills knowledge from a privileged teacher policy (with ground-truth wrench) to the deployable student, enabling robust behavior under partial observability.

## Results

- Real-world experiments demonstrated robots reliably infer partner intent and perform stable cooperative transport over uneven terrains (e.g., stairs, gravel) with varying payloads and partners.
- PAINT achieved lower linear and angular tracking errors and higher intent alignment compared to baselines across 2 kg, 4 kg, and 8 kg payloads in quantitative comparisons.
- The framework successfully scaled to decentralized multi-robot teams (2-4 robots) transporting payloads up to 28 kg (in simulation) and 8 kg (in real-world), without centralized planning or explicit state sharing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Partner intent for cooperative transport can be reliably inferred from purely joint-level proprioceptive signals, eliminating the need for external force-torque sensors and enhancing robustness to sensor noise and fragility.
> - A hierarchical control structure effectively combines terrain-robust locomotion with intent-aware response, allowing legged robots to maintain stability and compliance on challenging uneven terrains.
> - The framework enables partner-agnostic and payload-agnostic cooperation, demonstrating zero-shot generalization to varying payload masses, human/robot partners, and scaled multi-robot teams through local proprioceptive intent inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is increasingly focused on human-robot collaboration (HRC), particularly in tasks such as collaborative transport. This area holds applications in factory automation, construction, and field operations, where robots can potentially reduce human physical workload. A core challenge in cooperative transport is enabling robots to infer a partner's motion intent through physical interaction while simultaneously maintaining stable locomotion, especially in complex and unstructured environments.

Existing approaches to cooperative transport broadly fall into model-based and learning-based categories. Model-based methods, which often leverage impedance or admittance control, prioritize compliance and are effective under reliable state observability. However, their reliance on accurate physical models can limit adaptability to varied contact dynamics. Learning-based approaches offer greater tolerance to contact variability and can adapt to complex payload-coupled dynamics, facilitating richer coordination behaviors.

A common limitation across many intent-aware transport systems is their dependence on external sensors, such as Force-Torque (FT) sensors, to estimate interaction intent. While informative, FT sensors can be expensive, fragile, and susceptible to noise induced by locomotion, making them less suitable for deployment in high-impact or high-wear environments. Researchers have explored lightweight alternatives that infer intent from joint states and body dynamics, but these often require extensive training or struggle with robustness across diverse terrains.

Furthermore, much of the prior work has been validated primarily on flat, structured laboratory terrains, with limited exploration of robustness on uneven ground, where maintaining stable locomotion under load is critical. The majority of studies also focus on single human-robot pairs, and scaling these interaction mechanisms to larger, heterogeneous multi-robot teams remains an active area of research. Current multi-robot systems often rely on centralized control, motion capture infrastructure, or predefined team configurations, which restrict portability and flexibility in field deployment.

This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a framework that leverages purely proprioceptive feedback for intent inference, aiming for a lightweight and robust solution that functions across diverse terrains, partners, and team sizes, without relying on external FT sensors or explicit payload pose tracking. The focus on legged robots aligns with the increasing interest in their robust locomotion capabilities on uneven terrain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a control pipeline that enables a robot to interpret and follow a leader's motion intent during collaborative transport. This intent is specifically defined as the force and torque that the leader applies to the payload to guide its movement. The work aims to facilitate partner-agnostic collaboration, meaning the robot should be capable of interacting with various leaders, including humans and other robots, and in heterogeneous teams of flexible sizes. A crucial aspect of the objective is maintaining robust locomotion over uneven terrains while simultaneously inferring and responding to the partner's intent. Additionally, the proposed pipeline is intended to be lightweight and easily deployable, minimizing system overhead for practical applications.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges identified in the existing literature:

1. **Difficulty in Capturing and Modeling Motion Intent:** Accurately capturing and modeling motion intent across diverse environments and with heterogeneous human-robot teams is complex. Many current systems rely on external FT sensors for intent estimation, but these sensors are often expensive, fragile, and prone to noise from locomotion, hindering their practical deployment in real-world scenarios like factories or construction sites.
2. **Limited Robustness on Uneven Terrain:** Most existing intent-aware frameworks have been validated primarily on flat, structured laboratory environments. Their performance and robustness on uneven terrains, where stable locomotion under load is a significant challenge, have not been sufficiently explored. A practical system requires the robot to maintain both stable movement and accurate intent response in such conditions.
3. **Challenges in Multi-Robot Scaling:** Previous studies predominantly focus on single human-robot transport pairs. Extending these interaction mechanisms to larger teams introduces additional complexities. Current multi-robot transport systems often depend on centralized tracking, motion capture infrastructure, remote control, or predefined team configurations, which limit their scalability and portability for infrastructure-free deployment, particularly in outdoor or field settings with flexible team sizes or heterogeneous partners.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a hierarchical learning framework that infers partner intent directly from joint-level proprioceptive signals, aiming to bypass the need for external sensors and provide a scalable, terrain-robust solution for collaborative transport with legged robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces PAINT (Partner-Agnostic INTent-aware cooperative transport), a hierarchical learning framework designed for cooperative transport using only joint-level proprioceptive signals. The framework decouples intent understanding from terrain-robust locomotion through a two-level control architecture: a High-Level (HL) transport policy and a Low-Level (LL) locomotion backbone.

**A. Hierarchical Framework:**

1. **High-Level (HL) Policy:** This policy is responsible for inferring partner intent and generating appropriate motion commands.
 - **Student HL Policy:** In real-world deployment, the student policy operates with partial observability, receiving only joint proprioceptive histories (arm joint positions, velocities, and previous arm joint position commands) over a short history window (H steps).
 - **Output:** The HL policy outputs a planar base motion command for the LL policy (linear velocities in x/y and yaw rate) and target arm joint positions.
 - **Teacher HL Policy (Training Only):** During training in simulation, a "privileged" teacher policy has access to both proprioceptive histories and ground-truth interaction wrench data (force in x/y, torque in yaw) applied to the payload. This teacher learns an admittance-like mapping from interaction wrenches to arm and base motion commands.

2. **Low-Level (LL) Policy:** This policy is a pre-trained, velocity-tracking locomotion backbone.
 - **Function:** It robustly executes the planar base motion commands received from the HL policy, maintaining robot stability on diverse uneven terrains.
 - **Input:** Locomotion proprioception (leg joint data, predicted external wrenches, base twist, local height scan) and the base command from the HL policy.
 - **Output:** Base joint position commands (leg actions).
 - **Status During HL Training:** The LL policy is kept fixed to simplify the optimization process for the HL policy.

**B. Intent Estimator in HL Student Policy:**

A core component of the student HL policy is an intent estimator network.
- **Purpose:** To infer the unobservable interaction wrench at the payload directly from the accessible arm proprioceptive histories.
- **Design:** The estimator takes the arm proprioceptive observation history (o_trans) as input and outputs an estimated interaction wrench vector, comprising estimated planar force and yaw torque. This estimated wrench is then concatenated with o_trans and fed as input to the actor network of the student HL policy.
- **Training:** In simulation, where ground-truth interaction wrench is available, the intent estimator is trained using a regression loss that minimizes the squared difference between the estimated and ground-truth forces and torques. This process equips the student policy with physically meaningful interaction cues.

**C. Teacher-Student Training with KL Regularization:**

A teacher-student training scheme is employed for the HL policy to enhance deployability and response to partner intent.
- **Privileged Teacher:** The teacher policy is trained first using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), having full access to ground-truth interaction wrench.
- **Deployable Student:** The student policy is then trained, using only proprioceptive observations and the output of the intent estimator. Its objective combines a standard reinforcement learning (RL) expected return term (J_RL) and a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence distillation term (J_KL). The KL term encourages the student's action distribution to imitate the teacher's, effectively distilling the teacher's knowledge into the student policy, which operates under partial observability. An asymmetric actor-critic structure is used during training, where the critic has access to additional privileged observations (e.g., end-effector/payload position, velocity, acceleration) to improve value estimation.

**D. Interaction Wrench Generation in Simulation:**

To provide diverse and smooth interaction cues during training, a time-varying, piecewise-linear force and yaw torque profile is applied to the payload in simulation. This profile includes ramp-up, hold, and ramp-down phases, avoiding impulsive transients and simulating physically plausible partner guidance. Zero-wrench segments are also introduced probabilistically to encourage compliant behavior.

**E. Task Objective and Reward Design:**

The reward function guides the learning process to achieve compliant transport and stable payload handling.
- **Primary Task Rewards:** Exponentially weighted rewards are designed to align the robot's base linear velocity and yaw rate with the desired motion indicated by the interaction wrench (scaled by coefficients B_force and B_torque, analogous to admittance control gains).
- **Stability Penalties:** Penalties are applied for payload height deviations outside a safe range to ensure stable transport and prevent excessive tilting or dropping.
- **Regularization Terms:** Additional regularization terms penalize joint torques, joint velocity and acceleration (DoF penalty), action changes, and arm posture deviations from a default configuration, promoting smooth and consistent robot behaviors.

**F. Implementation Details:**

Policies are trained in Isaac Gym using parallel environments on a single GPU. Payload mass is randomized (0-10 kg) to improve generalization. Domain randomization is applied to proprioceptive observations and interactive wrenches to enhance robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer. Both teacher and student HL policies, as well as the intent estimator, utilize three-layer MLP networks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both qualitative and quantitative evidence to support the effectiveness of the PAINT framework across various scenarios.

**A. Real-World Transport Across Diverse Situations:**
- **Proprioceptive Intent Inference:** Real-world experiments demonstrated that the robot can reliably infer partner intent using only arm proprioceptive histories, without the need for external FT sensors or explicit payload tracking (Fig. 3a).
- **Terrain Robustness:** The learned HL policy generated smooth planar base commands that the LL locomotion backbone tracked robustly, enabling stable cooperative carrying over uneven terrains such as stairs and gravel, despite terrain-induced perturbations.
- **Payload and Partner Agnosticism:** The method remained effective across variations in payload geometries and weights, and maintained stable cooperative transport when partnering with different leaders, including humans and a humanoid robot. This supports the framework's partner-agnostic and payload-agnostic capabilities.

**B. Quantitative Studies Across Different Payload Masses:**
- **Performance Comparison:** Quantitative results (Table II) compared PAINT-4 (4-step proprioception history) against baselines like Damped Arm, Pure RL-4, BC Distillation-4, and Force Estimator-4 across payload masses of 2 kg, 4 kg, and 8 kg. PAINT consistently achieved lower linear and angular tracking errors, indicating better alignment with commanded motion.
- **Constraint and Alignment Metrics:** PAINT generally produced lower end-effector constraint forces and torques, suggesting more compliant interaction, and exhibited stronger intent alignment (higher cosine similarity between commanded force and end-effector velocity direction) compared to most baselines.
- **Intent Estimation Accuracy:** While Force Estimator-4 sometimes achieved competitive FT estimation accuracy, PAINT-4's overall tracking performance was often superior, suggesting that the teacher-student distillation process contributed to better policy execution beyond just accurate intent estimation.
- **Effect of Temporal History:** Ablation studies demonstrated that temporal history is critical for performance under partial observability. Reducing history steps degraded both tracking and interaction performance, while longer histories (e.g., PAINT-8 compared to PAINT-4) generally led to further improvements in robustness metrics.

**C. Scaling to Multi-Robot Teams:**
- **Decentralized Coordination:** The same trained transport policy was successfully deployed on teams of 2 to 4 legged robots. These multi-robot teams demonstrated the ability to jointly transport large and heavy payloads (e.g., a sofa, a desk, a barrel) in simulation and an 8 kg long box with a human leader in the real world (Fig. 3b).
- **Payload Scalability:** Despite being trained in a single-agent setting with payloads up to 10 kg, the framework maintained high intent alignment for multi-robot teams carrying payloads up to 28 kg (Fig. 5), indicating generalization to higher payload masses and team sizes not seen during training.
- **Local Intent Inference:** This coordination was achieved without centralized planning or explicit state sharing, with each robot inferring the team-level intent solely from its individual proprioceptive histories, leveraging payload-coupled interaction signals.

**D. Scaling to Different Robot Embodiments:**
- **Transferability:** The PAINT framework was transferred to a different robot embodiment, specifically an ANYmal without an arm. In this configuration, the HL policy took proprioceptive signals from leg joints as input.
- **Robust Transport:** Both the ANYmal with an arm and without an arm achieved robust cooperative transport in simulation and real-world trials over rough and stepping terrains (Fig. 3c). This suggests that the intent estimator can infer interaction wrenches from proprioception of either arm or leg joints.
- **Unseen Partner Profiles:** The measured wrench data closely tracked synthetic human-like interaction wrench profiles generated by an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process (Fig. 6), indicating that the pipeline can facilitate smooth and compliant collaboration with unseen partner interaction profiles.
- **Heterogeneous Teams:** Real-world experiments also demonstrated heterogeneous multi-robot transport, with two different robot embodiments jointly carrying an 8 kg long box with a human leader.

**E. Saliency Analysis of Intent Estimation:**
- **Dynamic Attention:** A gradient-based saliency analysis revealed how the intent estimator dynamically shifts its attention across individual arm joints under varying interaction modes (Fig. 4). Different joint channels showed higher sensitivity to specific force or torque components (e.g., shoulder rotation for y-direction force, flexion joints for x-direction force, forearm rotation for yaw torque).
- **Temporal Lag:** A consistent temporal lag was observed between the onset of external interaction and the emergence of salient responses, suggesting an integration period for the estimator to accumulate joint-evolution patterns before forming a stable intent representation. This supports the use of proprioceptive histories.
- **Decentralized Scaling Insight:** The saliency analysis offers insight into the successful scaling to multi-robot teams, implying that each robot can infer team-level interaction cues locally by leveraging distinct attention patterns on payload-coupled proprioceptive signals.

**F. Failure Cases and Limitations:**
- **Lack of Active Obstacle Avoidance:** The framework's primary limitation is its lack of explicit perception or planning capabilities for active obstacle avoidance. The HL policy passively follows the leader's motion, which can lead to collisions and locomotion failure if the leading partner does not provide sufficient corrective guidance in environments with obstacles such as walls or narrow structures (Fig. 7).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PAINT framework represents a significant contribution to the field of collaborative robotics, particularly for legged robots engaged in transport tasks.

**Significance:**
- **Lightweight and Efficient Deployment:** By inferring partner intent purely from joint-level proprioceptive histories, the framework eliminates the reliance on external force-torque sensors or explicit payload tracking. This design choice results in a lightweight, cost-effective, and robust system that is less susceptible to sensor fragility and noise, simplifying deployment in demanding real-world environments.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The hierarchical learning architecture, combining a terrain-robust low-level locomotion backbone with a high-level intent-aware policy, enables stable cooperative transport across diverse and challenging terrains (e.g., stairs, gravel). The system's ability to operate with varying payloads, diverse partners (human and robot), and scale to multi-robot teams of flexible sizes, often with zero-shot transfer, demonstrates a high degree of generalization.
- **Decentralized Multi-Robot Coordination:** The successful extension to decentralized multi-robot transport, where each robot infers team-level intent locally from its own proprioceptive signals without centralized planning or explicit state sharing, provides a scalable paradigm for collaborative tasks involving multiple agents.
- **Teacher-Student Learning for Partial Observability:** The teacher-student training scheme, coupled with an intent estimator, effectively addresses the challenge of partial observability, allowing a deployable student policy to learn rich responses to partner intent from limited sensory input.
- **Scalability Across Robot Embodiments:** The demonstrated transferability to different robot embodiments by swapping the locomotion backbone, and the ability to infer intent from either arm or leg joints, indicates the framework's modularity and broad applicability.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Wider Adoption of Legged Robots:** The capabilities demonstrated by PAINT could accelerate the adoption of legged robots in a broader range of collaborative applications, particularly in unstructured or semi-structured environments such as construction sites, logistics warehouses, or field operations, where uneven terrain and dynamic conditions are common.
- **Reduced Human Workload:** By enabling robots to reliably assist in transporting heavy or unwieldy objects, the framework has the potential to significantly reduce the physical workload and associated risks for human workers, improving occupational safety and efficiency.
- **Flexible and Adaptable Robotic Teams:** The partner-agnostic and multi-robot scalable nature of PAINT facilitates the formation of more flexible and adaptable robotic teams. This could lead to dynamic team compositions that can be rapidly reconfigured to suit specific task requirements, without extensive re-programming or specialized infrastructure.
- **Foundation for Advanced Collaborative Behaviors:** The research provides a foundation for future advancements in collaborative robotics. The insights into proprioceptive intent inference could inform the development of more complex interaction modes, such as full 6D wrench inference, semantic-aware intent representations, more dexterous arm movements for payload reorientation, and integrated collaborative grasping and lifting functionalities, thereby making robot assistants more versatile and capable.
