# Parkour in the Wild: Learning a General and Extensible Agile Locomotion Policy Using Multi-expert Distillation and RL Fine-tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich and NVIDIA Switzerland developed a unified locomotion policy for quadrupedal robots, enabling them to perform agile parkour-like maneuvers across diverse terrains using direct depth image perception. The policy, trained through a multi-stage learning process involving expert distillation and reinforcement learning fine-tuning, successfully transfers from simulation to real-world ANYmal D robots, demonstrating robust navigation over obstacles such as stairs, narrow beams, and gaps without requiring specialized controllers.

## Problem

- Traditional legged robot controllers struggle with generalization, often failing when deployed in diverse, new environments not specifically optimized for.
- Existing systems frequently rely on precise terrain reconstruction, which is computationally expensive, prone to errors, and limits performance during rapid motion.
- Integrating multiple locomotion skills (e.g., jumping, climbing) into a single, unified controller remains complex, often requiring explicit switching between specialized policies.

## Method

- A multi-stage ==reinforcement learning== pipeline is proposed, beginning with training specialized expert policies for nine distinct terrain types in simulation.
- Knowledge from these multiple expert policies is distilled into a single foundation policy using DAgger, transitioning perception input from elevation maps to direct depth images.
- The distilled policy is then fine-tuned with reinforcement learning on a diverse set of terrains, including new environments and real-world 3D scans, to improve generalization and discover novel strategies.

## Results

- The fine-tuned policy achieved 80-**95%** success rates across various complex terrains, including those unseen during expert training, demonstrating high generalization capabilities.
- The learned policy successfully deployed on an ANYmal D quadrupedal robot, enabling parkour-like maneuvers across diverse real-world obstacles, including stairs, narrow beams, and gaps.
- The approach effectively uses depth images with a custom noise model for sim-to-real transfer, allowing robust navigation at 50Hz on onboard robot hardware while actively adapting body posture for improved perception.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly using depth images as perception input eliminates the need for precise state estimation and terrain mapping, enhancing robustness during agile movements.
> - A multi-stage learning approach, combining expert skill training, multi-expert distillation, and reinforcement learning fine-tuning, can produce a unified policy that outperforms individual experts and generalizes to unseen terrains.
> - Starting reinforcement learning fine-tuning from a distilled policy significantly accelerates learning compared to training from scratch, allowing emergent behaviors beyond expert demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Legged robots have the potential to navigate diverse and challenging terrains that wheeled vehicles cannot access, making them valuable for applications like search and rescue, inspection, and exploration. However, developing control systems that allow these robots to move with agility and adaptability across varied environments remains a significant challenge in robotics.

The paper "Parkour in the Wild: Learning a General and Extensible Agile Locomotion Policy" by Nikita Rudin, Junzhe He, Joshua Aurand, and Marco Hutter from ETH Zurich and NVIDIA Switzerland presents a novel approach to developing a unified locomotion controller for quadrupedal robots. This controller enables robots to perform parkour-like maneuvers across diverse terrains and obstacles without requiring specialized controllers for each scenario.

### 2. The Challenge of Agile Locomotion

Traditional approaches to legged locomotion face several limitations:

1. **Generalization challenges**: Controllers optimized for specific terrains often fail in new environments.
2. **Perception limitations**: Many systems rely on precise terrain reconstruction, which is prone to errors and computationally expensive.
3. **Skill integration difficulty**: Combining multiple locomotion skills (jumping, climbing, etc.) into a unified controller is complex.

Previous work has addressed these challenges using various approaches:

- **Model Predictive Control (MPC)**: Uses physics models to predict and optimize future motion, but struggles with contact dynamics and uncertain terrain.
- **Terrain-specific controllers**: Provides high performance in specific environments but lacks generalization.
- **Skill libraries with high-level selectors**: Combines multiple specialized controllers but requires explicit switching between skills.

### 3. Multi-Stage Learning Approach

The authors propose a three-stage approach to develop a general locomotion policy:

#### 3.1. Expert Skill Training

The first stage involves training specialized expert policies for nine basic terrain types:
- Walking on flat ground
- Climbing up steps
- Climbing down steps
- Jumping over gaps
- Crouching under obstacles
- Jumping over low walls
- Walking on stepping stones
- Crossing narrow beams
- Climbing over boulder piles

Each expert is trained using reinforcement learning (RL) in simulation. The experts use elevation maps (height scans) of the terrain and proprioceptive information about the robot's state as inputs, outputting joint position targets.

#### 3.2. Multi-Expert Distillation

The second stage distills knowledge from all expert policies into a single foundation policy using the DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) algorithm. This involves:

1. Collecting trajectories where each expert demonstrates its specialized skill
2. Training a student policy to imitate the actions of all experts across all terrains
3. Introducing depth images as the perception input instead of elevation maps

This transition from elevation maps to depth images is crucial as it eliminates the need for accurate state estimation and terrain mapping, which are often unreliable during fast movements.

#### 3.3. RL Fine-tuning

While distillation creates a unified policy, its performance is typically lower than the specialized experts. The final stage involves fine-tuning the distilled policy using reinforcement learning on a mixed training set that includes:

- All the original terrains used by experts
- New, unseen terrains to improve generalization
- Real-world 3D scans to bridge the sim-to-real gap

During fine-tuning, the policy can discover new strategies that might be more effective than those demonstrated by the experts, particularly for complex scenarios.

### 4. Perception with Depth Images

A key innovation in the approach is using depth images directly as input to the locomotion policy rather than relying on terrain elevation maps. This has several advantages:

1. **No need for precise state estimation**: The robot can perceive obstacles directly from its onboard sensors.
2. **Ability to see overhanging obstacles**: Unlike elevation maps, depth images capture 3D structure.
3. **Robustness to occlusions**: The system can handle partially obscured terrain.

To ensure successful sim-to-real transfer, the authors developed a detailed noise model that simulates real-world depth camera limitations:

The noise model includes:
- Edge noise around depth discontinuities
- Missing data (holes) in certain regions
- Camera blind spots
- Gaussian blur to simulate limited resolution

Real-world images undergo parallel processing: clipping extreme depth values, cropping sides, downsampling, and applying Gaussian blur to match the simulation inputs.

### 5. Network Architecture

The locomotion policy uses a neural network architecture with three main components:

1. **Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)**: Processes depth images to extract terrain features
2. **Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)**: Maintains temporal information about the robot's motion
3. **Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)**: Combines proprioceptive data, command inputs, and visual features to produce joint position targets

This architecture enables the policy to:
- Process visual information about the terrain ahead
- Remember past states and actions
- React to user commands like direction and speed

The network was compared against simpler architectures without the LSTM component, showing that temporal memory significantly improves performance, especially on dynamic terrains.

### 6. Distillation and Fine-Tuning Results

The distillation process creates a policy that can imitate all expert behaviors but with reduced performance. The subsequent RL fine-tuning substantially improves this performance:

Key findings from the fine-tuning process:

1. **Initial performance gap**: The distilled policy starts with moderate performance (40-60% success rate) across tasks.
2. **Rapid improvement**: Fine-tuning quickly improves success rates to 80-95% across all terrains.
3. **Transfer learning advantage**: Starting from a distilled policy enables much faster learning than training from scratch.
4. **Emergent behaviors**: The fine-tuned policy discovers new strategies not shown by experts, such as adapting body posture to improve visibility.

The fine-tuned policy demonstrates capabilities beyond what the individual experts were trained for, including successfully navigating terrains that combine multiple challenges.

### 7. Comparison with Alternative Approaches

The authors compared their multi-expert distillation and RL fine-tuning approach against two alternative methods:

1. **Hierarchical controller**: A high-level policy selects between expert controllers
2. **VAE-based skill encoding**: Encodes expert skills in a latent space using variational autoencoders

The results show that:

- The proposed approach achieves highest success rates on both original expert terrains and new unseen environments
- For complex tasks like gap-climbing, hierarchical approaches struggle significantly
- When initialized from scratch, the proposed RL method learns much more efficiently than alternatives

Another experiment compared different neural network architectures for processing perception inputs:

This comparison revealed that while height maps provide slightly better performance when available, depth-based approaches with proper network architectures (CNN-LSTM) can achieve comparable results while being more robust to real-world sensor limitations.

### 8. Real-World Implementation

The learned policy was successfully deployed on the ANYmal D quadrupedal robot in various real-world environments. The robot demonstrated impressive capabilities:

1. **Agile navigation**: Climbing stairs, traversing narrow beams, and leaping over obstacles
2. **Robustness to disturbances**: Handling slippery surfaces, moving platforms, and visual distractors
3. **Active perception**: Adapting body posture to improve visibility of upcoming obstacles
4. **Graceful recovery**: Maintaining balance even when feet get caught in cracks or obstacles move unexpectedly

The policy runs at 50Hz on the robot's onboard computer, processing depth images from a front-facing camera and proprioceptive feedback to generate joint position commands.

### 9. Significance and Future Directions

This work represents a significant advancement in legged robot locomotion for several reasons:

1. **Unified control**: Combines multiple locomotion skills in a single policy without explicit switching
2. **Perception-based navigation**: Uses visual information directly rather than relying on precise mapping
3. **Extensibility**: Demonstrates that new skills can be added through repeated fine-tuning without losing existing capabilities
4. **Real-world applicability**: Successfully transfers from simulation to physical robots in complex environments

The approach has potential applications in:
- Search and rescue operations in disaster zones
- Inspection of hard-to-reach infrastructure
- Exploration of unknown or hazardous environments
- General-purpose mobility for service robots

Future work could extend this approach by:
- Incorporating additional sensors like RGB cameras or lidar
- Adding manipulation capabilities alongside locomotion
- Scaling to even more diverse environments and obstacle types
- Further improving the sim-to-real transfer for more complex behaviors

The authors position their distilled and fine-tuned policy as a foundation model for legged locomotion that can be continuously improved and adapted to new scenarios without starting from scratch for each new task.
