# Learning to Optimize in Model Predictive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> This work from the University of Washington introduces L2O-MPC, a framework for learning the update rule in sampling-based Model Predictive Control, which consistently achieves higher success rates with substantially fewer samples than traditional MPPI across multiple simulated robotics tasks.

## Problem

- Sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) performance degrades significantly with fewer samples due to increased noise in gradient estimates.
- Computational constraints often necessitate a limited number of samples, making robust and efficient MPC challenging for real-time and resource-constrained applications.
- Standard, hand-designed update rules in MPC may not optimally contend with the high noise present in sample-constrained regimes.

## Method

- A learned optimizer, L2O-==MPC==, replaces the standard update rule in sampling-based MPC, trained to make more effective use of limited samples.
- The optimizer receives structured input, including warm-started control distributions and individual trajectory costs from Halton sequence sampling, rather than aggregated noisy gradients.
- Utilizing an architecture inspired by GRUs, the learned optimizer integrates warm-start information with network-generated updates, learning how much of each to incorporate.
- The system is trained through imitation learning using the DAGGER algorithm, mimicking an expert MPC controller operating with a high number of samples.

## Results

- L2O-MPC consistently surpassed the success rates of baseline MPPI across three simulated robotics tasks (CARTPOLE, FRANKA REACHER, FRANKA OBSTACLES), particularly in sample-constrained scenarios.
- On the FRANKA REACHER task, L2O-MPC maintained **100%** success with **4x** fewer samples and achieved **63.33%** success with **16x** fewer samples than standard MPPI.
- For FRANKA OBSTACLES, L2O-MPC exhibited only a **4%** performance drop despite an **8x** reduction in samples and generalized to novel environments with varying obstacle counts, sometimes completing tasks more efficiently.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Applying the 'learning to optimize' framework to MPC update rules, rather than just neural network optimization, can improve control performance under sample constraints.
> - Feeding a learned optimizer individual trajectory costs alongside structured sampling (e.g., Halton sequences) provides more valuable information than aggregated noisy gradients alone.
> - Imitation learning, specifically the DAGGER algorithm, offers an effective method for training these learned optimizers without requiring full differentiability or extensive reinforcement learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a control framework used in various real-world sequential decision-making problems, including robotics applications like autonomous helicopter aerobatics, aggressive off-road driving, manipulation, and humanoid locomotion. Recent theoretical work has unified many popular MPC algorithms under the framework of dynamic mirror descent (DMD), an online learning algorithm. This unification highlights MPC's connection to optimization techniques, providing an avenue for performance improvement.

A growing area of research involves integrating machine learning with MPC. Existing approaches largely focus on:
- **Learning or fine-tuning dynamics models:** This involves using machine learning to create or refine the system's predictive model (e.g.,–).
- **Introducing learned cost-shaping terms:** Modifying the objective function with a machine-learned component (e.g.,).
- **Coupling MPC with learned value functions:** Utilizing learned value functions to guide or improve MPC decisions (e.g.,–).
- **Learning warm-starts for MPC:** Using learned strategies to provide better initial solutions for the MPC optimizer (e.g.,).
- **Operating in learned latent spaces:** Performing MPC with high-dimensional observations, low-level skills, or controls transformed into a more efficient latent representation (e.g.,–).
- **Differentiating through optimal controllers/planners:** Learning components of the optimization pipeline end-to-end by making them differentiable (e.g.,–).

In parallel, the "learning to optimize" framework has emerged, where the update rule of an optimization algorithm is learned by a function approximator, often a neural network, rather than being hand-designed. While much of this work targets optimizing deep neural networks, where large parameter spaces necessitate coordinate-wise optimizers, the application to control presents different challenges and opportunities.

This work distinguishes itself by applying the learning-to-optimize framework specifically to the update rule within sampling-based MPC, explicitly *not* focusing on learning dynamics, costs, or value functions. Instead, it aims to improve the optimization process itself, particularly in scenarios where computational constraints limit the number of samples available, leading to noisy gradient estimates and suboptimal performance. The authors leverage the gradient-based interpretation of sampling-based MPC algorithms to learn a more effective update rule, which is a departure from most existing machine learning applications in MPC.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to learn how to update the control distribution more effectively in sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC), particularly when operating under a limited number of samples. This focus is motivated by a critical trade-off inherent in sampling-based MPC algorithms:
- **Computational Efficiency vs. Performance:** Sampling-based MPC algorithms generate control sequences by sampling from policies, rolling out dynamics to compute an approximate gradient of the objective function, and then using this gradient to update the sampling policy. Complex dynamics or cost functions make each rollout computationally expensive. To reduce computation time, fewer samples can be used.
- **Gradient Noise:** A reduction in the number of samples directly increases the noise in the sample-based gradient approximation. This noise can lead to poorer control performance, as the updates to the control distribution are less accurate.

Therefore, the core motivation is to develop a method that can contend with this noise and maintain or improve performance even with fewer samples. The authors propose to achieve this by:
1) **Learning a better update rule:** Instead of relying on a fixed, manually designed update rule (such as those derived from dynamic mirror descent), they suggest replacing it with a learned optimizer. This learned optimizer is designed to integrate the available information more effectively in sample-constrained regimes.
2) **Leveraging structured sampling:** They argue that simply using the noisy gradient as input to a learned optimizer might discard useful information contained in individual trajectory samples and their costs. By employing structured sampling techniques (specifically Halton sequences), the relationship between control sequences and the current distribution parameters becomes deterministic. This allows the learned optimizer to process individual sample costs directly, providing more comprehensive information than a collapsed gradient vector.
3) **Training via imitation learning:** To avoid the complexities of differentiating through the online optimization process and the high sample complexity of reinforcement learning, they propose using imitation learning. The learned controllers are trained to mimic an "expert" MPC controller that has access to a substantially larger number of samples and thus produces more accurate updates.

In summary, the key objective is to enhance the viability of sampling-based MPC on computationally constrained platforms by learning to make better use of limited, noisy samples, thereby improving performance over standard MPC implementations operating with the same sample budget.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented involves leveraging the dynamic mirror descent (DMD) interpretation of sampling-based MPC to integrate a learned optimizer, trained via imitation learning, into the control loop.

**A. Background: Model Predictive Control and MPPI**
The paper first establishes the context of MPC, framing it as a sequential decision problem where a policy's parameters ($\theta_t$) are optimized over a horizon $H$ to minimize a total cost $C(X_t, U_t)$. Practical MPC approximates this optimization, often using a warm-start ($\tilde{\theta}_t$) from the previous time step. MPC algorithms are then shown to align with the DMD framework, where parameters are updated using a Bregman divergence and approximate gradients of a per-round loss function $\hat{J}(\cdot; x_t)$.

Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control is introduced as a specific instance of DMD-MPC. MPPI utilizes an exponential utility objective and approximates gradients via Monte Carlo sampling and likelihood-ratio derivatives. For non-differentiable cost functions, this results in a weighted sum of control sequence gradients, where weights are determined by the softmax of trajectory costs. The noise in these gradients scales inversely with the number of samples. With a factorized Gaussian control distribution and KL divergence as the Bregman divergence, the MPPI update rule for means ($\mu_{t+h}$) and covariances ($\Sigma_{t+h}$) is derived as a convex combination of the warm-started parameters and a weighted sum of sampled controls/moments.

**B. Learning to Optimize Framework**
The learning-to-optimize framework is adopted, where the iterative update rule $\theta_{t+1} = m_\phi(\theta_t, t)$ is replaced by a learned optimizer $m_\phi$ parameterized by $\phi$. This contrasts with prior work mostly targeting neural network optimization. The authors note that previous work often uses coordinate-wise optimizers due to large parameter spaces, but their application allows for joint optimization of the entire planning horizon.

**C. Design of the Learnable Optimizer**
A key design choice is to provide the learned optimizer with more information than just the noisy gradient. Instead of using the aggregated gradient, the optimizer receives the individual components that contribute to the gradient calculation. Specifically, the input to the optimizer includes the current warm-started mean ($\tilde{\mu}_t$) and covariance ($\tilde{\Sigma}_t$) distributions for the entire horizon, and the costs ($C^{(1:N)}_t$) of the $N$ sampled trajectories. The individual control samples themselves are excluded from the input by leveraging:
- **Structured Sampling with Halton Sequences:** Halton sequences, a low-discrepancy sequence, are used to generate control samples from the Gaussian policies. This deterministic sampling method ensures that all sampled control sequences are a fixed function of the current mean and covariance. Therefore, the optimizer can infer the sampled control sequences from $\tilde{\mu}_t$ and $\tilde{\Sigma}_t$ if it requires them, allowing for a more compact input representation. This enables the learned optimizer to jointly optimize the entire trajectory (i.e., control parameters across all time steps) using only cost information.
- **GRU-Inspired Update Rule:** The standard MPPI update (Equation 12) is a convex combination of previous parameters and weighted samples, resembling a recurrent neural network's hidden state update. Inspired by Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), the authors propose an update rule where the gating terms ($g^\mu_t, g^\sigma_t$) and the proposed updates ($h^\mu_t, h^\sigma_t$) are learned by a neural network:
 $\mu_t = (1 - g^\mu_t) \odot \tilde{\mu}_t + g^\mu_t \odot h^\mu_t$
 $\Sigma_t = (1 - g^\sigma_t) \odot \tilde{\Sigma}_t + g^\sigma_t \odot h^\sigma_t$
 Here, $g^\mu_t, g^\sigma_t$ are passed through a sigmoid function to ensure they are between 0 and 1. The learned optimizer $m_\phi$ outputs $g^\mu_t, g^\sigma_t, h^\mu_t, h^\sigma_t$ based on $\tilde{\mu}_t, \tilde{\Sigma}_t,$ and $C^{(1:N)}_t$. This architecture allows the network to learn how much information from the warm-start (memory) to preserve and how much of its own proposed update to incorporate.

**D. Imitation Learning for Training the Optimizer**
Since the optimization process occurs online and is not assumed to be differentiable end-to-end, and to avoid the high sample complexity of reinforcement learning, imitation learning is chosen.
- **Expert and Learner:** The expert is a standard MPPI controller with an unrestricted (large) number of samples, allowing it to achieve strong performance. The learner is also an MPPI controller, but its standard update rule is replaced by the learned optimizer and it has access to a limited number of samples ($M \le N$, where $N$ is the expert's sample count).
- **DAGGER Algorithm:** Standard behavioral cloning (offline training) was found to be insufficient due to covariate shift between expert and learner distributions. Therefore, the DAGGER (Dataset Aggregation) algorithm is employed. DAGGER is an interactive online learning algorithm that collects corrective labels from the expert on states visited by the learner.
 - **Training Loop:** DAGGER starts with a bootstrapped dataset from expert rollouts. In each iteration, rollouts are performed where the expert's action is applied with probability $\beta_k$ and the learner's action with probability $1-\beta_k$. The expert is always run to provide a target for training. The mixing probability $\beta_k$ decreases over iterations (e.g., $\beta_k = p^k$), gradually increasing the learner's influence.
 - **Data Collection:** For each time step in a rollout, the warm-started control distribution parameters, the trajectory costs (using only the $M$ samples available to the learner), and the expert's updated control distribution parameters are collected. This aggregated data is then used to train the learned optimizer parameters $\phi$.
 - **Network Architecture:** The learned optimizer $m_\phi$ is implemented as a two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with ReLU activations and dropout for regularization. ADAM optimizer is used for training.

This comprehensive approach combines insights from online learning, sampling-based control, and learning-to-optimize, specifically tailoring the input representation and learning algorithm to the challenges of sample-constrained MPC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation was conducted on three simulated robotics tasks: CARTPOLE, FRANKA REACHER, and FRANKA OBSTACLES, comparing the proposed L2O-MPC (Learning to Optimize MPC) against a baseline MPPI controller with the same number of samples. The expert controller, used for training L2O-MPC, had access to a significantly higher number of samples.

**A. Success Rate (Table I)**
- **CARTPOLE:**
 - At 8 samples, both MPPI and L2O-MPC achieved a 100% success rate.
 - As samples decreased to 4, MPPI's success rate dropped to 60%, while L2O-MPC maintained 96.67%.
 - At 2 samples, MPPI achieved 10% success, whereas L2O-MPC still reached 90%.
 - This indicates L2O-MPC's ability to maintain high performance with fewer samples on this task.
- **FRANKA REACHER:**
 - Both controllers achieved 100% success at 64 samples.
 - MPPI's performance significantly declined with fewer samples (e.g., 20% at 8 samples, 3.33% at 2 samples).
 - L2O-MPC showed robustness, maintaining 100% success down to 16 samples, 80% at 8 samples, and 63.33% at 4 samples. Even at 2 samples, L2O-MPC (16.67%) substantially outperformed MPPI (3.33%).
 - L2O-MPC demonstrated resilience against a 4x reduction in samples while maintaining 100% success.
- **FRANKA OBSTACLES:**
 - This task had an inherent upper bound of 80% success due to unfeasible randomly generated scenarios. Both controllers matched this at 512 samples.
 - MPPI's success rate degraded progressively with fewer samples (e.g., 33.33% at 32 samples, 6.66% at 16 samples).
 - L2O-MPC consistently matched or outperformed MPPI. It incurred only a 4% performance decrease (from 80% to 76.67%) with an 8x decrease in samples (from 512 to 64). At 32 samples, L2O-MPC (66.67%) nearly doubled MPPI's performance.
 - These results suggest that L2O-MPC can generalize to novel environments sampled from a similar distribution as the training data, even when those environments contain a different number of obstacles (as shown qualitatively in Figure 1).

**B. Trajectory Statistics for FRANKA OBSTACLES (Table II)**
- **Average Relative Trajectory Length:**
 - At higher sample counts (512, 256), L2O-MPC exhibited slightly longer trajectories than MPPI (1.091 and 1.030 relative length, respectively), suggesting it might be slower to reach the goal.
 - However, at lower sample counts (128 down to 16), L2O-MPC consistently achieved *shorter* relative trajectory lengths (e.g., 0.854 at 16 samples). This is interpreted as L2O-MPC reaching the goal more often and thus completing the task more efficiently than MPPI in sample-constrained regimes.
- **Average Relative Jerk:**
 - The L2O-MPC trajectories generally showed higher average jerk values than MPPI (values greater than 1), indicating less smooth motions. This effect became more pronounced at lower sample counts (e.g., 1.527 at 16 samples).
 - Despite being jerkier, L2O-MPC demonstrated higher success rates at lower sample counts.

**C. Qualitative Observations (Figures 1 and 2)**
- Visual examples of the FRANKA OBSTACLES task confirmed that while MPPI with few samples quickly diverged, L2O-MPC with the same limited samples was able to reach the goal and avoid obstacles.
- L2O-MPC, trained only on environments with two obstacles, demonstrated the ability to handle novel environments with three obstacles, further supporting its generalization capabilities.

In summary, the main findings consistently show that L2O-MPC is capable of making better use of limited samples than standard MPPI. It consistently matches or outperforms MPPI across various sample counts and tasks, demonstrating improved robustness to gradient noise, maintaining higher success rates, and sometimes leading to more efficient task completion in sample-constrained scenarios, albeit with potentially jerkier motions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a novel direction for integrating machine learning into model-based control algorithms by focusing on learning the optimization update rule itself, rather than the more common approaches of learning dynamics, cost functions, or value functions. This orthogonal approach expands the scope of machine learning applications in control.

The key significance of this work lies in its demonstration that:
1. **Improved Performance in Sample-Constrained Regimes:** The learned optimizer (L2O-MPC) effectively contends with noisy gradient estimates that arise from using a small number of samples in sampling-based MPC. It consistently matches or surpasses the performance of a standard MPPI controller operating with the same limited sample budget across diverse robotics tasks.
2. **Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** By enabling effective control with fewer samples, the method directly addresses the trade-off between speed and accuracy in sampling-based MPC. This can lead to faster decision-making cycles and reduce the computational load, which is critical for real-time applications.
3. **Increased Viability for Resource-Constrained Platforms:** The ability to perform well with significantly fewer samples indicates the potential to deploy sophisticated MPC controllers on embedded systems or platforms with limited computational resources, where the high computational demands of traditional sampling-based MPC might be prohibitive. This could broaden the applicability of MPC in robotics and autonomous systems.
4. **Leveraging Structured Information:** The methodology highlights the utility of providing more structured information (individual sample costs rather than aggregated gradients) to a learned optimizer, especially when combined with structured sampling techniques like Halton sequences. This shows that the process of gradient computation can discard valuable information that a learned system might utilize.
5. **Generalization Capabilities:** The empirical results on the FRANKA OBSTACLES task suggest that the learned controllers can generalize to novel environments, even those with variations (e.g., different numbers of obstacles) not explicitly seen during training, provided they are drawn from a similar distribution.

Potential future impacts include the development of more efficient and robust model-predictive controllers for real-world robotic systems. This work opens avenues for further research, such as exploring the use of reinforcement learning to train optimizers that might surpass expert performance, or investigating how to learn better sample generation strategies in conjunction with improved update rules. The methodology offers a foundation for applying a broader range of optimization and machine learning techniques to enhance the core mechanisms of model-based control.
