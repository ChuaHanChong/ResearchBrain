# Demonstrating CavePI: Autonomous Exploration of Underwater Caves by Semantic Guidance

> [!summary] Summary
> The University of Florida's RoboPI laboratory introduces CavePI, an Autonomous Underwater Vehicle designed for autonomous exploration of underwater cave environments. CavePI navigates by tracking semantic visual cues, specifically cavelines, and was validated through extensive laboratory and challenging real-world field trials, demonstrating robust depth control and real-time semantic perception on edge hardware.

## Problem

- Exploring underwater caves is dangerous and challenging for human divers due to confined spaces, low visibility, complex geometries, and the absence of traditional navigation aids like GPS.
- Existing Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) are often too large, costly, or not specifically designed for the unique demands of confined underwater cave environments, such as caveline tracking or operating in extreme low-light conditions.
- A need exists for a portable, cost-effective, and long-endurance AUV capable of autonomous navigation to support scientific exploration in these hazardous, GPS-denied settings.

## Method

- The RoboPI laboratory developed **CavePI**, a compact (8.6 kg, <$3,500) AUV with a 4-degrees-of-freedom motion model, featuring a downward-facing camera for caveline detection and a comprehensive suite of sensors for navigation.
- A semantic guidance system employs a ==DeepLabV3== segmentation head with a ==MobileNetV3== backbone for real-time caveline detection, integrated with a ==PID-based Pure Pursuit controller== for autonomous navigation.
- A digital twin of CavePI was created in Gazebo to facilitate cost-effective pre-mission planning, simulate complex cave environments, and refine control algorithms before physical deployments.

## Results

- CavePI demonstrated robust depth control, maintaining desired depth within approximately **2 cm** error in lab tests and within **±10 cm** in open-water field trials.
- The AUV successfully completed multiple caveline tracking loops in controlled laboratory environments, with the MobileNetV3-DeepLabV3 perception model achieving **48.95% mIoU** at **18.2 FPS** on the Jetson Nano.
- Field trials revealed challenges in strong currents, leading to lateral drift, and in low-light cave conditions, where glare and backscattering impacted semantic perception, though the AUV could maintain tracking for up to **one minute** once manually aligned.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Semantic guidance, particularly caveline tracking, offers a robust and effective navigation strategy for AUVs in GPS-denied and feature-deprived underwater cave environments.
> - Real-time semantic perception for AUV navigation is achievable on resource-constrained edge-AI hardware by using computationally lightweight deep learning models.
> - Thorough validation across laboratory, simulation, and diverse real-world field environments is essential for identifying practical limitations and informing the design of future autonomous underwater systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous underwater cave exploration is a critical yet profoundly challenging frontier in marine robotics. Underwater caves are invaluable for studying hydrogeology, managing water resources (especially in Karst topographies that supply freshwater to a quarter of the global population), understanding archaeological sites, and investigating unique ecosystems. However, these environments are inherently dangerous for human divers due to confined spaces, complex geometries, extreme low-light conditions, and the absence of traditional navigation aids like GPS.

Historically, AUV deployments have largely been constrained to open-water environments where obstacles are minimal. Existing state-of-the-art AUVs like SUNFISH, CUREE, and UX1 have advanced capabilities for marine exploration, often employing sophisticated SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) algorithms based on DVLs (Doppler Velocity Logs) and sonars. However, these platforms often come with significant limitations for cave exploration:
- **Size and Portability:** Many are large and heavy, requiring considerable logistical support for deployment, making them unsuitable for single-person operation or access to tight cave entrances. SUNFISH, for instance, weighs 48 kg, while CavePI is only 8.6 kg.
- **Cost:** High-end AUVs can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars, limiting accessibility for many research groups. CavePI, in contrast, boasts an overall cost of less than $3,500.
- **Specific Design for Caves:** Most AUVs are not specifically designed for the unique challenges of caves, such as navigating along a "caveline" (a guide string left by human divers) or handling extreme low-visibility and overhead obstacles. For example, some AUVs might rely on slow pitch control mechanisms unsuitable for turbulent cave environments, or lack dedicated downward-facing cameras for caveline tracking.
- **Autonomy Level:** While some commercial ROVs (Remotely Operated Vehicles) like BlueROV2 can be modified for autonomous tasks, they fundamentally require significant modifications and are not autonomous by design.

Within the realm of vision-based navigation, prior research has addressed challenges like lighting variability, backscattering, and image degradation in underwater environments through data fusion (visual, acoustic, inertial, depth), shadow-based mapping, and real-time stereo reconstruction. More recently, deep learning approaches have emerged for semantic scene parsing and guidance, such as vision transformers (ViTs) for caveline detection (e.g., CL-ViT, CaveSeg). However, these models often require significant computational resources, limiting their real-time deployment on resource-constrained edge devices common in compact AUVs.

This work addresses these critical gaps by demonstrating CavePI, a novel AUV that prioritizes portability, cost-effectiveness, and autonomous semantic guidance in GPS-denied, low-visibility, and feature-deprived underwater cave environments. By developing a bespoke hardware platform integrated with a computationally efficient yet robust deep visual perception module, CavePI aims to offer a practical, deployable solution that bridges the gap between high-cost, high-capability AUVs and the specific demands of confined cave systems. It also explicitly validates its system in diverse real-world conditions, providing valuable insights into the practical challenges of such deployments, a crucial but often underrepresented aspect in published research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **demonstrate a complete system design and algorithmic integration for semantically guided autonomous underwater cave exploration using a novel AUV, CavePI.**

The motivation for this work stems from several critical needs:
- **Scientific Exploration:** To provide valuable insights into Earth’s least explored ecosystems, advancing knowledge in fields such as archaeology, hydrology, and geology. Underwater caves serve as important records of historical climate and geological events and are vital for monitoring groundwater flow.
- **Safety and Efficiency:** To offer a safer and more efficient alternative to human divers in inherently hazardous underwater cave environments, minimizing risks and improving the precision of data collection and mapping.
- **Addressing AUV Limitations:** To overcome the limitations of existing AUVs (e.g., large size, high cost, insufficient endurance, lack of specific design features like a dedicated down-facing camera) that hinder their effective deployment in confined and challenging cave settings. CavePI is explicitly designed to be single-person deployable, compact, low-cost (under $3,500), and to offer long endurance (4+ hours).
- **Leveraging Semantic Cues:** To develop a robust visual servoing framework that utilizes semantic understanding of the environment, specifically "cavelines" (guide strings) and other navigational markers (arrows, cookies), which are common features in human-explored caves. This semantic guidance provides a 1D retraction of the 3D environment, simplifying navigation.
- **Real-time Performance on Edge Devices:** To design a perception and control system that can run computationally lightweight yet robust deep visual perception modules in real-time on resource-constrained edge-AI hardware, crucial for prolonged autonomous missions.
- **Pre-mission Planning and Testing:** To develop a digital twin of CavePI to support cost-effective pre-mission planning, rigorous testing, and iterative refinement of algorithms in a simulated environment before high-risk physical deployments.
- **Generalizability:** The long-term goal is to generalize the system for exploring any overhead structures (e.g., inside ship hulls, pipelines, dams) that may or may not have predefined markers, expanding the applicability beyond just natural caves.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a comprehensive methodology encompassing hardware design, software integration, perception algorithms, control strategies, digital twin simulation, mechanical analysis, and extensive experimental validation in both controlled and real-world environments.

#### 3.1. CavePI System Design

- **Hardware:** CavePI is a compact, 8.6 kg AUV with a 4-degrees-of-freedom (DOF) motion model for navigating narrow passages. Its core structure is a 6.35mm thick acrylic cylindrical enclosure, rated for 65 meters depth.
- **Sensory Bay:** Equipped with a forward-facing fisheye camera (160° FOV) for diver interaction and future obstacle avoidance, a downward-facing low-light camera (80°x64° FOV) crucial for caveline detection, a Ping2 sonar for altitude, and two onboard lights for low-light conditions.
- **Computational Bay:** Integrates two single-board computers (SBCs): an NVIDIA Jetson Nano for perception (GPU-accelerated processing) and a Raspberry Pi 5 for planning and control. A Pixhawk flight controller bridges software commands to hardware (thrusters, lights) and provides 9-DOF IMU data for attitude estimation. A Bar-30 pressure sensor provides high-precision depth readings. A 14.8V (18Ah) LiPo battery pack offers over 6 hours of endurance.
- **Locomotion Bay:** Features four thrusters arranged to allow control over surge, heave, roll, and yaw motions.
- **Software Middleware:** An end-to-end ROS2 Humble-based framework ensures synchronized operation of all components.

#### 3.2. Semantic Guidance (Tracking by Detection)

- **Perception Task:** Semantic segmentation is chosen over object detection for caveline detection, providing precise pixel-level contours crucial for accurate heading estimation, especially given the caveline's irregular shape.
- **Model Selection:** Considering the Jetson Nano's resource constraints (limited 2GB shared GPU memory), a DeepLabV3 segmentation head is combined with lightweight feature extractors. MobileNetV3 (Large) was initially chosen for its efficiency, achieving 18.2 FPS, but was found slightly insufficient in turbid conditions. ResNet101 was also tested, offering marginal mIoU improvement but significantly lower inference speed (1.4 FPS). The MobileNetV3 backbone was ultimately chosen for real-time performance (3.6 Hz control loop minimum).
- **Dataset and Training:** The open-source CL-ViT dataset was augmented with 150 manually annotated lab images (total 3,300 images). Models were fine-tuned for 50 epochs using Adam optimizer and cross-entropy loss.
- **Model Optimization:** Models are converted to ONNX format and optimized using NVIDIA's TensorRT SDK for fast inference on the Jetson Nano.

#### 3.3. Autonomous Control

- **Visual Servoing:** The core algorithm extracts caveline contours from the segmentation map. The centroid of the farthest detected contour (farthest from the image center in the direction of travel) is identified as the next waypoint. The heading angle (ψ) from the image center to this waypoint guides the robot.
- **Controller:** A PID-based Pure Pursuit controller on the Raspberry Pi 5 uses the calculated heading angle as an error signal to generate precise thruster commands. A separate PID controller uses Bar-30 sensor data to maintain a user-defined target depth.
- **Recovery:** If the caveline is lost, CavePI executes a circular search pattern. If unsuccessful, it signals with flashing lights and navigates toward the last-seen arrow direction (towards the cave exit).
- **Teleoperation:** An optional tether-based teleoperation module allows manual control via joystick.

#### 3.4. CavePI Digital Twin (DT)

- **Modeling:** A detailed URDF model of CavePI is created, replicating its CAD components, links, and joints.
- **Simulation Environment:** Simulated in Gazebo's underwater environment, including Gazebo plugins for sensor emulation (cameras, IMU, pressure, sonar) and realistic environmental forces (buoyancy, thrust, hydrodynamic drag).
- **Simplified Perception:** For DT, computationally lighter edge detection and contour extraction techniques are used for caveline identification due to the lack of real-world perception challenges in simulation.
- **Functionality:** Used for preliminary testing, fine-tuning new control algorithms (e.g., PID parameters), and simulating complex cave environments (narrow passages, dead ends) before physical deployment, ensuring safer and more economical evaluations.
- **VR Interface:** An immersive VR interface allows users to experience virtual missions from egocentric and global perspectives, facilitating interactive simulations and teleoperation.

#### 3.5. Mechanical Stability Evaluation

- **Finite Element Analysis (FEA):** The structural strength of critical components, specifically the custom-designed aluminum dome connector (interface for the forward-facing camera), is evaluated using FEA methods.
- **Parameters:** Tetrahedral elements are used for meshing. The Von Mises yield criterion is applied to assess maximum stresses, total deformation, and factor of safety (FoS) at the maximum operational depth of 65 meters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CavePI spanned mechanical integrity, controlled laboratory experiments, digital twin simulations, and challenging real-world field trials.

#### 4.1. Mechanical Stability

- FEA of the dome connector, with 4.6 million nodes and 3.3 million elements, calculated a maximum equivalent stress of 15.3 MPa at 65m water depth.
- The maximum deformation observed was 0.005 mm.
- The Factor of Safety (FoS) was calculated to be approximately 15.76, significantly exceeding the typical requirement of >2 for hydrostatic applications and >4 for hydrodynamic applications, confirming the dome connector's robust stability for the intended operating pressure.

#### 4.2. Caveline Following Performance (Laboratory)

- **Setup:** Experiments were conducted in a 2m x 3m water tank with varying caveline patterns (rectangular loops, curves, spirals, vertical slopes) at a target depth of 0.35m.
- **Depth Control:** The depth controller demonstrated high robustness, maintaining the desired depth with a mean error of approximately 2 cm, even without trial-specific parameter tuning.
- **Tracking Accuracy:** The tracking error (perpendicular distance from camera optical center to caveline) showed higher deviations during initial depth stabilization and occasional perception failures (e.g., misidentifying tank edges as caveline segments). However, CavePI consistently completed multiple loops, indicating the overall robustness of the control strategy.

#### 4.3. PID Controller Tuning

- Through a grid search method, optimal PID gains were identified for both the heading and depth controllers.
- **Heading Controller:** Kp = 3.4 and Kd = 0.9 resulted in a minimized mean tracking error of 13.3 cm with a standard deviation of 20.0 cm.
- **Depth Controller:** Kp = 600 and Kd = 50 yielded a mean depth error of 1.91 cm with a standard deviation of 1.38 cm.

#### 4.4. ROS Prototyping and Gazebo Simulation

- The digital twin proved invaluable for preliminary testing and control algorithm design, particularly for evaluating buoyancy, thrust, and hydrodynamic drag.
- It facilitated optimal sensor placement and initial PID controller tuning.
- However, the authors noted that final controller tuning had to be performed on the physical system due to complexities like nonlinear drag forces and variations between the CAD model and real hardware, which cannot be perfectly replicated in simulation.
- The VR interface demonstrated an innovative way to conduct interactive virtual missions and allows switching between autonomous and teleoperation modes.

#### 4.5. Field Experimental Demonstration

- **Environments:** 15 open-water trials in shallow spring riverine areas (2-6m depth) with strong currents and turbidity, and 10 trials in deep natural underwater grottos and caves (15-30m depth) characterized by low-light and noisy conditions.
- **Open-Water (Springs):**
 - **Depth Control:** Remained robust, maintaining depth within ±10 cm, similar to lab results.
 - **Heading Control:** Proved less resilient, exhibiting higher tracking errors and significant lateral drift due to strong currents, especially when traveling perpendicular to the flow. This occasionally led to loss of caveline tracking.
 - **Design Influence:** The AUV's slightly back-heavy design induced a pitch-up motion during upstream travel, further compromising stability.
- **Low-Light (Caves/Grottos):**
 - **Hardware Limitation:** These trials used an earlier 3-thruster CavePI iteration (less stable with no dedicated roll control) compared to the current 4-thruster design.
 - **Perception Challenges:** Despite onboard lights, glare and backscattering severely impacted semantic perception. The lightweight segmentation models (MobileNetV3) struggled, leading to misidentification of submerged tree roots, mosses, and other structures as cavelines, and occasional complete failure to detect the line.
 - **Tracking:** While misidentification occurred, CavePI could maintain tracking for up to one minute once manually repositioned onto the correct caveline by support divers.
 - These results underscored the need for more powerful computational platforms and robust segmentation models for truly autonomous cave operations.

#### 4.6. Perception Performance (Onboard)

- **MobileNetV3-DeepLabV3:** Achieved a mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) of 48.95% at an inference rate of 18.2 FPS on the Jetson Nano.
- **ResNet101-DeepLabV3:** Achieved a mIoU of 50.48% but at a significantly slower 1.4 FPS.
- The choice of MobileNetV3 prioritized real-time operation (exceeding the 3.6 Hz control loop requirement) over marginal mIoU improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Demonstrating CavePI" paper marks a significant step forward in autonomous underwater robotics, particularly for challenging confined and GPS-denied environments like underwater caves. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

#### 5.1. Scientific and Environmental Impact

- **Unlocking Access:** CavePI provides a portable, cost-effective, and safe platform for exploring and mapping underwater caves, which are otherwise too hazardous or inaccessible for human divers. This enables critical research in hydrogeology (groundwater flow), archaeology (preserving historical records), and marine biology (studying unique ecosystems).
- **Water Resource Management:** The insights gained from cave exploration, particularly in Karst topographies, are directly applicable to understanding and managing vital freshwater resources.

#### 5.2. Advancements in Marine Robotics

- **Practical System Demonstration:** The paper's primary contribution is the demonstration of a fully integrated, functional AUV specifically designed and rigorously tested for complex underwater cave navigation. This moves beyond theoretical algorithms to a deployable, real-world system.
- **Semantic Guidance for GPS-Denied Environments:** It highlights the effectiveness of semantic guidance (caveline tracking) as a robust navigation strategy in environments where traditional localization methods fail. This approach leverages existing human-laid infrastructure, making initial deployments more feasible.
- **Edge-AI Deployment:** The successful integration of deep learning models on resource-constrained edge devices (Jetson Nano) for real-time perception is a crucial engineering achievement, proving that sophisticated AI can be miniaturized for autonomous operation.
- **Comprehensive Validation:** The extensive validation across laboratory, simulation (digital twin), and diverse real-world field environments (including challenging open-water currents and low-light caves) provides invaluable data. The transparency in reporting both successes and failure modes (e.g., issues with currents, light conditions, perception errors) is vital for the broader research community to identify future research directions.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The availability of the system design, code, and data on GitHub (https://github.com/uf-robopi/CavePI_AUV/) is a major contribution that fosters reproducibility, collaboration, and accelerates further research and development in the field.

#### 5.3. Future Trajectories and Broader Applications

- **Lessons for Future AUV Design:** The identified limitations (e.g., need for better sway control in currents, more powerful compute for perception, improved thruster placement for 6-DOF control, handling backscattering/glare) provide clear roadmaps for the next generation of AUVs.
- **Generalizability of Overhead Exploration:** The long-term vision of adapting CavePI for exploring other overhead structures (ship hulls, pipelines, dams) without predefined markers signifies a significant potential impact beyond caves, applicable to infrastructure inspection, maintenance, and defense.
- **Human-Robot Interaction:** The integration of visual tags for diver-robot interaction, offering a scalable and practical alternative to tethered communication, opens avenues for more flexible human-robot collaborative missions in underwater settings.
- **Advanced Autonomy:** Future work integrating more powerful compute, adaptive control frameworks, multi-modal sensor fusion (360° sonar), advanced SLAM (e.g., SVIn2), and zero-shot semantic understanding will push the boundaries toward truly resilient and safety-critical autonomous operations in unknown or partially mapped environments.

In conclusion, CavePI demonstrates a well-engineered, pragmatic approach to solving a formidable challenge in marine robotics. By proving the feasibility of semantically guided navigation in extreme underwater environments and transparently outlining its current capabilities and limitations, this work not only advances the state-of-the-art but also lays a robust foundation for future autonomous underwater exploration.
