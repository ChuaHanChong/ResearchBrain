# Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning (MaxRL), developed by researchers primarily at Carnegie Mellon University, introduces a sampling-based framework that approximates maximum likelihood optimization for tasks with binary outcome feedback. MaxRL demonstrates superior scaling with increased computational resources, mitigates issues like pass@k degradation in Large Language Models (LLMs), and achieves up to 20x test-time scaling efficiency on mathematical reasoning benchmarks by generating a higher number of correct solutions.

## Problem

- Many modern learning problems (e.g., code generation, multi-step LLM reasoning) rely on binary outcome feedback, implicitly defining a likelihood of success, but direct maximum likelihood optimization is precluded by non-differentiable sampling.
- Standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) is used as a workaround but only optimizes a first-order approximation of the true maximum likelihood objective.
- Conventional RL methods lead to issues like underexploration of hard, low-success inputs and 'distribution sharpening' (pass@k degradation) in LLMs, where solution diversity is lost.

## Method

- MaxRL introduces a ==compute-indexed family of objectives== that smoothly interpolates between standard expected reward and exact maximum likelihood, allowing more faithful approximation with increased sampling compute.
- It proposes a simple, ==on-policy gradient estimator== that averages score functions exclusively from successful trajectories, proving it to be an unbiased estimator for the compute-indexed MaxRL objective.
- The framework employs a ==control variate for variance reduction== by normalizing rewards with the per-task mean, and provides a unifying weight-function view to characterize various RL objectives.

## Results

- MaxRL closely matches cross-entropy performance in image classification and significantly outperforms REINFORCE on maze navigation, demonstrating superior scaling with additional training compute.
- On GSM8K, MaxRL showed greater resistance to overfitting and pass@k degradation, maintaining healthier output diversity and achieving higher peak performance over longer training durations compared to RLOO and GRPO.
- When training Qwen3 models on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, MaxRL consistently Pareto-dominated GRPO, achieving higher pass@1 while simultaneously improving pass@k, leading to up to **20x** test-time scaling efficiency gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Standard RL's objective (expected pass rate) optimizes only the leading (first-order) term of a Maclaurin expansion of the true log-likelihood gradient.
> - The maximum likelihood gradient can be equivalently expressed as the expected score function of a model *conditioned on successful outcomes*.
> - Increasing sampling compute within MaxRL doesn't just reduce variance but directly allows the algorithm to optimize a progressively more accurate approximation of the maximum likelihood objective itself.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself at the intersection of two foundational machine learning paradigms: Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation and Reinforcement Learning (RL). Maximum likelihood is a cornerstone of modern generative and predictive models, known for its robustness and scalability in differentiable settings. Reinforcement learning, conversely, excels in sequential decision-making problems with non-differentiable rewards, enabling agents to learn complex behaviors through interaction with an environment.

The authors identify a critical gap in the current application of RL, especially in "correctness-based" tasks. Many modern problems, such as navigation, program synthesis, structured prediction, and multi-step reasoning in LLMs, ultimately aim to produce a correct output. While these problems implicitly define a likelihood of success, current RL methods are predominantly used as a workaround for the non-differentiability of the success outcome, rather than optimizing a truly principled objective like maximum likelihood. Standard RL algorithms, by maximizing expected reward (or pass rate), are shown to optimize only a "first-order approximation" of the maximum likelihood objective. This leads to issues like:

- **Underexploration of hard problems:** The inverse-probability reweighting inherent in maximum likelihood places greater emphasis on hard, low-success inputs. Standard RL, lacking this reweighting, tends to marginalize learning signals from such challenging instances.
- **Distribution sharpening/Pass@k degradation:** In LLM training, this often manifests as a reduction in the diversity of correct solutions (pass@k degradation) and a tendency for models to become overconfident in a narrow set of solutions, often referred to as "distribution sharpening." This phenomenon has been observed in recent RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR) applications, where LLMs trained with standard RL objectives can lose their ability to generalize or discover novel solutions.

MaxRL directly addresses these limitations by proposing a *fundamentally different objective* for reinforcement learning. Instead of merely tweaking existing RL algorithms (which often differ only in their advantage calculations or off-policy updates), MaxRL introduces a framework to approximate the maximum likelihood objective itself. This places MaxRL within the burgeoning research area focused on improving the objectives and exploration strategies for RL, particularly in the context of LLMs, to achieve better scaling, generalization, and robustness beyond what is achievable with traditional expected-reward maximization. It builds upon ideas from related works that consider non-linear functions of pass rate or the theoretical characterization of RL objectives, but distinguishes itself by providing a concrete, compute-indexed family of objectives with an unbiased estimator, offering a direct path to higher-fidelity likelihood optimization with increasing computational budget.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to introduce a principled, sampling-based framework called Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning (MaxRL) that approximates maximum likelihood (ML) optimization using reinforcement learning (RL) techniques, specifically for settings with binary outcome feedback.

The motivation for MaxRL stems from a critical observation:
1. **Mismatch between Objective and Method:** In many modern learning problems—like navigation, code generation, and multi-step reasoning with LLMs—success is determined by an external verifier after a stochastic generation process, resulting in a binary outcome. From an end-to-end perspective, the model implicitly induces a likelihood over correct rollouts. Maximizing this likelihood would be the principled approach, analogous to cross-entropy in supervised learning. However, the non-differentiability of the intermediate sampling process precludes direct ML optimization.
2. **RL as a Workaround:** Reinforcement learning is typically used in these scenarios not because it offers a superior objective, but because its policy gradient methods can handle non-differentiable sampling.
3. **Suboptimality of Standard RL:** The authors formally demonstrate that standard reinforcement learning, which optimizes the expected pass rate (equivalent to pass@1), only optimizes a *first-order approximation* of the true maximum likelihood objective. The ML objective, specifically its gradient, inherently reweights learning signals to place greater emphasis on *hard, low-success inputs* (via an inverse-probability weighting), a critical aspect missing from conventional RL.

Based on this, the key objectives and contributions of the paper are:
- **Formalize the Gap:** To rigorously show that correctness-based reinforcement learning is a latent-generation maximum likelihood problem, and that standard RL only optimizes its first-order approximation.
- **Bridge the Gap with Compute-Indexed Objectives:** To introduce a family of objectives that smoothly interpolates between standard expected reward (RL) and exact maximum likelihood as more sampling compute is allocated. This family is derived from a Maclaurin expansion of the log-likelihood in terms of `pass@k` events.
- **Develop an Unbiased Estimator:** To derive a simple, on-policy gradient estimator whose expected value exactly matches these compute-indexed objectives. Crucially, this estimator has the property that *increasing sampling compute improves the optimized objective itself*, rather than merely reducing the variance of a fixed objective.

In essence, the motivation is to leverage the robustness of RL against non-differentiability while regaining the principled optimization behavior and desirable properties (like better scaling with data/compute, and improved handling of hard problems) characteristic of maximum likelihood.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MaxRL’s methodology is built on a theoretical insight regarding the relationship between maximum likelihood and reinforcement learning in correctness-based settings, followed by the derivation of a novel gradient estimator.

1. **Formalizing Correctness-Based Tasks:**
 - The paper considers tasks where a model `p_θ(y|x)` generates an output `y` for an input `x`, and a binary reward `r(x,y) = I{y=y*(x)}` indicates correctness.
 - For latent generation models, where `y = f(z)` for `z ~ m_θ(z|x)`, the probability of correctness (pass rate) is `p_pass_θ(x) = E_z[I{f(z)=y*(x)}]`.
 - The Maximum Likelihood (ML) objective is `J_ML(θ) = E_x[log p_pass_θ(x)]`, while the standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) objective is `J_RL(θ) = E_x[p_pass_θ(x)]`.

2. **Maclaurin Expansion and RL's Approximation:**
 - The core theoretical contribution is to show that the log-likelihood `log p_pass_θ(x)` admits a Maclaurin expansion in terms of `fail@k(x) = (1 - p_pass_θ(x))^k`.
 - Differentiating this expansion reveals that the ML gradient `∇_θ J_ML(x)` is an infinite harmonic mixture of `∇_θ pass@k(x)` terms, with `1/k` weighting. This means higher-order `pass@k` events (i.e., multiple successes in `k` trials) contribute to the ML gradient, especially for small `p_pass_θ(x)`.
 - Crucially, the authors demonstrate that standard RL (`∇_θ J_RL(x) = ∇_θ pass@1(x)`) corresponds to retaining *only the leading (first-order) term* of this ML gradient expansion.

3. **The MaxRL Objective Function:**
 - Inspired by this, MaxRL defines a **compute-indexed family of objectives**, `J^(T)_MaxRL(x)`, by truncating the Maclaurin expansion of the log-likelihood to a finite order `T`.
 - When `T=1`, MaxRL recovers standard RL. As `T` approaches infinity, it recovers exact maximum likelihood. This parameter `T` directly controls the "order of correctness events" considered during learning. The key idea is that allocating more sampling compute allows for the estimation of higher-order terms, thus approximating ML more faithfully.

4. **Gradient Estimators for MaxRL:**
 - **Conditional Form of ML Gradient (Theorem 1):** A pivotal insight is that the ML gradient can be expressed as an expectation *conditioned on success*: `∇_θ J_ML(x) = E[∇_θ log m_θ(z|x) | f(z)=y*(x)]`. This means the ML gradient is the average score function *only from successful trajectories*.
 - **Empirical Gradient Estimator:** MaxRL leverages this insight to propose a simple, REINFORCE-style estimator `bg_N(x)`. For `N` sampled trajectories `z_1..., z_N`, it calculates the score function `S_i = ∇_θ log m_θ(z_i|x)` for each. It then averages these scores *only for the `K` successful trajectories* (where `r_i=1`). If `K=0`, the gradient is set to zero.
 - **Estimator-Objective Equivalence (Theorem 2):** A critical theoretical result proves that this empirical estimator `bg_N(x)` is an *unbiased estimator* for the MaxRL gradient `∇_θ J^(N)_MaxRL(x)` with truncation level `T=N`. This means increasing the number of rollouts `N` doesn't just reduce variance, but actually allows the algorithm to optimize a *better approximation of the maximum likelihood objective itself*.
 - **Variance Reduction:** To mitigate the high variance typical of REINFORCE-style estimators (especially when `K` is small), MaxRL employs a control variate by subtracting the unconditional average score, `V_N`. The final advantage calculation normalizes the reward by the *per-task mean reward* (i.e., `K/N`), differing from other common baselines like RLOO and GRPO.
 - **On-Policy Implementation:** The algorithm is presented as a simple on-policy implementation, modifying the advantage calculation in a standard policy gradient framework.

5. **Unifying Weight-Function View:**
 - The paper introduces a conceptual framework where various RL objectives (MaxRL, RL, GRPO, ML) can be characterized by a scalar weight function `w(p)` that determines how learning signal is allocated across inputs of varying difficulty (`p` is the pass rate).
 - MaxRL's `w_T(p)` is shown to interpolate between `w_RL(p)=1` (no reweighting) and `w_ML(p)=1/p` (strong emphasis on hard problems) as `T` increases.
 - This analysis highlights that GRPO's heuristic normalization results in a `w_GRPO(p)` that upweights low-pass-rate inputs (as `1/√p`) but then inverts for very easy inputs (increasing as `p → 1`), unlike likelihood-based objectives.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of MaxRL across diverse settings consistently demonstrates its superior performance and scaling properties compared to existing RL objectives (REINFORCE with RLOO baseline and GRPO).

1. **Comparison with Exact Maximum Likelihood (ImageNet):**
 - In a controlled image classification setting where exact ML (cross-entropy) is computable, MaxRL (with sufficient rollouts, e.g., 1024) closely matches the performance of cross-entropy training.
 - In stark contrast, REINFORCE (even with high sampling budgets) largely fails to achieve meaningful improvements from low initial pass rates.
 - Gradient norm analysis shows MaxRL and cross-entropy concentrate learning signals on harder tasks (low pass rates), whereas GRPO focuses on medium-difficulty tasks and REINFORCE struggles to generate gradients on hard tasks. This confirms MaxRL's theoretical alignment with ML's learning dynamics.

2. **Infinite Data Regime (Procedurally Generated Maze Navigation):**
 - In an environment with 1 million unique mazes, MaxRL demonstrates significantly better scaling with additional training compute (more rollouts per prompt).
 - MaxRL at its lowest compute budget (4 rollouts) often surpasses RLOO and GRPO at their highest budgets (128 rollouts) across all `pass@k` metrics.
 - This indicates that MaxRL more effectively leverages increased sampling to improve model performance, showcasing its efficiency in data-rich, compute-scalable environments.

3. **Data-Scarce Regime (GSM8K Mathematical Reasoning):**
 - Training a SmolLM2-360M-Instruct model on a fixed GSM8K dataset for many epochs, MaxRL exhibits greater resistance to overfitting.
 - While RLOO and GRPO show substantial `pass@k` degradation (loss of output diversity) with prolonged training, MaxRL maintains healthier `pass@k` values, often exceeding the base model.
 - MaxRL achieves a higher peak performance, improving consistently over a longer training duration (up to 50 epochs), whereas competing methods saturate or degrade earlier. This suggests MaxRL fosters more robust and generalizable learning.

4. **Large Reasoning Model Training (Qwen3 on POLARIS-53K and Math Benchmarks):**
 - MaxRL consistently Pareto-dominates GRPO across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME, BeyondAIME, MATH-500, Minerva) for both Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-4B-Base models.
 - It achieves higher `pass@1` performance while simultaneously *improving* `pass@k` (diversity), reversing the `pass@k` degradation commonly observed with GRPO.
 - This leads to significant practical benefits, including up to **20x test-time scaling efficiency gains** when using a perfect verifier to filter solutions, as the model produces a greater number of correct options.

5. **Characteristically Different Optimization Dynamics:**
 - Beyond performance metrics, MaxRL fundamentally alters the optimization process. It generates larger gradient norms for harder prompts (low pass rates) and smaller norms for easier ones, akin to cross-entropy.
 - This translates into MaxRL consistently enabling models to generate at least one correct rollout for a larger fraction of training problems throughout training, demonstrating superior learning signal extraction from challenging tasks.

In summary, MaxRL consistently outperforms existing RL objectives, demonstrates superior scaling with compute and data, mitigates common RL challenges like distribution sharpening, and offers substantial efficiency gains in real-world LLM reasoning applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MaxRL represents a significant advancement in reinforcement learning, particularly for correctness-based tasks and the training of powerful reasoning models like Large Language Models (LLMs). Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Principled Optimization for Non-Differentiable Settings:** MaxRL offers a principled framework to approximate maximum likelihood, the gold standard for many supervised learning tasks, in settings where direct gradient computation is impossible due to non-differentiable sampling. This bridges a long-standing gap between two fundamental ML paradigms, providing a more theoretically grounded approach to problems currently tackled heuristically by RL.

2. **Enhanced Scalability with Compute and Data:** The core innovation of MaxRL is its ability to leverage increased computational resources (more rollouts) to optimize a progressively more accurate approximation of the maximum likelihood objective itself, rather than merely reducing the variance of a fixed objective. This inherent scaling property means that as compute budgets grow, MaxRL-trained models can continue to improve, making it highly relevant for the resource-intensive development of future AI systems.

3. **Mitigation of RL's Current Limitations in LLMs:** MaxRL directly addresses critical issues observed in RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR) for LLMs, such as "distribution sharpening" and "pass@k degradation." By emphasizing learning from harder, low-pass-rate problems and promoting solution diversity, MaxRL enables models to generalize better and retain a broader range of reasoning capabilities. This can lead to more robust, flexible, and human-like AI assistants that are not overly confined to a narrow set of "safe" answers.

4. **Significant Inference-Time Efficiency Gains:** The observed improvement in `pass@k` translates directly into substantial test-time efficiency. For applications with perfect verifiers (e.g., code generation, mathematical theorem proving), MaxRL-trained models can achieve up to 20x speedup by generating fewer samples to find a correct solution. This reduces computational costs and latency in deployment, making advanced AI reasoning more practical and accessible.

5. **Reconceptualizing RL Limitations:** MaxRL's success suggests that some limitations attributed to reinforcement learning with foundation models might stem more from the *choice of objective function* (i.e., optimizing expected reward) rather than inherent flaws in RL optimization or sampling mechanisms. This opens new avenues for research into alternative, more principled objectives for various RL scenarios.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The paper lays a strong foundation and explicitly outlines promising future work, including generalizing MaxRL to continuous or arbitrarily valued rewards, extending it to multi-turn reinforcement learning, and adapting it for off-policy settings (like PPO-style training). Success in these areas could broaden MaxRL's applicability across an even wider range of complex AI tasks, from robotics to complex dialogue systems.

In essence, MaxRL offers a fresh perspective and a powerful new tool for training AI models in scenarios where correctness feedback is binary and non-differentiable. Its ability to scale effectively, mitigate common RL pitfalls, and align more closely with the theoretical desiderata of maximum likelihood could profoundly impact the development of next-generation intelligent systems.
