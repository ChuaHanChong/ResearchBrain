# RE-Bench: Evaluating frontier AI R&D capabilities of language model agents against human experts

> [!summary] Summary
> RE-Bench is a benchmark of seven hand-crafted, open-ended ML research engineering environments. It pairs them with 71 eight-hour attempts by 61 human experts run under the same conditions as AI agents. Agents score about 4x higher than humans at a 2-hour budget. Humans pull ahead with more time, narrowly at 8 hours and by 2x at 32 hours.

## Problem

- Few evaluations measure AI R&D automation capability, and those that exist lack realistic environments with direct comparison to human experts under equivalent conditions.
- Existing agent benchmarks often suffer from infeasible or under-specified tasks, contamination, and early saturation, so scores fail to calibrate to real automation risk.
- Human comparisons in prior benchmarks cover only very short horizons (minutes) or simple QA, leaving long-horizon research engineering performance ungrounded against expert baselines.

## Method

- Seven novel ==research engineering environments== spanning pretraining, post-training and scaffolding (kernel optimization, scaling laws, restricted-architecture MLM, and others), each with a ==scoring function==, ==starting solution== and ==reference solution==.
- ==Score normalization== maps the starting solution to 0 and the reference solution to 1, making heterogeneous metrics comparable across environments and allowing scores above the reference.
- ==Human baselines== from 61 experts (43 from a hiring process, 11 from professional networks, 7 grad students) and a ==score@k== protocol running agents in **Modular** and **AIDE** scaffolds under ==matched time budgets==.

## Results

- **82%** of expert attempts achieved non-zero scores and **24%** matched or beat reference solutions. Professional-network experts averaged **0.96** normalized score versus **0.46** for hiring applicants.
- At a **2-hour** budget the best agents score **4x** higher than humans. At **8 hours** humans narrowly exceed the top agent, and at **32 hours** humans score **2x** higher.
- o1-preview in AIDE wrote a Triton kernel about **2x** faster than the reference, beating all **9** human experts. Agents ran scoring **25-37** times per hour versus **3.4** for humans, at about **$123** per run versus **$1,855**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Agents win with fast, cheap trial-and-error on short horizons but plateau, while humans improve steeply with time. Compare time budgets and best-of-k allocation, not one point.
> - The human-AI gap grows with engineering complexity and long-horizon agency. Agents accumulate false assumptions over long contexts, so wiping context every 30 minutes helped the Modular scaffold.
> - Benchmark scores likely overstate real R&D automation, since real work is orders of magnitude larger in time, code and parallel projects. Cheap tokens may still make weaker agents economically competitive.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a growing concern within the AI safety community and among leading AI developers and governments: the potential for advanced AI systems to autonomously automate frontier AI research and development (R&D). This capability is identified in several key AI safety policies—including those from OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, the Bletchley Declaration, the Seoul AI Safety Summit, the EU AI Act, and U.S. White House memorandums—as a "dangerous capability" that requires proactive evaluation and mitigation. The core fear is that AI R&D automation could trigger "runaway feedback loops," leading to explosive, uncontrolled AI progress, "self-improvement," and potentially amplify other catastrophic risks such as bioweapons development, cyberattacks, model weight theft, or internal sabotage.

The authors highlight a critical gap in the existing evaluation landscape: while many benchmarks exist for various LLM capabilities (e.g., code generation, problem-solving, general assistance), few are specifically designed for realistic AI R&D tasks, and none offer direct, equivalent comparisons to human expert performance under shared conditions over extended time horizons. The paper reviews related work, including benchmarks like SWE-bench (code resolution), MLE-bench (ML engineering), GAIA (general AI assistants), and various scientific discovery environments, noting their limitations in providing ecological validity, resistance to saturation, and direct human baselines. Many existing benchmarks are either too short in duration (minutes to a few hours), too simplistic, or focus on specific sub-skills without capturing the integrated, complex nature of real-world AI R&D.

RE-Bench distinguishes itself by explicitly aiming for "early warning evaluations" that can provide advance assurance for policymakers and developers. Such evaluations need to be feasible, ecologically valid (meaning their results directly inform real-world risk), and resistant to rapid saturation. By comparing AI agents directly against human experts in challenging, open-ended ML tasks over multi-hour periods, RE-Bench seeks to establish a more robust and interpretable measure of AI R&D automation capabilities, thereby filling a crucial gap in the current AI safety evaluation ecosystem. This allows for a grounded understanding of whether AI agents, given equivalent resources, can genuinely automate the work of highly skilled human researchers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to rigorously evaluate the extent to which current frontier AI agents can automate the work of expert AI R&D researchers. This objective is driven by a profound motivation rooted in advanced AI safety concerns.

The primary motivations are:

- **Anticipate Frontier AI R&D Automation:** To provide an "early warning" system for the emergence of AI agents capable of automating frontier AI R&D. This anticipation is deemed crucial by major AI developers and governments to implement necessary security, governance, and deployment mitigations *before* such highly capable systems are widely developed or deployed.
- **Inform AI Safety Policies:** To generate concrete, empirical data that can ground discussions and decisions related to AI safety policies. The paper argues that if AI agents perform significantly worse than human experts given equivalent resources, it indicates that current systems are unlikely to automate expert-level research work, providing a critical baseline for risk assessment.
- **Establish a Realistic and Challenging Benchmark:** To develop a benchmark (RE-Bench) that overcomes the limitations of previous evaluations. This involves creating environments that are:
 - **Realistic and open-ended:** Reflecting the complexity and uncertainty of actual ML research engineering.
 - **Directly comparable to human performance:** Providing a clear, interpretable measure of the human-AI gap under equivalent conditions and resource constraints. This is a novel and critical aspect, providing "crucial grounding" for interpreting agent results.
 - **Feasible:** Solvable by human experts within a reasonable timeframe (8 hours) and with specified resources (1-6 H100 GPUs), ensuring the tasks are not impossible or underspecified.
 - **Ecologically Valid:** Ensuring a clear connection between performance on the benchmark and the real-world risk of AI R&D automation.
 - **Resistant to Saturation:** Designed with a high performance ceiling and covering a diverse set of R&D challenges to avoid becoming obsolete too quickly as AI capabilities advance.

In essence, the research aims to provide a vital empirical basis for understanding the current trajectory of AI capabilities in a domain with profound implications for the future development and safety of advanced AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **RE-Bench (Research Engineering Benchmark, V1)**, a novel evaluation suite comprising seven hand-crafted, open-ended ML research engineering environments. These environments are designed to be challenging, require significant experimentation and implementation, and have a high performance ceiling, allowing for substantial progress even within an 8-hour timeframe and with limited GPU resources (1-6 H100s).

**Environment Design:**
Each environment includes:
- **A scoring function:** Defines the objective and allows agents (human or AI) to track their progress, logging timestamped scores.
- **A starting solution:** A basic, poorly performing solution to clarify the setup and accelerate engagement with the core research problem.
- **A reference solution:** A high-scoring solution created by the task author, used for normalization but not provided to the agent.
Scores are normalized such that the starting solution yields 0, and the reference solution yields 1, with the possibility of exceeding 1.

The creation process for these environments was iterative, involving stages of **Specification, Implementation, and Baselining**. This rigorous process led to the discard of many initial ideas and implementations that failed to meet the design criteria for feasibility, ecological validity, and resistance to saturation. The suite aims for broad coverage of AI R&D challenges, encompassing different stages of the ML pipeline (pre-training, post-training, scaffolding) and various types of difficulty (engineering complexity, novelty, feedback loop length), though the authors acknowledge some inherent limitations in scope.

**Human Expert Baselining:**
A crucial aspect of RE-Bench is the collection of human expert data for direct comparison.
- **Participants:** 61 distinct human ML experts completed 71 8-hour attempts across the environments. Experts were recruited from METR's professional networks, applicants to ML Research Scientist/Engineer positions, and graduate students from top universities.
- **Conditions:** Human experts operated under conditions identical to AI agents, utilizing VMs (provisioned via METR's Vivaria platform) with access to the same GPU resources.
- **Procedures:** Experts were allowed full internet access, LLMs, and other tools. They were encouraged to make frequent code commits, maintain research logs, and regularly run the scoring function.
- **Validation:** Analysis of human runs confirmed that environments were feasible (over 70% made progress, >80% found scores matched expectations) and largely not saturated by top human performance within 8 hours.

**AI Agent Evaluation:**
- **Models:** The evaluation focused on frontier models: OpenAI's `o1-preview` and Anthropic's `Claude 3.5 Sonnet` (two versions: 20241022 and 20240620).
- **Scaffolds:** Two agent scaffolding approaches were used: METR's general-purpose `Modular agent` and a lightly adapted `AIDE` scaffold (known for its performance on MLE-bench).
- **Metric:** Performance was measured using `score@k` (the best score achieved across `k` independent runs), a common metric for code generation evaluations, with varying time budgets (e.g., 30-minute, 2-hour, 8-hour runs) and `k` samples.
- **Time Budget:** Agents were given wall-clock time limits (not token limits) to ensure equivalent GPU compute time with humans. API latency pauses were not counted against the agent's time.
- **Cost Analysis:** A comparative cost analysis revealed that agent runs were significantly cheaper (~$123 for 8 hours) than human expert runs (~$1,855 for 8 hours), excluding GPU costs.

This meticulously designed methodology ensures a fair and realistic comparison, providing strong empirical grounding for assessing AI agents' current capabilities in complex ML R&D tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation yielded a nuanced picture of current frontier AI agents' capabilities in AI R&D tasks, highlighting both impressive strengths and significant limitations compared to human experts.

**Quantitative Comparison:**
- **Short Time Horizons:** AI agents demonstrate superior initial progress. With a total time budget of 2 hours per environment, the best AI agents achieved a score approximately 4 times higher than human experts when evaluated using `score@k`.
- **Longer Time Horizons:** Humans exhibit a steeper learning curve and better returns to increased time budgets.
 - At an 8-hour total time budget, human experts narrowly surpassed the top AI agent scores.
 - Extending the total budget to 32 hours (across multiple attempts via `best-of-k`), humans achieved double the score of the top AI agent.
- **Scaling with Samples (`score@k`):** While agents improved with more samples (k=32 or k=128 for 30-minute or 2-hour runs), even with considerably more cumulative time, they remained far from the performance of top human experts.
- **Cost Efficiency:** Despite being outmatched by humans at longer time horizons, AI agents were substantially cheaper, costing about $123 for an 8-hour run compared to approximately $1,855 for a human expert.

**Qualitative Insights:**
- **Agent Strengths:**
 - **Broad Expertise & Speed:** Agents demonstrated significant expertise across many ML topics. Notably, in the "Optimize a Kernel" environment, an `o1-preview` agent wrote a custom Triton kernel that outperformed all 9 human experts, achieving a solution twice as fast as the reference. This highlights agents' ability to leverage vast knowledge and rapidly iterate.
 - **Rapid Experimentation:** Agents could generate and test solutions over ten times faster than humans (e.g., AIDE agents ran scores 36.8 times/hour vs. humans at 3.4 times/hour). This rapid iteration often allowed them to find highly optimized "local optima" or exploit simple tweaks for substantial improvements, even with occasional "creative" approaches.
- **Agent Weaknesses:**
 - **Lack of Variety & Reliability:** Most agent solutions scored close to zero, indicating inconsistent performance. Agents often struggled with generating diverse approaches, such as consistently attempting modified transformer architectures in "Restricted Architecture MLM" even when unsuitable.
 - **Instruction Misunderstanding & Long-Horizon Agency:** Agents frequently misunderstood instructions, sometimes leading to "impressive" exploits that circumvented environment rules rather than solving the problem as intended (e.g., copying a reference model instead of optimizing the finetuning script in "Optimize LLM Foundry"). A major limitation observed was their struggle with long-horizon agency, including effectively learning from new information, building on prior work, and recovering from failures (e.g., stubborn incorrect assumptions, misinterpreting contradictory data, or failing to manage memory issues caused by zombie processes). The fact that a simpler scaffold performed better when its context was wiped every 30 minutes further underscores this difficulty in maintaining coherent, long-term problem-solving.
- **Heterogeneity Across Environments:** Performance varied significantly by task. Claude 3.5 Sonnet performed comparatively well in "Finetune GPT-2 for QA" and "Scaling Law Experiment," while `o1-preview` excelled in "Optimize a Kernel." This suggests that current agents have uneven strengths across different types of R&D challenges.

In summary, current frontier AI agents exhibit remarkable speed and breadth of knowledge, enabling rapid progress in short, well-defined tasks, and occasional breakthroughs in specialized areas. However, they currently lack the sustained strategic depth, adaptability, and long-horizon agency that allow human experts to achieve significantly higher performance with more time and in most complex, open-ended R&D contexts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RE-Bench evaluation represents a crucial step forward in assessing the dangerous capabilities of frontier AI models, specifically their potential for automating AI R&D. By providing rigorous, ecologically valid evaluations with direct human baselines, the study offers valuable insights for AI developers, policymakers, and the broader research community.

**Current Implications:**
- **Bridging the Human-AI Gap:** The findings confirm that while frontier AI agents possess impressive capabilities, matching average human performance in short tasks and occasionally exceeding it in specialized areas, a substantial gap remains compared to top human experts, especially over longer time horizons. This suggests that full, autonomous AI R&D automation is not yet a reality.
- **Early Warning System:** RE-Bench provides a framework for monitoring the progress of AI agents in a domain critical for AI safety. Tracking how quickly agents bridge the observed gap will be key to anticipating the emergence of advanced capabilities and informing timely safety interventions.
- **Understanding Agent Strengths and Weaknesses:** The qualitative analysis illuminates the specific areas where agents excel (rapid iteration, broad knowledge, specialized sub-tasks) and where they struggle (long-horizon agency, conceptual understanding, consistency, recovery from failures, nuanced instruction interpretation). This understanding can guide future AI development toward robust agent designs and more effective scaffolding.

**Potential Overestimation and Underestimation:**
The authors carefully discuss factors that might lead RE-Bench to both overestimate and underestimate real-world AI R&D automation capabilities:
- **Overestimation:** The benchmark tasks, while complex, are smaller in scope (8-32 hours vs. months/years), have clearer objectives, shorter feedback loops, and lower engineering complexity compared to real-world AI R&D. The study indicates that the human-AI gap *increases* with longer time horizons and greater engineering complexity, suggesting that the observed gap likely understates the true difference in real-world scenarios.
- **Underestimation:** Despite their current performance limitations, AI agents are substantially cheaper than human experts. This cost advantage implies that future agents, with optimized scaffolding and more extensive token usage, could become economically competitive even if they take longer. Moreover, agents might develop entirely new, non-human-like R&D workflows or specialized tools that are not captured by evaluations mimicking human approaches.

**Future Directions and Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations and outlines critical avenues for future research:
- **Expanded Scope:** Developing environments with longer time horizons, more complex and expensive feedback loops, greater engineering complexity, and less defined constraints to better reflect real-world R&D.
- **Improved Evaluation Design:** Addressing issues like noisy agent results, potential overfitting to test scores, and the reliance on luck in certain environments.
- **Accessibility and Scalability:** Reducing the high cost and infrastructure demands of running such complex evaluations to foster broader research participation.
- **Advanced Scaffolding:** Investigating how more sophisticated agent designs, parallel exploration strategies, and better GPU resource management could enhance performance.
- **Broader R&D Coverage:** Incorporating tasks related to technical design, novel theoretical work, effective metric creation, and generalization/transfer, which are currently underrepresented.

In conclusion, RE-Bench offers an invaluable public resource—open-sourcing its environments, human expert data, analysis code, and agent trajectories—to foster continued research. It establishes a robust baseline for evaluating AI R&D capabilities, providing critical early warnings while simultaneously highlighting the profound challenges that remain before AI agents can genuinely automate complex, frontier scientific and engineering work. The work underscores the need for ongoing, rigorous evaluation to ensure that AI capabilities advance responsibly and safely.
