# TRACED: Transition-aware Regret Approximation with Co-learnability for Environment Design

> [!summary] Summary
> TRACED is a framework for Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) that enhances reinforcement learning agent generalization to novel environments. It refines task difficulty approximation by combining value-function loss with a novel transition-prediction error and incorporates a "Co-Learnability" metric to capture cross-task transfer. This approach achieves superior zero-shot generalization and significantly faster training convergence compared to existing UED methods on MiniGrid and BipedalWalker environments.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents frequently overfit to training environments, leading to poor zero-shot generalization to novel, unseen tasks.
- Current Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) frameworks approximate task difficulty (regret) mainly using value-function loss, which is incomplete and does not account for the agent's understanding of environmental dynamics.
- Existing UED approaches often select training tasks independently, failing to explicitly model or leverage the transfer effects and relationships between different tasks.

## Method

- Introduces a "Transition-aware ==Regret Approximation==" (ATPL) that combines traditional Positive Value Loss with a new transition-prediction error from a learned dynamics model.
- Develops a lightweight "Co-Learnability" metric to quantify the observed reduction in difficulty of other tasks after training on a specific task, capturing positive transfer effects.
- Integrates these components into a unified "Task Priority" score, which guides the UED curriculum to select tasks that are both challenging and offer high transfer potential.

## Results

- Achieved superior zero-shot generalization on MiniGrid and BipedalWalker environments, outperforming strong baselines like ACCEL and CENIE, often matching their performance in approximately half the training time.
- Generated curricula that ramped up task complexity significantly faster than baselines, with TRACED introducing challenging BipedalWalker levels (**14.6%**) where ACCEL generated none.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the transition-prediction error (ATPL) and the Co-Learnability metric are essential contributors to the framework's improved performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurate regret approximation in DRL requires considering not only the agent's value estimation errors but also its ability to correctly model the environment's transition dynamics.
> - Explicitly quantifying the "Co-Learnability" between tasks, representing how training on one task impacts the learning or performance on others, can lead to more efficient and generalizable curricula.
> - Combining task-specific difficulty (incorporating dynamics error) with inter-task transfer potential provides a more principled and effective criterion for curriculum generation in UED.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents have demonstrated success in various domains, including games, continuous control, and robotics. A persistent challenge in this field is the generalization of these agents to novel, unseen environments. Traditional approaches often involve hand-crafting diverse training environments, a process that is often intractable given the variability of real-world scenarios. Moreover, agents frequently overfit to their training data, leading to suboptimal performance when encountering out-of-distribution tasks.

Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) emerged as a potential solution to this generalization problem. UED frameworks typically employ a co-evolutionary mechanism where a "teacher" module dynamically generates training tasks, and a "student" agent learns a robust policy from this evolving curriculum. A common strategy within UED involves guiding curriculum design by measuring "regret," defined as the disparity between optimal performance and the agent's current performance on a given task. Early UED methods, such as PAIRED, trained a teacher to maximize regret between protagonist and antagonist policies.

However, accurately computing true regret in complex environments is often infeasible, as it requires knowledge of the optimal value function. Consequently, existing UED methods resort to proxy metrics for regret, such as Positive Value Loss (PVL) or Maximum Monte Carlo (MaxMC) returns. Approaches like Prioritized Level Replay (PLR) and its extension, PLR$^\perp$, store and prioritize replay of tasks with high approximated regret. ACCEL further enhances this by introducing an evolutionary mechanism that mutates high-regret levels to generate progressively more complex challenges. While effective, these methods primarily approximate regret solely through value-function loss. This narrow focus can overlook other factors contributing to an agent's difficulty, such as its ability to model the environment's dynamics.

Beyond regret-based methods, other UED research directions include incorporating environmental novelty (e.g., CENIE, which uses Gaussian Mixture Models to quantify how unfamiliar a new task is) or diversity (e.g., DIPLR, which measures Wasserstein distance between occupancy distributions). Curriculum Reinforcement Learning (CRL) also aims to accelerate training by sequencing tasks, often adapting based on agent progress, but typically assumes access to the target task distribution, a distinction from UED's focus on unknown distributions. Despite these advancements, the explicit modeling of how training on one task influences learning or performance on other tasks—referred to as "Co-Learnability"—has largely been unexplored in UED.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to improve the zero-shot generalization capabilities of deep reinforcement learning agents to unseen environments by developing a more effective and sample-efficient Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) curriculum. The authors identify two main motivations and corresponding technical objectives:

1. **Refine Regret Approximation:** Current UED methods, exemplified by approaches like PLR$^\perp$ and ACCEL, typically approximate task difficulty or "regret" (the gap between optimal and current agent performance) based primarily on value-function loss (e.g., Positive Value Loss - PVL). The authors argue that this approximation is insufficient because true regret is also influenced by how well the agent's learned model predicts the environment's dynamics. A poor understanding of dynamics can hinder an agent's ability to achieve optimal performance, regardless of its value function estimates.
 - **Objective:** To augment the traditional value-function-based regret proxy with a "transition-prediction error" term. This new term is designed to explicitly quantify the mismatch between the agent's learned dynamics model and the true environment dynamics, thereby providing a more comprehensive and accurate estimate of task difficulty. The motivation is that a more faithful regret approximation will lead to the generation of more impactful training tasks, accelerating the student agent's learning process.

2. **Model Cross-Task Relationships and Transfer Effects:** Existing UED methods generally select training tasks based solely on their individual difficulty or regret scores. This approach overlooks the potential for transfer learning, where training on one task might facilitate or impede learning on other related tasks. Understanding these inter-task relationships could enable the teacher module to select tasks that not only challenge the agent but also maximize positive transfer across the broader task space, leading to more robust and generalizable policies.
 - **Objective:** To introduce a lightweight metric, termed "Co-Learnability," that quantifies how training on one task benefits or impacts performance on other tasks. This metric aims to capture the transfer patterns between tasks without incurring significant computational overhead. The motivation is to move beyond individual task difficulty and incorporate a notion of "curriculum utility," prioritizing tasks that foster broader skill development and accelerate learning across a distribution of tasks.

By addressing these two motivations, the authors aim to develop a unified framework that guides curriculum generation more effectively, leading to faster learning and superior zero-shot generalization compared to existing UED methodologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TRACED (Transition-aware Regret Approximation with Co-learnability for Environment Design) builds upon existing co-evolutionary UED frameworks, particularly ACCEL, by introducing two core methodological innovations: a refined regret approximation and a Co-Learnability metric.

#### 3.1. Regret Approximation via Transition Prediction Loss

The work posits that conventional regret proxies, such as Positive Value Loss (PVL), which focus solely on value-function error, are incomplete. To address this, the authors decompose the one-step regret at a state-action pair $(s, a)$ to highlight the "future value gap," which is influenced by discrepancies between the learned dynamics ($\hat{P}$) and the true dynamics ($P$).

1. **Theoretical Motivation:** The one-step regret decomposition (Equation 2 in the paper) shows that the difference between optimal value and the policy's Q-value can be broken down into: (i) value estimation error, (ii) reward gap, and (iii) future value gap. The authors highlight that term (iii) is impacted by both value-function error and the accuracy of the learned transition model. Appendix R provides a theoretical analysis, bounding the dynamics-induced component of the future value gap by the transition loss.
2. **Average Transition Prediction Loss (ATPL):** To explicitly account for the dynamics mismatch, TRACED introduces ATPL. A separate transition dynamics estimator, $f_\phi$, is trained to predict the next state $\hat{s}_{t+1}$ given the current state $s_t$ and action $a_t$. This estimator is implemented as a recurrent model (LSTM-based for MiniGrid, MLP-LSTM for BipedalWalker).
 - The one-step reconstruction loss $L_{trans}(s_t, a_t)$ is minimized during training ($L_1$ loss for MiniGrid pixel observations, $L_2$ loss for BipedalWalker continuous states).
 - ATPL for an episode $\tau$ is defined as the average of these one-step reconstruction losses over the episode: $\text{ATPL}(\tau) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^T L_{trans}(s_t, a_t)$.
3. **Combined Regret Estimate:** The refined regret approximation combines PVL and ATPL: $\text{Regret}(\tau) = \text{PVL}(\tau) + \alpha \text{ATPL}(\tau)$, where $\alpha > 0$ is a scaling factor balancing the two terms. This combined scalar is stored in a Task Difficulty Buffer (TDB) for each sampled task.

#### 3.2. Task Priority Construction

TRACED aims to identify tasks that are both challenging for the agent and offer high transfer potential to other tasks. This is achieved by combining Task Difficulty and Co-Learnability into a single Task Priority score.

1. **Task Difficulty:** Defined as the most recent approximated regret (PVL + $\alpha$ ATPL) for a given task stored in the TDB. If a task has not been sampled, its difficulty is initialized to zero.
2. **Co-Learnability (CL):** This metric quantifies the average reduction in difficulty of *other replayed tasks* when a specific task $i$ is trained upon.
 - $\text{CoLearnability}_i(k) = \frac{1}{|T_{k+1}|} \sum_{j \in T_{k+1}} (\text{TaskDifficulty}(j, k) - \text{TaskDifficulty}(j, k+1))$, where $T_{k+1}$ is the set of tasks replayed at time $k+1$.
 - A positive value indicates that training on task $i$ at time $k$ led to a decrease in difficulty (improvement) for other tasks $j$ replayed at $k+1$. This is a lightweight, observational approximation of transfer effects.
3. **Task Priority:** The Task Priority score combines these two measures: $\text{TaskPriority}(i, t) = \text{Rank}(\text{TaskDifficulty}(i, t) + \beta \text{CoLearnability}(i, t))$.
 - $\beta > 0$ is a scaling factor that controls the trade-off between individual task challenge and its transfer benefits.
 - A rank transform is applied to the combined score, converting raw magnitudes into relative orderings. This mitigates the impact of extreme values and ensures a balanced curriculum. Lower ranks (higher priority) are preferred for sampling.

#### 3.3. Overall UED Workflow

TRACED integrates these components into a UED loop largely adopted from ACCEL, modifying only the task scoring mechanism.

1. **Teacher-Student Interaction:** At each curriculum update step, the teacher either explores a new level from a generator G (with probability $p_{replay}$) or samples a batch of existing levels from a replay buffer $\Lambda$ (with probability $1 - p_{replay}$) based on their `TaskPriority` score (inverse proportionality to rank).
2. **Policy Update and Regret Calculation:** The student agent interacts with the selected levels, and its policy is updated using PPO. After interaction, the `Regret(τ)` for each level is computed using the combined PVL and ATPL, and recorded in the TDB.
3. **Task Mutation:** In the replay phase, a subset of tasks with the lowest regret (easiest tasks for the agent) are mutated by an editor module to create new, potentially more challenging variants. These mutated tasks replace their parents in the buffer.
4. **Buffer Updates:** For all updated or newly generated tasks, `TaskDifficulty` is recomputed. `CoLearnability` is updated after the next curriculum step once subsequent difficulty changes are observed. Finally, `TaskPriority` is updated for all tasks in the buffer.

The process iterates, continually generating and replaying tasks to adapt the curriculum to the agent's evolving capabilities, driving towards robust generalization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TRACED was conducted across two procedurally generated domains: MiniGrid (partially observable maze navigation) and BipedalWalker (continuous control locomotion in challenging terrain). Comparisons were made against several baselines, including Domain Randomization (DR), PLR$^\perp$, ADD, ACCEL (the primary baseline due to shared framework), and CENIE (a state-of-the-art method, for BipedalWalker only).

#### 4.1. Zero-Shot Generalization Performance

- **MiniGrid:** TRACED demonstrated superior zero-shot generalization. At 10,000 PPO updates, TRACED achieved a higher mean solved rate than all baselines evaluated at 20,000 updates (Figure 5). Its Interquartile Mean (IQM) also consistently surpassed other methods, indicating more reliable performance across different runs. TRACED exhibited similar or better transfer performance than ACCEL while requiring approximately half the wall-clock training time (Table 1).
- **PerfectMaze Variants:** On highly complex PerfectMazeLarge and PerfectMazeXL environments, TRACED at 10,000 updates achieved the highest solved rate on PerfectMazeLarge and closely matched the best 20,000-update baseline performance on PerfectMazeXL (Figure 7, Table 13), indicating scalability to extreme task complexities.
- **BipedalWalker:** TRACED delivered consistent gains across all aggregate metrics (median, IQM, mean, optimality gap) on six held-out BipedalWalker terrains. At 10,000 updates, TRACED's performance matched or exceeded that of ACCEL-CENIE (a state-of-the-art method) at 20,000 updates (Figure 8, Table 14). It also showed consistent outperformance over baselines in return progression across various terrains during training (Figure 9).

#### 4.2. Emergent Curriculum Complexity

- **MiniGrid:** An analysis of emergent maze complexity metrics (shortest path length and number of blocks) revealed that TRACED, and specifically the ACCEL+ATPL variant, generated curricula that ramped up difficulty significantly faster than ACCEL alone (Figure 6). This suggests that the transition-prediction error component is instrumental in driving the rapid escalation of task complexity, matching the agent's learning progress.
- **BipedalWalker:** Categorizing levels into Easy, Moderate, and Challenging based on environmental hyperparameters, TRACED progressively shifted its level buffer composition from easy to more moderate and challenging tasks. By 10,000 updates, TRACED introduced a non-trivial proportion (14.6%) of challenging levels, which was maintained thereafter. In contrast, ACCEL generated no challenging levels even at 20,000 updates, with its buffer predominantly comprising moderate levels (Table 2). This demonstrates TRACED's ability to create a steadily escalating curriculum.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- Ablation studies confirmed the individual contributions of the two key components. TRACED (full model) consistently outperformed both the "ATPL only" variant (TRACED - CL) and the "Co-Learnability only" variant (TRACED - ATPL) across various metrics in both MiniGrid (Figure 10) and BipedalWalker (Table 4). This indicates that both the transition-prediction error and Co-Learnability are essential for achieving the observed performance gains.
- Sensitivity analyses on the scaling factors $\alpha$ (ATPL weight) and $\beta$ (Co-Learnability weight) showed that balancing these terms effectively yielded additional performance benefits, though TRACED demonstrated robustness across a range of hyperparameter settings (Tables 3, 5, 6).
- An ablation on the number of PPO workers confirmed that TRACED's performance advantages were robust to changes in the parallelization setup and not an artifact of specific worker configurations (Table 7).

#### 4.4. Comparison with SFL (Sampling for Learnability)

- In the multi-agent JaxNav benchmark (Appendix Q), TRACED demonstrated performance comparable to SFL (a learnability-driven UED method) across Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) and mean win rate metrics. TRACED consistently outperformed Robust PLR, the strongest baseline from the SFL paper, even though TRACED does not rely on learnability-driven evaluation during training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TRACED introduces a methodological refinement to Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) that addresses key limitations in how task difficulty is approximated and how inter-task relationships are considered. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **More Accurate Regret Approximation:** By incorporating a transition-prediction error term (ATPL) alongside traditional value-function loss, TRACED provides a more comprehensive and accurate proxy for task regret. This is significant because it moves beyond a solely reward-centric view of difficulty to acknowledge the agent's understanding of environmental dynamics. This refined approximation allows the UED teacher to identify truly challenging and informative tasks more effectively, leading to faster curriculum ramp-up and more efficient learning for the student agent. Any DRL setting relying on regret approximation stands to benefit from this, potentially boosting sample efficiency across various algorithms.

2. **Explicit Modeling of Cross-Task Transfer:** The introduction of Co-Learnability offers a novel, lightweight mechanism to quantify how training on one task influences learning outcomes or difficulty on other tasks. This concept allows UED curricula to prioritize tasks that not only challenge the agent but also yield broader, positive transfer effects across the task distribution. This is crucial for developing truly generalist agents, as it guides the curriculum towards tasks that build foundational skills applicable to a wider range of scenarios, rather than merely solving isolated challenges.

3. **Enhanced Zero-Shot Generalization and Sample Efficiency:** Empirically, TRACED demonstrates superior zero-shot generalization capabilities on complex, procedurally generated environments (MiniGrid and BipedalWalker) compared to strong baselines, including state-of-the-art methods like CENIE. Crucially, these performance gains are achieved with significantly fewer training updates and reduced wall-clock time (approximately half). This accelerated learning and improved generalization are critical for practical applications of DRL, where computational resources and the ability to adapt to unseen variations are paramount.

4. **Broader Applicability and Future Research Directions:** The modular nature of TRACED's components—the refined regret approximation and the Co-Learnability metric—suggests broad applicability. The transition-prediction error term can be integrated into other regret-based UED frameworks or even general RL algorithms to improve their internal models of the environment. The Co-Learnability concept, currently an observational measure, opens avenues for future research into more sophisticated causal estimators or learned models of task relationships, potentially leading to even more intelligent curriculum designs. The work's success in multi-agent settings also indicates its potential for scaling to complex cooperative and competitive scenarios.

Overall, TRACED contributes to the advancement of adaptive curriculum learning by providing principled mechanisms for identifying more effective training tasks, thereby paving the way for more robust, generalizable, and sample-efficient DRL agents in open-ended learning environments.
