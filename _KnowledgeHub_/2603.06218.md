# Few-Shot Neural Differentiable Simulator: Real-to-Sim Rigid-Contact Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> A few-shot real-to-sim framework that calibrates MuJoCo's contact parameters from 3 real trajectories via CMA-ES, scales them into 3000 synthetic contact-rich trajectories, and trains a mesh-based FIGNet-style GNN simulator made fully differentiable through surrogate gradients of collision-detection nearest points. It matches identified MuJoCo accuracy, beats Brax, and supports gradient-based optimization of pushing velocity.

## Problem

- Analytical simulators are physically stable but sensitive to unmeasurable contact stiffness, damping, and friction, and their constraint solvers scale poorly in contact-rich multi-object scenes.
- Learning-based GNN simulators need massive training data, sourced either from cheap inaccurate simulation or accurate but expensive and slow real-world collection.
- Existing differentiable simulators inherit weak analytical contact models, and collision detection routines like GJK/EPA are discontinuous, blocking end-to-end gradient flow.

## Method

- ==Contact parameter identification==: ==CMA-ES== via Optuna minimizes a positional-plus-angular trajectory loss over MuJoCo's solimp, solref, and friction coefficient using few real rollouts.
- ==Contact-aware data scaling==: the calibrated simulator generates diverse synthetic trajectories by varying object count, geometry, mass, and initial states, enriching distribution rather than merely augmenting.
- ==Differentiable GNN simulator==: ==FIGNet==-style mesh/object nodes with ==message passing== predicts node accelerations, ==Verlet integration== plus ==shape matching==, and ==surrogate nearest-point gradients== dp/dq = JH through Coal collision detection.

## Results

- Contact parameter identification cut MuJoCo's average test trajectory error from **1.14** to **0.73**, though a residual gap to real-world contact dynamics remained after calibration.
- Trained only on scaled data from **3** real trajectories expanded to **3000**, the GNN matched identified MuJoCo, slightly beat its positional error, and outperformed all three Brax pipelines.
- Ablation: training on rotation-augmented real data alone gave higher positional and angular errors than data scaling; gradient-based velocity optimization converged within **10** epochs across runs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Calibrate a trusted analytical simulator first, then use it as a data generator: few-shot system identification converts scarce real data into large physically plausible training distributions.
> - Distribution enrichment across object count, geometry, and mass beats symmetry-preserving augmentation, since the learner needs unseen interaction configurations rather than relabeled copies.
> - Contact-pair sets can be treated as locally fixed within a timestep when the detection threshold is generous, making discontinuous collision routines effectively differentiable via surrogate Jacobians.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate physics simulation is a foundational element for developing and controlling robotic systems, particularly for tasks involving complex contact interactions such as grasping, assembly, or tool use. The existing landscape of physics simulation for robotics generally presents a trade-off between physical accuracy, computational efficiency, and data requirements.

Traditional analytical simulators, exemplified by systems like MuJoCo and IsaacLab, offer physically consistent and stable simulations. However, these systems often encounter computational challenges when scaled to complex scenes with numerous interacting objects. Their reliance on constraint-based solvers can become resource-intensive in contact-rich environments. Furthermore, the performance of analytical simulators is highly dependent on accurately calibrated physical parameters (e.g., contact stiffness, damping, friction coefficients), which are frequently difficult to measure precisely in real-world scenarios. Recent advancements have led to differentiable analytical simulators (e.g., Brax, Dojo), which enable gradient-based optimization for tasks such as motion planning and reinforcement learning. Nevertheless, these differentiable analytical simulators are still subject to the limitations of their underlying contact models and parameter sensitivity.

In parallel, data-driven or learning-based simulators have emerged, demonstrating potential for both accuracy and computational efficiency, often leveraging architectures like Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). These models can learn complex dynamics and generalize across different object types and parameters. However, a significant drawback of learning-based approaches is their demand for extensive training data. This data is typically acquired either through inexpensive but potentially inaccurate simulations or via costly and time-consuming real-world collection. Prior efforts in learnable simulation have explored learning components of physics (e.g., signed distance functions) or entire dynamics models using GNNs.

The "real-to-sim" paradigm represents another relevant area, aiming to bridge the discrepancy between simulated and real-world dynamics. Approaches such as ContactNets utilize fiducial markers to obtain real-world object poses for simulator validation, while others like GradSim and DANO optimize physical parameters or learn neural fields directly from images using differentiable rendering.

Collision detection, a critical component of contact simulation, poses a specific challenge for differentiability. Many collision detection algorithms are either non-differentiable or provide only approximate gradients, which can hinder end-to-end gradient-based optimization in differentiable simulators.

This paper positions itself to address these challenges by proposing a novel framework that combines the physical consistency derived from analytical formulations with the representational capacity of GNN-based models. It aims to overcome the data scarcity issue for learning-based simulators through a few-shot real-to-sim approach, and to achieve full differentiability by introducing surrogate gradients for collision detection. The work seeks to provide a simulator that is accurate, scalable, and differentiable, thereby advancing capabilities for robotic learning and control in contact-rich environments without requiring extensive real-world data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a fully differentiable, GNN-based rigid-body simulator capable of accurately modeling complex real-world contact dynamics using only a limited amount of real-world data. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations:

1. **Bridging the Reality Gap for Robotic Learning:** Robotic learning and control tasks, especially those involving manipulation, inherently depend on intricate contact interactions. Existing analytical simulators often fail to accurately capture the nuances and complexities of these real-world contact dynamics, leading to a "reality gap" where policies learned in simulation do not transfer effectively to physical robots. The motivation is to create a simulator that generates dynamics closely aligned with reality, thereby enhancing the utility of simulation for robotics.

2. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Learning-Based Simulators:** While learning-based simulators (e.g., those using GNNs) demonstrate promise in terms of accuracy and computational efficiency, they typically necessitate vast quantities of training data. Collecting such extensive and diverse real-world contact data is often prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and resource-intensive. The researchers are motivated to overcome this limitation by developing a method that allows for effective training of GNN-based simulators with only a few real-world observations.

3. **Enabling Gradient-Based Optimization in Contact-Rich Scenarios:** Differentiable simulators are instrumental for gradient-based optimization algorithms, which are widely used in modern robotics for tasks like system identification, trajectory optimization, and reinforcement learning. However, achieving end-to-end differentiability in simulators that model rigid-body contact is challenging, primarily due to the non-differentiable nature of collision detection. A key motivation is to devise a method that makes collision detection differentiable, thus allowing the entire GNN-based simulator to support efficient gradient propagation for complex contact scenarios.

4. **Improving Physical Accuracy and Scalability:** Analytical simulators, despite their physical grounding, can struggle with computational scalability in complex, multi-object contact scenes. Simultaneously, their sensitivity to difficult-to-measure parameters limits their real-world fidelity. The paper is motivated to combine the benefits of analytical approaches (initial parameter calibration for realism) with the representational power and potential scalability of GNNs to create a simulator that is both accurate in modeling diverse contact interactions and efficient for large-scale applications.

In summary, the work seeks to develop a robust, data-efficient, and fully differentiable simulator that can accurately model complex rigid-body contact, thereby advancing the capabilities of robotic learning and control by providing a more realistic and optimizable simulation environment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates contact parameter identification, data scaling, and a differentiable GNN-based simulator. The methodology is structured into three main stages.

**A. Contact Parameter Identification**
The initial step involves calibrating an existing high-fidelity analytical simulator to align its dynamics with real-world observations.
- **Data Input:** A limited set of real-world trajectories for rigid bodies is collected, including positions and orientations over time. Velocities are derived using backward finite differences.
- **Objective:** To identify optimal contact parameters (denoted as θ) that minimize the discrepancy between simulated and real-world trajectories. The discrepancy is quantified by a loss function (Equation 1) that sums the squared positional differences and angular differences between real and simulated 6D poses over all objects and timesteps. A positional scaling factor is incorporated.
- **Testbed Simulator:** MuJoCo is chosen as the testbed simulator for identification due to its established physical models.
- **Parameters Identified:** The focus is on critical contact parameters in MuJoCo, specifically `solimp` (defining impedance function shape), `solref` (specifying time constant and damping ratio of the spring-damper model), and the coefficient of lateral friction (μ). These parameters govern stiffness, dissipation, and stability of contact.
- **Optimization:** Since MuJoCo is a non-differentiable simulator, a gradient-free optimization algorithm, CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy), is employed to solve the optimization problem.
- **Outcome:** The identified parameters (θ*) are intended to ensure that subsequent data generation produces physically realistic trajectories that are consistent with real-world observations.

**B. Contact-Aware Data Scaling**
Following parameter identification, this stage addresses the data requirements for training the GNN-based simulator.
- **Purpose:** To generate a large, diverse synthetic dataset that captures a wide range of contact interactions while maintaining physical realism, based on a limited real-world seed.
- **Process:** The identified MuJoCo simulator, calibrated with the parameters from stage A, is used as a data generation tool. Starting from a base scene with a few objects, the method systematically varies the number of objects, their properties (geometry, mass), and initial states. This systematic exploration creates a rich dataset with diverse contact scenarios.
- **Distinction from Data Augmentation:** Unlike simple data augmentation (e.g., applying transformations), this method enriches the distribution of training data by generating novel, physically realistic scenarios, aiming to enhance the GNN's generalization capabilities.
- **Outcome:** A large synthetic dataset of contact-rich trajectories, which serves as the primary training data for the GNN-based simulator.

**C. Differentiable GNN-based Simulator**
This is the core learning-based simulation component, designed for full differentiability. It comprises a collision detection algorithm, a GNN solver, and a shape matching module.

1. **Mesh-based GNN Solver:**
 - **Architecture:** The GNN solver adapts the FIGNet architecture.
 - **Representation:** Each rigid body is represented as a triangle mesh.
 - **Graph Construction:** A graph is constructed where nodes represent mesh vertices and object properties, and edges encode spatial relationships (mesh-mesh, object-mesh, face-face).
 - **Message Passing:** A standard L-layer message-passing framework is used. Nodes and edges are updated iteratively through learnable Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs).
 - **Output:** The GNN decodes final node features into accelerations for each mesh node. These accelerations are then integrated using a Verlet integrator to update positions.
 - **Shape Matching:** During inference, a shape matching algorithm is applied to compute rigid transformations for each object, projecting predicted node positions to the transformed mesh to maintain topological consistency. This process involves standard differentiable matrix operations.

2. **Surrogate Gradients of Collision Detection:**
 - **Challenge:** Collision detection algorithms, such as GJK and EPA used for convex meshes in the Coal library, are typically non-differentiable due to discontinuities.
 - **Approach:** The authors derive surrogate gradients to enable backpropagation through the discrete collision detection (DCD) process.
 - **Assumption:** For a slightly larger distance threshold (dϵ) used to define potential contact pairs, the set of detected contacts is considered redundant and sufficiently rich. This implies that for small motion variations within a timestep, the contact pair set can be treated as fixed, obviating the need to differentiate the detection process itself.
 - **Gradient Derivation:** The relative velocities of nearest points between colliding objects are formulated using the kinematic mapping between generalized velocities and positions. A contact Jacobian matrix is derived, mapping generalized velocities to these relative velocities. The surrogate gradients of the nearest points with respect to generalized positions are then computed using this Jacobian and the kinematic mapping (Equations 5, 6, 7). These gradients can be efficiently computed once contact pairs and nearest points are determined by DCD.
 - **Outcome:** This derivation renders the entire GNN-based simulator fully differentiable, supporting both forward simulation and backward gradient propagation.

**D. Experimental Setup**
- **Real-World Data Collection:** A tabletop setup with two 3D-printed cubes equipped with AprilTags is used. Four Intel RealSense D435if cameras track 6D poses via TagSLAM at 60 Hz.
- **Datasets:** Three real-world trajectories (approx. 20 frames each) are collected for contact parameter identification. For training, this dataset is scaled to 3000 trajectories using the identified MuJoCo. For a baseline, the 3 real-world trajectories are also augmented to 3000 by rotation. A separate test set of 14 trajectories with novel initial conditions is collected.
- **Baselines:** Performance is compared against MuJoCo, three pipelines of Brax (generalized, positional, spring), and their GNN with simple data augmentation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed framework in learning rigid-contact dynamics from limited real-world data and supporting gradient-based optimization.

**A. Contact Parameter Identification Results**
- **Effectiveness of Identification:** The gradient-free CMA-ES method successfully identified contact parameters for MuJoCo using the limited real-world trajectory data.
- **Improved Accuracy:** The average trajectory error on the test set for MuJoCo was reduced from 1.14 to 0.73 after contact parameter identification (Figure 4). This indicates that calibrating MuJoCo with real-world data improved its ability to replicate observed dynamics.
- **Visualization:** Visual comparisons (Figure 5) show that the identified MuJoCo simulation trajectories align more closely with real-world trajectories compared to the uncalibrated version, particularly in contact scenarios.
- **Residual Gap:** Despite the improvement, a discrepancy remained between the identified MuJoCo trajectories and the real-world data, suggesting that even calibrated analytical models may not fully capture the complexities of real-world contact interactions.

**B. Simulation Evaluation**
- **Performance vs. Identified MuJoCo:** The GNN-based simulator, trained exclusively on the scaled synthetic data generated by the identified MuJoCo, achieved positional and angular error levels comparable to, and in some cases slightly lower than, the identified MuJoCo itself (Figure 7a, 7b). This finding suggests that the data scaling pipeline effectively enabled the GNN to learn rigid-contact dynamics that are competitive with a high-fidelity analytical simulator.
- **Effectiveness of Data Scaling:** The simulator trained using the proposed contact-aware data scaling method demonstrated lower positional and angular errors compared to a version trained with simple data augmentation (Figure 7a, 7b). This indicates that the data scaling approach, which generates diverse and physically realistic scenarios from the calibrated MuJoCo, is more effective than basic augmentation for enhancing GNN accuracy.
- **Performance vs. Brax:** The GNN-based simulator significantly outperformed all three pipelines of the differentiable simulator Brax (generalized, positional, spring) in both positional and angular error metrics (Figure 7a, 7b). This suggests that Brax's analytical models and contact formulations, even after identification, were less effective at aligning with real-world contact dynamics compared to the GNN-based approach learning from scaled data.
- **Generalization to Complex Scenarios:** An experiment involving a single cube striking an array of ten cubes (a "bowling-like" scenario) demonstrated the GNN-based simulator's ability to accurately capture complex multi-body interactions and near-instantaneous contact behaviors, aligning closely with the ground truth generated by the identified MuJoCo (Figure 6). This highlights its potential for generalization to contact-rich and computationally intensive situations beyond the simple two-cube interactions in the real-world dataset.

**C. Gradient-Based Optimization**
- **Differentiability for Optimization:** The fully differentiable design, enabled by the surrogate gradients for collision detection, supports gradient-based optimization.
- **Demonstrated Optimization Task:** An example showcased the optimization of an initial pushing velocity for a blue cube to make a green cube stop at a red target area after collision (Figure 1).
- **Convergence:** The optimization process successfully converged within 10 epochs across multiple runs, indicating the practical utility of the differentiable simulator for solving complex rigid-contact tasks using gradient descent.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic simulation and learning by addressing several limitations of current approaches, offering a framework with the potential for significant impact.

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity for Learning-Based Simulators:** The proposed few-shot real-to-sim data scaling pipeline represents a significant step towards enabling data-efficient training of learning-based physics simulators. By effectively generating large, diverse synthetic datasets from a minimal set of real-world trajectories, the framework mitigates the high cost and labor associated with extensive real-world data collection. This could broaden the applicability of data-driven simulation methods in robotics, making them more accessible to researchers and practitioners with limited resources.

2. **Improved Simulation Fidelity for Real-World Dynamics:** The GNN-based simulator, trained on the contact-aware scaled data, demonstrates an ability to replicate real-world trajectories with an accuracy comparable to a high-fidelity analytical simulator (MuJoCo) and superior to other differentiable analytical baselines (Brax). This enhanced fidelity of simulated contact dynamics is critical for reliable robotic learning and control, as it reduces the "reality gap" and improves the transferability of learned policies from simulation to physical robots.

3. **Enabling Full Differentiability for Complex Contact:** The derivation of surrogate gradients for collision detection is a key technical contribution. By rendering the entire simulator end-to-end differentiable, the framework unlocks the potential for efficient gradient-based optimization in scenarios involving complex rigid-body contacts. This allows for the application of advanced optimization techniques, such as those used in reinforcement learning, trajectory optimization, and system identification, to tasks that were previously challenging due to the non-differentiable nature of contact events.

4. **Facilitating Gradient-Based Robotic Control and Planning:** The demonstrated capability for gradient-based optimization (e.g., optimizing initial velocities to achieve a target state) suggests that the differentiable simulator can serve as a powerful tool for developing and evaluating robotic control policies. It could accelerate policy learning in contact-rich manipulation tasks, enable more precise trajectory planning, and facilitate inverse dynamics problems where physical parameters need to be inferred.

5. **Generalization to Multi-Object Interactions:** The simulator's performance in complex multi-body interaction scenarios, such as the bowling-like experiment, indicates its potential to generalize to novel scenes beyond the specific configurations used for initial calibration. This generalization capacity is crucial for robotic applications that involve interactions with multiple objects in unstructured environments.

While the approach currently depends on the accuracy of contact parameter identification and assumes generalization of these parameters across scenarios, and also requires 6D poses from the real world, the proposed framework offers a promising direction. Future work on more sophisticated contact representations and integration with vision systems could further enhance its robustness and applicability, ultimately advancing the capabilities of robotic manipulation and control by providing a powerful, accurate, and optimizable simulation environment.
