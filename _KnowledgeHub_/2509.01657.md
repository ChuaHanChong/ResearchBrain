# Data Retrieval with Importance Weights for Few-Shot Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Importance Weighted Retrieval (IWR) provides a principled method for few-shot imitation learning by utilizing Gaussian Kernel Density Estimates and importance sampling to select relevant demonstrations from large prior datasets. This approach consistently improves policy performance in simulated and real-world robot manipulation tasks, demonstrating up to 30% higher success rates compared to existing heuristic retrieval methods.

## Problem

- Few-shot imitation learning struggles to effectively leverage limited task-specific demonstrations for new tasks.
- Existing retrieval-based methods for augmenting limited data rely on heuristic nearest-neighbor (L2 distance) selection, which lacks principled justification.
- These heuristic retrieval rules lead to high-variance data selection and neglect the distribution of the prior data, introducing bias into the retrieved dataset.

## Method

- **IWR** re-interprets data retrieval as an ==importance sampling== problem, aimed at correcting for distribution shifts between the target and prior datasets.
- It models the probability density functions of both the target and prior data in a learned latent space using ==Gaussian Kernel Density Estimates (KDEs)==.

## Results

- IWR consistently outperformed baseline retrieval methods (Behavior Retrieval, Flow Retrieval, SAILOR) in simulated Robomimic and LIBERO-10 tasks, achieving an average success rate improvement of **4.4%** to **5.8%**.
- On real-world tasks using the Bridge V2 dataset, IWR demonstrated even more substantial gains, with up to a **30%** increase in success rates compared to Behavior Retrieval on long-horizon tasks like "Eggplant."
- Qualitative analysis shows IWR retrieves more relevant and temporally diverse data from the prior dataset, effectively correcting for biases towards common initial states or irrelevant tasks present in data retrieved by baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The traditional L2-distance-based retrieval rule is mathematically equivalent to a highly localized, high-variance Gaussian KDE of the target data, explaining its limitations.
> - Explicitly accounting for the prior data distribution (the denominator in the importance weight ratio) is critical to mitigate retrieval bias and select more representative samples.
> - Using well-calibrated, smoother Gaussian KDEs provides more robust and lower-variance density estimates for effective importance weight calculation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot imitation learning (IL) has seen remarkable progress, largely propelled by the availability of large-scale robot datasets containing hundreds to thousands of demonstrations. These datasets, exemplified by projects like DROID, OpenX, and Bridge, enable deep learning-based policies to achieve impressive performance on complex manipulation tasks. However, a significant practical challenge remains: deploying robots in new environments or for unseen tasks typically requires collecting a new, extensive set of task-specific demonstrations. This data collection process is often prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and skill-intensive, severely limiting the scalability and adaptability of IL systems.

**Few-shot imitation learning** aims to overcome this data bottleneck by enabling robots to learn new tasks from a very limited number of demonstrations. One promising avenue within few-shot IL is **retrieval-based imitation learning**. This approach involves drawing relevant samples from large, pre-existing general-purpose robot datasets to augment a small, task-specific "target" dataset. The core idea is that even if a new task has only a few demonstrations, there might be useful, similar behaviors or state-action pairs hidden within a massive prior dataset that can aid the learning process.

Existing retrieval-based methods, such as Behavior Retrieval (BR), Flow Retrieval (FR), and SAILOR (SR), typically operate by:
1. Learning a latent embedding space for state-action pairs (or optical flow, or skill chunks).
2. Identifying data points in the prior dataset whose latent embeddings are "closest" (e.g., in terms of L2 distance) to those in the target dataset.
3. Using these retrieved samples to co-train a policy alongside the limited target demonstrations.

While these heuristic methods have shown practical success, the paper argues that their underlying design choices, particularly the reliance on nearest-neighbor L2 distance for data selection, lack a principled mathematical justification. This "intuitive" approach to retrieval is susceptible to high variance and fails to account for the distribution of the prior data itself when making retrieval decisions.

This work critically analyzes these shortcomings and proposes a novel, principled approach rooted in **importance sampling** and **kernel density estimation (KDE)**. By reframing retrieval as an importance sampling problem, the authors provide a probabilistic lens to understand and improve data selection. This positions the paper as a crucial theoretical and practical advancement in the field of data-efficient robot learning, pushing beyond heuristics towards more robust and mathematically grounded retrieval mechanisms. It contributes to the broader trend of making robot learning systems more robust and adaptable by intelligently leveraging existing data resources.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are twofold:
1. **To provide a principled, probabilistic interpretation of retrieval-based few-shot imitation learning:** The authors observe that while existing retrieval methods have been empirically successful, their design relies heavily on intuition, particularly the use of nearest-neighbor L2 distance in latent space for data selection. A key motivation is to move beyond these heuristics and establish a more rigorous mathematical foundation for retrieval.
2. **To develop an improved data retrieval mechanism that addresses the shortcomings of current methods:** Based on their probabilistic re-interpretation, the paper aims to correct the inherent biases and high variance associated with the conventional nearest-neighbor retrieval rule.

The specific motivations driving these objectives stem from two identified shortcomings of prior retrieval approaches:

- **High-Variance Nearest-Neighbor Estimates:** The common practice of selecting prior data based on its minimum L2 distance to any point in the target dataset is shown to be equivalent to a Gaussian Kernel Density Estimate (KDE) of the target data distribution in the limit as the kernel bandwidth approaches zero. This implies that existing methods implicitly rely on highly localized, high-variance estimates that are susceptible to noise and outliers in the limited target dataset. A more robust estimation, considering contributions from multiple target data points, is needed.
- **Neglect of Prior Data Distribution:** Current retrieval rules primarily focus on how "close" a prior data point is to the target distribution (approximating the numerator of an importance weight). However, they largely ignore the distribution of the prior data itself (the denominator). This leads to a retrieved data distribution (p_ret) that is closer to the product of p_t and p_prior, rather than the desired p_t, introducing bias. The motivation is to overcome this bias by explicitly accounting for the prior data distribution, akin to importance sampling.

In summary, the paper is motivated by the need for more mathematically sound, robust, and effective data selection strategies in few-shot imitation learning, enabling better policy learning from limited demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method, **Importance Weighted Retrieval (IWR)**, involves replacing the heuristic L2-distance-based retrieval rule with a principled approach derived from importance sampling, implemented using Gaussian Kernel Density Estimates (KDEs). The approach comprises three main steps:

1. **Representation Learning:**
 - Similar to existing retrieval methods, IWR assumes access to a pre-trained embedding function, $f_\phi$, which maps raw state-action pairs (or sequences/optical flow) into a low-dimensional latent representation, $z$.
 - The paper explicitly states compatibility with various existing representation learning techniques, such as those used by Behavior Retrieval (VAE over state-action pairs), Flow Retrieval (VAE over optical flow), and SAILOR (skill-based representation learning for sub-trajectories). The only constraint is that the latent dimension should be sufficiently small for practical Gaussian KDE computation. This allows IWR to be a modular improvement that can be layered on top of diverse underlying representation learning strategies.

2. **Improved Density Modeling with Gaussian KDEs:**
 - The paper reinterprets the traditional L2-distance retrieval rule: $min_{(s',a') \in D_t} ||f_\phi(s,a) - f_\phi(s',a')||^2_2 < \zeta$. It shows that this rule is equivalent to a hard threshold on a soft-max approximation (log-sum-exp) of a Gaussian KDE of the target data, where the bandwidth $h \to 0$. This implies that prior methods implicitly use a highly peaked, high-variance KDE.
 - IWR addresses this by directly modeling the probability density functions (PDFs) of both the target data ($p_t$) and the prior data ($p_{prior}$) in the learned latent space using **Gaussian KDEs**.
 - Specifically, for a dataset $D$, the KDE estimate for a point $z$ is given by:
 $p_{KDE}(z) = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{z' \in f_\phi(D)} \left((2\pi)^d |h^2\Sigma|\right)^{-1/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2} (z-z')^\top (h^2\Sigma)^{-1} (z-z')\right\}$
 where $d$ is the latent dimension, $h$ is the bandwidth parameter, and $\Sigma$ is the sample covariance matrix.
 - By using well-calibrated bandwidths (e.g., a multiplicative factor of Scott's rule) and considering the full covariance matrix, IWR produces smoother, lower-variance density estimates that aggregate information from all data points, rather than just the nearest neighbor.

3. **Importance Weight Estimation and Data Retrieval:**
 - The central idea of IWR is motivated by importance sampling. To estimate an expectation under the target distribution $p_t$ using samples from the prior distribution $p_{prior}$, one should weight samples from $p_{prior}$ by the importance weight ratio $p_t / p_{prior}$. This ratio corrects for the bias introduced by sampling from $p_{prior}$ instead of $p_t$.
 - IWR applies this principle by estimating the importance weights for each potential prior data point as $p_{KDE,t}(z) / p_{KDE,prior}(z)$, where $p_{KDE,t}$ is the KDE of the target embeddings and $p_{KDE,prior}$ is the KDE of the prior embeddings.
 - In practice, the prior dataset $D_{prior}$ can be very large, so $p_{KDE,prior}$ is estimated using random batches from $D_{prior}$.
 - Finally, data is retrieved from $D_{prior}$ by selecting those state-action pairs with the highest estimated importance weights, exceeding a certain threshold $\eta$. This thresholding mechanism is consistent with prior retrieval works, although the paper acknowledges that an importance resampling procedure could provide an unbiased estimate but might suffer from higher variance.
 - The retrieved data $D_{ret}$ is then combined with the target dataset $D_t$ to co-train the policy using a weighted behavior cloning objective:
 $max_\theta \alpha E_{(s,a)\sim p_t}[\log\pi_\theta(a|s)] + (1-\alpha) E_{(s,a)\sim p_{ret}}[\log\pi_\theta(a|s)]$.

In essence, IWR transforms data retrieval from a geometric nearest-neighbor search into a probabilistic density ratio estimation problem, leading to a more principled and effective way of identifying relevant and diverse prior data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling experimental results across both simulated and real-world environments, demonstrating the consistent superiority of Importance Weighted Retrieval (IWR) over existing retrieval baselines.

1. **Consistent Performance Improvements Across Tasks:**
 - **Simulated Environments:**
 - On the **Robomimic Square Assembly** task, where retrieval of relevant data is crucial, IWR achieved a success rate of 84% $\pm$ 2.8, significantly outperforming Behavior Retrieval (69% $\pm$ 5.0), SAILOR (40% $\pm$ 4.9), and Flow Retrieval (79% $\pm$ 5.0).
 - Across a suite of **5 challenging LIBERO-10 tasks** (Mug-Microwave, Mug-Mug, Mug-Pudding, Soup-Cheese, Soup-Sauce), IWR consistently improved performance. For instance, on "Mug-Pudding," IWR achieved 45% $\pm$ 1.4 success, compared to 33% $\pm$ 3.3 for BR, 14% $\pm$ 0.9 for SR, and 17% $\pm$ 2.9 for FR. The average success rate improvement was 5.8% over SAILOR, 4.4% over Flow Retrieval, and 5.8% over Behavior Retrieval.
 - **Real-World Tasks (Bridge Dataset):**
 - On three real-world tasks using the **Bridge V2 dataset** (Corn, Carrot, Eggplant), IWR showed even more significant improvements. For the "Eggplant" long-horizon task, IWR achieved 20/20 partial successes and 11/20 full successes, drastically outperforming Behavior Retrieval (8/20 partial, 3/20 full). This translates to an average success rate increase of 30% compared to Behavior Retrieval on real-world tasks.

2. **Broad Applicability to Different Latent Spaces:**
 - IWR is shown to be largely independent of the specific latent space learning method. When combined with other existing retrieval methods' latent spaces (e.g., FR-IWR, SR-IWR), it generally leads to improved policy performance across simulated and real-world tasks. This modularity highlights IWR's potential as a general enhancement for retrieval-based IL. However, the authors note that IWR's performance may degrade with non-smooth latent spaces (e.g., BYOL), suggesting a requirement for underlying representations that are amenable to KDE.

3. **Qualitative Analysis of Retrieved Data:**
 - Analysis of the retrieved data distributions for tasks like "Mug-Pudding" reveals how IWR improves selection. While Behavior Retrieval might retrieve irrelevant tasks or disproportionately sample from initial, less informative phases of demonstrations, IWR **corrects for these biases**. It retrieves a higher percentage of tasks directly relevant to the target and a more balanced distribution of samples across different temporal phases of the task (e.g., more samples from "pick up" or "transfer" stages rather than just "reach"). This demonstrates IWR's ability to select higher-quality, more diverse, and more temporally appropriate data.

4. **Ablation Studies Confirm Design Choices:**
 - **Importance Weights ($p_{prior}$ denominator):** Removing the normalization by $p_{prior}$ (i.e., only considering $p_t$) generally resulted in decreased performance, confirming the importance of the full importance weight ratio for mitigating bias.
 - **Bandwidth Parameters:** IWR proved relatively robust to the specific choice of Gaussian KDE bandwidth, with smoother KDEs (larger bandwidths) showing slightly better average performance.
 - **Retrieval Thresholds:** Similar to prior work, the amount of data retrieved (determined by a threshold $\eta$) impacts performance, emphasizing the need for appropriate tuning, but IWR demonstrated strong performance across reasonable thresholds.

In summary, IWR consistently and significantly outperforms baseline methods by providing a more principled and effective way to select relevant data. This is attributed to its use of lower-variance KDE estimates for density modeling and its explicit consideration of importance weights to correct for the prior data distribution's bias.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Importance Weighted Retrieval (IWR) holds significant implications for the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the data efficiency challenge for real-world deployment.

1. **Principled Foundation for Retrieval:** The most profound impact of this work is its shift from heuristic, intuition-based data retrieval to a **mathematically principled framework** rooted in importance sampling and kernel density estimation. By demonstrating the equivalence of the common L2 distance retrieval rule to a degenerate KDE, the authors not only explain its shortcomings but also provide a robust theoretical basis for improvement. This elevates retrieval-based imitation learning from a set of practical tricks to a more rigorous scientific discipline.

2. **Enhanced Few-Shot Imitation Learning:** IWR's consistent and substantial performance gains, especially in real-world scenarios, directly translate to more capable and reliable few-shot robot learning systems. By intelligently selecting more relevant and diverse prior data, IWR effectively **reduces the need for extensive task-specific demonstrations**. This is critical for scaling robot deployment to a wider array of tasks and environments, as it mitigates the most significant bottleneck: data collection.

3. **Improved Data Quality and Diversity:** The qualitative analysis reveals that IWR not only selects "more" relevant data but "better" relevant data. By correcting for biases towards common initial states or irrelevant tasks present in prior datasets, IWR ensures that the retrieved data is genuinely useful and diverse across different phases of a task. This leads to policies that are more robust and less prone to common failure modes (e.g., early grasping, missing objects).

4. **Modular and Easily Integrable:** A key strength of IWR is its **minimal overhead and modularity**. It can be easily integrated with existing retrieval methods by simply replacing their L2-distance-based selection mechanism with importance-weighted KDEs. This means that future advancements in latent space learning or policy architectures can readily leverage IWR to further enhance their data efficiency without requiring a complete redesign. This "drop-in" nature positions IWR to become a standard practice in retrieval-based IL.

5. **Broadening the Scope of Robot Learning:** By making few-shot learning more effective, IWR contributes to expanding the range of tasks that robots can learn and perform without prohibitive human intervention. This has direct implications for applications in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and domestic robotics, where task novelty and adaptability are common.

**Limitations and Future Work:** The authors openly acknowledge limitations, which also hint at future research directions. The reliance on Gaussian KDEs can become computationally intensive and numerically unstable in very high-dimensional latent spaces, restricting the size of the learned representations. Future work could explore more advanced, scalable methods for importance weight estimation (e.g., using density ratio estimators or generative models). Additionally, the current evaluation is primarily focused on pick-and-place tasks due to dataset constraints; extending retrieval to more complex, dexterous, or long-horizon tasks, and understanding what constitutes an "effective" latent space for IWR, are promising avenues.

Overall, IWR represents a significant step forward in making data-efficient robot learning more robust and principled, paving the way for more adaptable and autonomous robotic systems.
