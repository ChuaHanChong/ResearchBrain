# ACWM-Phys: Investigating Generalized Physical Interaction in Action-Conditioned Video World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology developed ACWM-Phys, a benchmark suite featuring diverse physical interaction environments, and ACWM-DiT, a latent video diffusion transformer, to evaluate the physical generalization of action-conditioned video world models. The evaluation revealed that while models achieve high fidelity on in-distribution data, they struggle with out-of-distribution physical changes, particularly in complex dynamics like deformable objects and particle systems.

## Problem

- Existing action-conditioned world model (ACWM) benchmarks lack physical diversity, primarily focusing on egocentric navigation or rigid-body manipulation tasks.
- It is unclear whether current ACWMs learn general physical laws or merely memorize visual appearance patterns specific to their training data.
- A systematic evaluation framework was needed to assess ACWM generalization across diverse physical interactions such as deformable objects, fluids, granular materials, and complex kinematic chains.

## Method

- Introduction of **ACWM-Phys**, a new ==benchmark== with eight simulated environments spanning rigid-body, deformable-object, particle, and kinematic dynamics.
- Design of specific ==in-distribution (InD) and out-of-distribution (OoD) evaluation protocols== with controlled physical shifts to systematically assess generalization capabilities.
- Development of **ACWM-DiT**, a reproducible ==diffusion-based video world model== baseline using a ==video VAE== and a ==transformer backbone== with robust action conditioning.

## Results

- ACWM-DiT-S demonstrated strong in-distribution performance (e.g., Push Rope **M-MSE 2.61**, **SSIM 0.988**) but showed substantial out-of-distribution degradation, with Robot Arm (**∆M-MSE +40.35**) and Cloth Move (**∆M-MSE +29.99**) experiencing the largest drops.
- Out-of-distribution robustness correlated with physical complexity and action/state dimensionality; tasks with low-dimensional, geometrically clear structures generalized more reliably.
- Ablation studies confirmed that larger model scales improve OoD robustness, cross-attention benefits high-dimensional action spaces, and temporally-aware latent spaces are superior for dynamic video tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current ACWMs achieve high fidelity on in-distribution data but exhibit significant performance degradation under out-of-distribution physical shifts, especially in tasks involving complex dynamics and high-dimensional control.
> - Scaling model capacity consistently improves out-of-distribution robustness more than in-distribution accuracy, suggesting that larger models better internalize physical structures.
> - Temporally-aware latent representations derived from causal VAEs are crucial for capturing dynamic dependencies and enhancing model performance and generalization for action-conditioned video prediction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Action-conditioned world models (ACWMs) represent a paradigm for learning predictive models of the physical world from visual observations and control signals. These models aim to forecast future observation sequences based on agent actions, with potential applications in robot planning, policy learning, and data augmentation, reducing the need for hand-crafted dynamics models. Recent advancements in diffusion-based video generation have improved the visual fidelity and temporal consistency of generated sequences, further stimulating interest in pixel-space world models.

Despite these advancements, existing ACWMs and their associated benchmarks exhibit limitations in terms of physical diversity. A substantial portion of current research focuses on egocentric navigation, where actions primarily involve camera motion and objects infrequently deform or interact. Other works concentrate on narrow robot manipulation domains, typically involving rigid-body pick-and-place tasks. The physical world, however, encompasses a broader spectrum of interaction regimes, including deformable objects, granular and fluid particle systems, and complex kinematic chains, each governed by distinct dynamics. It remains largely uninvestigated whether current diffusion-based ACWMs can effectively learn and generalize across these diverse interaction modes, particularly when the underlying physics diverges from the training distribution.

Prior research on physics in video diffusion models has explored their capacity to capture physical principles and serve as implicit world models. These studies often examine aspects such as physical law consistency, intuitive physics, and physical reasoning ability in generated videos. However, much of this work is centered on text-to-video (T2V) or image-to-video (I2V) generation, where models produce visually plausible dynamics from passive prompts or observations. Consequently, these benchmarks primarily assess whether models reflect physics in generated videos rather than their ability to predict physically grounded futures under explicit action control. This paper addresses this gap by focusing on action-conditioned settings within ACWMs and providing a benchmark designed for systematic evaluation of physical generalization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically evaluate the capacity of Action-Conditioned World Models (ACWMs) to understand and generalize across diverse physical interactions. This objective is driven by several motivations arising from limitations in the current state of ACWM research and benchmarks.

First, a critical motivation stems from the **lack of physical diversity in existing ACWM benchmarks**. Current benchmarks largely restrict their scope to either egocentric navigation tasks, where physical interactions are minimal and typically involve camera movement rather than object deformation or interaction, or to specialized robot manipulation datasets that primarily feature rigid-body interactions like picking and placing. This narrow focus fails to represent the richness of physical interactions present in the real world, which include complex phenomena such as the dynamics of deformable objects (e.g., ropes, cloth), granular materials, fluid systems, and intricate kinematic chains. Each of these categories involves fundamentally different physical laws and interaction patterns.

Second, there is an **unaddressed question regarding the generalization capabilities of current ACWMs**. It is unclear whether the advanced diffusion-based ACWMs, despite their progress in visual fidelity, can effectively learn and generalize across these diverse physical interaction modes. Specifically, there is concern that these models might silently fail when the underlying physical dynamics in a novel situation depart from the distribution observed during training. This represents a significant hurdle for deploying ACWMs in real-world scenarios where unanticipated physical conditions are common.

Third, the researchers identify a **need for controlled and reproducible evaluation environments**. Existing real-robot benchmarks, while valuable, often introduce sensor noise and variability that complicate the precise measurement of generalization gaps. To systematically diagnose model capabilities for physically grounded world modeling, an environment that allows for controlled data collection, reproducible evaluation, and precise manipulation of distribution shifts is essential.

To address these motivations, the study sets out with several specific objectives:
- To **introduce ACWM-Phys**: A new benchmark suite specifically designed to evaluate action-conditioned prediction under diverse physical dynamics. This benchmark is developed in a clean, controllable simulation environment with a carefully designed action space.
- To **cover a broad spectrum of physical interactions**: ACWM-Phys is designed to include training and evaluation data spanning rigid-body dynamics, kinematics, deformable-object interactions, and particle dynamics.
- To **enable comprehensive generalization assessment**: The benchmark includes both in-distribution (InD) and out-of-distribution (OoD) evaluation protocols. These protocols feature controlled shifts in interaction patterns or scene configurations that are physically motivated, allowing for precise measurement of generalization gaps.
- To **provide a strong baseline**: The research aims to introduce ACWM-DiT, a latent video diffusion transformer, to serve as a robust and standardized baseline for evaluating models on ACWM-Phys, thereby establishing a starting point for future architectural developments.
- To **derive insights into model behavior**: Through systematic experiments, the study seeks to understand how ACWMs learn different types of physics and generalize beyond their training distributions, ultimately guiding the design of more robust and physically intelligent world models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces ACWM-Phys, a new benchmark, and ACWM-DiT, a diffusion-based world model baseline, to investigate generalized physical interaction.

#### 3.1. ACWM-Phys: A Benchmark Suite for Rich Physical Interactions

ACWM-Phys is a benchmark composed of eight robotic simulation environments, categorized into four types of physical interaction:
- **Rigid-Body Dynamics**:
 - **Push Cube**: Involves moving one to five colored cubes using a circular pusher. The action space (a ∈ R²) specifies the absolute 2D target position of the pusher.
 - **Stack Cube**: Utilizes a Franka Panda robot to place a red cube onto a green cube. The action space (a ∈ R⁷) denotes delta 6-DoF end-effector pose plus a gripper command.
- **Deformable-Object Dynamics**:
 - **Push Rope**: Employs a pole pusher to deform a flexible rope within the PyFlex simulator. The action space (a ∈ R²) represents the pole's horizontal displacement.
 - **Cloth Move**: Focuses on pushing a cloth over a fixed sphere using dual arms, with a shared 3D end-effector displacement action (a ∈ R³). An ablation study also explores an 8-D per-arm action space.
- **Particle Dynamics**:
 - **Push Sand**: Involves rearranging granular material in PyFlex using a board pusher. The action space (a ∈ R⁷) encodes the board's 3D pose delta.
 - **Pour Water**: Simulates pouring fluid by moving and tilting a cup. The action space (a ∈ R⁴) provides Cartesian and tilt-angle deltas from a spring-damper controller.
- **Kinematics**:
 - **Robot Arm**: Features a 7-DoF Franka Panda robot in Isaac Sim, with cuRobo planning. The action space (a ∈ R⁷) specifies the per-joint angle delta.
 - **Reacher**: Controls a two-link MuJoCo arm, where the action space (a ∈ R²) directly specifies joint torques.

The benchmark contains over 15,000 simulated trajectories, each with paired image observations, actions, and evaluation labels.

**In-Distribution (InD) and Out-of-Distribution (OoD) Evaluation Protocols**:
A core aspect of ACWM-Phys is the design of physically motivated distribution shifts for OoD evaluation. These shifts are not random perturbations but targeted changes in physical parameters or workspace regions. Examples include:
- **Rigid**: Unseen cube counts or shifted target placement.
- **Deformable**: Changes in rope length or cloth size.
- **Particle**: Increased particle count or shifted water levels.
- **Kinematics**: Expanded goal workspaces or unseen goal regions.
Since all environments are fully simulated, these OoD shifts are precisely reproducible and free from sensor noise, enabling accurate measurement of generalization gaps. Models are trained exclusively on InD data and evaluated on both InD and OoD test trajectories.

**Evaluation Metrics**: The study utilizes standard metrics including Mean Squared Error (MSE), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR). Additionally, a novel **Masked-MSE (M-MSE)** metric is introduced. M-MSE computes MSE specifically on pixels exhibiting sufficient ground-truth temporal change, thereby emphasizing errors in motion-relevant regions and down-weighting static backgrounds. The weight for each pixel is defined as 0.01 + `max_t,c |o_t,c,h,w - o_1,c,h,w|`, where `o_t,c,h,w` is the pixel value.

#### 3.2. ACWM-DiT: An Action-Conditioned Video Diffusion Transformer in Latent Space

ACWM-DiT serves as a latent video diffusion model baseline, built on a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture.
- **Video VAE**: A frozen, pretrained Wan 2.1 causal VAE is used to encode video observations into a compact latent space. This VAE applies 8x spatial compression and 4x temporal compression, mapping a video of T frames to latent tokens of spatial resolution H/8 x W/8 with 16 channels and a latent length T_l (padded/trimmed to 37 tokens).
- **Video DiT Backbone**: The denoiser is a bidirectional (non-causal) Transformer that processes spatiotemporally patchified latents. Its architecture consists of N stacked transformer blocks, each comprising a spatial self-attention layer followed by a temporal self-attention layer. Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) are applied in both layers. Conditioning frames are input at a noise level of σ=0, while predicted future frames are noised at the current diffusion step σ.
- **Action Conditioning Module**: Actions are integrated into the model via a dedicated `ActionEmbedder`. An MLP projects each action vector (of dimension d_a) to the DiT's hidden dimension (d). Subsequently, a 1D strided convolution downsamples this pixel-rate action sequence to the latent temporal resolution (matching the VAE's 4x temporal compression). The resulting action embedding is summed with the per-step timestep embedding to form a joint conditioning signal `c`. This `c` is then injected into every DiT block through Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN), modulating the scale and shift parameters after each layer normalization.
- **Training Setup**: The model is trained using a flow matching objective in the latent space, with a linear interpolation path and a shift parameter s=5.0. Training losses are weighted by a Gaussian envelope centered at diffusion step 500 to prioritize supervision on intermediate denoising levels. Models are trained for 100k steps with a batch size of 4 on 8 H100 GPUs, using the AdamW optimizer. 1000 noise levels are used for training, and 50 inference denoising steps are used for evaluation. The first frame of a sequence is consistently provided as a clean history input, with the model predicting the subsequent future frames.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using ACWM-DiT on the ACWM-Phys benchmark yielded several findings regarding in-distribution performance, out-of-distribution generalization, and the impact of architectural and data choices.

#### 4.1. Main Results

**In-Distribution (InD) Performance:**
ACWM-DiT-S demonstrated strong performance across all four physics categories within the training distribution.
- Environments with simpler, repetitive dynamics achieved the highest fidelity. For instance, **Push Rope** exhibited an M-MSE of 2.61 and SSIM of 0.988, and **Reacher** achieved an M-MSE of 5.63 and SSIM of 0.992, suggesting near-perfect structural similarity and low error in motion-relevant regions. This indicates effective capture of both dynamic foreground behavior and global spatial coherence.
- Tasks involving complex interactions posed greater challenges. **Stack Cube** showed an M-MSE of 10.93 and SSIM of 0.889, with lower SSIM attributed to the substantial foreground motion of the robot arm. **Cloth Move** presented the highest M-MSE at 63.68 and SSIM of 0.920, indicating that large-scale deformation leads to considerable errors in dynamic physical regions even within the training distribution.

**Out-of-Distribution (OoD) Generalization:**
Under distribution shifts, ACWM-DiT-S consistently experienced performance degradation, particularly in motion-sensitive regions.
- The most significant drops in performance were observed for **Robot Arm** (∆M-MSE = +40.35, ∆SSIM = −0.067) and **Cloth Move** (∆M-MSE = +29.99, ∆SSIM = −0.056). This degradation is attributed to the presence of unseen articulated configurations and large-scale cloth deformation, which introduce complex motions beyond the training distribution. This suggests that the model may rely on learned visual regularities rather than fully internalizing general physical dynamics.
- Tasks with low-dimensional geometric constraints, such as **Push Cube** (∆SSIM = −0.001) and **Reacher** (∆SSIM = 0.000), demonstrated relative stability, maintaining performance close to their InD levels.
- **Push Sand** showed an increased motion-region error (∆M-MSE = +9.32) but retained moderate structural similarity (OoD SSIM = 0.941, ∆SSIM = −0.034), indicating difficulty in precisely predicting fine-grained particle redistribution.
- **Pour Water** was more stable in M-MSE (∆M-MSE = +2.40), possibly due to the repeatability of the pouring trajectory, but still experienced a drop in SSIM (∆SSIM = −0.037) under unseen water volumes.

**Case Studies:**
- **Pour Water**: The model generally predicted pouring trajectories and fluid dynamics for InD water levels. However, under OoD shifts (fewer or more water), it generated visually plausible but physically imperfect fill levels, occasionally underestimating water amounts. This highlights a discrepancy between perceptual quality and accurate physical understanding.
- **Push Cubes**: The model accurately tracked rigid-body trajectories in InD scenarios and generalized well to OoD settings (e.g., unseen workspace positions, more cubes). However, some OoD videos showed instances where cubes abruptly disappeared, indicating limitations.

**Generalization Summary:**
The robustness of OoD generalization was influenced by both physical complexity and action/state dimensionality. Tasks with low-dimensional, visually clear geometric structures exhibited more reliable transfer to unseen configurations. In contrast, contact-rich deformation, particle dynamics, and high-DoF control led to greater performance degradation, implying that current diffusion-based world models predominantly rely on appearance statistics rather than fully learning underlying physical structures.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

**Model Scale:** Scaling the model capacity from DiT-S to DiT-M consistently improved both InD and OoD performance, with larger gains observed in OoD scenarios. This suggests that increased model capacity aids in internalizing physical structure rather than merely memorizing training appearances. However, gains from DiT-M to DiT-L were more modest, indicating diminishing returns at the current data scale.

**Action Conditioning:**
- Cross-attention conditioning proved beneficial for environments with high-dimensional action spaces. For **Robot Arm (d_a=7)**, cross-attention significantly improved both InD and OoD performance. For **Cloth Move (d_a=8)**, it modestly improved OoD performance, despite a slight decrease in InD performance. This suggests better binding between high-dimensional commands and complex motions.
- For low-dimensional controls (**Push Cube** and **Push Rope**, d_a=2), AdaLN performed comparably to or slightly better than cross-attention, indicating that AdaLN is sufficient for simpler displacement controls.

**Latent-Space Choice:** A temporally-aware causal VAE (Wan 2.1, with 4x temporal compression) consistently outperformed a frame-independent image VAE (FLUX VAE, with 1x temporal compression) on both InD and OoD scenarios for **Pour Water** and **Robot Arm**. This highlights the advantage of temporally-aware latent representations in capturing complex dynamics, even for stochastic particle interactions.

**Data Efficiency:**
- **Pour Water** demonstrated graceful degradation with reduced training data, retaining reasonable performance even at 25% data. This is likely due to its dynamics being governed by a single, repeatable pouring motion with limited geometric variability.
- **Push Cube**, conversely, degraded sharply with less data, indicating that its diverse cube configurations and push directions require broad trajectory coverage for effective generalization.

**Action Dimensionality:**
- Higher-dimensional action spaces generally increased prediction difficulty. For **Push Cube**, introducing a second pusher (d_a=4) increased interaction complexity and resulted in higher MSE for both InD and OoD.
- However, higher-dimensional actions could also improve OoD generalization when they provided more informative control signals. For **Cloth Move**, using the full 8-DoF per-arm action space, compared to a reduced 3-DoF shared action, substantially improved OoD MSE. This suggests that richer action signals aid the model in inferring two-arm cloth dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of action-conditioned world models by addressing critical gaps in benchmark diversity and generalization assessment. The key contributions are:

- **Introduction of ACWM-Phys**: This benchmark is presented as the first to span four distinct physical interaction regimes—rigid-body, deformable, particle, and kinematics—with controlled in-distribution (InD) and out-of-distribution (OoD) evaluation protocols across eight varied environments. Its fully simulated nature enables precise data collection, reproducible evaluations, and systematic analysis of model capabilities for physically grounded world modeling, overcoming limitations of real-world or narrowly focused benchmarks.
- **Development of ACWM-DiT Baseline**: The study establishes ACWM-DiT as a robust, diffusion-based latent transformer baseline. This model achieves strong performance across the diverse environments, providing a standardized starting point for future architectural and algorithmic developments aimed at improving physical understanding.
- **Diagnostic Insights into Generalization**: The systematic experiments reveal that current ACWMs exhibit strong InD fidelity but suffer significant OoD degradation, particularly for tasks involving complex physical interactions like deformable dynamics, particle systems, and high-DoF control. This observation suggests that models frequently rely on learned visual appearance patterns rather than fully internalizing underlying physical laws. Conversely, tasks with simpler, low-dimensional geometric constraints generalize more effectively.
- **Architectural and Data Design Guidance**: The ablation studies provide specific design insights:
 - Larger model capacities improve OoD robustness, indicating a better internalization of physical structures.
 - Cross-attention conditioning is more beneficial for high-dimensional action spaces, facilitating structured spatial-temporal grounding.
 - Temporally-aware causal VAEs outperform frame-independent encoders, highlighting the importance of integrated temporal context in latent representations.
 - While higher action dimensionality can increase prediction complexity, it can also enhance OoD generalization by offering richer control signals and observations in certain scenarios.

The potential impact of this work is multifaceted:
- **Diagnostic Tool**: ACWM-Phys can serve as a diagnostic tool for the research community, enabling a more granular understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of world models in handling diverse physical phenomena.
- **Guidance for Future Research**: The findings are expected to guide the design of future physically grounded world models, encouraging the development of architectures that explicitly represent physical structure rather than implicitly learning from visual statistics.
- **Advancing Embodied AI**: By pushing the boundaries of physical generalization in world models, this work contributes to the development of more capable and robust intelligent agents for robot planning, decision-making, and interaction with complex environments.

**Limitations**

The authors acknowledge two main limitations:
1. **Real-time Rendering**: ACWM-DiT, as a bidirectional diffusion model, provides high visual prediction quality but operates slowly during inference. This characteristic precludes real-time applications. Future work is suggested to explore autoregressive diffusion models or techniques like diffusion forcing to enable real-time world-model rollouts.
2. **Simulation Environment**: ACWM-Phys is built in simulation. While this offers advantages for controlled OoD evaluation, it does not fully replicate the complexity of real-world physics, sensor noise, or robot interaction. Bridging this gap would require efforts in sim-to-real transfer, the use of more realistic simulators, or the integration of human demonstration data collected in physical environments.
