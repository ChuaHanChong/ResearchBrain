# Z-1: Efficient Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Z-1 is an efficient reinforcement learning post-training framework designed for flow-based Vision-Language-Action models, enhancing the performance of SFT-initialized policies on complex robotic manipulation tasks. This framework achieved an 80.6% average success rate across 24 RoboCasa tasks, marking a 13.2 percentage point improvement over its imitation-learned baseline and outperforming prior state-of-the-art methods.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, primarily trained via imitation learning, are limited by demonstration data coverage and struggle with error accumulation over long-horizon, compositional tasks.
- Applying reinforcement learning (RL) to VLA models faces challenges including computationally expensive online interaction for flow-based actions and difficulties with sparse rewards in long-horizon tasks.
- A trade-off exists between training stability (freezing the VLM backbone) and policy adaptability (updating the VLM) when applying RL post-training to VLA models.

## Method

- A two-stage pipeline begins with per-scene ==supervised fine-tuning (SFT)== of the "π"_0.5 model on public RoboCasa demonstrations.
- Task-wise ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== is applied, adapting the ==flow-SDE formulation== for tractable policy ratios and optimizing a clipped group-relative objective.
- Four complementary modules are integrated: ==Shared-Prefix/Tree-Structured Prefix Branching== for efficient rollouts, ==Success-Aware Reward Decay== for refined sparse rewards, and ==Selective VLM–Action Expert Joint Training== for adaptive co-adaptation.

## Results

- The Z-1 RL framework achieved an average success rate of **80.6%** on **24** RoboCasa tasks, demonstrating a **13.2 percentage point** improvement over its SFT-initialized baseline (**67.4%**).
- Z-1 RL surpassed previously published state-of-the-art results on RoboCasa, including GR00T (**49.7%**), Video Policy (**63.3%**), and X-WAM (**79.2%**), with Z-1 showing **1.4 pp** improvement over X-WAM.
- The approach showed substantial gains in categories demanding precise interaction, such as drawer tasks (from **83.4%** to **96.1%**) and sink/faucet tasks (from **63.2%** to **94.3%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Efficient rollout construction using shared prefixes and tree-structured branching reduces redundant simulation, focusing the learning signal on critical interaction phases.
> - Success-Aware Reward Decay effectively refines sparse success signals by ordering successful rollouts based on completion time, improving optimization stability without complex reward shaping.
> - Selective VLM–Action Expert Joint Training allows for targeted co-adaptation of perception and action modules, addressing failures stemming from visual grounding or language-conditioned perception.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Modern robotic manipulation increasingly relies on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which integrate high-level language instructions, visual observations, and low-level continuous control. Recent VLA systems, such as RT-2, OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, and $\pi_{0.5}$, have demonstrated proficiency in complex manipulation tasks by leveraging semantic and visual priors from foundation models. These models typically derive their capabilities from human expert demonstrations through imitation learning techniques like behavior cloning (BC) or supervised fine-tuning (SFT).

However, the effectiveness of imitation learning is constrained by the coverage and quality of the demonstration data. In tasks involving long horizons or compositional actions, minor prediction errors can accumulate, leading the robot into states not adequately represented in the training data. A significant limitation of BC and SFT is their inability to learn from policy failures or optimize for sparse task success through trial-and-error interactions. This limitation has motivated the development of interaction-based post-training methods for VLA policies.

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training has emerged as a promising direction to extend VLA policies beyond their imitation-based initialization. By collecting online rollouts and optimizing for task-level rewards, RL allows policies to improve from their own experiences. Prior work in this area, including $\pi$RL, ReinFlow, VLA-R1, and SOP, has explored various online RL fine-tuning strategies for VLA models.

This research, presenting Z-1, positions itself within this emerging field of RL post-training for flow-based VLA models. Z-1 is built upon the $\pi_{0.5}$ base model, specifically focusing on how to efficiently and stably apply RL post-training. The work differentiates itself from other concurrent VLA advancements, such as those focusing on new world models (e.g., X-WAM), richer geometric representations (e.g., depth-aware VLA models), or generalist architectures (e.g., GR00T). Instead, Z-1 concentrates on algorithmic improvements for RL post-training, particularly regarding rollout construction, reward calibration, and module adaptation, while exclusively utilizing publicly available demonstration data. This focus aims to address the challenges of costly online interaction, sparse rewards, long horizons, and the stability-adaptability trade-off when updating vision-language backbones in flow-based VLA policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop an efficient and stable reinforcement learning post-training framework, named Z-1, for flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This framework aims to enhance the performance of existing SFT-initialized VLA policies on complex robotic manipulation tasks without requiring additional private demonstrations.

The motivation for Z-1 stems from several identified challenges in applying RL post-training to VLA models, particularly those based on flow-based action generation:

1. **Limitations of Imitation Learning**: Most existing VLA policies are primarily trained via behavior cloning (BC) or supervised fine-tuning (SFT) from fixed expert demonstrations. While these methods provide stable policy initialization, their performance is inherently limited by the coverage of the demonstration data. In tasks requiring long horizons or complex compositions of actions, small prediction errors can accumulate, leading the robot into out-of-distribution states not adequately represented in the training data. Furthermore, BC and SFT lack a direct mechanism for the policy to learn from its own failures or to optimize for sparse task success signals through trial-and-error.

2. **High Cost of Online Interaction**: Generating online rollouts for flow-based VLA models is computationally expensive. Traditional RL approaches that sample each full trajectory independently can incur significant simulation costs, especially for long-horizon tasks, making efficient data collection a critical concern.

3. **Challenges with Sparse Rewards and Long Horizons**: Robotic manipulation tasks often feature sparse success rewards, where a positive signal is only received upon task completion. This provides limited credit assignment, especially over long horizons. Additionally, binary task outcomes may not distinguish between different qualities of successful trajectories (e.g., faster vs. slower completion), leading to a coarse reward signal.

4. **Stability and Adaptability of VLM Training**: A common practice in VLA-RL is to freeze the vision-language module (VLM) backbone during post-training, updating only the action expert (AE). While this approach can improve training stability, it may limit the policy's ability to correct failures that stem from inaccurate visual grounding, spatial reasoning, or language-conditioned perception, rather than solely low-level control errors. Conversely, updating the entire VLM backbone can introduce instability. There is a need for a strategy that balances stability with the necessity for representation adaptation.

To address these challenges, Z-1 aims to introduce a modular Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) design. This design specifically targets enhancing rollout efficiency, stabilizing optimization in the presence of sparse rewards, and enabling adaptive co-adaptation between perception and action modules in flow-based VLA policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Z-1 employs a two-stage training pipeline built upon the pretrained $\pi_{0.5}$ base model, utilizing only publicly available RoboCasa demonstration data.

**Stage 1: Per-scene Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**
The initial stage involves adapting the $\pi_{0.5}$ model to the RoboCasa environment through supervised fine-tuning. Due to the limited size of the publicly released RoboCasa dataset (1,199 demonstrations), SFT is performed per scene category rather than per individual task. This strategy enhances data efficiency by allowing tasks within the same scene to share similar object layouts, visual contexts, and manipulation primitives. Both the vision-language module (VLM) and the action expert (AE) are updated during SFT. For scenes with very limited data (door, drawer, microwave), a two-stage SFT schedule is used: initial fine-tuning on all RoboCasa demonstrations, followed by further fine-tuning on scene-specific data. A stable SFT checkpoint with a held-out validation loss of approximately 0.01 is selected for each scene category as the initialization for the subsequent RL stage.

**Stage 2: Per-task Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) Post-training**
Following SFT, Z-1 applies task-wise GRPO post-training to selected tasks. This task-specific optimization prevents gradient interference across heterogeneous tasks and allows each task to utilize a specialized configuration of Z-1 modules. The GRPO algorithm works by sampling a group of rollouts, calculating a group-relative advantage for each rollout (normalized by the group mean and standard deviation of returns), and then optimizing a clipped group-relative objective. For flow-based action generation, Z-1 adapts the flow-SDE formulation to compute tractable transition likelihoods and policy ratios, enabling gradient computation for action chunks. The core GRPO objective is formulated as:

$L_{GRPO}(\theta) = - \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^G |B_i|} \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{(x,a) \in B_i} \min (\rho_{\theta}(x, a)A_i, \text{clip}(\rho_{\theta}(x, a), 1 - \eta, 1 + \eta) A_i)$,
where $\rho_{\theta}(x, a)$ is the policy ratio $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|x)}$, $A_i$ is the group-relative advantage, $B_i$ is the set of trainable action chunks in rollout $i$, and $\eta$ is the clipping parameter.

To address the challenges of efficiency, stability, and adaptability in VLA-RL, Z-1 integrates four complementary modules:

1. **Shared-Prefix GRPO**: This module addresses the inefficiency of redundant simulation in long-horizon tasks and focuses the learning signal on task-critical interaction phases. Instead of executing each rollout in a GRPO group independently from the start, a leader rollout executes a common prefix (up to a prefix length $P$). The simulator state at this branching point is then cloned, and all subsequent rollouts execute independent suffixes. Prefix chunks are masked and excluded from the policy-gradient loss, ensuring that reward differences are attributed to suffix behaviors. This reduces redundant computation and provides a cleaner optimization signal when approach phases are already reliable.

2. **Tree-Structured Prefix Branching**: This module generalizes Shared-Prefix GRPO by introducing progressive, hierarchical branching within the prefix. Instead of a single branching point, intermediate branching points create a tree of rollout clusters, making early segments shared by more rollouts and later segments more diverse. Only the fully shared root segment is masked, allowing partially shared segments to contribute to policy updates. This reduces over-masking, enables approach-phase refinement, and makes better use of rollout data, although it can introduce stability trade-offs.

3. **Success-Aware Reward Decay**: This mechanism refines sparse success rewards by introducing an ordering among successful rollouts based on completion time. For successful trajectories, the original success score $r$ is decayed by a factor $\gamma^{d_i}$, where $d_i$ is the number of action chunks until first success. Failed rollouts retain their original failure reward. For groups where all rollouts succeed, instead of mean normalization, the advantage is calculated as $A_i = R_i - \min_j R_j$. This ensures that earlier successes receive positive advantages without penalizing slower successful rollouts, thereby preserving the sparse success objective while providing finer group-relative feedback.

4. **Selective VLM–Action Expert Joint Training (Sel-JT)**: While default GRPO updates only the action expert (AE) (freezing the VLM for stability), Sel-JT enables joint training of the VLM and AE for tasks where AE-only adaptation is deemed insufficient. The decision to enable joint training is made based on training-stage diagnostics such as SFT success rate, early AE-only GRPO progress, and observed failure modes. This allows for co-adaptation of perception, grounding, and action generation when failures arise from visual grounding or language-conditioned perception, without incurring unnecessary training costs or representation drift for all tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Z-1 was conducted on 24 standard RoboCasa manipulation tasks, using task success rate as the primary metric.

**Overall Performance and Improvement over SFT:**
- Z-1 RL achieved an average success rate of 80.6% across all 24 RoboCasa tasks.
- This represents a substantial improvement of 13.2 percentage points over its SFT initialization (Z-1 SFT), which had an average success rate of 67.4%.
- Z-1 RL consistently improved Z-1 SFT's performance across all seven evaluated categories.

**Comparison with State-of-the-Art Baselines:**
- Z-1 RL outperformed previously published RoboCasa results from GR00T, GR00T N1.5, Video Policy, and X-WAM.
- Specifically, Z-1 RL's average success rate of 80.6% surpassed GR00T (49.7% by 30.9 pp), GR00T N1.5 (59.7% by 20.9 pp), Video Policy (63.3% by 17.3 pp), and X-WAM (79.2% by 1.4 pp).
- While the average gain over X-WAM (the strongest published baseline in comparison) was modest, Z-1 RL demonstrated superior performance across 5 out of 7 categories. This is notable as X-WAM integrates depth information, which Z-1 RL does not.

**Category-Level Performance:**
- Significant improvements were observed in categories requiring precise interaction after reaching the target object:
 - Drawer tasks: Improved from 83.4% to 96.1%.
 - Sink/faucet tasks: Improved from 63.2% to 94.3%.
 - Door tasks: Improved from 93.2% to 97.0%.
 - Pick-and-place tasks: Improved from 53.9% to 70.9%.
- Microwave tasks already showed high success rates after SFT (94.8%) and maintained this performance with RL.
- Stove tasks remained challenging for all methods, with Z-1 RL improving over Z-1 SFT but still underperforming X-WAM in this category. This suggests the need for stronger representation adaptation or targeted exploration for stove manipulation.

**Ablation and Analysis:**

1. **Effect of Shared Prefix and Prefix Tree (on TurnOnSinkFaucet):**
 - Both Shared-Prefix GRPO and Tree-Structured Prefix Branching achieved higher success rates than vanilla GRPO. This indicates that reducing variation in early trajectory segments makes group-relative comparisons more informative.
 - Flat Shared-Prefix GRPO provided more stable improvements due to a cleaner optimization signal, focusing on post-prefix manipulation behavior.
 - Tree-Structured Prefix Branching achieved the strongest final success rate and a faster policy loss decrease by allowing more approach-phase chunks to contribute to the update. However, it presented stability trade-offs due to exposing more prefix-region actions to updates.

2. **Effect of Selective VLM–Action Expert Joint Training (on TurnOnStove):**
 - Selective VLM–Action Expert Joint Training (GRPO + Sel-JT) consistently achieved a higher success rate and lower policy loss compared to the AE-only GRPO baseline.
 - This result demonstrates that for tasks with perception-sensitive failures (e.g., accurate visual grounding of stove knobs), updating the VLM alongside the AE provides a more effective learning signal, allowing co-adaptation of perception, grounding, and action generation. AE-only adaptation showed limited improvement.

3. **Effect of Success-Aware Reward Decay (on PnPStoveToCounter):**
 - Both Success-Aware Reward Decay and a Reward Penalty variant improved over standard GRPO, indicating the utility of completion time information beyond binary success.
 - The Reward Penalty variant strongly reduced average completion steps but resulted in slower success-rate improvement, as penalizing successful but slower rollouts could be overly aggressive.
 - Success-Aware Reward Decay achieved faster and more stable success-rate improvement while still reducing completion steps. By using a non-negative minimum-baseline ordering for all-success groups, it avoided penalizing slower successful rollouts, instead providing positive advantages to earlier completions.

These findings collectively indicate that Z-1's modular GRPO post-training framework substantially improves the performance of flow-based VLA policies, particularly in tasks requiring precise manipulation, by enhancing rollout efficiency, stabilizing optimization, and enabling adaptive co-adaptation of perception and action.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Z-1: Efficient Reinforcement Learning for Vision-Language-Action Models" offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation and reinforcement learning.

**Key Contributions:**
1. **Validation of RL Post-training for Flow-based VLA Models**: Z-1 demonstrates that systematic Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) post-training can substantially improve the performance of flow-based VLA policies. This is particularly significant because flow-based models, while powerful for continuous control, present unique challenges for applying traditional RL.
2. **Addressing Limitations of Imitation Learning**: The work provides a practical and effective solution to overcome the inherent limitations of behavior cloning and supervised fine-tuning. By enabling VLA models to learn from their own failures and optimize directly for task success through interaction, Z-1 pushes performance beyond the boundaries set by fixed demonstration data.
3. **Efficiency and Stability in VLA-RL**: The introduction of novel modules—Shared-Prefix GRPO, Tree-Structured Prefix Branching, Success-Aware Reward Decay, and Selective VLM–Action Expert Joint Training—directly addresses critical challenges in VLA-RL:
 - **Reduced Simulation Cost**: Prefix-based rollout construction (Shared-Prefix and Tree-Structured Branching) significantly reduces redundant computation in long-horizon tasks, making online interaction more efficient.
 - **Enhanced Reward Signal**: Success-Aware Reward Decay provides a finer, completion-aware reward signal for sparse-reward tasks without requiring complex dense reward shaping or additional reward models, improving optimization stability.
 - **Adaptive Learning**: Selective VLM–Action Expert Joint Training allows for targeted co-adaptation of perception and action, balancing training stability with the necessity for representation adjustments in perception-sensitive tasks.
4. **Open-Data-Driven Performance**: Z-1 achieves state-of-the-art performance on the RoboCasa benchmark using only publicly released demonstration data. This highlights the potential to significantly improve robot policies without reliance on proprietary or extensive private datasets, making advanced robotics research more accessible.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancement of Generalist Robotics**: By demonstrating how existing VLA models can be efficiently refined, Z-1 contributes to the development of more robust and capable generalist robot policies, which are essential for real-world deployment across diverse, complex environments.
- **Improved Task Success in Challenging Domains**: The notable improvements in contact-rich tasks (e.g., drawer manipulation, sink/faucet control) indicate that Z-1's approach is particularly beneficial for scenarios where fine manipulation and interaction are critical, which are often difficult for imitation-learned policies.
- **Foundation for Future RL Research**: The modular design and the specific algorithmic innovations in Z-1 provide a strong foundation for future research in VLA-RL, inspiring further exploration into efficient rollout strategies, advanced reward shaping, and adaptive module training.
- **Practical Applicability**: The framework's ability to boost performance with public datasets and without complex reward engineering makes it a practical option for researchers and developers aiming to improve VLA model capabilities in real-world robotic applications.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its achievements, Z-1 also highlights remaining challenges. Its performance on stove tasks, for instance, remains less competitive than some baselines (e.g., X-WAM), indicating that long-horizon transport, complex spatial reasoning, and robust recovery from intermediate failures still pose difficulties. The authors suggest future work could focus on incorporating stronger exploration mechanisms, richer visual-spatial representations, and developing cross-task RL post-training strategies to address these limitations and further enhance the generalizability and robustness of VLA policies.

In summary, Z-1 represents a methodical advance in the application of reinforcement learning for post-training Vision-Language-Action models, offering an effective and efficient framework to improve policy performance and expand the capabilities of robots in complex manipulation tasks.
