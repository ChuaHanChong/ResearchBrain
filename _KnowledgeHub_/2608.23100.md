# Shaping the Evolutionary Dynamics of Robot Morphology via Adaptive Control Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> AdaControl is an adaptive control-scheduling algorithm for robot co-design. It fits a tanh learning-curve model to separate a morphology's convergence speed (morphological intelligence) from its performance ceiling (true potential), then extends per-generation PPO training only until survivors are no longer biased toward fast learners. On voxel-based soft robots, a plain genetic algorithm with AdaControl matches exhaustive control at 65-81% less compute and rivals generative-model co-design baselines.

## Problem

- Prior co-design treats controller configuration as a fixed subjective choice, ignoring how inner-loop learning budgets reciprocally shape which morphologies survive across generations.
- Earlier morphological-intelligence measures conflate convergence speed with performance evaluated at arbitrary truncated iteration counts, hiding a morphology's ceiling from the selection process entirely.
- Existing evidence that simpler controllers raise diversity came from rigid robots on flat terrain with few-dozen-parameter controllers, leaving relevance to soft-body, learning-based co-design unclear.

## Method

- A ==tanh learning-curve model==, performance equals true potential times tanh of normalized iterations times convergence rate, whose two parameters are estimated per morphology by non-linear regression on observed curves.
- **AdaControl** runs ==pseudo natural selection== each generation, computes the ratio of survivor-average to population-average morphological intelligence, and extends all controllers by fixed increments until that ratio drops below a threshold.
- Population-level bias monitoring plugged into a plain ==genetic algorithm== outer loop with ==PPO== inner loop, replacing fixed iteration budgets and individual-level fitness-based schedulers such as FitControl.

## Results

- Averaged PPO iterations per evaluation fell to **1064**, **610**, and **584** on Carrier, Pusher, and BridgeWalker, cutting control-learning cost **65-81%** versus the **3000**-iteration exhaustive baseline.
- Across **250** top-5% morphologies, convergence speed and performance ceiling were uncorrelated (**Spearman rho 0.05**, **p 0.23**), confirming the two dimensions are orthogonal rather than redundant.
- AdaControl matched strong control on best true potential per evaluation, beat MorphVAE, LASeR, and FitControl on diversity, and stabilized survivor intelligence that rose steadily under **200**-iteration training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Truncated inner-loop evaluation is a selection bias, not just noise: it rewards fast learners and silently discards slower morphologies with higher ceilings.
> - The reported morphological Baldwin effect appears to be an artifact of premature control learning rather than a general tendency of embodied evolution.
> - Stricter bias thresholds hurt diversity by removing selection stochasticity, so principled evaluation, not search sophistication, is the real co-design bottleneck.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work is situated within the field of embodied intelligence and robot co-design, which explores the joint optimization of robot morphology (body) and control (brain) to achieve adaptive behaviors. This approach is inspired by observations in nature, where living organisms leverage their physical form and interaction with the environment to simplify the demands on their neural control systems, a concept termed morphological computation or morphological intelligence.

Prior research in robot co-design typically frames the problem as a bi-level optimization. The inner loop involves learning a controller for a given morphology and evaluating its performance, while the outer loop uses evolutionary algorithms to refine the morphological designs based on these fitness evaluations. A significant finding in this area has been the "morphological Baldwin effect," which suggests that evolution inherently favors morphologies that learn control faster. This phenomenon, reported in studies such as Gupta et al. (2021), implies a one-directional influence where morphology aids control learning. However, many co-design studies either fix control configurations arbitrarily or employ simplified controllers, potentially overlooking the reciprocal influence of control learning settings on the evolutionary trajectory of morphology.

Some preliminary work, such as Goff and Hart (2021), indicated that simpler controllers might promote higher morphological diversity in rigid robots, but the generalizability of these findings to more complex scenarios like soft robots with numerous control parameters remained unclear. Furthermore, alternative strategies like Lamarckian inheritance or policy transfer, aiming to mitigate the high computational cost of per-morphology control learning, have shown susceptibility to premature convergence, suggesting that dedicated per-morphology optimization remains a robust approach.

This paper addresses a gap in the literature by systematically investigating the two-way interaction between control learning and morphological evolution. It shifts focus from solely how morphology affects control learning (morphological intelligence) to also examine how the configuration of control learning, particularly its duration, reciprocally shapes the evolutionary process and the resulting morphological designs. By doing so, it provides a more holistic account of brain-body co-evolution within learning-based robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to provide a comprehensive understanding of the interplay between control learning and morphological evolution in robot co-design. This involves examining how the configuration of control learning influences the selection and progression of morphological designs across generations, a perspective largely underexplored in previous work.

The specific objectives and motivations are:

1. **To formally decompose morphological contributions to control learning:** The authors aim to go beyond a general understanding of morphological intelligence by distinguishing between the speed at which a controller learns for a given morphology and the maximum performance that morphology can ultimately achieve. This decomposition is motivated by the observation that fast initial learning does not necessarily correlate with high ultimate performance, a distinction that could be masked by prematurely terminated control learning in existing co-design frameworks.
2. **To quantify these morphological properties:** The research seeks to establish a mathematical framework to quantify "morphological intelligence" (convergence speed) and a new concept, "true potential" (performance ceiling), directly from observed control learning curves using non-linear regression. This quantification allows for systematic analysis of evolutionary dynamics at both individual and population levels.
3. **To investigate the impact of control complexity on evolutionary bias:** A central motivation is to determine if and how the duration of control learning (control complexity) influences natural selection. Specifically, the authors hypothesize that insufficient training may systematically undervalue morphologies with high true potential but slower learning rates, thereby biasing evolution towards fast learners.
4. **To re-evaluate the morphological Baldwin effect:** Based on the above hypothesis, the paper aims to determine if the widely recognized morphological Baldwin effect, which posits that evolution favors faster-learning morphologies, might be an artifact of prematurely curtailed control learning rather than a universal evolutionary principle.
5. **To develop an adaptive control learning algorithm:** Motivated by the potential biases identified, the authors propose an adaptive algorithm, AdaControl, designed to dynamically adjust control learning durations. This algorithm aims to ensure unbiased fitness evaluation by allocating sufficient, but not excessive, computational resources for control learning, thereby optimizing co-design efficiency and outcome.
6. **To enhance robot co-design performance:** Ultimately, the research seeks to demonstrate that a principled approach to scheduling control learning can lead to more efficient optimization, the discovery of higher-performing robots, and greater morphological diversity compared to methods that use fixed control complexities or rely on complex generative models. This addresses the significant computational bottleneck associated with the inner loop of bi-level co-design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a bi-level optimization framework for robot co-design, integrating established algorithms for morphological evolution and controller learning, and introduces a novel framework for quantifying morphological properties and an adaptive control learning algorithm.

#### 3.1. Robot Platform and Tasks

- **Robot Type:** Voxel-based Soft Robots (VSRs), simulated in the Evolution Gym (EvoGym) environment. VSRs consist of interconnected elastic cubic blocks (voxels) with different material properties (empty, rigid, soft, horizontal/vertical actuator).
- **Physics:** Modeled by a mass-spring system with cross-braced springs and penalty-based frictional contact.
- **Robot Structure:** 5x5 voxel grid, offering a large design space.
- **Tasks:** Three benchmark tasks from EvoGym: Carrier-v0, Pusher-v0 (object manipulation), and BridgeWalker-v0 (locomotion on deformable terrain).

#### 3.2. Co-design Framework

- **Outer Loop (Morphological Evolution):** A standard Genetic Algorithm (GA) is used. It maintains a population of VSR morphologies, evaluates their fitness, and selects top-performing individuals. These survivors undergo random voxel mutations to generate the next generation.
- **Inner Loop (Control Learning):** For each candidate morphology, a dedicated sensorimotor controller is optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm. Controllers are parameterized as multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs). Task performance, measured as cumulative episodic reward, serves as the morphology's fitness.

#### 3.3. Quantification of Morphological Properties

- **Definitions:**
 - **True Potential (A):** Defined as the upper limit of task performance a morphology can achieve after its controller's learning process has fully converged.
 - **Morphological Intelligence (MI, α):** Defined as the convergence speed of the control learning process towards the morphology's true potential.
- **Mathematical Model:** The dependence of task performance, $f(x, C)$, on morphology $x$ and control complexity $C$ (number of RL iterations) is modeled by a hyperbolic tangent function:
 $f(x, C) = A \cdot \tanh \left( \frac{C}{C^*} \cdot \alpha \right) + \epsilon$
 where $C^*$ is the number of iterations for full saturation (empirically set to 3000), and $\epsilon$ is a noise term.
- **Estimation:** Parameters $A$ and $\alpha$ are estimated for each morphology by performing non-linear regression (gradient descent) on its observed learning curve, minimizing the mean squared error between the model and actual performance data points.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup and Baselines

- **Control Complexity:** Fixed control complexities were tested by varying PPO iterations (200, 500, 1000, 2000, 3000). 3000 iterations were considered "strong control" as learning typically converged by this point.
- **Evaluation Budget:** All experiments were allocated 1000 robot evaluations.
- **Baselines:**
 - GA with various fixed PPO iteration counts.
 - MorphVAE and LASeR: State-of-the-art co-design methods based on deep generative models.
 - FitControl: An adaptive method that adjusts per-morphology training based on a target fitness improvement, adapted to a synchronous population-based framework.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Morphological Intelligence (MI):** Convergence speed.
 - **Maximal True Potential:** The true potential of the best morphology found, evaluated with strong controllers (3000 PPO iterations). This metric reflects optimization efficiency.
 - **Morphological Diversity:** Average pairwise edit distance among high-performing morphologies (top 5% by true potential).

#### 3.5. AdaControl Algorithm

- **Mechanism:** AdaControl is an adaptive algorithm that dynamically adjusts the number of PPO iterations for control learning in each generation.
- **Process:**
 1. Starts with a minimal number of PPO iterations (L=500).
 2. Performs a "pseudo selection" based on the current fitness, identifying tentative "survivors."
 3. Estimates the MI for all robots in the population.
 4. Calculates a ratio `r` of the average MI of these tentative survivors to the average MI of the entire population.
 5. If `r` exceeds a predefined threshold `r_thr` (set to 1.1) and the maximum iteration limit `U` (2000) has not been reached, all controllers receive an additional increment of training (I=100 iterations). Steps 2-5 are repeated.
 6. Once `r <= r_thr` or `iters >= U`, the control learning is deemed sufficient to ensure unbiased selection, and the genetic algorithm proceeds to mutation and generation of the next population.
- **Threshold Selection:** The `r_thr` was selected by sweeping values and analyzing their impact on morphological diversity and computational cost, aiming for a balance that maximizes diversity without excessive computation.

#### 3.6. Interpretability Analysis

- A preliminary investigation explored correlations between MI and physical attributes of the morphologies, such as energy efficiency (Cost of Work, COW), number of empty voxels, and number of soft voxels.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provided several key findings regarding the interaction between control learning and morphological evolution.

#### 4.1. Underestimation of True Potential and Evolutionary Bias

- **Validation of True Potential:** Experiments confirmed that 3000 PPO iterations were sufficient for most learning curves to converge (average convergence between 1731 and 2216 iterations across tasks), establishing a reliable benchmark for "true potential."
- **Underestimation by Weak Control:** Weak control complexities (200, 500, 1000 PPO iterations) consistently and statistically significantly underestimated the true potential of morphologies compared to strong control (Fig. 5). This underestimation decreased as the number of iterations increased, becoming insignificant at 2000 iterations.
- **Bias in Natural Selection:** Weak control complexities systematically biased natural selection. When survivors chosen by weak control were re-ranked by their true potential, their average rank was significantly lower, indicating a deviation from selecting truly optimal morphologies (Fig. 6).
- **Preference for Fast Learners:** Morphologies evolved under weak control (e.g., 200, 500 PPO iterations) exhibited steeper initial learning curves and higher average morphological intelligence (MI) compared to those evolved under strong control (Fig. 7, Fig. 8). Stronger control (2000, 3000 iterations) tolerated slower but ultimately higher-performing learners.
- **Reinterpretation of the Morphological Baldwin Effect:** The findings suggest that the morphological Baldwin effect, observed as an increasing MI trend, is primarily an artifact of prematurely terminated control learning. When control learning is insufficient to distinguish between fast initial learners and those with higher long-term potential, selection is biased towards the former.

#### 4.2. Impact on Co-Design Performance and AdaControl's Efficacy

- **Morphological Intelligence Dynamics:** Under weak control, population-average MI of survivors showed a consistent upward trend across generations, indicating a compounding bias towards fast learners. Strong control maintained a stable and moderate MI. AdaControl effectively stabilized MI growth, significantly reducing the bias compared to weak control (Fig. 9), while using substantially fewer PPO iterations than strong control.
- **Optimization Efficiency:**
 - **By Robot Evaluations:** AdaControl achieved maximal true potential comparable to strong control across all tasks (Fig. 10). It also demonstrated performance competitive with or superior to state-of-the-art generative-model-based co-design methods (MorphVAE, LASeR).
 - **By Cumulative PPO Iterations:** AdaControl exhibited superior computational efficiency, discovering higher-performing morphologies for the same cumulative PPO budget, or achieving comparable performance with up to 80% less computation compared to exhaustive strong control (Fig. 11, Table 2). This advantage stems from AdaControl's targeted allocation of learning resources.
 - **Comparison with FitControl:** AdaControl significantly outperformed FitControl, an individual-level adaptive scheduling method, both in terms of optimization efficiency and computational cost, suggesting the importance of population-level bias monitoring.
- **Morphological Diversity:**
 - Diversity of high-performing morphologies generally increased with higher fixed control complexity.
 - AdaControl achieved morphological diversity that substantially surpassed the trend predicted by its average computational cost, even exceeding the diversity of strong control while consuming less than half the computation in some tasks (Fig. 12). This indicates that AdaControl effectively promotes exploration of the design space by mitigating selection bias.
 - AdaControl also yielded higher diversity than generative-model-based baselines and FitControl.

#### 4.3. Threshold Selection for AdaControl

- The selection of $r_{thr} = 1.1$ for AdaControl was justified by an analysis showing it balanced MI bias correction with the preservation of selection stochasticity, leading to optimal morphological diversity. Stricter thresholds could reduce diversity despite higher computational investment, possibly by leading to premature convergence of selection (Fig. 13).

#### 4.4. Interpretability Analysis

- **Energy Efficiency:** Morphologies with higher MI exhibited lower Cost of Work (COW), suggesting that morphologically intelligent soft robots can exploit passive body-environment dynamics more effectively for energy-efficient behavior (Fig. 14a).
- **Structural Composition:** MI showed a non-monotonic (inverted U-shaped) relationship with the number of empty and soft voxels (Fig. 14b, 14c). This suggests that moderate compliance and sparsity facilitate control learning by reducing structural constraints and enabling adaptable deformations, but excessive sparsity or softness might introduce interaction dynamics that are too complex to be easily exploited by controllers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the fields of evolutionary robotics, embodied intelligence, and robot co-design, with both theoretical and practical implications.

#### 5.1. Fundamental Insights into Brain-Body Co-evolution

The work provides a more nuanced and holistic understanding of brain-body co-evolution. By formally decoupling morphological intelligence (convergence speed) from true potential (performance ceiling), the paper reveals that the interaction between body and control is multi-dimensional. This challenges the previous, often one-sided, view of morphological intelligence and establishes a framework for analyzing the reciprocal influence of learning configuration on evolutionary outcomes.

#### 5.2. Reinterpretation of the Morphological Baldwin Effect

A major theoretical impact is the reinterpretation of the morphological Baldwin effect. By demonstrating that this effect largely arises from prematurely terminated control learning, the paper suggests that it is not necessarily a universal principle reflecting evolution's inherent preference for faster learners, but rather an artifact of insufficient fitness evaluation. This re-contextualization has implications for understanding evolutionary dynamics in learning-based systems and calls for careful consideration of learning duration in future studies.

#### 5.3. Algorithmic Advancement for Efficient Robot Co-design

The proposed AdaControl algorithm represents a practical advancement in robot co-design. By adaptively scheduling control learning based on population-level morphological intelligence, AdaControl addresses the critical computational bottleneck of bi-level optimization.
- **Computational Efficiency:** AdaControl significantly reduces the computational cost of control learning (up to 80% compared to exhaustive training) while achieving comparable or superior optimization efficiency. This makes large-scale robot co-design more feasible.
- **Enhanced Performance and Diversity:** The algorithm not only helps in finding higher-performing designs but also promotes greater morphological diversity. This ability to explore a broader design space without excessive computational overhead is crucial for discovering novel and robust robot morphologies.
- **Prioritizing Evaluation Fidelity:** The results suggest that the fidelity of fitness evaluation (i.e., unbiased assessment of true potential) is a more fundamental determinant of co-design success than the sophistication of the morphological search strategy (e.g., using generative models). This insight could guide future research towards optimizing evaluation processes rather than solely focusing on advanced search algorithms.

#### 5.4. Future Directions

The research opens several avenues for future investigation:
- **Generalizability:** Verifying the findings across different morphological representations (e.g., rigid, modular robots), task domains (e.g., complex manipulation, dynamic locomotion), and physical robotic platforms would strengthen the general applicability of AdaControl and the underlying theoretical framework.
- **Adaptive Threshold Mechanisms:** The task-dependent nature of the optimal MI ratio threshold ($r_{thr}$) suggests the need for adaptive mechanisms that can self-calibrate this parameter during evolution, further enhancing the robustness and autonomy of AdaControl.
- **Broader Control Complexity:** Investigating how other aspects of control complexity, such as neural network architectures, learning algorithms, and reward function designs, interact with morphological evolution within this framework could yield deeper insights into brain-body co-evolution.

In summary, this work provides both a refined theoretical understanding of brain-body interplay in robot co-design and a practical, computationally efficient method for developing diverse and high-performing robots.
