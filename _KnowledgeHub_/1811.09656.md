# Hierarchical visuomotor control of humanoids

> [!summary] Summary
> DeepMind researchers developed a hierarchical visuomotor control system for 56-DoF humanoid agents, integrating egocentric vision and proprioception to select and sequence low-level motor skills. This system enabled the humanoid to solve complex navigation and foraging tasks, outperforming end-to-end reinforcement learning by effectively managing high-dimensional inputs and outputs.

## Problem

- Simultaneously managing high-dimensional visual input and high-dimensional action spaces for full-body humanoid control in reinforcement learning.
- Integrating perception, motor control, and memory for complex humanoid agents in a scalable and flexible manner.
- Addressing limitations of existing end-to-end reinforcement learning and hierarchical methods for robust and versatile visuomotor control of high-DoF humanoids.

## Method

- A hierarchical control system separates low-level proprioception-driven motor control from a high-level, vision-informed behavioral selection or modulation.
- Low-level 'control fragments' are generated by training distinct policies to track short motion capture clips (0.1 to 0.3 seconds), enabling fine-grained, random-access switching of micro-behaviors.
- A high-level controller, using egocentric camera images and an LSTM for temporal memory, learns to select and sequence these control fragments to solve complex, sparse-reward tasks.

## Results

- The control fragments approach consistently achieved the best performance across tasks such as 'Walls,' 'Forage,' and 'Gaps,' significantly outperforming end-to-end and other structured controllers.
- The baseline end-to-end approach only succeeded on the 'Go-to-target' task with jarring movements and failed entirely on more complex tasks like 'Forage.'
- The system successfully synthesized 12 different single-clip policies comprising 359 fragments for the 'Gaps' task, demonstrating scalability to diverse low-level skills, and enabled memory-dependent behavior in the 'Heterogeneous Forage' task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hierarchical control, particularly using fine-grained 'control fragments,' is more effective than end-to-end learning for complex visuomotor tasks in high-DoF humanoids.
> - The control fragment method offers a scalable way to create and manage large repertoires of low-level motor skills, reducing the need for laborious manual transition design.
> - Integrating memory (via LSTM) into the high-level controller allows agents to exhibit memory-dependent behavior, solving tasks that require retaining past information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a significant challenge in reinforcement learning (RL): simultaneously managing high-dimensional input spaces, such as visual perception, and high-dimensional action spaces, characteristic of robotic control for multi-degree-of-freedom (DoF) bodies. Previous advancements have enabled the training of high-dimensional vision-based policies for tasks like game-playing and navigation (e.g., Jaderberg et al., 2018; OpenAI, 2018; Wayne et al., 2018), and have improved RL methods for controlling high-DoF bodies in simulation, sometimes surpassing optimal control techniques. This paper aims to synthesize these advancements by tackling both high-dimensional input and output concurrently in the context of full-body visuomotor control for humanoids.

The research draws inspiration from multiple scientific and engineering domains. From neuroscience, it builds upon findings in motor control suggesting that locomotion and reaching are prestructured, with subcortical structures coordinating a motor repertoire and cortical systems using visual input to evoke complex movements (Flash & Hochner, 2005; Bizzi et al., 2008; Grillner et al., 2005).

In robotics, the concept of "movement primitives" has been explored since Ijspeert et al. (2002), with subsequent work focusing on learning, constructing, deploying, and sequencing these primitives (Ijspeert etal., 2003; Kober & Peters, 2009; Sentis & Khatib, 2005; Kober & Peters, 2014; Konidaris et al., 2012). Research has also addressed increasing the complexity of control inputs to these primitives (Neumann et al., 2014) and coupling them with external perceptual inputs (Kober et al., 2008).

The animation literature has similarly pursued physically simulated characters capable of distinct, flexibly sequenced movements. This includes early work like the "virtual stuntman" (Faloutsos et al., 2001b;a) and more recent advancements using RL to schedule "control fragments" for specialized short movements (Liu et al., 2012; Liu & Hodgins, 2017; 2018). Notably, prior animation work had not yet integrated visual input with these control fragments. In the context of RL, motor primitives and control fragments can be conceptualized as specialized instances of "option" sub-policies (Sutton et al., 1999).

This paper contributes to this multi-disciplinary landscape by demonstrating a concrete method for coupling control-fragment-like low-level movements with a vision and memory-based high-level controller to solve complex tasks. It also demonstrates the scalability of this approach to a greater number of control fragments than previously presented. The work aims to advance the development of integrated agents with vision, memory, and motor control capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to construct complex humanoid agents that can integrate perception, motor control, and memory. To manage the inherent complexity of this goal, the authors propose a factorization of the problem: separating low-level motor control, which primarily relies on proprioception, from high-level coordination of these skills, which is informed by visual perception.

The motivation behind this hierarchical approach is multifaceted:
- **Developing a Flexible Architecture:** The authors aim to develop an architecture that supports flexible, task-directed motor control for a relatively high-DoF humanoid body. This involves combining pre-training of specialized low-level motor controllers with a high-level, task-focused controller responsible for selecting and sequencing these low-level sub-policies.
- **Feasibility Assessment:** A key objective is to evaluate the feasibility of achieving full-body visuomotor control for humanoids by comparing various strategies for integrating vision with motor control.
- **Simplification of System Design:** The hierarchical structure is intended to simplify the engineering of a visuomotor system. By modularizing the control problem, the authors seek to reduce the overall complexity of task-directed exploration, which can be challenging in high-dimensional state and action spaces.
- **Leveraging Advances in Imitation Learning:** The approach builds upon recent advancements in imitation learning to create robust and flexible low-level motor controllers using motion capture data for humanoids. These pre-learned motor skills are then coordinated and sequenced by the high-level system, which is trained via reinforcement learning to optimize for sparse task rewards.
- **Scalability to a Wide Repertoire of Behaviors:** The authors are motivated to demonstrate that their approach can scale to a larger number of low-level control fragments than typically seen in prior work. This is critical for enabling a richer and more versatile range of behaviors in complex environments.
- **Advancing Integrated Agent Capabilities:** Ultimately, the research is motivated by the broader goal of making progress towards integrated intelligent agents that can effectively combine perceptual processing, memory-based reasoning, and sophisticated motor control to interact with dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a hierarchical control system for a humanoid agent. This system separates control into low-level motor coordination, driven by proprioception, and high-level behavioral selection or modulation, informed by vision and proprioception based on task context.

#### 3.1. Low-Level Motor Controller Creation

The low-level controllers are derived from motion capture (mocap) data.
- **Humanoid Body:** A 56-DoF humanoid model, actuated via position control, is used.
- **Single-Clip Tracking Policies:** For each short mocap clip (2-6 seconds), a distinct policy, denoted as π_θ(a|s, t), is trained. This policy's objective is to maximize a discounted sum of rewards (r_t) by tracking the reference motion. A normalized time variable `t` (0 to 1) is included in the state, resetting for cyclical behaviors. Episodes are initialized along the mocap trajectory and terminate if non-end-effector body parts contact the ground.
- **Reward Function:** The reward `r_t` is defined as `exp(-βE_total / w_total)`. `E_total` is a weighted sum of energy terms: `E_qpos` (joint angles), `E_qvel` (joint velocities), `E_ori` (body root quaternion), `E_ee` (end-effector vectors), `E_vel` (translational velocities), and `E_gyro` (root rotational velocities). These terms quantify the deviation from the reference motion, and `β` is a sharpness parameter (set to 10).
- **Proprioceptive Observations:** Low-level policies receive exclusively egocentric proprioceptive observations, including joint angles and velocities, root-to-end-effector vectors, root-frame velocimeter, rotational velocity, root-frame accelerometers, and 3D orientation relative to the z-axis.
- **Reinforcement Learning for Low-Level Policies:**
 - **Pre-training:** Policies are initially pre-trained using supervised learning to produce target poses from the mocap data, which aids subsequent RL convergence.
 - **Off-policy RL:** Training utilizes a distributed actor-critic setup with a replay buffer and target networks (similar to Hausman et al., 2018; Lillicrap et al., 2015; Heess et al., 2015).
 - **Q-function Learning:** The Q-function is learned off-policy using TD-learning with importance-weighted Retrace (Munos et al., 2016).
 - **Actor Learning:** The actor is learned off-policy using SVG(0) (Heess et al., 2015). Policy updates are performed conservatively, incorporating a term to restrict KL divergence between new and old policies (Heess et al., 2015; Schulman et al., 2017). A stochastic policy with fixed noise (σ = 0.1) is used, as learning variance led to premature convergence.

#### 3.2. Varieties of Low-Level Motor Control Schemes

The paper investigates different methods for structuring and combining these low-level controllers:
- **Structured Controllers:** These schemes involve a pre-designed relationship between selection variables and behavior.
 - **Steerable Controller:** A single locomotion policy (e.g., running) is augmented with an additional input: a turning radius. The policy is trained to track a mocap reference trajectory that has been parametrically distorted to include turning.
 - **Switching Controller:** A single policy is trained to switch between a discrete set of pre-defined behaviors (e.g., stand, run, turn left/right). Training involves random starts in mocap clips and transitions between compatible clips according to a hand-designed transition graph, typically at the end of gait cycles.
- **Cold-Switching of Behaviors and Control Fragments:** This approach defers the sequencing of behaviors to the high-level controller, without explicit training of transitions within the low-level policies.
 - **Control Fragments:** Mocap behaviors are segmented into very short "micro-behaviors" or control fragments (0.1 to 0.3 seconds). Each fragment represents a sub-policy associated with a specific temporal segment of a motion. This allows for fine-grained control and more flexible locomotion compared to switching full behaviors. This method is designed to scale easily to hundreds of fragments without extensive manual intervention, and the high-level controller performs random-access switching among them.

#### 3.3. High-Level (HL) Policy Training for Task Solving

The high-level controller integrates the low-level skills to solve complex tasks.
- **Architecture:** The HL controller receives proprioceptive data and egocentric RGB camera images. Proprioceptive inputs are encoded by a linear layer, images by a ResNet (Appendix A). These encoded streams, along with previous reward and action, are concatenated and fed into an LSTM for temporal memory and decision-making, ultimately outputting a stochastic policy and a value function.
- **Interface with Low-Level Controllers:**
 - For the steerable controller, the HL policy outputs a continuous 1D steering angle.
 - For switching and control fragment controllers, the HL policy outputs a 1-of-K index to select a discrete low-level policy or fragment.
- **HL Policy Training Details:**
 - Training is off-policy, using a replay buffer populated by data from distributed actors.
 - **Steerable Controller:** Policy is a Gaussian distribution for steering angle, trained with SVG(0). The Q-function is an MLP trained with Retrace.
 - **Switching/Control Fragments:** Policy is a multinomial distribution over discrete behaviors. Policy gradient is applied using N-step empirical returns with V-Trace for baseline estimation. The policy gradient loss is only active when a new action (behavior selection) is sampled (every `T` steps, where `T` is the duration of a gait cycle or fragment).
- **Query-Based Control Fragment Selection (Alternative):** An alternative interface, based on Wolpertinger (Dulac-Arnold et al., 2015), was explored. The HL policy would produce a continuous "query-action" vector, which is then compared against feature-key vectors of control fragments. The fragment whose key is nearest the query is selected, with Boltzmann exploration guided by Q-values. This approach aimed to make the high-level policy less precise by leveraging the Q-function for robust action selection.
- **Core Tasks:** The system was evaluated on tasks implemented in the MuJoCo physics engine (Todorov et al., 2012):
 - **Go-to-target:** Navigate to a target in an open plane.
 - **Walls:** Run forward while avoiding solid walls.
 - **Gaps:** Run forward, jumping between separated platforms.
 - **Forage:** Collect colored balls in a procedurally-generated maze.
 - **Heterogeneous Forage:** Collect balls with dynamically assigned positive/negative rewards, requiring memory to selectively collect positive ones.
 - All tasks, except Go-to-target, provided 64x64 egocentric camera images to the high-level agent.
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against an end-to-end RL approach (training a flat policy from scratch) and a simple rolling ball body to isolate motor control complexity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated various approaches to hierarchical visuomotor control across a set of core tasks, demonstrating differences in performance and flexibility.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison Across Approaches

- **Overall Superiority of Control Fragments:** The approach utilizing discrete switching among control fragments consistently demonstrated the best performance across the majority of the core tasks (Fig. 6).
- **End-to-End Control Limitations:** The baseline end-to-end approach, which trained a single flat policy from scratch, only achieved success on the Go-to-target task, and even then, its movements were described as visually jarring. This approach failed entirely on more complex tasks like Forage, indicating its inadequacy for integrated visuomotor control of high-DoF humanoids in challenging environments.
- **Structured Controller Performance:**
 - **Steerable Controller:** This controller could complete the Go-to-target task but suffered from inefficiencies. Its fixed turning radius prevented direct approaches to targets, leading to longer paths and lower scores. It was also capable of navigating the Walls task.
 - **Switching Controller (with hand-designed transitions):** This controller showed faster initial learning and produced visually smoother movements due to its predefined transitions. However, its predefined nature resulted in a lack of flexibility, limiting its asymptotic task performance. While it could traverse the maze in the Forage task, it struggled to make sharp turns required to collect all objects effectively. It also completed the Walls task.
- **Advantages of Control Fragments:**
 - **Walls Task:** The control fragments approach enabled sharper turns and quicker adjustments, allowing the agent to achieve higher velocities and better task completion.
 - **Forage Task:** This method facilitated the construction of complex maneuvers, including rotations and abrupt turns, which were critical for collecting objects efficiently within the maze.
 - **Gaps Task:** The control fragments approach successfully synthesized 12 different single-clip policies (including 8 jumps) comprising 359 fragments, enabling the agent to navigate between separated platforms. This demonstrated the scalability of the method to a larger, more diverse set of low-level skills without the laborious manual pre-training of transitions required by the switching controller.
 - **Heterogeneous Forage:** Equipping the high-level controller with an LSTM allowed the control fragments agent to exhibit memory-dependent behavior, successfully performing the task that required remembering reward values of different colored balls.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Control Fragment Characteristics

- **Fragment Length (Appendix B, Fig. A.4):** Experiments on Go-to-target indicated that shorter fragment lengths (1 or 3 time steps, corresponding to 0.03s or 0.09s) led to more stable training. While longer fragments sometimes showed faster initial learning, they generally resulted in lower ultimate performance. A fragment length of 3 time steps (0.09s) was found to offer a favorable balance between behavioral coherence and task performance.
- **Number of Fragments:** Increasing the number of fragments by duplicating existing ones did not negatively impact training performance. Furthermore, incorporating additional functionally redundant policies (e.g., 8 behaviors versus 4) improved training speed. The high-level controller demonstrated the ability to ignore extraneous fragments unrelated to locomotion.

#### 4.3. Query-Based Control Fragment Selection

- The alternative query-based approach for control fragment selection underperformed the discrete action selection method (Fig. 6). Analysis revealed that the policy's query actions lacked precision, exhibiting a high-variance proposal distribution (Fig. A.3). Additionally, the most preferred control fragment was not consistently the one with the most similar reference key, suggesting that the underlying premise of this approach (that similar fragments should be preferentially explored) may not have held in this specific context.

#### 4.4. Analysis of Trained High-Level Policies

- **Transition Patterns (Appendix C, Fig. A.5):** For a trained agent on the Forage task, the high-level controller with discrete selection generated structured transitions. Movements largely remained within the boundaries of a specific clip or behavior type. The agent learned to prefer certain fast-walk-and-turn movements over explicit transitions from standing to running or discrete left/right turns provided by some fragments.
- **Visual Saliency (Fig. 7):** Saliency maps, which visualize the intensity of the gradient of the selected action's log-probability with respect to input pixels, indicated that action selection was sensitive to the borders of the target balls and environmental walls. This suggests that the agent's perceptual representations were influenced by task goals and potential actions, demonstrating the emergence of sensorimotor affordances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of embodied AI and reinforcement learning by presenting a scalable and effective approach for hierarchical visuomotor control of high-DoF humanoid agents.

**Key areas of significance include:**

- **Addressing High-Dimensional Challenges:** The work demonstrates a viable method for tackling the dual challenges of high-dimensional perceptual input (egocentric vision) and high-dimensional motor action spaces in complex humanoid agents. It provides a concrete example of how to integrate these elements for task completion.
- **Validation of Hierarchical Control:** A significant finding is the clear superiority of hierarchical control over end-to-end learning for complex humanoid tasks. The hierarchical approach enabled agents to solve tasks (e.g., Forage, Gaps) that a flat, end-to-end policy failed to address, highlighting the benefits of structured learning and skill reuse.
- **Scalable Skill Repertoires:** The introduction and successful deployment of "control fragments" represent a technical contribution to creating and managing large repertoires of low-level motor skills. This method reduces the reliance on laborious manual curation and explicit transition design, which has traditionally limited the scalability of skill-based approaches in robotics and animation.
- **Ecological Observation Setting:** By moving from hand-designed observation features to an egocentric, front-facing camera, the research advances towards a more ecologically valid observation setting for embodied agents, aligning closer to how real-world robots perceive their environment.
- **Integration of Capabilities:** The demonstration of memory-dependent control behavior in the Heterogeneous Forage task, coupled with visual perception and motor control, marks progress towards integrated intelligent agents capable of addressing tasks requiring a combination of perceptual, cognitive, and physical challenges.
- **Bridge to Robotics and Animation:** The work serves as a bridge between the RL community and the robotics/animation fields. It builds upon concepts like movement primitives and control fragments, while advancing them through data-driven machine learning, neural network parameterization, and deep RL techniques, moving beyond sampling-based planners or linear-feedback policies.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper identifies several avenues for future work, including refining the naturalness of motor skills for broader environmental interactions, developing methods to automatically mitigate movement artifacts (such as jitter from frequent switching) without human intervention, and exploring more complicated, compositional tasks. The potential of query-based action selection, despite its initial underperformance, is also acknowledged as an area for further investigation.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art in visuomotor control for humanoids by demonstrating a practical and scalable hierarchical framework, contributing to the development of more capable and intelligent embodied agents.
