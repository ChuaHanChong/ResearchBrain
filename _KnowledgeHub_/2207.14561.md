# Cyclic Policy Distillation: Sample-Efficient Sim-to-Real Reinforcement Learning with Domain Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Nara Institute of Science and Technology developed Cyclic Policy Distillation (CPD) to enhance sample efficiency and stability in zero-shot sim-to-real reinforcement learning for robotic control. This method partitions randomized domains, cyclically learns local policies with knowledge transfer, and distills them into a global policy, enabling successful real-world transfer for tasks like ball dispersal.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) with Domain Randomization (DR) suffers from high sample inefficiency and unstable learning when tackling complex tasks with broad parameter randomization ranges.
- The 'reality gap' between simulation and the real world makes zero-shot sim-to-real transfer challenging without further real-world interaction.
- Existing methods for improving DR's efficiency either require real-world samples, struggle with curriculum design, or are inefficient due to independent learning within sub-domains.

## Method

- The proposed **Cyclic Policy Distillation** (CPD) method divides the randomized domain into multiple sub-domains with similar adjacent ranges.
- It employs a cyclic learning process for local policies, incorporating a monotonic policy improvement scheme that dynamically mixes policies from neighboring sub-domains to enhance knowledge transfer.
- After local policies converge, they are distilled into a single global policy for zero-shot deployment to the real world, avoiding unstable simultaneous distillation.

## Results

- CPD achieved up to 6 times higher sample efficiency and consistently outperformed baseline methods in total reward across various simulation tasks (Pendulum, Pusher, Swimmer, HalfCheetah).
- In a real-robot ball-dispersal task, CPD demonstrated effective zero-shot transfer, successfully knocking down 13/14 juggling balls and 11/14 bead balls, significantly surpassing SAC-DR and SAC baselines.
- The method proved robust to different domain partitioning patterns (plane, edge, grid) and exhibited reasonable learning costs compared to other advanced multi-domain RL techniques.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically optimizing the policy mixture rate (`m`) between current and neighboring sub-domain policies improves sample efficiency and total reward, outperforming static or no mixing.
> - A cyclic transition order between sub-domains, rather than random transitions, is crucial for effective knowledge transfer and learning efficiency.
> - Distilling *converged* local policies into a global policy is more stable and effective for zero-shot transfer compared to attempting distillation with incomplete or concurrently learning policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising methodology for enabling robots to autonomously acquire control policies through interaction with environments. A significant area within this field is "sim-to-real," where policies are learned in simulations and subsequently transferred to real-world applications. Within sim-to-real, "zero-shot learning" is a key focus, aiming to deploy policies learned purely in simulation directly to real-world environments without further real-world training.

A primary challenge in zero-shot sim-to-real is the "reality gap"—discrepancies between simulation and the real world. Domain randomization (DR) is a widely adopted technique to mitigate this gap by randomizing simulation parameters (e.g., physics, sensors) over a wide range during training, thereby encouraging the learned policy to be robust to variations present in the real world. However, as tasks become more complex, requiring broader randomization ranges and more parameters, traditional DRL with DR often faces significant issues:
- **Sample Inefficiency:** A large number of samples are required to learn an effective policy.
- **Learning Instability:** Policy updates can become unstable due to the vast and frequently changing parameter spaces, leading to gradient instability.

These challenges have led to the exploration of "few-shot learning" approaches, such as differentiable simulators and system identification methods. These techniques adjust simulation parameters or policies by utilizing a limited amount of real-world data. While they can achieve sample efficiency, they necessitate interaction with the real world, potentially leading to human-in-the-loop settings and complicating deployment in environments that are difficult to set up or initialize (e.g., granular material tasks).

This research positions itself as a development within the zero-shot DRL with DR paradigm, aiming to improve its sample efficiency and learning stability without requiring real-world samples during training. Specifically, it focuses on dynamics randomization, as opposed to visual randomization. Existing methods to enhance sample efficiency in DRL with DR often fall into three categories:
1. Limiting the range of randomized parameters.
2. Employing curriculum learning to gradually expand the domain range.
3. Dividing the full domain into several sub-domains and learning local policies.

The paper identifies that these existing methods suffer from issues such as the need for real-world samples for posterior range adjustment, difficulty in curriculum design, or inefficiency due to independent learning within sub-domains. The current work aims to address the inefficiency resulting from independence by proposing a mechanism for structured sharing of learned values and policies among sub-domains, particularly focusing on the idea that similar sub-domains (i.e., those with similar parameters) should have similar Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), enabling beneficial knowledge transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the sample efficiency and learning stability of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) with domain randomization (DR) for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. This objective is driven by the observation that traditional DRL with DR often requires an extensive number of samples and suffers from unstable policy updates when the range of randomized parameters is broad, especially in complex tasks.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and identified gaps in current sim-to-real approaches:
- **Mitigating Sample Inefficiency:** The necessity for vast quantities of simulation data to train robust policies under wide domain randomization ranges makes the learning process resource-intensive and slow. The authors aim to develop a method that reduces the sample requirements to achieve effective policy learning.
- **Addressing Learning Instability:** The frequent switching of randomized parameters in DR can lead to considerable sample variation and volatile policy update directions, causing instability and hindering performance. The research seeks a mechanism to stabilize the learning process.
- **Improving Knowledge Transfer between Sub-domains:** While previous methods divide large randomized domains into smaller sub-domains to manage complexity, they often fail to leverage potential synergies between these sub-domains. A core motivation is the hypothesis that if sub-domains are similar (e.g., "neighboring" in parameter space), their corresponding MDPs, values, and policies might also be similar. This similarity could be exploited to accelerate learning through knowledge transfer, rather than treating sub-domains as entirely independent entities.
- **Achieving Robust Zero-Shot Transfer:** The goal is to learn policies purely in simulation that can be directly and effectively applied to real-world environments without any real-world training or fine-tuning, thereby avoiding the complexities and costs associated with real-world interaction.
- **Application to Complex Manipulation Tasks:** The paper is particularly motivated by challenging tasks involving multiple particulate objects, such as powder manipulation or soil excavation. These tasks are difficult for DRL due to issues like automatic initialization, enormous patterns of observation samples, and irregular object shapes. The chosen "ball-dispersal task" serves as a simplified, yet representative, environment to demonstrate the method's applicability to such complex scenarios.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a novel scheme called Cyclic Policy Distillation (CPD) that leverages structured domain partitioning, cyclic learning, and knowledge transfer based on expected performance improvements, culminating in the distillation of local policies into a single global policy for sim-to-real deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Cyclic Policy Distillation (CPD), is designed to improve the sample efficiency and stability of DRL for zero-shot sim-to-real learning with domain randomization. CPD consists of three main steps: dividing the randomized domain into sub-domains, cyclically learning local policies, and distilling these local policies into a single global policy. The Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm serves as the base DRL method, and both the actor and critic networks incorporate LSTM layers to handle dynamic parameter distinction.

#### 3.1. Dividing a Randomized Domain into Sub-domains

CPD begins by partitioning the full range of randomized parameters into *N* smaller sub-domains. This division is performed based on a "target domain parameter," meaning a specific parameter or set of parameters is chosen for explicit splitting. The crucial design choice is that sub-domains with adjacent index numbers are configured to have similar randomization ranges. This similarity is fundamental for the subsequent knowledge transfer mechanism. The number of divisions, *N*, is a configurable parameter that involves a trade-off between sample efficiency and learning stability; a smaller *N* means larger sub-domain ranges, potentially leading to instability, while a larger *N* might increase overall computational cost.

#### 3.2. Cyclic Learning of All Local Policies

For each sub-domain, a dedicated "sub-agent" is assigned, comprising a local policy (π^(n)) and a local value function (Q^(n)). The learning process for these local policies is performed cyclically across the sub-domains, with knowledge transfer occurring between neighboring sub-domains.

**Execution Flow:**
1. **Initialization:** When transitioning to a new sub-domain *n*, the local value function Q^(n) is initialized by copying Q^(n-1) from the previously learned sub-domain.
2. **Interaction and Update:** The agent interacts with the current sub-domain, collecting samples (s^(n), a^(n), r^(n)). The local value function Q^(n) and local policy π^(n) are updated using the underlying DRL algorithm (SAC).
3. **Cyclic Transition:** The learning process proceeds by cyclically transitioning between sub-domains. This involves a "forward transition" from n=1 to N, followed by a "backward transition" from n=N to 1. This entire cycle is repeated until convergence.
4. **Monotonic Policy Improvement (Policy Mixing):** To accelerate learning and ensure stability, CPD incorporates a monotonic policy-improvement scheme inspired by Conservative Policy Iteration (CPI). This scheme updates the current local policy π^(n) by linearly mixing it with a *neighboring* local policy π^(n') (from the previous sub-domain in the cycle). The mixture rate *m* is dynamically determined. Unlike the original CPI, which computes *m* using maximum reward and stationary distribution, CPD estimates *m* using a method experimentally shown to work well in recent studies. The overall loss function for updating local policy π^(n) combines the standard DRL loss (L_RL) with a policy mixing loss (L_MI), which is based on the KL divergence between the current policy and the mixed policy. The term *m* is designed to evaluate the expected performance improvement by mixing the current policy with the neighboring one, where *m=0* means no mixing and *m=1* means direct copying.

#### 3.3. Global Distillation

After the cyclic learning of all local policies has converged, these *N* learned local policies (π^(n)) are integrated into a single global policy (π^(g)). This distillation step is performed explicitly *after* the local policies are learned, which contrasts with prior methods that attempt simultaneous learning and distillation of incomplete policies. The authors argue that distilling incomplete policies can lead to unstable global policy updates and increased learning times.

The global distillation process involves iterating the following steps until convergence:
1. **Rollout Collection:** Rollouts (state-action-reward sequences) are collected in each sub-domain *n*, but notably, they are collected using the *global policy π^(g)*. This is done for sample efficiency, as rollouts from a single global policy can be more efficient than those from multiple local policies.
2. **Local Policy Evaluation:** The action probabilities of the *learned local policies* π^(n) are evaluated for the observations collected using π^(g).
3. **Global Policy Distillation:** The global policy π^(g) is then updated (distilled) to mimic the behaviors of the local policies. This is achieved by optimizing a loss function based on the KL divergence between the global policy π^(g) and each of the local policies π^(n) across all sub-domains.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness and sample efficiency of Cyclic Policy Distillation (CPD) were evaluated through both simulation experiments (Pendulum, Pusher, Swimmer, HalfCheetah) and a real-robot ball-dispersal task.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiment Results: Effect of Policy Mixing (Pendulum Task)

The study investigated the impact of the policy mixture rate `m` in Eq. (7) on learning performance:
- `m = opt` (dynamically optimized rate): Achieved the highest sample efficiency and a 10% higher final total reward compared to other settings.
- `m = 0` (no exploitation of neighboring policies): Performed better than `m = 1`.
- `m = 1` (direct copying of neighboring policies): Resulted in lower performance, indicating that naive exploitation of neighboring policies can be detrimental due to potential domain gaps.
- The dynamically updated `m` (m=opt) typically started low in early learning stages (when neighboring policies were less informative) and increased as learning progressed. It decayed almost monotonically within a fixed sub-domain, suggesting a smooth transition towards using current sub-domain samples. Sharp rises corresponded to sub-domain transitions.

#### 4.1.2. Evaluation of Number and Method of Divided Ranges (Pendulum Task)

- **Number of Partitions (N):**
 - For N >= 4, final performance was comparable.
 - At 54,000 samples (halfway through training), N <= 4 yielded higher performance with significant differences, suggesting a trade-off. N=4 was selected for subsequent experiments as it provided the best balance of sample efficiency and final performance.
- **Partitioning Method (Plane, Edge, Grid):**
 - CPD demonstrated robustness to different partitioning patterns (plane, edge, grid), showing no significant performance differences in terms of total reward and sample efficiency. The "plane" method was used for subsequent experiments.

#### 4.1.3. Comparison with Other Methods and Ablation Studies (Pendulum, Pusher, Swimmer, HalfCheetah)

CPD was compared against baseline methods (SAC-DR, P2PDRL, DnC, DiDoR) and ablation studies (CPD(m=0), CPD(rand)):
- **Overall Performance:** CPD consistently achieved the highest total reward and sample efficiency across all four simulation tasks.
 - For Pendulum, CPD was 3 to 6 times more sample-efficient than other methods. SAC, P2PDRL, and DiDoR achieved significantly lower total rewards.
 - For HalfCheetah, CPD was the only method that learned effective policies within a practical number of samples. SAC-DR, while fast initially, reached only about 40% of CPD's maximum reward.
 - For Pusher and Swimmer, CPD also demonstrated superior total reward and sample efficiency.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - CPD (rand), which transitions between sub-domains randomly instead of cyclically, showed less sample efficiency than CPD (m=0), which did not exploit neighboring policies at all. This highlights the importance of the cyclic, neighboring sub-domain transition in CPD.
 - CPD (m=0) performed worse than the full CPD, indicating the effectiveness of the policy mixing mechanism.

#### 4.1.4. Learning Cost

- CPD exhibited moderate learning time, being approximately 24% slower than SAC-DR (which has no distillation) but 30% faster than P2PDRL, and also faster than DnC. Its learning cost is reasonable given its performance benefits.

#### 4.2. Real-Robot Experiment Results (Ball-Dispersal Task)

The ball-dispersal task, involving knocking down a pile of balls, was used to validate CPD's effectiveness in a real-world zero-shot setting. This task presents challenges due to the innumerable ball-placement patterns and initialization difficulties.
- **Learning Performance (Simulation):** SAC-DR converged faster initially but quickly became unstable and achieved a lower total reward. CPD, in contrast, showed stable performance improvement, achieving approximately 40% better total reward than other baselines in simulation.
- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Policies learned in simulation were tested directly on a real robot with two types of balls (juggling and bead balls).
 - **CPD:** Successfully knocked down almost all balls (13/14 for juggling, 11/14 for beads), demonstrating effective zero-shot transfer despite scale and noise differences between simulation and reality.
 - **SAC-DR:** Only managed to knock down about half of the juggling balls (6/14) and largely failed for the more difficult bead balls (1/14).
 - **SAC (without DR):** Failed completely, unable to even reach the balls (0/14 for both).
- Snapshots confirmed that CPD's learned policy could generalize from randomized simulation observations (with varied scale and noise) to the real-world environment, successfully performing the ball-dispersal task.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research on Cyclic Policy Distillation (CPD) presents a significant advancement in the field of sim-to-real reinforcement learning, particularly for zero-shot transfer with domain randomization. Its contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Improved Sample Efficiency and Learning Stability for Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real:** CPD directly addresses two critical limitations of DRL with domain randomization: the high sample requirements and learning instability when dealing with wide parameter ranges. By segmenting the domain and employing structured knowledge transfer, the method enables more efficient and stable learning, which is crucial for practical robotic applications where extensive real-world data collection is infeasible or costly.
- **Effective Knowledge Transfer Mechanism:** The core innovation of cyclic learning and policy mixing between *neighboring* sub-domains, guided by a monotonic policy-improvement scheme, provides a novel and effective way to share learned information. This avoids the pitfalls of naive knowledge transfer (which can degrade performance) and the inefficiencies of completely independent sub-domain learning. This structured approach to leveraging similarities across sub-domains enhances the generalization capability of local policies.
- **Robust Generalization to the Real World:** The demonstration of CPD's ability to achieve high success rates in a complex real-robot ball-dispersal task, even with different types of balls and significant sim-to-real observation gaps (scale, noise in depth images), underscores its potential for robust zero-shot transfer. This indicates that policies learned purely in simulation can be directly deployed to real-world scenarios without any real-world training, a highly desirable trait in robotics.
- **Applicability to Challenging Manipulation Tasks:** The successful application to the ball-dispersal task, which is presented as a simplified analog for handling granular materials (like sand or powder) and addresses difficulties such as numerous object patterns and complex initialization, broadens the scope of DRL in areas previously considered very challenging. This opens new avenues for autonomous manipulation in unstructured and deformable environments.
- **Methodological Contribution to Multi-Domain RL:** CPD contributes a practical framework for learning in environments characterized by multiple, varied domains. The cyclic transition between sub-domains and the late-stage global distillation strategy provide a structured alternative to simultaneous learning and distillation, which can be prone to instability.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies areas for future work, such as automating the selection of division numbers and parameters, parallelizing the learning process, and providing theoretical justifications for monotonic policy improvement in randomized environments. These discussions indicate the potential for further development and refinement of the CPD framework.

In summary, CPD offers a principled and empirically validated approach to make zero-shot sim-to-real DRL more practical and effective for complex robotic tasks, laying groundwork for more robust autonomous systems operating in variable and uncertain real-world conditions.
