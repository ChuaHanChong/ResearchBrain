# S2A2: Audio-Visual Imitation Learning for Manipulation Tasks Using Acoustic Spatial Information

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Kyoto University and RIKEN developed the Spatial-Spectral Audio Action (S2A2) framework, an audio-visual imitation learning approach that integrates visual features with both acoustic spatial maps and spectrograms. This framework enabled robots to perform manipulation tasks requiring acoustic cues when visual information was ambiguous or insufficient, demonstrating higher success rates in simulation and real-world robot experiments.

## Problem

- Vision-centric robotic manipulation struggles with partial observability due to occlusions, lighting variations, visually identical objects, or non-visual internal object states.
- Prior acoustic-aware manipulation largely utilized single-channel audio for signal information, neglecting rich acoustic spatial cues from sound source localization.
- Advanced robot audition techniques for extracting spatial acoustic information from microphone arrays were not directly integrated into manipulation policy learning for complex tasks.

## Method

- Proposed the **Spatial-Spectral Audio Action (S2A2)** framework, a multimodal imitation learning system that fuses visual features with ==acoustic spatial maps== and ==noise-suppressed spectrograms==.
- Acoustic spatial maps are generated from multi-channel microphone array data using the ==MUSIC method==, projected onto a 2D workspace grid.
- Spectrograms are enhanced via ==Spotforming==, a source separation technique, to focus on the acoustic signal of the identified sound source candidate.

## Results

- The S2A2 framework consistently achieved the highest success rates across four acoustic-aware manipulation tasks in simulation, especially for tasks requiring both acoustic spatial and signal information.
- Latent feature visualizations demonstrated that S2A2 models generated more structured and separable representations of sound types and spatial coordinates, correlating with improved task performance.
- Real-robot experiments validated S2A2's effectiveness, showing the framework with the ACT policy significantly outperformed vision-only baselines in complex Localization & Identification and Exploratory tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Both acoustic spatial and signal information provide distinct, complementary cues to visual perception, significantly enhancing robotic manipulation capabilities in visually ambiguous scenarios.
> - The effectiveness of integrating acoustic information is task-dependent; including unnecessary acoustic modalities or removing relevant ones can lead to performance degradation if not matched to task requirements.
> - The choice of multimodal policy architecture impacts the efficiency of acoustic information integration, with some architectures (e.g., ACT, Diffusion Policy) showing more substantial gains than others.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has significantly advanced through imitation learning, where robots acquire complex skills from human demonstrations. Recent developments in deep learning have enabled visuomotor policies that directly map high-dimensional image observations and robot states to continuous actions, facilitating diverse manipulation tasks. However, a fundamental limitation of many existing imitation learning approaches is their reliance on visual observability. These methods often assume that manipulation targets are consistently visible and that task goals can be visually inferred. This assumption breaks down in common real-world scenarios involving lighting variations, occlusions, the presence of visually identical or similar objects, or when an object's internal state or interaction-induced events are not visually apparent.

To address this partial observability, research has explored the integration of non-visual modalities such as touch and sound. Acoustic information, in particular, offers rich, complementary cues that are not conveyed by object appearance alone. Sounds generated by contact or collision can reveal object material properties and contact states. Furthermore, sounds accompanying interactions like pouring, shaking, or flowing can provide insights into quantities or flow rates of materials. Importantly, robots can actively explore their environment by eliciting acoustic cues through actions such as shaking or tapping objects, revealing information otherwise unavailable through static visual observation.

Prior research in acoustic-aware robotic manipulation has primarily focused on using single-channel audio from contact microphones or microphones near the gripper to identify timbre, contact events, or object states. Concurrently, the field of robot audition has focused on sound source localization, tracking, and separation using microphone arrays, typically applied to tasks like dialogue, navigation, and source tracking. However, the integration of advanced spatial acoustic information, derived from microphone arrays, directly into manipulation policies for imitation learning remains limited. This work aims to bridge this gap by proposing a framework that leverages both acoustic spatial information (where a sound originates) and acoustic signal information (what the sound signifies) alongside visual data for robust manipulation, particularly in scenarios where visual cues are insufficient.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of vision-centric robotic manipulation, particularly when tasks involve objects that are visually ambiguous, occluded, or require an understanding of their internal state or dynamic interactions. In such contexts, visual information alone provides incomplete observability, hindering a robot's ability to accurately identify targets, determine goals, or plan exploratory actions. Acoustic information presents a valuable, underexploited modality that can augment visual perception.

The authors identified that acoustic information inherently contains two distinct yet complementary types of cues:
1. **Acoustic Signal Information:** What sound is being produced, reflecting properties like material, contact type, or internal state. This is typically captured as a spectrogram.
2. **Acoustic Spatial Information:** Where a sound originates, providing location cues crucial for target selection. This requires techniques like sound source localization using microphone arrays.

Existing approaches to integrating acoustics in robot manipulation largely utilize only signal information, often from single-channel microphones. Conversely, advanced robot audition techniques capable of extracting spatial acoustic information from microphone arrays have primarily been applied to navigation or human-robot interaction, with limited direct application in manipulation policy learning. This gap motivated the development of a framework that can effectively fuse these diverse acoustic data types with visual information for complex manipulation tasks.

Against this background, the key objectives of this work were threefold:

1. **Introduce Acoustic-Aware Manipulation Tasks:** To systematically investigate the utility of acoustic information, the authors defined a novel set of manipulation tasks. These tasks are specifically designed such that the manipulation target, destination, or required active exploration cannot be uniquely determined from visual information alone, thus necessitating the integration of acoustic spatial and/or signal information.
2. **Propose the Spatial-Spectral Audio Action (S2A2) Framework:** To address the limitations of vision-only or single-channel acoustic approaches, the authors aimed to develop a multimodal imitation learning framework, S2A2. This framework integrates visual features with both acoustic spatial maps (representing sound source likelihood and position derived from microphone arrays) and spectrograms (representing sound signal information).
3. **Systematically Evaluate Acoustic Information Contribution:** The research sought to quantitatively assess how acoustic information contributes to policy learning. This involved evaluating the S2A2 framework and its variants (with and without specific acoustic pipelines) on the newly defined acoustic-aware tasks, using multiple policy architectures, in both simulated and real-world robotic environments. The goal was to verify the effectiveness of the proposed acoustic representations in practical manipulation scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on the Spatial-Spectral Audio Action (S2A2) framework, designed for acoustic-aware manipulation tasks where visual information alone is insufficient for decision-making.

**Problem Formulation:**
The problem is framed as an imitation learning task. The robot learns a policy $\pi_\theta$ that predicts a sequence of actions $a_{t:t+H-1}$ given an observation $o_t$. The observation $o_t$ comprises an RGB image $I_t$, multi-channel acoustic information $A_t$, and proprioception $s_t$. From $A_t$, two key acoustic representations are extracted: an acoustic spatial map $S_t$ and a spectrogram $G_t$. The policy is trained to minimize an imitation learning loss (e.g., behavioral cloning loss) over a dataset of expert trajectories.

**Acoustic-Aware Manipulation Tasks:**
Four specific manipulation tasks were designed to evaluate the contribution of different acoustic capabilities:
1. **Localization Task:** The robot must grasp one of two visually indistinguishable objects, only one of which emits a continuous sound. This task primarily requires acoustic spatial information.
2. **Identification Task:** The robot grasps a single object that emits one of two distinct sounds and places it into a box corresponding to that sound. This task requires acoustic signal (timbre) identification.
3. **Localization & Identification (L&I) Task:** Two visually indistinguishable objects are present, with one continuously emitting one of two sounds. The robot must identify the sounding object (localization) and place it in the box corresponding to its sound (identification). This task requires both acoustic spatial and signal information.
4. **Exploratory Task:** Two visually indistinguishable objects are present, neither emitting sound at rest. Only one emits sound when moved. The robot must actively grasp and shake objects to identify the sounding one and place it in a designated box. This task involves active exploration in addition to acoustic spatial and signal processing.

**Processing Pipeline:**
The S2A2 framework incorporates three distinct processing pipelines for visual, acoustic spatial, and acoustic signal information:

1. **Acoustic Spatial Map Pipeline:**
 - **Input:** Multi-channel acoustic data from microphone arrays placed on the workspace plane.
 - **Processing:** Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) is applied. The Multiple Signal Classification (MUSIC) method is then used on the multi-channel signal of each array to estimate the likelihood of sound source direction (Direction-of-Arrival or DOA score).
 - **Output:** The log-normalized DOA scores are projected onto a 224x224 2D map corresponding to the workspace, incorporating a distance-based attenuation term from each array center. This results in an N-channel acoustic spatial map ($S$), where N is the number of arrays.
 - **Encoding:** A ResNet-18 model, with input channel count adjusted to N, serves as the spatial encoder, trained from scratch to produce a feature map.

2. **Spectrogram Pipeline:**
 - **Input:** Multi-channel acoustic data and the acoustic spatial map from the previous pipeline.
 - **Processing:** The acoustic spatial map is used to compute source presence likelihood, and peak extraction identifies the most likely sound source candidate. Spotforming, a technique for source separation that combines multiple arrays with Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF), is then applied to the identified source candidate to generate a spectrogram. This emphasizes the acoustic signal information at the target position.
 - **Output:** A noise-suppressed single-channel log-power spectrogram ($G$).
 - **Encoding:** A ResNet-18 with a single input channel, trained from scratch, acts as the spectral encoder, producing a feature map.

3. **Visual Information Pipeline:**
 - **Input:** RGB images from cameras (front and side in simulation, top-down and end-effector in real robot).
 - **Processing & Encoding:** The visual information directly feeds into the vision encoder component of the chosen multimodal policy network.

**Multimodal Policy:**
The features from the visual, acoustic spatial, and spectrogram pipelines, along with proprioception, are integrated by a multimodal policy. The study evaluated four distinct policy architectures for action generation:
- **ACT (Action Chunk Transformer):** Uses a transformer encoder-decoder with a VAE for action sequence mapping.
- **Diffusion Policy:** Employs a U-Net as a noise prediction model within a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) framework.
- **VQ-BeT (Vector Quantized Behavior Transformer):** A decoder-only transformer that generates actions represented by discrete codes from a Residual Quantized Variational Autoencoder (RQ-VAE).
- **$\pi_0$ (Vision-Language-Action Flow Model):** A large-scale pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that uses Flow Matching for action generation.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Simulation:** Utilized an integrated environment combining the Genesis physics simulator and the Pyroomacoustics acoustic simulator. A Franka Emika Panda robot with two cameras (front and side) and four circular microphone arrays (8 microphones each) positioned around the workspace was used. Experiments compared the full S2A2 model against a vision-only baseline, a "w/o Spec." variant (vision + acoustic spatial), and a "w/o Spat." variant (vision + acoustic signal). Success rate over 100 trials was the primary metric. Latent feature visualizations using t-SNE were also conducted.
- **Real-Robot:** Experiments were performed on the right arm of an ILOHA bimanual manipulator. Visuals were captured by a top-down and an end-effector mounted RealSense camera. Acoustic data were collected from four TAMAGO-03 circular microphone arrays placed at the corners of a rectangular workspace. Evaluation focused on the L&I and Exploratory tasks, comparing the S2A2 model with ACT against a vision-only baseline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted in both simulation and real-robot environments provided insights into the effectiveness and characteristics of the S2A2 framework.

**Simulation Experiments:**

1. **Task Success Rate Comparison:**
 - The S2A2 framework, utilizing all three processing pipelines (visual, acoustic spatial, and acoustic signal), consistently achieved the highest success rates across all four acoustic-aware manipulation tasks.
 - For tasks primarily requiring only one type of acoustic information, such as the Localization task (acoustic spatial) or the Identification task (acoustic signal), models incorporating only the relevant acoustic pipeline (e.g., "w/o Spec." for Localization, "w/o Spat." for Identification) performed comparably to the full S2A2 model.
 - The L&I task, which necessitates both acoustic spatial and signal information, showed a notable advantage for the complete S2A2 model, achieving significantly higher success rates compared to models lacking either acoustic component or the vision-only baseline.
 - The results indicated that including acoustic processing pipelines that did not provide task-relevant information, or removing necessary acoustic components, could degrade performance, sometimes even below the vision-only baseline. This suggests that under conditions of limited demonstration data, redundant or mismatched acoustic inputs might hinder policy learning.
 - The degree of performance improvement attributed to acoustic information varied depending on the underlying policy architecture. ACT and Diffusion Policy exhibited substantial improvements over the baseline, while VQ-BeT showed more modest gains. The $\pi_0$ policy's performance was inconsistent, showing strong results on some tasks (e.g., Identification) but significantly lower on others (e.g., Exploratory). This implies that the efficiency of integrating acoustic information is sensitive to the policy's design and its capacity to process and utilize multimodal cues.

2. **Latent Feature Visualization (t-SNE):**
 - Visualization of intermediate-layer latent features (e.g., from Diffusion Policy on the L&I task) demonstrated that the S2A2 model produced latent spaces where distinct sound types and spatial coordinates (e.g., Y coordinate of the target object) were relatively well-separated and structured. This structured representation in the latent space corresponded to the high task success rate.
 - Conversely, models that lacked the necessary acoustic information showed weaker separation and less structured latent representations for the relevant acoustic properties.
 - In the exploratory task, ACT's latent space formed a clear structure correlating with the sound source's position, which was consistent with its higher performance on this task, while Diffusion Policy's latent space appeared less organized. This qualitative analysis supported the quantitative findings regarding policy effectiveness.

**Real-Robot Experiments:**

- Real-robot experiments were conducted for the L&I and Exploratory tasks, which represent more complex scenarios requiring comprehensive acoustic processing or active exploration.
- The S2A2 model, specifically implemented with the ACT policy, consistently outperformed the vision-only baseline in these real-world tasks. This confirmed the applicability and effectiveness of the proposed tasks and the S2A2 framework beyond simulation.
- Examples of robot interaction showed the integration of the acoustic spatial map and spectrogram in real-time decision-making, demonstrating the robot's ability to localize sounding objects and identify their sound types for successful manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing the limitations of vision-centric systems through the comprehensive integration of acoustic information. The proposed Spatial-Spectral Audio Action (S2A2) framework represents a step towards more robust and versatile robotic agents operating in complex, partially observable environments.

The key significance and potential impact areas are:

1. **Enabling Manipulation under Partial Visual Observability:** By introducing "acoustic-aware manipulation tasks" and demonstrating the S2A2 framework's efficacy, this work shows a pathway for robots to perform tasks where visual cues are ambiguous, occluded, or insufficient. This is particularly relevant for industrial settings, domestic assistance, or hazardous environments where objects might be visually identical, hidden, or identifiable only by their sound.
2. **Advancing Multimodal Imitation Learning:** S2A2 systematically integrates visual data with both acoustic spatial information (derived from advanced robot audition techniques like MUSIC with microphone arrays) and acoustic signal information (using Spotforming for source separation). This comprehensive multimodal approach extends the capabilities of imitation learning beyond prevalent visuo-tactile or visuo-language systems, highlighting the distinct and complementary role of spatial acoustics.
3. **Validation of Acoustic Representation Importance:** The systematic evaluation across various tasks and policy architectures quantitatively demonstrates that the utility of acoustic information is task-dependent. It emphasizes the importance of using acoustic representations that are matched to the specific capabilities a task requires (e.g., localization vs. identification). The finding that unnecessary acoustic modalities can degrade performance in certain contexts provides a guideline for designing efficient multimodal policies.
4. **Foundation for Active Acoustic Exploration:** The success in the "Exploratory task" suggests that robots can learn to actively interact with objects to elicit informative sounds, moving beyond passive perception. This capability is critical for tasks requiring object property inference (e.g., identifying a full vs. empty container, material sorting) that cannot be determined visually.
5. **Benchmarking and Future Research Directions:** The introduced acoustic-aware manipulation tasks serve as a benchmark for future research in multimodal robot learning, particularly for the integration of auditory perception. The findings regarding policy architecture dependency for effective acoustic integration also open avenues for designing more sophisticated multimodal fusion mechanisms, especially for large-scale pre-trained models.

While the real-robot validation was conducted on a single platform in a controlled environment, the demonstrated effectiveness lays the groundwork for generalizing to diverse robotic platforms (e.g., bimanual, mobile robots) and more complex environmental conditions, including those with multiple simultaneous sound sources. Future work integrating these acoustic capabilities with other modalities like touch and language, along with robust sim-to-real transfer methods, could further enhance the adaptability and intelligence of robotic systems.
