# Scaling Robot Supervision to Hundreds of Hours with RoboTurk: Robotic Manipulation Dataset through Human Reasoning and Dexterity

> [!summary] Summary
> Stanford AI Lab researchers introduce RoboTurk, an extended platform enabling large-scale, crowdsourced data collection for robot manipulation on physical robots. The system successfully gathered over 111 hours of human-supervised demonstrations for three complex tasks, generating a dataset significantly larger than previous human-in-the-loop collections and demonstrating that task progress can be learned from diverse human strategies.

## Problem

- Existing large-scale robot datasets, often self-supervised, suffer from a low signal-to-noise ratio due to a high proportion of irrelevant or unsuccessful data.
- Human demonstration methods provide high-quality data but have traditionally been limited in scale and task complexity, often requiring specialized hardware or local operation.
- A lack of standardized platforms for large-scale, crowdsourced human-in-the-loop data collection on physical robots exists, hindering the development of robust robot learning policies.

## Method

- Extended the RoboTurk platform, originally for simulation, to facilitate remote crowdsourced teleoperation on physical robot arms, addressing challenges like limited robot resources, control latency, and safety.
- Implemented a mutual exclusion system for robot access, utilized low-pass filtering on user input, and placed teleoperation servers proximate to robots to manage latency.
- Designed three complex manipulation tasks—Object Search, Tower Creation, and Laundry Layout—requiring both low-level dexterity and high-level cognitive reasoning, allowing for diverse solution strategies.

## Results

- Collected over 111 hours of real-world robot manipulation data, comprising 2144 demonstrations across three tasks from 54 unique users within one week, representing a significant increase in scale for human-supervised datasets.
- Quantitative analysis showed users' task completion times decreased with experience, and dexterity (measured by phone orientation change) increased, while effort remained constant.
- A modified Time Contrastive Network trained on the Laundry Layout dataset successfully learned an embedding space that effectively captured task progress and could serve as a reward function.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human operators exhibit measurable skill improvement over time in teleoperating real robots, primarily through increased dexterity rather than just faster movements.
> - Crowdsourcing on real robots effectively captures a wide diversity of solution strategies for complex manipulation tasks, which is crucial for training generalized robot policies.
> - Meaningful reward signals and task progress can be inferred from large-scale, diverse human demonstrations using methods like a modified Time Contrastive Network, even when direct behavioral cloning is challenging.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large, richly annotated datasets has facilitated significant advancements in fields such as computer vision and natural language processing, enabling the creation and evaluation of various learning algorithms. However, replicating this success in robotic manipulation has presented challenges. The research landscape in robotics has explored several data collection methodologies, each with specific limitations.

Prior approaches include self-supervised data collection and off-policy reinforcement learning, which have generated large datasets, sometimes exceeding 1000 hours of robot interaction. However, these methods often result in data with a low signal-to-noise ratio due to a substantial portion of data being collected through random controls. This can make the process of acquiring high-quality data prohibitively time-consuming, limiting the complexity of achievable tasks. Additionally, the specification and evaluation of a reward function for complex robotic tasks can be non-intuitive.

In contrast, methods relying on human demonstrations, often used in imitation learning, can provide higher quality data by implicitly defining successful task executions, thereby obviating the need for explicit reward function specification. However, these approaches have traditionally been limited in terms of the scale of data collected and the complexity of the tasks addressed. Many existing teleoperation systems for human data collection are restricted to local operation, require specialized hardware like VR headsets, or demand powerful client-side computational resources, which hinders their deployment on large-scale crowdsourcing platforms.

The current work addresses a gap in this landscape by aiming to combine the data magnitude typically achieved by self-supervised methods with the data quality characteristic of human-in-the-loop approaches. It leverages and extends the RoboTurk platform, previously used for simulation, to facilitate large-scale crowdsourced data collection on physical robots through remote teleoperation. This approach seeks to overcome the limitations of prior methods by enabling the collection of diverse, high-quality data for complex, real-world robotic manipulation tasks at an unprecedented scale for human-supervised data. The paper positions itself as addressing the core problem of large-scale crowdsourcing on real robots, specifically focusing on robust real-time remote interaction, safety, and data management for a diverse pool of operators.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is two-fold, seeking to address existing limitations in robot manipulation data collection:

1. **To increase the quantity and maintain the quality of human-supervised manipulation data:** Prior human-in-the-loop data collection efforts have yielded high-quality demonstrations but have been limited in scale. Conversely, self-supervised methods have achieved large data volumes but often suffer from low signal-to-noise ratios, with a significant portion of data being irrelevant or unsuccessful. A core objective is to collect an order of magnitude more manipulation data through human supervision while ensuring that the quality of these demonstrations is preserved. This aims to provide the benefits of expert demonstrations (e.g., implicitly defined successful task execution, avoiding complex reward function design) at a scale comparable to or exceeding self-supervised approaches.

2. **To collect diverse data on challenging manipulation tasks:** Many complex robotic tasks require both fine-grained dexterity and high-level cognitive reasoning. Existing data collection methods often do not guarantee the diversity of solution strategies. A key objective is to gather data on such challenging tasks from a broad range of human operators. This approach is intended to capture a diverse set of emergent behaviors and solution strategies, which is crucial for training robust and generalized robot learning policies capable of handling variations in task execution.

Beyond these primary motivations, the work is driven by the necessity to overcome specific challenges associated with scaling human-in-the-loop data collection to real robots:
- **Technical challenges of real-time remote interaction:** Replicating the success of large-scale datasets from computer vision and natural language processing in robotics is difficult because expert demonstrations must be provided in real-time. This necessitates robust remote interaction methods that can tolerate network latency and actuation delays inherent to physical robots.
- **Extending simulation platforms to physical robots:** The existing RoboTurk platform was designed for simulated environments. Its extension to physical robots introduces new complexities, including managing limited robot resources, accounting for hardware-level delays, implementing robust safety measures for novice users, and synchronizing multiple sensor data streams from various robots.
- **Designing tasks that leverage human capabilities:** The selection of tasks is motivated by the need for scenarios that require both human reasoning for strategic planning and human dexterity for precise manipulation, while also admitting multiple valid solution paths to encourage diversity.

In summary, the core objectives are to build a scalable, safe, and robust platform for crowdsourced data collection on physical robots, generate a large-scale and diverse dataset of human demonstrations for complex manipulation tasks, and empirically validate the platform's efficacy and the dataset's utility for robot learning research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research leveraged and extended the RoboTurk platform, initially designed for data collection in simulated robotic environments, to enable remote teleoperation and data acquisition on physical robot arms.

**Original RoboTurk Overview (for simulation):**
The original RoboTurk platform facilitated crowdsourced data collection in simulation using low-latency teleoperation. Users connected to a website to receive a video stream from the simulated environment and controlled a robot arm using their smartphone as a 6-DoF motion controller. The simulation itself ran on remote cloud servers to ensure consistent quality of service irrespective of client-side computing resources. Key components included:
- **User Endpoint:** A user's smartphone transmitted position and orientation changes via WebRTC, while a web browser displayed a real-time video feed. Users could enable/disable control for ergonomic adjustments.
- **Coordination Server:** Managed user sessions, creating and terminating teleoperation instances.
- **Teleoperation Server:** A dedicated process for each user, hosting a simulator instance, streaming frames, and processing control commands.

**Extensions for Real Robots:**
Adapting RoboTurk for physical robots required addressing several new challenges:
- **Limited Resources:** Unlike simulations, physical robots are finite resources. A mutual exclusion principle was implemented where the coordination server managed access to robot arms. Users were queued, and a lock was placed on a robot to ensure only one operator controlled it at a time.
- **Control Latency:** Physical robots introduce stochastic delays from hardware actuation and network communication, in addition to client-server network delays. To mitigate this, a low-pass filter was applied to user input, rejecting high-frequency commands to ensure smoother and more responsive teleoperation. Teleoperation servers were moved to machines physically proximate to the robots to minimize control command latency.
- **Robot Safety:** Safety measures were crucial for novice users operating physical robots. The RoboTurk smartphone application validated phone poses to encourage slow, deliberate movements. Participants received a 5-minute tutorial and had a direct communication channel to human monitors to respond to unsafe control actions.
- **Data Collection:** Real-world data collection is more complex than saving simulator states. The system utilized `rosbag`, a Robot Operating System (ROS) tool, to record messages from multiple, asynchronously emitting sensor streams (publisher-subscriber topics) simultaneously.

**Data Collection Setup and Sensors:**
The data was collected using three Sawyer robot arms. Each robot's workspace was equipped with:
- A front-facing webcam, providing the primary video stream to the teleoperator.
- A top-down Kinect depth camera.
The following data streams were recorded:
- RGB images from the front-facing webcam at 30Hz.
- RGB and Depth images from the top-down Kinectv2 sensor at 30Hz.
- Robot sensor readings (joint positions, end-effector pose, etc.) at approximately 100Hz.

**Task Design:**
Three robotic manipulation tasks were designed to require both low-level dexterity and high-level cognitive reasoning, with multimodal solution spaces to encourage diverse strategies:
1. **Object Search:** Objective: Find three instances of a specified target object category (plush animals, plastic water bottles, or paper napkins) within a cluttered bin and place them into their corresponding bins. Constraints included not placing objects outside the bin. Task complexity stemmed from object detection in clutter, varied object properties (e.g., crumpled napkins, translucent bottles), and dexterous manipulation to dig, push, pick, and fit.
2. **Tower Creation:** Objective: Build the tallest possible stable tower by stacking an assortment of cups and bowls. Task instances were diversified by randomly sampling 10 objects from 7 bowl varieties (28 items total) and 3 cup varieties (12 items total) and randomizing their initial placement. This task required physical reasoning about object properties and stability.
3. **Laundry Layout:** Objective: Flatten an item of clothing (hand towel, jeans, or t-shirt) on a table without folds. Each task reset randomized the item's initial configuration. This task demanded visual and physical reasoning to understand fabric states and anticipate responses to contact.

**Data Collection Procedure:**
A total of 54 participants contributed data over one week. Each user completed an initial supervised hour (including a 5-minute tutorial) and could then opt for unsupervised data collection. All data collection was performed remotely.

**Evaluation Methods:**
- **Quantitative User Performance:** Analyzed user skill improvement over time by tracking task completion times, "average effort" (L2 norms of phone translations), and "average orientation change" (of the phone controller).
- **Qualitative User Feedback:** Participants completed NASA TLX (Task Load Index) surveys to assess perceived mental, physical, and temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration.
- **Data Analysis:**
 - **Task Complexity:** Qualitatively assessed Object Search by comparing start and end configurations of the bin contents.
 - **Diversity of Demonstrations:** Qualitatively analyzed variations in solution strategies for Laundry Layout (different intermediate steps for similar start/end states) and Tower Creation (different tower structures from identical object sets).
 - **Reward Signal Inference:** Trained a modified Time Contrastive Network (TCN) on Laundry Layout demonstrations. Modifications included a hierarchical triplet loss (intra-chunk and inter-chunk losses) and an auxiliary loss for terminal frames, designed to learn embeddings capable of capturing both high and low-frequency temporal similarity and task progress.
 - **Behavioral Cloning (BC):** Attempted to train policies directly using BC on the Laundry Layout task, mapping RGB images to robot joint positions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the platform's capabilities, the characteristics of the collected data, and its potential utility for robot learning.

**Dataset Scale and Characteristics:**
- The project successfully collected over 111 hours of robot manipulation data across 54 unique users and three distinct, challenging manipulation tasks within a single week. This quantity of data is reported to be 1-2 orders of magnitude larger than previous robot datasets collected via human supervision (Table I).
- The dataset comprises 2144 individual task demonstrations, with an average task length of 186 seconds, indicating the complexity and long-horizon nature of the tasks.

**Platform Performance and User Adaptation:**
- **Skill Improvement:** Quantitative analysis of user performance indicated a clear trend of skill improvement with experience. Task completion times steadily decreased as users gained more experience (Figure 4, left). This improvement was not primarily due to faster overall movements, as the average effort exerted (estimated by the square L2 norms of phone translations) remained largely invariant across experience levels (Figure 4, middle). Instead, users demonstrated increased dexterity, evidenced by an increase in average phone orientation change with experience (Figure 4, right).
- **Skill Variation:** A wide range of skill levels was observed among participants, particularly for the Object Search and Laundry Layout tasks, with some users completing tasks significantly faster than others (Figure 5). For the Tower Creation task, users tended to utilize the entire allotted time, resulting in a more uniform distribution of completion times.
- **User Workload:** NASA TLX evaluations indicated that users perceived the Tower Creation task as requiring the highest total workload across all metrics (mental, physical, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration). Object Search and Laundry Layout tasks registered comparatively lower and similar workload scores (Table II).

**Task Complexity and Data Diversity:**
- **Object Search Complexity:** Qualitative analysis of the Object Search task, by comparing the start and end configurations of the cluttered bin, demonstrated substantial changes in object arrangement. This indicated that operators performed significant work to reorient the bin's contents, underscoring the task's inherent complexity and the effort required for successful completion (Figure 6).
- **Diversity of Solutions:** The dataset effectively captured a diversity of solution strategies due to the unstructured nature of the tasks and the varied approaches of 54 different human operators (Figure 7).
 - For the Laundry Layout task, demonstrations starting and ending with similar towel configurations frequently exhibited distinct intermediate manipulation sequences. For example, one user might grab a towel side and push, while another might lift and drag a corner, both leading to a flattened towel.
 - In the Tower Creation task, even when starting with an identical set of objects, users constructed architecturally different towers that achieved comparable heights, showcasing creative problem-solving and diverse structural compositions.

**Utility of the Dataset for Learning:**
- **Reward Signal Learning:** By training a modified Time Contrastive Network (TCN) on the Laundry Layout dataset, the researchers demonstrated that a meaningful embedding space could be learned. The negative L2 distance in this embedding space to a target "successful" frame (e.g., a flattened towel) provided a "reasonable reward function" that captured task semantics and could effectively measure task progress (Figure 8). Frames closer to the goal state in the embedding space corresponded to a towel being nearly flat, while frames further away represented states where the robot was still significantly manipulating the towel.
- **Behavioral Cloning:** Initial attempts to train policies using Behavioral Cloning (mapping RGB images to robot joint positions) on the entire Laundry Layout dataset were largely unsuccessful, attributed to the high diversity of demonstrations. However, some success was achieved by narrowing the training data to specific, less diverse segments of demonstrations, such as movements to grasp a towel corner.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents several areas of significance and potential impact within the field of robotic manipulation and broader AI research:

- **Addressing the Data Bottleneck in Robotics:** The most immediate significance is the successful demonstration of collecting a large-scale, high-quality dataset of robot manipulation demonstrations through remote human teleoperation. This directly addresses a critical bottleneck in robot learning: the scarcity of extensive, expert-annotated data compared to fields like computer vision and natural language processing. By providing over 111 hours of human-supervised data, the work significantly advances the potential for data-driven approaches in robotics.

- **Validation of Scalable Crowdsourcing for Real Robots:** The extended RoboTurk platform demonstrates a robust and scalable framework for crowdsourcing teleoperation on physical robots. This platform accounts for practical challenges such as limited robot resources, network and hardware latency, and operational safety for novice users. The success of this platform suggests a viable model for future, even larger-scale data collection efforts, potentially generalizing to different robot platforms and manipulation domains.

- **Enabling New Research Avenues in Robot Learning:** The resulting dataset, characterized by its scale, quality, diversity of solutions, and the complexity of the tasks (requiring both high-level reasoning and low-level dexterity), opens up new avenues for research. It can serve as a valuable resource for developing and evaluating algorithms in areas such as:
 - **Multimodal density estimation:** Understanding and modeling the varied ways a task can be solved.
 - **Video prediction:** Forecasting future robot states and outcomes from visual inputs.
 - **Reward function learning:** Inferring implicit reward structures from human demonstrations, as demonstrated by the TCN experiment.
 - **Policy learning:** Training more generalized and robust robot policies, potentially with new approaches to handle the inherent diversity.
 - **Hierarchical task planning:** Decomposing complex, long-horizon tasks into sub-goals based on human strategies.
 - **One-shot and few-shot imitation learning:** Enabling robots to learn new tasks from a minimal number of examples.

- **Benchmarking Complex Manipulation Tasks:** The introduction of three challenging tasks (Object Search, Tower Creation, Laundry Layout) provides new benchmarks for robot learning algorithms. These tasks move beyond simpler pick-and-place scenarios, demanding sophisticated perception, reasoning, and dexterous manipulation, reflecting real-world complexities.

- **Insights into Human Motor Skills and Problem Solving:** The analysis of user performance, showing skill improvement and diverse solution strategies, offers insights into how humans adapt to teleoperation interfaces and solve complex physical problems. This data can inform the design of future human-robot interaction systems and policy learning algorithms that leverage human-like adaptability.

- **Open Access to Data:** Making the dataset publicly available (roboturk.stanford.edu/realrobotdataset) ensures its wide dissemination and use by the global research community, thereby maximizing its potential impact on accelerating progress in robot learning.

While direct policy learning from the entire diverse dataset proved challenging in initial attempts, the work lays a foundation for future innovations in addressing this complexity. The successful demonstration of learning meaningful reward signals from this data indicates a path forward for imitation learning in highly variable environments.
