# Do World Action Models Generalize Better than VLAs? A Robustness Study

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Huawei Technologies and the University of Toronto systematically compared World Action Models (WAMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies under various environmental and linguistic perturbations. The study found WAMs generally more robust to visual perturbations, benefiting from spatiotemporal priors from video pretraining, but significantly slower in inference speed compared to VLAs.

## Problem

- Robot action planning in real-world environments struggles with the inherent diversity and uncertainty, necessitating robust policies that can anticipate action consequences.
- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, despite recent advancements, exhibit limitations in generalization and robustness to contextual perturbations due to a potential lack of explicit physical world understanding.
- A central debate exists regarding whether foundation-based robot policies implicitly model world dynamics sufficiently or if explicit dynamic prediction capacities from world models offer superior generalization and robustness.

## Method

- A comprehensive comparative study was conducted, evaluating state-of-the-art VLA policies (e.g., ==π0.5==, ==OpenVLA==) and World Action Models (WAMs) (e.g., ==LingBot-VA==, ==Cosmos-Policy==), alongside hybrid approaches (==MOTUS==, ==VLA-JEPA==).
- Models were benchmarked on two manipulation tasks, ==LIBERO-Plus== (single-arm) and the newly introduced ==RoboTwin 2.0-Plus== (bimanual), under a robust taxonomy of 21 sub-dimensions across seven perturbation categories (e.g., camera, light, background, language).
- The methodology involved analyzing architectural differences, pretraining strategies (video-based vs. image-text), policy training schemes, prediction mechanisms, and practical considerations like inference speed.

## Results

- WAMs like LingBot-VA achieved **74.2%** overall success on RoboTwin 2.0-Plus under perturbations, outperforming π0.5 (**58.6%**), and Cosmos-Policy achieved **82.2%** on LIBERO-Plus, demonstrating strong visual robustness to noise, light, and layout changes.
- The VLA policy π0.5 achieved the highest overall success rate of **85.7%** on LIBERO-Plus, showing that extensive training with diverse robotic datasets can enable VLAs to achieve comparable robustness to WAMs.
- WAMs were consistently slower in inference, with WAMs being at least 4.8 times slower per inference compared to the fastest VLA, π0.5 (63 ms per chunk), primarily due to the computational cost of future-state diffusion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - WAMs derive their strong robustness to visual perturbations from spatiotemporal priors inherited from their backbones, which are pretrained on diverse internet-scale video data for video generation.
> - VLAs, typically pretrained on static image-text data, require extensive additional training with diverse dynamic visual and robotic datasets to implicitly acquire world dynamics, achieving comparable robustness to WAMs.
> - The explicit future-state diffusion processes in WAMs, while contributing to robustness, impose a significant computational overhead that results in substantially slower inference times compared to VLAs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot action planning in real-world environments is a complex challenge, primarily due to the diverse and uncertain nature of physical settings that necessitate robust understanding and prediction of environmental dynamics. Two prominent paradigms have emerged to address this: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World Action Models (WAMs).

VLA policies, built upon large-scale vision-language models (VLMs), have demonstrated capabilities across various robotic tasks by repurposing pre-trained models for action generation. However, their performance has been observed to be constrained by training data scope, leading to limitations in generalization to unseen scenarios and vulnerability to contextual perturbations. This suggests that VLAs may lack a fundamental understanding of physical world dynamics necessary for consistent planning.

World models, traditionally utilized as simulators for model training and evaluation, have recently been revisited as an alternative or complementary approach. WAMs are specifically designed with world models as their core, trained on extensive video data to predict future visual states. Their latent representations can then be adapted to decode robot actions. The hypothesis underlying WAMs is that their explicit dynamic prediction capacity, combined with spatiotemporal priors acquired from web-scale video pre-training, could enable more effective generalization compared to VLAs.

The present study directly addresses an ongoing debate within the field regarding the comparative advantages of world models over foundation-based robot policies. It investigates whether the explicit modeling of world dynamics by WAMs is indeed superior to the implicit world modeling that might occur within foundation-based VLAs, particularly in terms of generalization and robustness under varied environmental conditions. The work contributes to understanding the strengths and limitations of each paradigm and informs the development of more robust and generalizable robotic policies for real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a comparative study between prominent state-of-the-art Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies and recently released World Action Models (WAMs). The study aims to systematically evaluate their robustness and generalization capabilities under a range of contextual perturbations.

The motivation for this comparative analysis stems from several observations and hypotheses within the field of robotic control:

1. **Limitations of VLAs:** Despite notable successes, VLAs derived from large-scale vision-language models (VLMs) often exhibit limitations in generalization to novel situations and vulnerability to environmental changes such as visual distractions or cluttered environments. This suggests that VLAs may primarily learn correlations within their training data rather than a deep understanding of physical dynamics.
2. **Hypothesized Advantages of WAMs:** World Action Models (WAMs), built upon world models pre-trained on vast amounts of video data, explicitly predict future visual states conditioned on actions. It has been theorized that this explicit dynamic prediction, coupled with spatiotemporal priors learned from web-scale video content, could enable WAMs to generalize more effectively and exhibit greater robustness.
3. **Need for Robustness in Real-World Deployment:** Real-world robotic tasks require policies that can operate reliably despite variations in lighting, background, object arrangement, sensor noise, and task instructions. A comprehensive robustness study is essential to identify which architectural paradigms are better suited for practical deployment.
4. **Architectural and Training Differences:** VLAs typically leverage VLM backbones, pre-trained for next-token prediction, while WAMs utilize video generation models pre-trained for video synthesis. These fundamental differences in backbone models and pre-training objectives might lead to distinct generalization properties. The study aims to uncover how these differences manifest in performance under challenging conditions.
5. **Understanding Video Priors:** The research also aims to investigate the role and impact of video-based dynamic learning. This includes examining whether explicit integration of video priors in WAMs provides a clear advantage, how different integration methods (e.g., hybrid approaches) affect robustness, and whether data diversity in VLA training can compensate for the lack of explicit dynamic prediction.

By rigorously evaluating these models on perturbed benchmarks, the authors seek to provide insights into the underlying mechanisms that contribute to generalization and robustness in robot policies, thereby informing future research directions and development strategies for robust robotic AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study adopted a comparative experimental approach, evaluating state-of-the-art VLA and WAM policies on two manipulation benchmarks specifically designed to test robustness under various perturbations.

#### 3.1. Models Evaluated

The study selected a range of publicly available VLA and WAM checkpoints, along with hybrid approaches:
- **VLAs:** π0.5, X-VLA, π0, π0-FAST, OpenVLA-OFT, UniVLA, RIPT-VLA, HoloBrain0-GD, ABot-M0.
- **Hybrid VLA+WM:** MOTUS, VLA-JEPA. These models partially incorporate video-based dynamic learning or auxiliary world modeling tasks.
- **WAMs:** LingBot-VA, Fast-WAM, Cosmos-Policy, GE-Act. DreamZero and GigaWorld-Policy were excluded from quantitative evaluation due to proprietary datasets, computational cost, or lack of public checkpoints.

#### 3.2. Benchmarks

Two complementary benchmarks were used:
- **LIBERO-Plus:** An existing benchmark for single-arm manipulation tasks, extended with seven types of perturbations. It uses a 7-DoF Franka Panda robot with two 256x256 cameras.
- **RoboTwin 2.0-Plus:** An in-house benchmark built upon RoboTwin 2.0, focusing on bimanual manipulation with the Aloha-Agilex platform. It uses a dual-arm 14-DoF system with three 320x240 cameras. This benchmark implements a similar perturbation protocol to LIBERO-Plus.

#### 3.3. Perturbation Categories

Both benchmarks applied perturbations across seven axes, each with multiple sub-dimensions (totaling 21 sub-dimensions for RoboTwin 2.0-Plus, with 20 active by default):
1. **Camera:** Altering third-person camera viewpoint (distance, spherical position, orientation).
2. **Robot:** Varying initial joint configurations of the robot arms.
3. **Language:** Paraphrasing or altering task instructions (distraction, common-sense rewording, reasoning chain).
4. **Light:** Modifying illumination intensity, shadow direction, and color temperature (diffuse color, direction, specular, shadows).
5. **Background:** Changing table and scene textures (scene theme, surface appearance).
6. **Noise:** Applying photometric distortions to input images (motion blur, Gaussian blur, zoom blur, fog, glass blur).
7. **Layout:** Introducing task-irrelevant distractor objects and perturbing target object poses.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Protocol

For each task within the benchmarks, models were evaluated using 8 configurations:
- One "clean" baseline configuration without perturbations.
- Seven configurations, each activating exactly one perturbation dimension while keeping others at default (clean) values, to isolate the effect of each perturbation type.
Each configuration ran 50 episodes per task to ensure statistical significance.

#### 3.5. Model Architectures and Training Differences (Contextual)

The study implicitly relies on the fundamental architectural and training differences outlined in Section 2.3 and Table 2 of the paper:
- **WAMs:** Generally leverage video generation models (e.g., diffusion models) pre-trained on web-scale video data for future visual state prediction. Their embodied pre-training often focuses on jointly predicting future visual states and actions or decoding actions from predicted states (Inverse Dynamic Model - IDM).
- **VLAs:** Typically built on VLM backbones pre-trained on static image-text data for next-token prediction. To acquire dynamic understanding, they often require extensive and diverse embodied pre-training data, sometimes including human videos or multi-modal web data.
- **Hybrid Approaches:** Integrate elements of both, such as augmenting VLA policies with future-state prediction objectives or incorporating video generation backbones with separate VLM-based action decoders.

This methodological framework allowed for a systematic comparison of robustness profiles and an investigation into the hypothesized advantages of WAMs, while also examining the factors contributing to performance differences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presented its findings in response to four research questions, yielding detailed insights into the robustness, performance consistency, underlying explanations, and runtime characteristics of WAMs and VLAs.

**RQ 1. Are WAM-based policies robust to perturbations?**
WAMs generally demonstrated strong robustness to various perturbations.
- **RoboTwin 2.0-Plus:** LingBot-VA achieved an overall success rate of 74.2% under perturbations, significantly outperforming π0.5 (58.6%). It ranked first in five out of seven perturbation categories. Fast-WAM, despite lacking embodied pre-training, achieved 72.7% overall, placing it second to LingBot-VA.
- **LIBERO-Plus:** Cosmos-Policy achieved an 82.2% overall success rate, and GE-Act attained 80.3%. Notably, π0.5 also showed strong performance on LIBERO-Plus, achieving 85.7% overall, demonstrating that VLAs can achieve comparable robustness with extensive, diverse training.
- **Hybrid Approaches:** MOTUS (71.5% on RoboTwin 2.0-Plus) and VLA-JEPA (77.9% on LIBERO-Plus) exhibited intermediate robustness, suggesting that partial integration of video-based dynamic learning can improve performance.

**RQ 2. Is the performance advantage of WAMs consistent across different perturbation types?**
The robustness of WAMs varied across perturbation types:
- **Strong Visual Robustness:** WAMs like LingBot-VA (RoboTwin 2.0-Plus) and Cosmos-Policy/GE-Act (LIBERO-Plus) consistently showed high robustness to visually-based perturbations, including light, noise, and layout changes. Cosmos-Policy, for example, could effectively denoise images and predict future states accurately under noise and varying light conditions.
- **Challenges with Geometric Perturbations:** WAMs faced greater challenges with perturbations that altered the geometric configuration of the scene, specifically camera viewpoint and robot initial state variations. LingBot-VA, for instance, showed lower success rates in these categories compared to visual perturbations.
- **Role of Training Data Diversity (Fast-WAM Case Study):** Fast-WAM's performance highlighted the critical role of task-specific training data diversity. On RoboTwin 2.0-Plus, where it was finetuned on clean + domain-randomized demonstrations, it achieved 72.7% overall robustness. In contrast, on LIBERO-Plus, where it was trained on clean demonstrations only, its robustness sharply degraded across most perturbation types (e.g., 16.4% for camera, 37.7% for noise), resulting in a 51.5% overall success rate. This indicates that while video priors are necessary, diverse task-specific data is crucial for robust generalization, especially for WAM designs that jointly denoise state and action without explicit causal conditioning.

**RQ 3. How to explain the performance differences between VLAs and WAMs?**
Performance differences are primarily attributed to backbone model choices and training strategies:
- **WAMs' Spatiotemporal Priors:** WAMs benefit from video generation backbones pre-trained on web-scale video data, which provides fine-grained visual dynamics and temporal dependencies. This "physical knowledge" allows WAMs to achieve robustness with comparatively lightweight finetuning on downstream tasks, reducing reliance on extensive embodied pre-training for policy generation.
- **VLAs' Data Dependency:** VLAs, using VLM backbones primarily trained on static image-text data, inherently lack explicit priors over visual dynamics. To achieve comparable robustness, VLAs such as π0.5 require extensive and diverse training on robotic, video, and web data to implicitly acquire models of world dynamics.
- **Hybrid Insights:** Hybrid models like VLA-JEPA and MOTUS, which partially integrate video-based temporal learning or use video generation backbones, demonstrate improved robustness over pure VLAs, confirming that the incorporation of dynamic priors contributes to generalization.

**RQ 4. What are the runtime characteristics of WAMs and how do they compare to those of VLAs?**
Inference speed constitutes a major limitation for WAMs:
- **Slower Inference:** WAMs are consistently slower than VLAs due to the computational overhead of the future-state diffusion process. Compared to π0.5 (63 ms per action chunk), WAMs were at least 4.8 times slower.
- **Variability in WAM Speed:** Inference times for WAMs varied significantly (190 ms for Fast-WAM to 5230 ms for LingBot-VA in RoboTwin settings). This is heavily influenced by the number of denoising steps (state denoising being dominant), backbone size, and generation strategy (joint denoising vs. separate action decoding).
- **Optimization Efforts:** While Fast-WAM significantly reduced latency by omitting visual state generation at test time (190 ms, 3.0x slower than π0.5), a substantial gap remains for real-time deployment compared to efficient VLAs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study provides a systematic comparative analysis that clarifies the strengths and weaknesses of World Action Models (WAMs) relative to Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in the context of robotic manipulation under diverse perturbations. The findings carry several significant implications for the development and deployment of robotic AI:

1. **Validation of WAM Robustness:** The research empirically validates the hypothesis that WAMs, leveraging spatiotemporal priors from video generation backbones, exhibit strong robustness to various visual perturbations such as noise, lighting, and layout changes. This suggests a foundational advantage in understanding dynamic visual environments. This robustness is consistent across both single-arm and bimanual manipulation tasks, indicating a generalizable benefit.

2. **Identified Limitations of WAMs:** While strong in visual robustness, WAMs demonstrated limitations when faced with geometric perturbations like changes in camera viewpoint or the robot's initial state. This indicates that their inherent video priors provide less benefit for configurations that fundamentally alter the scene's geometric structure, highlighting a critical area for future research and improvement.

3. **Role of Data Diversity for VLAs:** The study demonstrates that highly trained VLAs, such as π0.5, can achieve comparable or even superior robustness to WAMs, particularly on certain benchmarks. This is attributed to extensive training on diverse robotic and web data, suggesting that rich data diversity can, to some extent, compensate for the lack of explicit world modeling in VLM backbones.

4. **Insights from Hybrid Approaches:** Hybrid models, which partially integrate video-based temporal learning, show intermediate robustness profiles. This finding emphasizes that the *manner* in which video priors are incorporated into a policy architecture matters, not merely their presence. Future work could explore optimal strategies for combining VLM semantic understanding with video-based dynamic prediction.

5. **Critical Inference Speed Challenge:** A major takeaway is the significantly slower inference speed of WAMs compared to VLAs, primarily due to the computational cost of visual state denoising. This poses a practical barrier to their real-world deployment in applications requiring real-time response. The efforts by models like Fast-WAM to mitigate this by omitting test-time visual state generation are promising, but a substantial latency gap persists.

6. **Data Efficiency of WAM Paradigm (with caveats):** Fast-WAM's performance on RoboTwin 2.0-Plus, achieving competitive robustness without extensive embodied pre-training, suggests that the WAM paradigm can be data-efficient by leveraging video backbone priors. However, its sharp performance degradation on LIBERO-Plus when trained on less diverse task-specific data underscores that while video priors are necessary, task-specific training data diversity remains essential for robust generalization, especially for certain WAM architectures.

In conclusion, this research provides a comprehensive empirical foundation for understanding the trade-offs between WAMs and VLAs. It underscores the potential of video-based dynamic priors for enhancing robotic policy robustness but also highlights the ongoing challenges related to inference efficiency and generalization to geometric variations. The findings inform future research by pointing towards the need for more efficient integration of dynamic priors, improved inference speeds, and continued exploration of data-centric approaches to achieve truly generalizable and robust robotic control systems.
