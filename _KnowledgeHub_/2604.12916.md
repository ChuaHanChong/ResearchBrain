# E2E-Fly: An Integrated Training-to-Deployment System for End-to-End Quadrotor Autonomy

> [!summary] Summary
> E2E-Fly is an integrated quadrotor training-to-deployment system pairing the differentiable simulator VisFly with structured reward-design and curriculum recipes, two-stage validation (sim-to-sim in AirSim plus motion-capture hardware-in-the-loop), and four-part sim-to-real alignment: system identification, latency compensation, domain randomization, and noise modeling. Six end-to-end tasks train under PPO or BPTT and transfer zero-shot to two open hardware quadrotors.

## Problem

- Existing quadrotor simulators optimize rendering or physics fidelity but rarely couple to hardware, leaving trained policies without a validated path to physical deployment.
- Prior integrated platforms such as Agilicious and AirGym omit differentiable physics learning and offer no reward-design guidance covering common quadrotor tasks.
- Sim-to-real alignment is fragmented across the literature: identification, latency, randomization, and sensor noise are studied separately, never as one reproducible protocol.

## Method

- Training stack uses the **VisFly** differentiable simulator with analytic first-order gradients (==BPTT==) alongside ==PPO==, plus a reward manual of dense progress, smoothness, orientation, velocity, and collision terms with ==curriculum== schedules.
- Two validation stages: a VisFly-to-AirSim interface exposing ==Betaflight==-compatible control channels, and a ==VIS-HIL== node rendering 30 Hz virtual RGB, depth, and segmentation from motion-capture pose of a real flying drone.
- Deployment on two open quadrotors (VIS-R onboard, VIS-H offboard) through **betaflight-ctrl**, a ==finite-state-machine== control bridge, with four alignment stages including ==step-response latency compensation== and ==inverse-depth noise injection==.

## Results

- BPTT converges in roughly **1,200** seconds on average versus PPO's **4,150** seconds, under **30%** of the time, at comparable throughput near **13,000-24,000** steps per second, and reaches higher final reward.
- Measured communication latency was about **90 ms** offboard and under **30 ms** onboard, compensated as **three** and **two** action-frame delays at **30 Hz** control, aligning real and simulated step responses.
- Curriculum PPO racing with obstacles hits **100%** success on **three** unseen track shapes above **10 m/s**; BPTT visual landing converges within **one million** steps; all **six** tasks transfer zero-shot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurate identification plus latency compensation can remove the need to randomize intrinsic dynamics parameters; state-based policies transferred zero-shot without any injected state noise.
> - Analytic gradients buy sample efficiency, not robustness: BPTT demands smooth differentiable rewards and drifts into local optima on long-horizon or sparse-reward tasks.
> - Depth-sensor gap narrows best by adding Gaussian noise to inverse depth, not raw depth; vision still needs noise modeling where state observations do not.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

End-to-end learning in quadrotor autonomy, which maps raw sensor observations directly to actuator commands via neural networks, represents a shift from conventional modular architectures. This data-driven paradigm aims to reduce system latency and mitigate error propagation, demonstrating performance in agile flight, obstacle avoidance, and high-speed trajectory tracking, sometimes surpassing traditional model-based controllers in dynamic environments. Differentiable physics learning, utilizing analytical gradients of quadrotor dynamics, has emerged as a method to improve learning efficiency and policy quality compared to black-box reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning.

Despite advancements in end-to-end methods, practical application to real-world quadrotors faces several challenges. These include the selection of appropriate simulators and learning algorithms, effective reward design, the need for rapid and safe validation, and accurate sim-to-real alignment techniques. Existing quadrotor research platforms often focus on either simulation fidelity or hardware integration, but rarely provide a comprehensive, unified workflow for end-to-end policy training and systematic, zero-shot transfer.

For instance, platforms like Agilicious focus on high-performance flight but do not offer a complete workflow for end-to-end policy training or systematic sim-to-real transfer. AirGym supports RL training and hardware validation, addressing some practical sim-to-real considerations, but lacks differentiable physical learning algorithms and guidance on reward design for common tasks. Other simulators, such as Flightmare, RotorS, and AirSim, offer varied levels of realism and sensor support but often do not integrate a full training-to-deployment pipeline with differentiable physics or comprehensive sim-to-real alignment.

The present work addresses these limitations by introducing a unified framework that integrates advanced simulators, learning algorithms, comprehensive reward design, structured validation, and detailed sim-to-real alignment protocols. It aims to bridge the gap in existing systems by offering a systematic, zero-shot transfer pipeline for end-to-end quadrotor control, thereby enhancing reproducibility and real-world deployment capabilities. This positions E2E-Fly as an attempt to unify previously fragmented efforts in the field into a cohesive system.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop E2E-Fly, an integrated framework designed to provide a systematic, zero-shot transfer pipeline for end-to-end quadrotor autonomy, spanning policy training, validation, and real-world deployment. The motivation for this development stems from several challenges in the existing research landscape:

1. **Inefficient Policy Training and Algorithm Integration:** Training end-to-end control policies requires appropriate simulators, learning algorithms, and effective reward designs. Existing systems often lack a unified platform that integrates high-performance differentiable physics learning with reinforcement learning, hindering efficient policy optimization. E2E-Fly aims to overcome this by incorporating a high-performance simulator (VisFly) that supports both differentiable physics learning (e.g., Back-Propagation Through Time - BPTT) and reinforcement learning (e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization - PPO), along with structured reward design guidelines.
2. **Lack of Robust and Safe Validation:** Quadrotor tasks involve inherent risks, as crashes can cause damage to hardware and sensors. Current validation methods are often insufficient or piecemeal, necessitating a quick and safe validation strategy before real-world deployment. The framework addresses this by introducing a two-stage validation strategy: sim-to-sim transfer (VisFly to AirSim) and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing, which allows policies to be evaluated under realistic conditions without physical collision risks.
3. **Challenges in Sim-to-Real Alignment:** Accurate transfer of policies from simulation to reality is challenging due to discrepancies arising from physical modeling inaccuracies, unmodeled sensor discrepancies, and control latency. These factors often hinder zero-shot transfer, requiring extensive fine-tuning. E2E-Fly seeks to establish a comprehensive sim-to-real alignment methodology, encompassing system identification, domain randomization, latency compensation, and noise modeling, to enable reliable zero-shot deployment.
4. **Absence of Unified Platform and Reproducibility:** The absence of a unified platform integrating the full technical stack from training to deployment makes it difficult to reproduce results and hinders the practical application of learning-based policies. The motivation is to provide a cohesive system that accelerates the train-to-deployment procedure, improves efficiency and safety during debugging and evaluation, and lowers the barrier for researchers and practitioners to develop robust end-to-end quadrotor controllers.
5. **Provision of Reference Hardware and Benchmarks:** To facilitate real-world evaluation and standardize development, there is a need for reference quadrotor platforms and benchmark tasks. The project addresses this by designing and releasing two quadrotor hardware platforms (VIS-R and VIS-H) and demonstrating the framework's effectiveness across six end-to-end control tasks, serving as benchmarks for evaluation.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to bridge the gap between theoretical advancements in end-to-end learning for quadrotors and their robust, reproducible, and safe application in physical environments by providing an integrated, full-stack solution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

E2E-Fly is structured into three main phases: training, validation, and deployment, integrating software and hardware components. The system overview is illustrated in Fig. 1.

**A. Training Phase**

1. **Fast Rendering Simulator: VisFly:**
 - E2E-Fly utilizes VisFly, a differentiable quadrotor simulator built on Habitat-Sim. It supports parallel multi-agent training with synchronized multi-modal perception (RGB, depth, segmentation) and high-frequency state/IMU data, achieving high frame rates (up to 6000 FPS at 256×256 depth resolution).
 - Its core features include a fully differentiable dynamics model in PyTorch, enabling analytical gradients and GPU-accelerated physics for efficient gradient-based learning.
 - VisFly provides interfaces for both Reinforcement Learning (RL) and differentiable simulation, supporting various control interfaces (e.g., mass-normalized collective thrust and body rates - CTBR).
 - The framework is enhanced in E2E-Fly with sim-to-sim and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) validation capabilities, a `betaflight-ctrl` interface, and integrated sim-to-real alignment techniques.

2. **Policy Training Algorithms:**
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Quadrotor control is formulated as an infinite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). The objective is to learn an optimal policy that maximizes the expected discounted return. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is employed as a representative black-box RL algorithm, relying on stochastic gradient estimates.
 - **Learning via Differentiable Simulation:** Back-Propagation Through Time (BPTT) is used to leverage first-order gradients from the differentiable simulator. This method computes analytical gradients, aiming for faster convergence and higher sample efficiency compared to gradient-estimation methods like PPO.
 - **Quadrotor Dynamics:** A fully differentiable quadrotor dynamics model is used as the state transition function. This model accounts for mass, moment of inertia, gravity, collective thrust, body torques, and air drag forces/torques. Motor dynamics are simulated using a first-order system, and thrust/torque generation by individual motors is modeled with quadratic functions of rotational speed.

3. **Reward Design for Quadrotor Tasks:**
 - A comprehensive reward design framework is proposed, combining both dense and sparse formulations.
 - **Dense Rewards:** Provide continuous, differentiable learning signals. Categories include:
 - *Progress:* Incentivizes movement towards the target (e.g., reducing distance to goal) or position/axis-wise alignment for stable tasks like hovering/landing.
 - *Smooth Action:* Penalizes large action changes or magnitudes to promote smooth control.
 - *Orientation:* Encourages alignment with desired yaw or roll angles, critical for perception-aware tasks or narrow gap traversal.
 - *Linear Velocity:* Suppresses aggressive motion or enforces adherence to speed profiles, with exponential penalties for exceeding desired speeds.
 - *Angular Velocity:* Penalizes high-frequency oscillations for stable attitude control.
 - *Collision Avoidance:* Discourages proximity to obstacles using inverse-distance or velocity-dependent terms.
 - **Sparse Rewards:** Event-driven signals for task completion (positive) and crashes (negative), serving as terminal feedback.
 - Table II provides reward design instructions for various foundational tasks.

4. **Curriculum Learning:**
 - For complex tasks (e.g., high-speed obstacle avoidance, racing), a structured curriculum learning strategy progressively guides the agent through increasingly challenging sub-tasks.
 - This approach starts with simpler environments and gradually increases complexity (e.g., adding obstacles, increasing speed), aiming to prevent convergence to local optima and improve robustness. Table III illustrates examples.

**B. Validation Phase**

1. **Sim-to-sim: From VisFly to AirSim:**
 - To mitigate risks of direct sim-to-real transfer, policies trained in VisFly are first validated in AirSim.
 - AirSim, built on Unreal Engine, provides high-fidelity physics and photorealistic sensor simulation.
 - An interface between VisFly and AirSim is established, along with an AirSim-compatible `betaflight-ctrl` package, allowing policies to be evaluated consistently across simulators without modification.

2. **Hardware-in-the-loop simulation: VIS-HIL node:**
 - This integrates a physical quadrotor into a simulated visual environment to validate control and perception algorithms safely.
 - A physical quadrotor operates within a motion-capture system (e.g., Vicon), and its real-time pose drives the VisFly simulator to generate virtual sensory input (RGB, depth, semantic segmentation).
 - The `VIS-HIL` node synchronizes the real quadrotor’s pose with the simulated environment, allowing the end-to-end policy to be executed on the real quadrotor while interacting with virtual obstacles. This reduces hardware damage risk.

**C. Deployment Phase**

1. **Quadrotor Platforms:**
 - Two custom quadrotor platforms are developed: `VIS-Real` (VIS-R) for onboard experiments and `VIS-Hardware-in-the-loop` (VIS-H) for offboard and HIL experiments.
 - VIS-R integrates an Intel Radax X4 onboard computer with an Intel RealSense D435i camera for real-time inference.
 - VIS-H uses an offboard processing architecture with a wireless data link and FPV camera, allowing complex models to be run on a ground station, enhancing safety during aggressive maneuver testing.
 - Both platforms use Betaflight-compatible flight controllers, 3000KV motors, and 3.5-inch propellers, with masses of 750g (VIS-R) and 470g (VIS-H).

2. **Low-Level Control Bridge: `betaflight-ctrl`:**
 - This open-source package facilitates communication between the onboard computer and the flight controller.
 - It includes a finite-state machine (FSM) with modes for System Initialization, Autonomous Ascent, Stabilized Hover, and Policy Control, enabling safe operation and rapid fallback.
 - The package publishes telemetry data, supports adaptive throttle-acceleration mapping, and offers various command modes (position, velocity, attitude-thrust, CTBR).

3. **Sim-to-Real Alignment:**
 - **System Identification:** This involves precisely measuring physical parameters (mass, arm length) and calculating the moment of inertia using a digital prototype. The motor-propeller system is characterized using a test stand (LY-5KGF) to fit quadratic thrust-speed mappings and identify motor time constants (Table IV).
 - **Latency Compensation:** Addresses delays from motor response and system communication. Step-response alignment is used, applying step signals to the real quadrotor and matching its response with simulation by introducing action-frame delays and tuning PID parameters.
 - **Domain Randomization:** Empirically validated domain randomization parameters are provided for initial configurations and task-specific environmental factors (Table V). A task-oriented randomization scheme is employed to balance robustness and training efficiency, with intrinsic physical parameters not randomized due to accurate system identification.
 - **Noise Modeling:** For perception-aware tasks, simulated sensor noise is incorporated. Gaussian noise is added to the inverse depth map to improve visual feature robustness. The Redwood depth noise model can also be employed for enhanced realism, especially for stereo RGB-D cameras (Table VI).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the E2E-Fly framework across six benchmark tasks, categorizing them into state-based (hovering, landing, tracking, racing) and vision-based (visual landing, racing in cluttered environments) tasks.

**A. Learning via Reinforcement Learning and Differentiable Simulation**

- **Comparison of PPO and BPTT:** A detailed comparison was conducted on four state-based tasks (hovering, landing, tracking, racing).
 - **Convergence and Reward:** BPTT demonstrated faster convergence and consistently achieved higher reward values compared to PPO across all tasks (Fig. 7).
 - **Training Efficiency:** While per-step computational cost (FPS) was comparable (e.g., ≈20000 its/s for PPO, ≈13000-24000 its/s for BPTT), BPTT required significantly fewer environment steps to converge. This resulted in substantially shorter overall training times for BPTT (average ≈1,200 seconds) compared to PPO (average ≈4,150 seconds) (Table VII).
 - **Reasoning:** The superior efficiency of BPTT is attributed to its use of precise analytical gradients from the differentiable dynamics model, enabling rapid and stable optimization with fewer iterations. PPO, relying on stochastic gradient estimates, requires more extensive sampling, leading to slower convergence. Both methods were capable of accomplishing the tasks in simulation (Fig. 6).

**B. Learning under Different Reward Functions**

- **Validation of Reward Design Guidelines:** An ablation study assessed the influence of individual reward components (progress, action smoothness, linear/angular velocity, sparse rewards) on task performance for state-based tasks, using both PPO (Fig. 8) and BPTT (Fig. 9).
 - **Effectiveness:** The proposed reward design guidelines effectively guided both PPO and BPTT to accomplish various state-based tasks.
 - **Component Importance:** Fundamental dense reward components (progress, action smoothness, linear velocity, angular velocity) were found to be essential for stability-oriented tasks (hovering, landing, tracking). The absence of any component degraded performance.
 - **Sparse Rewards for RL:** For RL algorithms like PPO, sparse terminal rewards (goal achievement, collision penalty) were critical for guiding the agent toward successful task completion by providing feedback on long-term cumulative returns.
 - **Task-Specific Rewards:** For tasks like landing, distinct progress rewards for the XY-plane and Z-axis were beneficial, while for racing, a heavily weighted progress reward was dominant.

**C. Learning with Visual Input**

- **Effectiveness for Vision-based Tasks:** The framework's effectiveness was demonstrated on two vision-based tasks: visual landing with semantic segmentation (trained with BPTT) and obstacle-aware racing with depth maps (trained with PPO).
 - **Obstacle-aware Racing:** PPO, combined with a curriculum learning strategy, successfully generalized to unseen environments, achieving a 100% success rate across three different racetracks (S-shaped, 3D circle, J-shaped) with speeds exceeding 10 m/s (Fig. 11, Fig. 12).
 - **Visual Landing:** BPTT achieved end-to-end single-step training using only semantic segmentation, without prior knowledge of the landing target. The policy exhibited generalization across diverse initial positions and landing platforms of varying shapes (triangular, circular, square) (Fig. 10). BPTT required only 1 million time steps for this task, showcasing high sample efficiency (Fig. 12).

**D. Zero-shot Sim-to-Real Transfer**

- **Alignment Effectiveness:** The comprehensive sim-to-real alignment methodology (system identification, latency compensation, domain randomization, noise modeling) resulted in a close match between simulated and real-world step response curves for angular velocity and thrust (Fig. 13, Fig. 14).
- **Zero-shot Deployment:** This alignment enabled reliable zero-shot deployment of trained policies to physical quadrotors (VIS-R platform) without task-specific fine-tuning. Once converged in simulation, policies could be executed directly on hardware while preserving functional characteristics.
- **Real-world Performance:** Task-level comparisons showed that learned policies maintained nearly equivalent performance across simulated (Fig. 6) and real environments (Fig. 15) for all six benchmark tasks, confirming the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed sim-to-real pipeline. The aggressive racing task with random obstacles was additionally validated via hardware-in-the-loop simulation on the VIS-H platform.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The E2E-Fly framework contributes to the field of quadrotor autonomy by providing a comprehensive, integrated system for the entire policy development lifecycle, from training to validation and real-world deployment. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Unified and Systematic Approach:** E2E-Fly represents a systematic unification of differentiable physical simulation, first-order gradient-based learning, structured validation, and real-world deployment into a cohesive workflow for quadrotor end-to-end control. This integration addresses the fragmentation observed in previous research, which often focused on isolated aspects of the problem.
2. **Enhanced Training Efficiency and Policy Quality:** By integrating a high-performance differentiable simulator (VisFly) and supporting algorithms like BPTT, the framework enables efficient policy optimization. The findings demonstrate that BPTT achieves significantly faster convergence and higher sample efficiency compared to black-box RL methods like PPO, allowing for rapid policy development. This directly translates to reduced computational resources and time for researchers.
3. **Robust and Safe Real-World Deployment:** The multi-stage validation strategy (sim-to-sim and hardware-in-the-loop) and the comprehensive four-stage sim-to-real alignment methodology (system identification, latency compensation, domain randomization, and noise modeling) enhance policy robustness. This systematic approach reduces the "sim-to-real gap," enabling reliable zero-shot transfer of policies to physical hardware without extensive fine-tuning. This minimizes the risks and costs associated with real-world testing, particularly for aggressive maneuvers.
4. **Reproducibility and Accessibility:** By providing an integrated software and hardware stack, including open-source hardware designs, a low-level control bridge (`betaflight-ctrl`), structured reward design guidelines, and detailed sim-to-real protocols, E2E-Fly aims to lower the barrier for researchers and practitioners. This fosters reproducibility of results and facilitates broader application of end-to-end methods in agile quadrotor flight.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework provides a robust platform for future research in several directions. It enables further exploration into combining the strengths of differentiable simulation (high sample efficiency, accurate control) and reinforcement learning (exploration capabilities, robustness to model inaccuracies) for complex, long-horizon tasks. The detailed methodologies for reward design, curriculum learning, and sim-to-real alignment can serve as baselines for developing more advanced autonomous flight capabilities.
6. **Demonstrated Versatility:** The successful application and zero-shot transfer of policies across six diverse benchmark tasks—including state-based tasks like hovering and high-speed racing, and vision-based tasks like visual landing and obstacle-aware racing—demonstrate the versatility and effectiveness of the E2E-Fly system for various quadrotor applications.

Overall, E2E-Fly offers a complete and reproducible platform that accelerates research iteration cycles and facilitates the practical application of learning-based end-to-end algorithms in autonomous quadrotor flight.
