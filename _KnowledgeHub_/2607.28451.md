# Machines that know they are aging: a framework for hardware-aware autonomous intelligence

> [!summary] Summary
> The Aging-Aware Autonomous Intelligence (AAAI) framework is proposed as a conceptual architecture to enable autonomous systems to integrate real-time hardware degradation into their reasoning and planning. This allows agents to adapt their behavior to proactively manage their remaining operational lifespan and enhance system resilience against physical aging.

## Problem

- Current AI architectures for autonomous systems implicitly assume underlying hardware remains in a pristine state, ignoring inevitable physical degradation of components like batteries and sensors.
- This hardware-agnostic assumption leads to "agnostic collapse," a mission failure mode where cumulative, unmonitored hardware degradation causes suboptimal operation or abrupt failure.
- A "cognitive gap" exists, preventing the systematic integration of continuous hardware health estimation into the AI's high-level reasoning, planning, and mission strategy.

## Method

- The **AAAI** (Aging-Aware Autonomous Intelligence) framework integrates hardware health into the cognitive processes of autonomous systems through three interconnected pillars: ==Hardware Self-Awareness==, ==Self-Adaptive Reasoning Engine==, and ==Survival-Centric Intelligence Layer==.
- It employs continuous, real-time ==physics-of-failure models== to estimate the health of critical subsystems, developing a dynamic "cognitive aging model."
- The framework dynamically modulates the system's operational parameters, such as task complexity, planning horizons, and resource allocation, based on its continuously changing hardware capabilities.

## Results

- The AAAI framework offers a unified cognitive architecture that bridges the ==cognitive gap== by systematically connecting hardware degradation to an autonomous system's intelligent decision-making.
- It enables autonomous systems to proactively adapt to hardware aging, modulating inference complexity, contracting planning horizons, and reordering task priorities to maintain operational continuity.
- The framework is hypothesized to enhance resilience, extend operational lifetimes, and improve safety and reliability for autonomous systems by allowing planned transitions through full-health, resource-conservation, and graceful withdrawal phases.

> Note: this is a conceptual/framework paper — no empirical benchmark results are reported, only architectural claims.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The paper identifies and defines "agnostic collapse" as a critical and distinct mission failure mode resulting from the AI's unawareness of its own hardware's gradual degradation.
> - Hardware health can be continuously monitored and represented by scalar values, updated using established physics-of-failure models and considering coupled degradation dynamics across subsystems.
> - Integrating hardware aging directly into an AI's cognitive layers enables dynamic adaptation of reasoning, strategic resource allocation, and a shift towards survival-centric intelligence for maximizing operational utility.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous systems are increasingly deployed in environments where their operational lifespan can extend over long durations, leading to inevitable hardware degradation. Existing artificial intelligence (AI) architectures generally operate under an implicit assumption that the underlying hardware remains in its original, pristine condition throughout its operational life. This assumption applies to various components, including batteries, sensors, processors, and memory modules. However, the physical realities of deployment, such as thermal cycling, radiation exposure, electrochemical changes, and sustained voltage stress, lead to predictable and well-documented degradation of these components.

The broader research landscape has addressed aspects of hardware reliability and degradation through several distinct approaches, but often without systematic integration into the AI layer that governs system behavior:

- **Physics-of-Failure Modeling:** Materials scientists and reliability engineers have extensively characterized hardware degradation mechanisms (e.g., Arrhenius kinetics for battery aging, time-dependent dielectric breakdown in silicon, radiation-induced bit errors in memory). This body of knowledge provides foundational models for predicting component wear and end-of-life.
- **Prognostics and Health Management (PHM):** Tools exist to forecast the remaining useful life of components (e.g., battery capacity decline). While these tools can predict when a component might fail, they typically do not provide mechanisms for the autonomous system's intelligence layer to modify its reasoning or planning based on this prognostic information in real time.
- **Hardware Redundancy:** Deploying redundant components can mask individual failures, thereby enhancing fault tolerance. However, redundancy is finite, adds cost, and increases weight and complexity, making it less suitable for constrained platforms. It addresses discrete failures rather than gradual performance degradation.
- **AI-Based Device Health Monitoring:** Recent advancements in on-chip sensors and AI for monitoring hardware health can detect degradation and facilitate local adjustments, such as resource allocation or protecting components from further wear. However, these often operate at a low-level hardware management layer without influencing higher-level cognitive functions like mission planning or strategic decision-making.
- **Autonomous Lifecycle Management and Proactive Life Extension:** Algorithms have been developed to balance hardware usage for longevity (e.g., CPU core management, aging-aware voltage scaling). Some work, such as ReaLM, optimizes model performance under reduced hardware power by exploiting neural network tolerance to numerical perturbations. These approaches target specific layers of optimization but lack a unified, system-wide cognitive response to degradation.
- **Cross-Layer Co-Design:** Methodologies like HAWQ-V2 optimize software-hardware interactions (e.g., precision allocation for neural networks) at design time. While effective for initial optimization, they do not provide runtime adaptation to evolving hardware health.

The paper identifies a "cognitive gap" in existing research: a lack of a unified architecture that connects continuous, multi-subsystem hardware health estimation with adaptive reasoning, planning, and mission strategy in real time. Prior work primarily focuses on monitoring, protecting, or locally adapting hardware, but it generally stops short of enabling the AI system itself to dynamically adjust its high-level objectives and behaviors in response to its own physical aging. The proposed Aging-Aware Autonomous Intelligence (AAAI) framework seeks to bridge this gap by integrating these disparate elements into a closed-loop cognitive system, allowing autonomous agents to systematically incorporate knowledge of their physical condition into their operational intelligence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this work arises from the inherent and often overlooked reality that autonomous systems, like all physical entities, are subject to wear and aging. While their software is designed to operate under ideal hardware conditions, the physical components—such as batteries, sensors, processors, and memory—undergo progressive degradation over time. This degradation is a well-understood physical process, yet it is rarely accounted for in the AI layer of deployed autonomous systems.

This mismatch between assumed and actual hardware capability leads to several critical problems:

- **Agnostic Collapse:** The paper introduces the concept of "agnostic collapse," a mission failure mode where hardware capabilities gradually decline below the thresholds assumed by the AI, without triggering conventional fault diagnostics. This leads to a steady deterioration of mission performance and eventual failure, not due to a single component fault, but due to accumulated, unacknowledged degradation. Examples include an autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) continuing to plan missions assuming full battery capacity or sensor accuracy, despite significant degradation.
- **Suboptimal Operation:** An AI system unaware of its hardware's declining state may either attempt computationally demanding operations (e.g., high-resolution sensing, long-range planning, deep-learning inference) that its diminished hardware can no longer reliably execute, leading to errors and wasted resources. Alternatively, it might err on the side of caution, defaulting to conservative "safe modes" unnecessarily early, thereby squandering usable residual capacity. Both scenarios result in inefficient and potentially unsafe operation.
- **Lack of Graceful Degradation:** In environments where maintenance is difficult or impossible (e.g., space, deep sea, medical implants), systems currently lack the ability to adaptively manage their remaining operational life. This can lead to abrupt mission failure rather than a planned and controlled winding down of operations.
- **Safety and Reliability Concerns:** In safety-critical applications, such as implantable medical devices or crewed spacecraft, unexpected hardware degradation leading to failure can have severe consequences, including the need for invasive interventions or catastrophic mission loss.

Given these motivations, the key objectives of the proposed Aging-Aware Autonomous Intelligence (AAAI) framework are:

- To integrate real-time hardware health status directly into the reasoning, planning, and mission execution layers of autonomous systems.
- To enable autonomous machines to proactively recognize, model, and respond to their own physical aging processes.
- To develop a closed-loop cognitive architecture that unifies existing knowledge from prognostics, lifecycle management, and hardware-aware computing.
- To facilitate self-adaptive behavior, where an AI system can dynamically adjust its task complexity, planning horizon, and resource allocation in response to its continuously changing hardware capabilities.
- To introduce "survival-centric intelligence" that treats remaining operational lifespan as a resource to be strategically allocated, thereby maximizing mission utility and enabling graceful degradation.
- Ultimately, the framework aims to improve the resilience, extend the operational lifetime, and enhance the safety and reliability of autonomous systems, particularly in inaccessible or safety-critical deployment contexts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Aging-Aware Autonomous Intelligence (AAAI) framework is a conceptual architectural design that integrates hardware health into the cognitive processes of autonomous systems. It is structured around three interconnected functional pillars, designed to provide a closed-loop system where physical state informs and adapts intelligent behavior. The methodology describes how this integration is achieved by building upon and unifying existing research strands.

**Pillar 1: Hardware Self-Awareness (Cognitive Aging Model)**
This pillar focuses on the continuous estimation and representation of the physical condition of critical hardware subsystems.
- **Health Representation:** The condition of each subsystem—power, sensing, memory, and computation—is represented by a continuous scalar value, *h* ∈, where 1 denotes fully healthy and 0 indicates failure.
- **Physics-of-Failure Models:** These health scalars are continuously updated in real-time using established physics-of-failure models. These models, developed by materials scientists and reliability engineers, characterize the degradation mechanisms of various components (e.g., Arrhenius activation kinetics for battery capacity fade, thermal aging for processor reliability, radiation damage for solar panels and memory cells).
- **Coupled Degradation Dynamics:** The framework emphasizes monitoring health indicators across subsystems as an interconnected process rather than tracking components in isolation. This acknowledges that environmental stresses (e.g., elevated temperatures, corrosive agents, radiation) often affect multiple components simultaneously, allowing for earlier identification of emerging platform-wide failures before any single component reaches a critical threshold.
- **Integration of Existing Technologies:** This pillar integrates current capabilities in on-chip sensing, time-dependent dielectric breakdown monitoring, and negative bias temperature instability tracking to provide real-time indices of hardware aging.

**Pillar 2: Self-Adaptive Reasoning Engine**
This pillar utilizes the hardware health information from Pillar 1 to dynamically modulate the system's cognitive processes, specifically task scope and complexity.
- **Dynamic Modulation:** As measured hardware capacity declines, the reasoning layer proportionally adjusts its operational parameters. For instance, under nominal conditions, the system might employ high-capacity deep learning models and extended planning horizons. When degradation is detected, it adapts by:
 - Substituting lightweight inference architectures for more resource-intensive ones.
 - Contracting its planning horizon to reduce computational load.
 - Reordering its task queue to prioritize operations with the highest utility-to-cost ratio, where 'cost' now includes hardware resources and remaining operational life.
- **Continuous Control Response:** This adaptation is designed as a continuous, proportional control response, rather than discrete mode transitions, allowing the system to track the degrading hardware envelope smoothly in real-time. This aims to maintain operational continuity even as hardware performance declines, contrasting with hardware-agnostic systems that would experience functional collapse.
- **Connecting to Resource Allocation:** This pillar integrates principles from autonomous lifecycle management and proactive life extension, which focus on balancing performance against longevity and preserving model performance despite reduced power, but elevates these considerations to the level of cognitive planning.

**Pillar 3: Survival-Centric Intelligence Layer**
This pillar extends the mission objective to actively manage the system's remaining operational capacity, treating it as a finite resource to be strategically allocated.
- **Maximizing Mission Utility:** The objective is to maximize overall mission utility over the remaining deployment period by distributing the remaining hardware life across competing mission priorities.
- **Adaptive Operational States:** The framework defines three operational modes for this strategic allocation:
 1. **Full-Health Operation:** The system operates normally with its full complement of capabilities.
 2. **Resource-Conservation Phase:** As degradation emerges, the system transitions to reduce resource consumption while preserving mission-critical functions. This involves less aggressive operations or more efficient algorithms to prolong functional life.
 3. **Graceful Withdrawal:** In the final stage of significant degradation, the system progressively shuts down non-essential subsystems according to a precomputed prioritization strategy. It restricts its behavior to a minimal operational core and reduces communication frequency to maximize the remaining operational duration. This is conceptualized as a planned, controlled withdrawal rather than an abrupt failure.
- **Integration with Graceful Degradation Concepts:** This pillar draws on concepts of graceful degradation and context-aware scheduling for mixed-criticality systems, applying them to the overarching mission strategy.

The AAAI framework's methodology lies in its architectural design, which integrates these three pillars into a coherent, closed-loop cognitive system. It aims to elevate hardware health from a low-level monitoring or local adjustment concern to a fundamental input for high-level reasoning, planning, and strategic decision-making in autonomous systems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper "Machines that know they are aging: a framework for hardware-aware autonomous intelligence" primarily presents a conceptual framework and architectural design rather than empirical results from an implemented system. Therefore, the "findings" are the articulation of this framework, its hypothesized capabilities, and the identification of a significant gap in current autonomous system design.

The main conceptual findings and proposed capabilities of the AAAI framework are:

- **Identification of the "Agnostic Collapse" Failure Mode:** The paper identifies and defines "agnostic collapse" as a distinct and critical failure mode in autonomous systems. This collapse arises not from a single component failure, but from the cumulative, unmonitored degradation of hardware, leading to a growing mismatch between the AI's assumed capabilities and the actual physical capacity of the platform. This highlights a fundamental design flaw in hardware-agnostic AI.
- **A Unified Cognitive Architecture for Hardware-Awareness:** The core result is the proposition of the AAAI framework itself, which integrates hardware health awareness directly into the intelligent decision-making process. This framework addresses the "cognitive gap" by connecting established knowledge of physical degradation to an autonomous system's reasoning, planning, and mission execution.
- **Continuous Hardware Self-Awareness:** The framework proposes a mechanism for continuous, real-time estimation of the health of critical subsystems (power, sensing, memory, computation) using physics-of-failure models. This enables the system to maintain an up-to-date "cognitive aging model" of its physical state, moving beyond simple fault detection to proactive degradation tracking.
- **Modeling of Coupled Degradation:** A key aspect is the recognition and modeling of interdependent degradation dynamics across subsystems. By understanding how environmental stresses affect multiple components simultaneously, the framework enables earlier detection of platform-wide degradation, which might otherwise be missed by isolated component monitoring.
- **Dynamic and Proportional Self-Adaptive Reasoning:** The framework demonstrates how an intelligence layer can dynamically adjust its operational parameters in response to changing hardware capabilities. This includes modulating inference complexity, contracting planning horizons, and reordering task priorities based on the utility-to-cost ratio, thereby maintaining operational continuity where an unaware system would fail.
- **Strategic Resource Allocation for Mission Longevity:** AAAI introduces "survival-centric intelligence" that treats remaining operational life as a resource to be optimized across mission objectives. This enables systems to transition through full-health, resource-conservation, and graceful withdrawal phases, thereby extending functional existence and allowing for planned rather than abrupt mission termination.
- **Bridging Interdisciplinary Gaps:** The paper highlights that the AAAI framework unifies elements from materials science, reliability engineering, prognostics, lifecycle management, and hardware-aware computing into a coherent, closed-loop system, demonstrating how these disparate fields can be integrated to enhance autonomous capabilities.
- **Acknowledgement of New Risks:** While proposing benefits, the framework also explicitly acknowledges the new category of risk it introduces: autonomous systems making consequential operational choices based on inferred hardware state that might be unanticipated or unauthorized. This necessitates robust safety architectures, auditable criteria for mode transitions, fail-safe defaults, and human-in-the-loop oversight.

In summary, the paper's findings are the theoretical construct of AAAI and its proposed mechanisms for allowing autonomous systems to "know they are aging," enabling more resilient, enduring, and safely manageable operations by aligning AI behavior with the physical realities of its supporting hardware. It sets a foundation for future research and development in this interdisciplinary domain.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Aging-Aware Autonomous Intelligence (AAAI) framework carries significant implications for the design, deployment, and operational longevity of autonomous systems across various critical domains.

- **Enhanced Resilience and Operational Lifetime:** By enabling autonomous systems to continuously monitor their hardware health and adapt their behavior accordingly, AAAI can significantly improve system resilience. Instead of failing abruptly due to unacknowledged degradation, systems can adjust their operational profiles to conserve resources, reduce strain, and maintain essential functions for longer. This proactive management of hardware wear can substantially extend the operational lifetime of autonomous platforms, particularly those in inaccessible environments where maintenance or replacement is impractical.

- **Improved Safety and Reliability in Critical Applications:** The impact on safety-critical domains is substantial. In contexts such as space missions (microsatellites), deep-sea robotics, and implantable medical devices (e.g., cardiac pacemakers), premature hardware failure can have catastrophic consequences or necessitate risky, costly interventions. AAAI's ability to forecast degradation and adapt behavior offers a mechanism to mitigate these risks, ensuring that systems operate within their actual capabilities and can execute planned, graceful withdrawals rather than unexpected collapses. This translates directly into safer operations and potentially fewer medical complications.

- **Paradigm Shift in Autonomous System Design:** The framework challenges the conventional assumption of stable hardware in AI design. It advocates for a shift from a hardware-agnostic view of computation to one of embodied intelligence, where the AI is intrinsically aware of and responsive to the physical substrate sustaining it. This redefinition of autonomy could foster a more holistic approach to system engineering, emphasizing the intertwined nature of software intelligence and hardware reality.

- **Catalyst for Interdisciplinary Research:** The successful implementation of AAAI necessitates deep integration across multiple scientific and engineering disciplines. It requires collaboration between materials scientists (for physics-of-failure models), reliability engineers (for prognostics), control theorists (for adaptive behavior), embedded systems developers (for real-time monitoring), and artificial intelligence researchers (for adaptive reasoning and planning). The framework serves as a conceptual blueprint for unifying these disparate fields towards a common goal, potentially driving new interdisciplinary research avenues.

- **Mitigation of "Agnostic Collapse":** By directly addressing the "agnostic collapse" failure mode, AAAI provides a systematic solution to a previously unacknowledged, yet pervasive, challenge in long-duration autonomous deployments. This capability ensures that systems align their computational demands with their actual physical capacity, preventing the silent deterioration that leads to mission failure.

- **Challenges and Future Considerations:** The paper also acknowledges critical limitations, which define areas for future impact. These include the variability in accuracy of physics-of-failure models under diverse deployment conditions, the inherent difficulty in exhaustively characterizing coupled degradation effects *a priori*, and the computational overhead of continuous health monitoring, which itself must be managed within declining hardware capacity. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for the practical realization of AAAI.

- **Ethical and Safety Implications:** The framework introduces a new category of risk, where autonomous systems, by adjusting their own decision-making based on inferred hardware state, might make unanticipated or unauthorized operational choices. This highlights the critical importance of developing robust safety architectures, including formal specification of mode-transition conditions, fail-safe defaults, and human-in-the-loop confirmation thresholds, especially for safety-critical applications. The framework's impact will thus also extend to the development of rigorous ethical guidelines and verification methodologies for such self-adaptive systems.

In conclusion, AAAI offers a foundational shift in how autonomous intelligence can be conceived and engineered, promising to yield systems that are not only more resilient and long-lasting but also inherently safer and more reliable in the face of inevitable physical aging. Its potential impact lies in transforming the operational paradigms for autonomous technologies across diverse and demanding environments.
