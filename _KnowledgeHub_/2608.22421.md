# Where World Models Break: Natural-Input Failure Discovery

> [!summary] Summary
> BasinLens recasts world-model evaluation as finite-budget natural-input failure discovery: it treats each environment-valid reset or action prefix as typed coordinates, pairs a Gaussian-process uncertainty-guided global proposal pool with typed single-coordinate local replacements, and returns ranked failure records later checked on disjoint seeds and radius-1 legal neighborhoods. On a 12,016-candidate PushT grid it reaches high-risk inputs in 54.7% of budget-64 runs versus 10.9% for random search.

## Problem

- Existing world-model benchmarks average error over benign randomized continuations, so rare condition-action combinations that trigger catastrophic localized prediction collapse remain hidden.
- Adversarial examples, adversarial policies and world-model attacks perturb inputs, opponents or conditioning channels, targeting classifiers or policies rather than a fixed model's prediction failures on valid inputs.
- Black-box optimizers such as GP-UCB can locate high-loss inputs but never test whether a discovered failure reproduces across fresh seeds or persists under nearby legal edits.

## Method

- Formalizes ==natural-input failure discovery==: under a fixed query budget, find environment-valid resets or action prefixes maximizing prediction risk aggregated as upper-tail ==CVaR==, minus a ==task-activity penalty==.
- **BasinLens** search couples a ==typed valid frontier==, replacing one semantic coordinate of high-score anchors with admissible values, and a ==GP-UCB global pool==, merged by an acquisition adding parent-score, anchor-proximity and novelty terms.
- ==Frozen-evidence protocol==: shortlist top-k discovery records, then re-evaluate on disjoint seeds and fresh ==radius-1 legal neighborhoods== without feeding results back into acquisition; Interaction, Beam and Frontier variants isolate components.

## Results

- On the **12,016**-candidate PushT grid at budget **64**, crossing risk threshold **1.60** happens in **54.7%** of BasinLens runs versus **40.6%** for GP-UCB (p=**0.061**, descriptive) and **10.9%** for random search.
- Selected inputs keep elevated risk on **16** disjoint seeds, mean CVaR **1.311** versus **1.186** for the default reset; radius-1 neighborhoods score **1.252** against **0.608** mid-score and **0.546** random controls.
- Input structure flips the winner: combinatorial-grid Beam and Interaction variants hit **0.963** and **0.961** top-5% rates versus **0.828** random, while GP-UCB leads the single-factor grid at **0.875**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aggregate fidelity can look strong while a coherent region of legal inputs collapses forecasts, so report discovered worst-case conditions beside mean benchmark scores.
> - A credible failure claim needs held-out-seed reproduction plus persistence under adjacent legal edits; otherwise an adaptive search maximum may be a noisy outlier.
> - Typed coordinate structure pays off only in heterogeneous, interacting input spaces; plain GP-UCB or random search stays competitive in low-dimensional homogeneous ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

World models are learned predictive models that estimate how an environment will evolve under a sequence of actions. They are used as internal simulators in model-based reinforcement learning, model-predictive control, planning, and policy training. Existing systems operate in several representation spaces, including pixels, discrete visual tokens, latent states, and pretrained visual features.

Much of the current evaluation literature measures average prediction error, physical fidelity, interactive performance, or downstream task success over randomized trajectories. These evaluations are useful for comparing models under typical conditions, but they may conceal localized regions of the valid input space where a model fails severely. A world model can therefore obtain a favorable average score while producing unreliable predictions for particular resets, goals, obstacle configurations, or action prefixes. Such failures may affect planning disproportionately because a single erroneous imagined trajectory can change the ranking of candidate actions.

The paper distinguishes its focus from several adjacent areas. Adversarial-example research generally seeks perturbations that cause classifiers or policies to fail, while adversarial policy research uses legal actions by an opponent to manipulate a target policy. Adaptive stress testing and scenario falsification search for conditions that cause an agent or system to violate a predefined requirement. Bayesian optimization searches expensive black-box functions, but ordinarily does not require that a discovered high-loss point reproduce across independent environment seeds or remain problematic under nearby valid changes.

The paper introduces “natural-input failure discovery” as a separate evaluation problem. Its objective is not to estimate average failure frequency or optimize a controller directly. Instead, it seeks concrete, executable, environment-valid inputs that cause a fixed world model to produce high prediction risk, and then tests whether those failures are reproducible and locally persistent. BASINLENS is presented as one structured implementation of this broader evaluation framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation is that world-model prediction errors can propagate through downstream planning. Planners compare predicted futures, so a localized error can cause the system to select an inferior action even when the model’s overall mean error is modest. The authors argue that evaluation should therefore identify the conditions under which the model becomes unreliable, not merely report aggregate performance.

The work has four main objectives:

1. **Formalize finite-budget failure discovery.** The authors define a setting in which a fixed model, environment, valid-input space, and query budget are given. The task is to find a small number of valid reset conditions or action prefixes with high prediction risk.

2. **Preserve natural validity and interpretability.** Inputs should be ordinary environment configurations accepted by reset or action interfaces. The method should not rely on pixel perturbations, invalid simulator states, or arbitrary movements in a normalized vector space.

3. **Separate discovery from evidence.** Since adaptive search can select noisy or extreme observations, a discovered case must be reevaluated on disjoint seeds. The paper additionally asks whether the risk persists in a neighborhood of nearby valid inputs.

4. **Assess where structured search is useful.** The authors investigate whether semantic structure in the input space improves search efficiency, while also examining settings in which standard random or Gaussian-process-based search remains competitive.

The broader scientific object is consequently a reproducible failure record containing the input specification, prediction interface, search score, evaluation seeds, held-out results, and behavior under legal nearby edits.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Formal Problem Definition

A valid natural input \(x\) belongs to an environment-defined set \(X\). It may specify reset variables, a target, an obstacle configuration, or an action prefix. Executing \(x\) with environment seed \(\xi\) produces a trajectory. At a chosen forecast origin and horizon, the fixed world model predicts a future sequence, which is compared with the corresponding environment future using a discrepancy function such as mean squared error.

The per-rollout risk is denoted \(\ell(x,\xi)\). Risk over a seed set is aggregated using an operator such as the mean, maximum, or upper-tail conditional value at risk (CVaR). The main PushT experiments use final-step CVaR, emphasizing the largest errors among evaluation seeds. Some protocols also subtract a task-activity penalty when a candidate produces an inactive or uninformative environment rollout.

The search may evaluate at most \(B\) candidates. The highest-scoring queried candidates form a shortlist. Crucially, the shortlist is frozen before held-out and neighborhood testing. Validation data are not fed back into the search or used to rerank the discovery trace.

#### 3.2. Typed Input Representation

Each input coordinate is assigned a semantic type, an admissible domain, and a valid edit operation. In PushT, for example, the input can contain the agent start, block start, and goal positions, represented by six coordinate values. Candidate combinations are retained only when they satisfy environment constraints, such as minimum distances between the agent, block, and goal.

The typed representation creates a valid-input graph. One-coordinate replacements define broad legal search moves, while radius-1 neighborhoods use adjacent values in an ordered coordinate grid. This distinction allows the method to perform larger local exploration while using a stricter definition of locality for validation.

#### 3.3. BASINLENS Search

BASINLENS combines global surrogate-based exploration with local typed replacement.

For global search, the method normalizes candidate coordinates and fits a Gaussian-process surrogate to observed risk values. It uses a Gaussian-process upper confidence bound, consisting of a predicted mean plus an uncertainty term. This enables proposals that are either predicted to have high risk or remain uncertain.

For local search, BASINLENS selects high-scoring evaluated inputs as anchors and generates valid candidates by replacing one typed coordinate. The candidate pool is then augmented with structural terms reflecting the score of the parent anchor, proximity to anchors, and novelty relative to previously evaluated inputs. The highest-scoring candidate in the merged global-local pool is queried next.

The method does not require model gradients or a differentiable simulator. It requires only that a candidate can be executed and assigned a prediction-risk score.

#### 3.4. Validation Protocol

The authors use three distinct forms of evidence:

- **Discovery:** Candidates are searched under a finite query budget.
- **Held-out point reproducibility:** Selected inputs are evaluated on disjoint environment seeds.
- **Local persistence:** All valid radius-1 neighbors around selected anchors are evaluated on fresh seeds, and their average risk is compared with neighborhoods around mid-score and random control anchors.

This design distinguishes an isolated noisy maximum from a repeatable point failure or a broader locally persistent failure region.

#### 3.5. Experimental Scope

The primary experiments use LeWorldModel on PushT and TwoRooms. Additional studies use DINO-WM on PushT and Wall, JEPA-WM on PointMaze, and DIAMOND and IRIS on Atari. Prediction risk is measured in different spaces depending on the model: state-probe MSE, visual-feature MSE, joint latent error, or future-frame MSE.

The main online PushT grid contains 12,016 valid candidates. Experiments use 64 paired algorithm restarts, eight search seeds per candidate, and query budgets of 16, 32, and 64. Random search and GP-UCB are the primary baselines. Cached component studies also evaluate frontier-only, interaction-aware, beam, and cross-entropy variants.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Finite-Budget Discovery

On the 12,016-candidate PushT grid, BASINLENS crossed a prespecified search-risk threshold of 1.60 in 54.7% of budget-64 runs. GP-UCB crossed it in 40.6% of runs, while random search did so in 10.9%. BASINLENS was significantly better than random search under the paired McNemar test, with \(p = 4.18 \times 10^{-6}\). Its advantage over GP-UCB at this single threshold was descriptive rather than conventionally significant, with \(p = 0.061\).

Mean best search scores at budget 64 were 1.572 for BASINLENS, 1.547 for GP-UCB, and 1.512 for random search. The BASINLENS comparison with GP-UCB had \(p = 0.0027\). Threshold-averaged discovery rates over thresholds from 1.50 to 1.65 were 0.572, 0.474, and 0.255, respectively.

#### 4.2. Reproducibility and Local Persistence

In the held-out point-validation protocol, 24 selected inputs with complete measurements had mean prediction CVaR of 1.311 on 16 disjoint seeds. The default reset evaluated on the same seeds had mean CVaR of 1.186. This supports reproducibility of elevated risk, although the protocol did not validate equivalent shortlists from every competing method and therefore does not establish comparative validation quality.

The neighborhood results provide evidence that the discovered failures are not confined to individual points. Around the eight highest-scoring anchors, fresh radius-1 neighborhoods had mean CVaR of 1.252. Neighborhoods around mid-score controls had CVaR of 0.608, and random controls had CVaR of 0.546. The reported exact permutation tests found significant differences against both control groups.

#### 4.3. Character of the Discovered Failures

The qualitative cases reveal several failure patterns. In TwoRooms, the model’s predicted future can stop advancing after early forecast steps even though the environment trajectory continues. In PushT, later requested future times may repeatedly map to an early predicted state, producing prediction stalling. In DINO-WM’s Wall setting, the environment crosses an opening while the predicted future remains on the original side, indicating a topological or transition-related error.

A fixed-geometry PushT comparison changed only the legal agent start while holding the block and goal constant. The predicted final block–goal distance ratio increased from 0.941 to 1.110, with the higher ratio occurring in 98.4% of 64 paired seeds. In TwoRooms, future-position MAE increased from 13.2 to 89.0 pixels for a selected start, while probe MAE changed from 1.13 to 1.66 pixels. This suggests that the observed discrepancy was not explained solely by the latent-to-state readout, although the authors appropriately interpret the LeWM result as a failure of the complete model-plus-readout pipeline.

#### 4.4. Dependence on Input Structure

The benefit of typed expansion varies with the input space. On a single-factor PushT grid, GP-UCB achieved a top-2% hit rate of 0.875, slightly above BASINLENS at 0.867. On a heterogeneous combinatorial grid, interaction-aware and beam variants reached top-5% hit rates of 0.961 and 0.963, compared with 0.828 for random search and 0.803 for standard BASINLENS.

DINO-WM experiments showed higher best-score discovery and reference-component coverage for BASINLENS than GP-UCB, although the frontier-only variant sometimes achieved slightly higher coverage. JEPA-WM PointMaze results also favored structured search at several budgets. In contrast, random search or UCB performed better in some DIAMOND and IRIS Atari action-prefix studies. These results indicate that typed structure is not universally advantageous; its value is greatest when coordinates have distinct meanings and interact combinatorially.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It proposes that world-model evaluation should include targeted discovery of valid, reproducible prediction failures rather than rely solely on average-case benchmarks. The separation between search, held-out point testing, and neighborhood testing provides a framework for reporting failures with stronger evidential boundaries.

If adopted, this approach could help developers identify weaknesses before world models are used for planning or control. Failure records could support model debugging, targeted data collection, robustness analysis, and controller safeguards. The local-persistence analysis is particularly relevant because a failure region may be more operationally important than a single anomalous configuration.

The results also provide a caution about interpretation. The candidate libraries are finite and do not estimate failure prevalence under deployment distributions. Risk magnitudes are not directly comparable across model interfaces, and the LeWM studies include a learned state probe. BASINLENS does not dominate every baseline, and the cross-model experiments vary simultaneously in model, task, metric, and input structure. Therefore, the work is best understood as a proposed evaluation standard and search implementation rather than evidence of a universally superior optimizer. Its potential impact lies in making hidden, environment-valid weaknesses more systematically discoverable and independently testable.
