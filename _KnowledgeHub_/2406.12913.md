# T-JEPA: A Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture for Trajectory Similarity Computation

> [!summary] Summary
> T-JEPA applies the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture to trajectory similarity computation, automating data augmentation and predicting high-level semantic information in the representation space. It consistently outperforms or remains competitive with existing self-supervised methods on five diverse trajectory datasets, demonstrating enhanced robustness to data quality issues and stronger generalization capabilities.

## Problem

- Existing self-supervised trajectory similarity methods rely heavily on manually defined data augmentation schemes, which are labor-intensive and limit the diversity of learned features.
- Current trajectory data transformations operate at a low 2D Euclidean level, failing to capture intricate high-level semantic variations.
- Real-world trajectory data often suffers from noise, discontinuity, and irregular or low sampling rates, posing challenges for robust representation learning.

## Method

- T-JEPA adapts the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) to learn ==trajectory representations== by predicting sampled target representations from corresponding contexts.
- The method automates data augmentation by sampling trajectory information directly in the embedding space, eliminating the need for manual, domain-specific augmentation strategies.
- An `AdjFuse` module is introduced to enrich contextual information by aggregating features from adjacent geographical cells, enhancing robustness to noisy or sparsely sampled data.

## Results

- T-JEPA demonstrated superior or competitive performance in most similar trajectory search tasks, especially outperforming baselines on sparser Foursquare check-in datasets.
- The method exhibited enhanced robustness to down-sampling and distortion, consistently outperforming baselines across most datasets for various noise levels, with the `AdjFuse` module proving critical.
- It showed stronger generalization capabilities, achieving higher Hit Ratios and Recall when approximating various heuristic similarity measures with a frozen encoder, indicating more adaptable learned representations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting missing trajectory components at a high semantic level in the representation space, rather than raw data reconstruction, leads to more generalized and semantically rich embeddings.
> - Automating data augmentation through representation space sampling effectively overcomes the limitations of manual, low-level augmentations prevalent in prior contrastive learning approaches.
> - Integrating local spatial context via the `AdjFuse` module significantly improves robustness for real-world trajectory data, particularly those with low or irregular sampling rates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Trajectory similarity computation is a fundamental component in the analysis of spatial movement patterns, with applications spanning traffic management, wildlife tracking, and location-based services. Historically, this field has been dominated by non-learning heuristic methods, such as Edit Distance on Real Sequence (EDR), Longest Common Subsequence (LCSS), Hausdorff distance, and Discrete Fréchet distance. These methods, while foundational, typically suffer from high computational complexity, often quadratic in trajectory length, and limited adaptability to varying trajectory characteristics or diverse similarity perspectives due to their reliance on hand-crafted rules.

The advent of deep learning has introduced methodologies to address these limitations. Initially, supervised deep learning models, including those based on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Transformers, and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), were employed to approximate established heuristic measures (e.g., NEUTRAJ, TrajGAT). While these approaches offer more robustness and adaptability than pure heuristics, their learning objective is constrained by approximating pre-defined labels, which restricts their ability to discover generalized and intrinsic trajectory representations.

More recently, self-supervised learning (SSL) has gained prominence in trajectory representation learning, moving beyond the limitations of supervised methods by leveraging the structure inherent in unlabeled data. Early SSL efforts, such as t2vec, utilized generative encoder-decoder architectures. A significant advancement in this area has been the application of contrastive learning, which excels at discerning similarities and differences between data samples. TrajCL, for instance, implemented a contrastive learning framework with novel data augmentation techniques and a dual-feature module, achieving state-of-the-art results in trajectory similarity computation.

However, existing contrastive learning-based methods, including TrajCL, present certain limitations. A primary concern is their heavy dependence on manually defined data augmentation schemes. Such augmentations necessitate considerable manual effort and domain knowledge to design transformations that preserve essential trajectory features while introducing beneficial variations. This manual intervention can limit the diversity of generated trajectories and constrain the model's ability to learn robust representations, particularly in unforeseen scenarios. Furthermore, these data transformations often operate at a low-level in 2D Euclidean space (e.g., down-sampling, distortion). Representations learned from such basic geometric changes may not effectively capture intricate, high-level semantic variations crucial for a comprehensive understanding of trajectory similarities.

Within this context, the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) has emerged as an alternative self-supervised learning paradigm, demonstrating success in domains such as computer vision (I-JEPA, V-JEPA), audio processing (A-JEPA), and time series analysis (S-JEPA). JEPA is characterized by its ability to automate the data augmentation process by resampling directly in the embedding space and its focus on predicting missing components at a high semantic level, rather than pixel-level reconstruction. This approach mitigates the need for manual augmentation and promotes the learning of more abstract, semantically rich representations.

This paper positions T-JEPA as the first application of the JEPA framework to trajectory similarity computation. It aims to integrate the benefits of JEPA—automated augmentation and high-level semantic understanding—to overcome the identified limitations of contrastive learning methods in trajectory analysis.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to advance trajectory similarity computation by developing a method that can learn more generalized and robust trajectory representations from unlabeled data. The work is primarily motivated by two limitations observed in current state-of-the-art self-supervised trajectory similarity methods, particularly those based on contrastive learning:

1. **Reliance on Manually Defined Data Augmentations:** Current contrastive learning approaches (e.g., TrajCL) depend heavily on manually engineered data augmentation schemes. These schemes require significant domain knowledge and manual effort to create effective transformations that introduce variation while preserving essential features of a trajectory. This dependency limits the diversity of augmented trajectories, potentially hindering the learning of truly generalized representations across a wide range of real-world scenarios that may not be covered by predefined augmentations. The manual nature also introduces a bottleneck in the development and scalability of these methods.
2. **Limited Scope of Trajectory Transformations:** The data transformations employed by existing methods are often restricted to low-level manipulations within 2D Euclidean space. While useful for geometric invariance, these transformations may fail to capture or induce variations at higher semantic levels. Consequently, the learned representations might not adequately discern intricate, abstract disparities in trajectory patterns that are semantically meaningful but not immediately apparent through basic geometric changes.

To address these limitations, the authors propose T-JEPA, driven by the following motivations:

- **Automated Augmentation:** To eliminate the need for manual and domain-specific data augmentation, T-JEPA leverages JEPA's mechanism of sampling and predicting trajectory information directly in the representation space. This automated resampling process aims to generate diverse learning targets without manual effort, thereby enhancing the variety of learned variations.
- **High-Level Semantic Understanding:** By performing resampling and prediction in the abstract representation space, T-JEPA is designed to infer missing trajectory components at a high-level semantic understanding. This is intended to enable the model to capture more complex relationships and variations beyond simple 2D geometric changes, leading to more meaningful and robust trajectory embeddings.
- **Enhanced Robustness to Real-World Data Challenges:** Trajectory data often suffers from inconsistencies, discontinuity, and irregular or low sampling rates due to GPS noise and device limitations. The motivation is to enhance the model's robustness against these common data quality issues.
- **Improved Generalization:** The ultimate goal is to produce trajectory representations that are highly generalized, allowing for accurate similarity computation across diverse routes, regions, and data types (e.g., GPS trajectories, Foursquare check-ins), and to adapt effectively to various downstream tasks or similarity metrics without extensive re-training.

In summary, the key objective is to build a self-supervised framework for trajectory similarity computation that overcomes the limitations of manual augmentation and low-level semantic understanding prevalent in previous methods, thereby learning more robust, generalized, and semantically rich trajectory representations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed T-JEPA method adopts and customizes the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) framework for trajectory similarity computation. The methodology encompasses a specific architectural design, a novel data sampling and prediction strategy, and a module for contextual feature enrichment.

#### 3.1. Model Overview and JEPA Structure Adaptation

T-JEPA retains the core JEPA concept of predicting representations of sampled targets from corresponding sampled contexts, all derived from a single input trajectory. The architecture comprises two main branches: a target encoder branch and a context encoder branch, along with a predictive module. Both encoders are implemented using Transformer encoder structures, while the predictor is a Transformer decoder. The target encoder's parameters are updated via the exponential moving average of the context encoder's parameters, a common practice in JEPA-based models.

#### 3.2. Main Components of T-JEPA

- **Targets:** Given an input trajectory *T*, it is first processed by the `AdjFuse` module (described below) and then fed into the target encoder ($E_{\bar{\theta}}$) to extract its full representations, $S_T = \{S_{T_1}, S_{T_2}..., S_{T_n}\}$. From these full representations, multiple targets, $S_T^{(i)}$, are generated by resampling $M$ times with replacement. Unlike image-based JEPA which uses varied aspect ratios for patch sampling, T-JEPA employs a customized sampling strategy. This strategy involves selecting one of three pre-defined target sampling ratios ($\{r_1, r_2, r_3\}$) randomly for each training iteration. Additionally, a successive probability *p* determines whether samples are taken successively from the trajectory representations, ensuring exposure to diverse sets of sampled targets and varying learning difficulty levels.
- **Context:** An initial context trajectory, $T'$, is randomly sampled from the full trajectory *T* using a mask $M_T$ with a sampling ratio $p_\gamma$. To ensure sufficient contextual information for inference, T-JEPA implements a one-by-one overlap removal strategy. This means that for each generated target $S_T^{(i)}$, the initially sampled context $T'$ has its overlaps removed specifically corresponding to the indices of that target. This process generates $M$ distinct context inputs, which are then passed through the `AdjFuse` module and subsequently the context encoder ($E_\theta$) to yield encoded context representations, $S_{T'}^{(i)}$.
- **Predictions:** The encoded context representations $S_{T'}^{(i)}$ are fed into a Transformer decoder, which serves as the predictor ($D_\phi$). Mask tokens *z*, augmented with positional embeddings, are incorporated into the predictor. The predictor's role is to generate predictions, $\tilde{S}_T^{(i)}$, that approximate the corresponding targets $S_T^{(i)}$. This prediction occurs at the representation level, emphasizing the learning of intrinsic data dependencies and higher-order information, rather than direct reconstruction of raw trajectory points. The training objective minimizes the Smooth $L_1$ loss between the predictions and their respective targets.

#### 3.3. Contextual Feature Enrichment: The AdjFuse Module

To enhance the quality and robustness of trajectory representations, particularly for noisy, low-sampled, or irregularly sampled GPS data, T-JEPA introduces the `AdjFuse` module.

- **Cell Representation:** The initial step involves partitioning the geographic study area into a grid of equally sized cells. Each trajectory point $(lon_i, lat_i)$ is mapped to a corresponding cell ID. To capture spatial relationships between cells, `node2vec` is employed to pre-train cell embeddings, treating the grid as a graph where cells are nodes and neighboring cells are connected by edges. This provides an initial node embedding $h_i$ for each cell.
- **AdjFuse Mechanism:** After obtaining the node embedding for a trajectory point $p_i$ (mapped to node $v_i$), the `AdjFuse` module queries its neighbors $N(i)$ to integrate their information. This process aims to enrich the contextual information of each point, compensating for potential discontinuities caused by sparse sampling or noise.
 - A learnable $3 \times 3$ kernel $W$ covers the current node $v_i$ and its neighbors. The kernel weights $w_j$ are normalized using a softmax function to obtain $W'$.
 - A convolution-like operation is performed by summing the product of normalized weights $w'_j$ and neighbor embeddings $h_j$, followed by an activation function $\sigma$, to produce an intermediate node embedding $\tilde{h}$.
 - Finally, a residual connection is applied: the original node embedding $h_i$ is added to a linear transformation ($\tilde{W}$) of $\tilde{h}$, resulting in the enriched node embedding $h'_i$. This residual connection ensures that useful initial node information is preserved.
 - The kernel slides along the trajectory points, extracting high-level local features. During training, the kernel is applied only to the sampled points in the context encoder branch, with weights shared across both branches. The output $H'$ then serves as input to the Transformer encoders.

#### 3.4. Inference and Downstream Tasks

After training, for trajectory similarity computation, the `AdjFuse` module and the context encoder $E_\theta$ are used to extract a fixed-dimensional representation F($T_i$) for any trajectory $T_i$ without any sampling process. These representations can then be directly compared using distance metrics to determine similarity. The learned backbone feature extractor F can also be integrated into other models for transfer learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of T-JEPA was evaluated through extensive experiments on five datasets: three GPS trajectory datasets (Porto, T-Drive, GeoLife) and two Foursquare check-in datasets (Foursquare-TKY, Foursquare-NYC). The performance was compared against two self-supervised baselines: t2vec and TrajCL, across tasks including most similar trajectory search, robustness to down-sampling and distortion, and generalization by approximating heuristic similarity measures.

#### 4.1. Most Similar Trajectory Search

This task evaluates the model's ability to identify a ground-truth trajectory variant (odd-indexed points) from a database, given another variant (even-indexed points) of the same original trajectory. The metric used is the mean rank of the ground truth.

- **Overall Performance:** T-JEPA generally outperformed the baselines across most datasets and varying database sizes. In Porto, TrajCL achieved a slightly lower mean rank (0.044 lower) than T-JEPA, indicating marginally superior performance. However, T-JEPA demonstrated stronger performance in T-Drive (0.114 lower mean rank than TrajCL) and GeoLife (0.158 lower mean rank than TrajCL).
- **Foursquare Datasets:** For the sparser Foursquare check-in datasets, T-JEPA showed significantly superior performance. In Foursquare-TKY, T-JEPA achieved mean ranks 6.88 times lower than t2vec and 2.92 times lower than TrajCL. In Foursquare-NYC, T-JEPA's mean ranks were 8.05 times lower than t2vec and 2.51 times lower than TrajCL. The consistent low mean ranks in NYC, despite its small database size, further indicate stable search performance. These results suggest that T-JEPA, particularly with its `AdjFuse` module, provides richer contextual information and a better understanding of sparse trajectory point correlations, which is beneficial for datasets with longer sampling intervals and larger regions.

#### 4.2. Robustness Evaluation

The models were tested for robustness against down-sampled and distorted trajectory data.

- **Down-sampling:** For down-sampling rates ($\rho_s$) ranging from 0.1 to 0.5, T-JEPA exhibited competitive performance. While TrajCL performed better in Porto at lower down-sampling rates, its performance significantly declined at higher rates (0.4 to 0.5), where T-JEPA achieved lower mean ranks. In T-Drive, T-JEPA outperformed TrajCL for $\rho_s$ from 0.1 to 0.3, but TrajCL showed better results at higher rates (0.4 and 0.5), possibly due to its explicit data augmentation strategy involving down-sampling. T-JEPA achieved the best performance on GeoLife, Tokyo, and NYC datasets across various down-sampling rates.
- **Distortion:** For various distortion rates ($\rho_d$), T-JEPA demonstrated robust performance. Excluding Porto, where TrajCL's overall mean rank was marginally lower (0.073) than T-JEPA's, T-JEPA outperformed both TrajCL and t2vec across all other four datasets. T-JEPA showed 1.62 times better performance in T-Drive, 10.92 times better in GeoLife, 3.04 times better in TKY, and 2.53 times better in NYC compared to TrajCL. The consistency of T-JEPA's performance suggests its unique predictive module, which emphasizes high-level semantic understanding in representation space, provides robustness against noisy points.

#### 4.3. Approximating Heuristic Measures

This experiment assessed the generalization ability of learned representations by training a 2-layer MLP decoder on top of a frozen pre-trained encoder to approximate four heuristic measures (EDR, LCSS, Hausdorff, Discrete Fréchet). Metrics included Hit Ratios (HR@5, HR@20) and Recall (R5@20).

- **Generalization Ability:** T-JEPA exhibited stronger generalization capabilities compared to TrajCL and t2vec.
 - In Porto and GeoLife, T-JEPA consistently achieved the highest HR@5, HR@20, and R5@20 across nearly all heuristic measures, with the exception of HR@20 for Fréchet in Porto. T-JEPA's average performance was 4.1% higher than TrajCL in Porto.
 - In T-Drive, although TrajCL showed better performance in Hausdorff and Fréchet, T-JEPA performed better on EDR and LCSS, resulting in an overall average performance 1.1% higher than TrajCL.
- These results suggest that T-JEPA's learned representations are more adaptable to various similarity measures, indicating a deeper and more generalized understanding of trajectory semantics, especially considering that T-JEPA does not rely on manual data augmentation schemes.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Evaluation

Visualizations of 5-NN queries (e.g., using Hausdorff measure in Porto and GeoLife) showed that T-JEPA successfully found more visually consistent similar trajectories, particularly for lower-ranked matches (ranks 3-5), compared to TrajCL. This qualitative evidence further supports the accuracy of T-JEPA's learned representations.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

- **Influence of `AdjFuse` Module:** Removing the `AdjFuse` module resulted in a slight improvement in mean rank for the 100k database size, but a significant degradation in performance under down-sampling (74% worse than T-JEPA with `AdjFuse`) and a minor drop under distortion. This confirms the `AdjFuse` module's critical role in enhancing robustness, particularly for low and irregularly sampled trajectories.
- **Influence of Target Sampling Ratios:** Experiments with lower ({0.05, 0.15, 0.25}) and higher ({0.30, 0.40, 0.50}) target sampling ratios indicated that the chosen default ratios ({0.10, 0.20, 0.30}) provided the most robust results. While higher ratios could sometimes improve performance in the 100k database search and distortion cases (by introducing more challenging samples), both lower and higher ratios led to performance drops in down-sampling scenarios (10.36% and 12.19% respectively). This highlights the importance of selecting appropriate sampling difficulty for effective learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

T-JEPA represents a notable advancement in the field of trajectory similarity computation, primarily through its novel application of the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) to this domain. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Pioneering Application of JEPA:** T-JEPA is presented as the first method to leverage the JEPA framework for trajectory modeling. This introduces a new paradigm for self-supervised learning in spatial-temporal data, potentially opening avenues for further JEPA-based research in related domains.
- **Enhanced Trajectory Representation Learning:** The method introduces automated data augmentation through resampling in abstract representation space. This eliminates the need for manual, domain-specific data augmentation schemes, which can be laborious and limit the diversity of learned features. By focusing on predicting missing components at a high semantic level, T-JEPA moves beyond low-level geometric transformations, leading to more generalized and semantically rich trajectory embeddings.
- **Improved Robustness to Real-World Data Challenges:** The `AdjFuse` module, designed to aggregate adjacent regional features, significantly enhances the model's robustness against common issues in GPS data, such as noise, low sampling rates, and irregular sampling intervals. This makes T-JEPA particularly well-suited for practical applications where data quality can be inconsistent.
- **Superior Generalization Abilities:** The experimental results demonstrate T-JEPA's strong generalization capabilities. Its ability to achieve high performance in both direct similarity searches and in approximating various heuristic measures, even with a frozen encoder, suggests that the learned representations capture intrinsic trajectory properties that are transferable across different evaluation criteria and downstream tasks.
- **Broadening Applicability:** By outperforming existing methods on diverse datasets, including challenging Foursquare check-in data with sparser mobility patterns, T-JEPA proves its adaptability to a wider range of mobility data types. This extends its potential utility beyond traditional GPS trajectories to other forms of sequential location data.
- **Potential for Downstream Applications:** More accurate, robust, and generalized trajectory similarity computation has a direct impact on numerous applications, including:
 - **Traffic Management:** Improved anomaly detection, route planning, and congestion analysis.
 - **Urban Computing:** Better understanding of urban mobility patterns, urban planning, and location-based services.
 - **Environmental and Wildlife Tracking:** Enhanced analysis of animal migration routes and behavioral patterns.
 - **Personalized Services:** More accurate recommendations in navigation and activity suggestions.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identification of challenging cases, such as closed-loop trajectories, also points towards future research directions. Integrating richer behavioral information (e.g., Points of Interest, road networks) and developing advanced geospatial knowledge fusion techniques could further enhance trajectory modeling accuracy.

In conclusion, T-JEPA provides a robust and generalized solution for trajectory similarity computation, addressing key limitations of prior self-supervised methods. Its innovative use of JEPA and the `AdjFuse` module contributes to the field by enabling more effective and automated learning of high-level trajectory semantics, with broad implications for various data-driven applications.
