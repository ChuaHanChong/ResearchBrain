# Toward Accurate Long-Horizon Robotic Manipulation: Language-to-Action with Foundation Models via Scene Graphs

> [!summary] Summary
> A layered robot-manipulation framework wires off-the-shelf foundation models (GPT-4.1 interaction, Gemini 2.5 Pro cognitive planning, Qwen2.5-VL perception) around a dynamically-updated scene graph, needing no domain-specific training. Across tabletop pick-and-place, puzzle, and organization tasks it reaches near-ceiling planning feasibility and perfect scene-graph correctness, degrading only under ambiguous instructions or occlusion.

## Problem

- LLM-only planners reason symbolically but lack grounding in spatial understanding, remaining weak at integrating reasoning with the physical world.
- VLM keypoint/overlay approaches generate trajectories directly but are error-prone and fail to generalize to long-horizon, multi-step manipulation planning.
- VLA models achieve closed-loop control but require massive task-specific training data and struggle to generalize beyond their training distribution.

## Method

- ==Layered architecture== separates cognition (Gemini 2.5 Pro long-horizon task-sequence reasoning), interaction (GPT-4.1 fast non-reasoning execution and function calling), perception (Qwen2.5-VL), and execution (cuRobo motion planning) into distinct ROS2 modules.
- Persistent ==NetworkX scene graph== encodes object affordances, positions, and containment relations, dynamically updated by the LLM after perception and manipulation actions to maintain spatial consistency.
- ==Dual VLM perception pathway== combines binary-confirmation bounding-box localization with multi-view point-cloud clustering, and direct pixel-to-3D point queries for placement, avoiding hallucinated detections.

## Results

- Across **10** experiment types (5-20 trials each) planning feasibility stayed at **95-100%** except occlusion task Exp III-C at **80%**, while scene-graph handling remained **100%** in every experiment.
- Task completion rate hit **100%** on fundamental and structured tasks (relocation, block stacking, Tower of Hanoi, sorting) but dropped to **20%** under underspecified language in Exp I-B1, recovering with added context.
- Cluttered occlusion-handling experiments were hardest: Exp III-B reached **75%** task completion and Exp III-C dropped to **60%**, isolating linguistic ambiguity and visual clutter as the two failure sources.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling slow multi-step reasoning from fast deterministic execution lets a layered pipeline match VLA-like task completion without any fine-tuning or task-specific datasets.
> - Scene graphs act as a persistent shared context that lets independently pretrained foundation models reason coherently about long-horizon, semantically constrained tasks.
> - System failures concentrate at the language-grounding and perception boundary, not deep in planning, so brevity of user instructions and scene clutter are the dominant reliability risks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is rapidly evolving, with a growing emphasis on enabling robots to understand and execute complex, high-level natural language commands in unstructured environments. This paper directly addresses several critical challenges within this landscape, particularly concerning long-horizon tasks and the effective integration of powerful pre-trained AI models.

Historically, robotic control often relied on meticulously programmed, task-specific instructions or extensive domain-specific training data. The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), collectively known as Foundation Models (FMs), has opened new avenues for natural language interfaces and enhanced perception in robotics. However, their direct application in complex manipulation tasks has revealed distinct limitations:
- **LLMs for Planning:** While LLMs excel at symbolic reasoning and generating sequences of actions from high-level instructions (e.g., RoboGPT, Instruct2Act), they often lack grounding in the physical world. They struggle with spatial understanding, require explicit object details, or rely heavily on prompt engineering and structured output parsing, limiting their adaptability to novel or dynamic scenes. They may also lack mechanisms for failure handling or introducing new logic beyond provided examples.
- **VLMs for Perception and Spatial Reasoning:** VLMs offer robust object recognition and visual grounding. However, many approaches rely on explicit visual cues like overlays or keypoints for trajectory generation, which may not generalize well to long-horizon planning or complex scenes. Furthermore, perception in these systems often remains somewhat decoupled from high-level reasoning, creating brittle pipelines vulnerable to error propagation.
- **Scene Graphs and Affordances:** Scene graphs provide a structured representation of the environment, encoding objects, their properties, and relationships. This is crucial for semantic contextualization and planning. Works like SayPlan and Hydra leverage scene graphs for navigation and mapping. Affordance-based reasoning, as seen in SayCan and VoxPoser, grounds LLMs in real-world feasibility. However, these systems often require large datasets for training affordance functions or lack comprehensive long-horizon planning capabilities when not integrated with the reasoning depth of advanced FMs.
- **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models:** Recent advances in VLA models, such as PaLM-E, RT-1, RT-2, GR00T, and Open-X, represent significant strides in directly mapping visual and language inputs to robot actions. These models scale training with massive datasets, enabling closed-loop control and language-conditioned reasoning. Nevertheless, their primary drawback is their "data-hungry" nature, requiring vast amounts of task-specific training data, which makes them expensive to develop, difficult to generalize to novel tasks or environments, and often limited to short-horizon tasks.

This paper positions itself strategically as a "middle ground" in this crowded landscape. Instead of pursuing data-intensive end-to-end VLA models, or relying solely on the symbolic reasoning of LLMs, it proposes a structured, layered framework that *integrates off-the-shelf foundation models* (LLMs for reasoning, VLMs for perception) with a *dynamically maintained scene graph*. This approach minimizes the need for domain-specific training or fine-tuning, thereby reducing the engineering burden, while aiming for high accuracy and robust performance in long-horizon tasks. By combining the strengths of different FMs and grounding them in a structured world representation, the authors aim to overcome the limitations of individual components and bridge the gap between language and action in a more scalable and adaptable manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research is to develop a highly adaptable and accurate framework for robotic manipulation that can interpret natural language commands and execute long-horizon tasks. The authors explicitly state their primary objective as: **"to develop a framework that integrates multiple foundation models—pre-trained large models—to enable perception, planning, and execution without requiring dedicated end-to-end training or fine-tuning, while maintaining high accuracy."**

From this overarching goal, several key specific objectives and motivations can be elucidated:
- **Zero-shot/Few-shot Generalization:** A primary driver is to achieve robotic manipulation capabilities without the need for extensive, task-specific datasets or fine-tuning. This directly addresses the data-hunger problem prevalent in many modern VLA models, aiming for a system that can readily adapt to novel objects and tasks.
- **High-level Command Interpretation:** The framework seeks to enable users to provide high-level verbal commands, expecting the robot to autonomously perceive the environment, generate a plan, and execute tasks based on this abstract input. This moves toward more intuitive and natural human-robot interaction.
- **Robust Task Sequencing:** The system aims to generate detailed, multi-step task sequences reliably. This is crucial for long-horizon tasks that involve multiple sub-goals and conditional logic.
- **Spatial Awareness and Grounding:** A significant motivation is to embed physical world understanding into the robot's reasoning. This is achieved through the dynamic maintenance of scene graphs, which provide explicit spatial relationships, object properties, and semantic details, allowing models to reason about the environment with greater accuracy.
- **Leveraging Off-the-shelf Foundation Models:** The authors are motivated by the immense capabilities of pre-trained foundation models (LLMs like GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro; VLMs like Qwen2.5-VL). The objective is to efficiently integrate these powerful, general-purpose models, each specialized for a particular facet of the manipulation workflow (interpretation, perception, planning, execution), rather than developing new models from scratch.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Approaches:** The paper explicitly positions its work to address the shortcomings of current methods, such as the lack of spatial grounding in LLM-only planners, the brittle nature of some VLM perception pipelines, and the data-intensive requirements of end-to-end VLA models.
- **Demonstrating Versatility and Effectiveness:** The framework is designed to be evaluated across a diverse range of tasks, from simple object relocation to complex puzzle-solving (like the Tower of Hanoi) and open-ended organizational tasks, to demonstrate its versatility and real-world applicability.

In essence, the research is driven by the vision of creating more intelligent, adaptable, and easily deployable robots that can perform complex manipulation tasks by effectively combining cutting-edge AI models with structured environmental representations, all while minimizing the demanding data requirements of previous approaches.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for accurate long-horizon robotic manipulation is built upon a modular, layered architecture that integrates multiple off-the-shelf foundation models with a dynamic scene graph, all orchestrated within the Robot Operating System 2 (ROS2) framework.

**A. Hardware Setup:**
The experimental setup utilizes a **UR10e collaborative robotic arm** (6 DOF) equipped with an **OnRobot RG6 gripper** for object manipulation. Perception is provided by a **Zivid 2 wrist-mounted 3D camera**, capable of capturing high-resolution RGB-D data for precise scene understanding.

**B. Scene Graph:**
Serving as the central knowledge base, the scene graph encodes spatial relationships, object properties (e.g., affordances like 'pickable'), semantic details, and coordinate information. Implemented using the **NetworkX library** with a hierarchical JSON representation, it facilitates consistent reasoning. Nodes represent entities (workspace, table, objects), while edges define containment relations. Critically, the scene graph is **dynamically updated by the LLM** throughout task execution. Perception outputs refine object positions, and manipulation actions (e.g., placing an object in a box) update containment and coordinates, ensuring the world model remains consistent. Initial scene graphs can be generated automatically by LLMs or provided manually for complex scenarios.

**C. Layered Architecture Design:**
The framework consists of four distinct layers, each managed by specialized models:

1. **Cognitive Layer (Reasoning and Planning):**
 - **Model:** Google's **Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview**.
 - **Functionality:** This layer is responsible for generating multi-step, constraint-aware task strategies for long-horizon planning. It synthesizes information from the scene graph (current state, object properties, relations), user goals, and definitions of available tools.
 - **Rationale for Choice:** Gemini 2.5 Pro was selected for its strong performance in reasoning, language comprehension, instruction following, and data analysis (essential for interpreting scene graphs), as evaluated on benchmarks like LiveBench.
 - **Design Note:** Being a powerful reasoning model, Gemini 2.5 Pro is relatively slow. Therefore, it is decoupled from real-time execution to maintain system responsiveness.

2. **Interaction Layer (Coordination and Execution Orchestration):**
 - **Model:** OpenAI's **GPT-4.1**.
 - **Functionality:** This layer acts as the system's primary interface, interpreting high-level natural language instructions from the user. It receives the detailed task plan from the Cognitive Layer and orchestrates its execution by coordinating tool calls (function calls representing motion primitives, perception queries, scene graph updates). GPT-4.1 monitors the return outputs of each function call, enabling dynamic re-planning or adjustments if necessary. It also communicates status updates, requests clarifications from the user, and manages feedback loops.
 - **Rationale for Choice:** GPT-4.1 provides robust natural language interpretation and strong function-calling capabilities, making it ideal for bridging the gap between user intent and robotic subsystems. Its faster response time compared to Gemini 2.5 Pro makes it suitable for execution control.
 - **Tools:** GPT-4.1 is equipped with several callable functions: `pick object`, `place object`, `ask vqa vlm`, `scan and update coordinates in scene graph`, `get a specific coordinate point using vlm`, `add object to scenegraph`, `edit scenegraph`, and `plan using advanced llm` (to communicate with Gemini).

3. **Perception Layer (Visual and Spatial Understanding):**
 - **Model:** **Qwen2.5-VL** (VLM) for visual processing, queried by GPT-4.1.
 - **Functionality:** Connects visual input from the 3D camera with semantic and geometric understanding. It uses an LLM-VLM dialogue system where GPT-4.1 issues structured queries to Qwen2.5-VL.
 - **Perception Pathways:**
 - **Bounding Box Localization:** GPT-4.1 first queries Qwen2.5-VL for binary classification ("Is object present?") to avoid hallucinations. For confirmed objects, Qwen2.5-VL provides 2D pixel-space bounding boxes. These are converted to 3D coordinates using depth data from the 3D camera. Multi-view reconstruction, table plane removal, outlier filtering, and clustering are used to segment objects. Centroids of matched clusters provide precise 3D locations.
 - **Specific Point Queries:** For tasks like finding a free placement spot, Qwen2.5-VL directly outputs pixel coordinates (x, y), which are projected into 3D space using the depth map, bypassing more intensive multi-view reconstruction.
 - **Rationale for Choice:** Qwen2.5-VL is chosen for its strong spatial reasoning, object-grounding capabilities, and adaptability to novel objects and arrangements without requiring retraining on domain-specific datasets.

4. **Execution Layer (Robot Motion Control):**
 - **Models:** Nvidia **cuRobo** (GPU-accelerated motion planner) and low-level motor controllers.
 - **Functionality:** Translates planned tasks from the Interaction Layer into safe and precise robot motions. cuRobo generates collision-free trajectories considering robot kinematics, grasp constraints, and workspace boundaries. Coordinated control of the arm and gripper ensures stable pick-and-place operations.
 - **Integration:** Seamless communication with higher layers via ROS2.

**D. Failure Handling:**
GPT-4.1 continuously tracks function calls and feedback. In case of failure, it alerts the user, attempts to re-plan, and retries. For repeated failures, it may suggest skipping the object or requesting user intervention (e.g., repositioning an obscured object).

This intricate methodology effectively decomposes complex robotic manipulation into manageable, specialized sub-problems, each addressed by an appropriate, powerful foundation model, all unified by a dynamically evolving representation of the physical world.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation aimed to assess the framework's generalization capabilities across diverse tasks without task-specific training, using four key metrics: Planning Feasibility (PF), Task Completion Rate (TCR), and Scene Graph Handling (SGH). The results, summarized in Table I, demonstrate both the strengths and limitations of the proposed approach.

**A. Overall Performance Metrics:**
- **Planning Feasibility (PF):** The framework consistently achieved high planning feasibility. PF was ≥ 95% in almost all experiments, with the exception of Experiment III-C (occlusion handling), which registered 80%. This indicates that the Cognitive Layer (Gemini 2.5 Pro) and Interaction Layer (GPT-4.1) were largely successful in generating logical and goal-aligned task sequences from user requests and scene graph information.
- **Scene Graph Handling (SGH):** The framework demonstrated perfect scene graph handling (SGH = 100%) across all experiments. This is a critical finding, confirming the robustness and accuracy of the LLM's ability to dynamically update the world model based on perception outputs and manipulation actions, maintaining consistent spatial and semantic reasoning.
- **Task Completion Rate (TCR):** TCR varied more significantly, reflecting the complexity of tasks and the clarity of user instructions.

**B. Performance Across Experiment Classes:**

- **Experiment I: Testing Fundamental Capabilities (Object Recognition, Positioning, Contextual Reasoning):**
 - **I-A (Relative Positioning):** 100% TCR. Successfully moved objects to specified relative positions (e.g., "between the apple and yarn"), demonstrating stable LLM-VLM dialogue and accurate VLM spatial localization.
 - **I-B1 (Semantic Clustering, Minimal Context):** 20% TCR. The underspecified instruction ("Move the lone isolated fruit near the other fruits") led to low performance, as the LLM could not provide sufficient context for the VLM's spatial reasoning. PF remained 100%, but execution failed.
 - **I-B2 (Semantic Clustering, Richer Context):** 100% TCR. By providing additional context ("one fruit is isolated. Move it close to where it belongs"), the framework's understanding and execution improved dramatically, highlighting its sensitivity to linguistic ambiguity and the importance of contextual cues.
 - **I-C (Outlier Detection):** 100% TCR. Successfully identified and moved a "mismatched item" (non-edible) to a container.
 - **I-D (Recipe-based Selection):** 80% TCR. Identified ingredients for "fried noodles" but showed a moderate decrease in completion, suggesting some difficulty in parsing specific object attributes or complex selection criteria in a cluttered scene.

- **Experiment II: Structured Benchmark-Inspired Scenarios (Iterative Spatial Reasoning, Constraint-aware Planning):**
 - **II-A (Block Stacking):** 100% TCR. Successfully stacked blocks based on instructions ranging from explicit ("Stack the blocks with the one in center as base") to abstract ("Build something tall"). This validated iterative spatial reasoning and scene graph updating.
 - **II-B (Tower of Hanoi):** 100% TCR. Successfully solved the long-horizon puzzle across multiple configurations, demonstrating robust constraint-aware action sequencing and rule adherence, with simplified perception using AprilTags to isolate the cognitive and execution layers.

- **Experiment III: Advanced Reasoning with Scene Graphs (Open-ended Scenarios, Occlusion Handling):**
 - **III-A (Autonomous Sorting):** 100% TCR. Correctly categorized items into "fruits" and "vegetables" and assigned them to appropriately sized boxes based on quantity, solely from the high-level command "Put the objects into boxes in an organized manner." This demonstrated strong categorization accuracy and inference from context.
 - **III-B (Autonomous Table Organization):** 75% TCR. Required inferring a multi-step plan from "Organize the table," including relocating a misplaced item, sorting others, and closing boxes. Failures occasionally arose from collisions with manipulatable objects, which were not dynamically modeled.
 - **III-C (Autonomous Table Organization with Occlusion):** 60% TCR. The most challenging scenario, requiring the robot to interpret an initially closed box (occlusion), identify a temporary placement for the lid using the VLM, and then proceed with organization. The lower TCR here points to increased difficulty in cluttered/occluded visual parsing for the VLM and unmodeled dynamic collisions.

**C. Strengths Identified:**
- **Contextual Reasoning via Scene Graphs:** Scene graphs proved highly effective in enabling multi-step planning and richer interpretation of high-level, often underspecified, user requests.
- **VLM Generalization:** Qwen2.5-VL demonstrated strong generalization capabilities, localizing novel objects and fine-grained affordances (e.g., lid knobs, temporary placement points) without requiring retraining.
- **Decoupled Architecture:** Separating the slow, reasoning-intensive planning (Gemini 2.5 Pro) from faster execution orchestration (GPT-4.1) resulted in coherent plans and reliable low-level control while maintaining responsiveness.

**D. Limitations Identified:**
- **Sensitivity to Ambiguous Language:** Sparse or ambiguous user requests occasionally caused the LLM to issue VLM queries without sufficient context, leading to perception errors (e.g., Exp. I-B1). This highlights error propagation between layers.
- **Lack of Dynamic Obstacle Modeling:** The motion planner did not model manipulatable objects as dynamic obstacles, leading to occasional execution failures due to collisions in cluttered scenes (e.g., Exps. III-B, III-C).
- **VLM Limitations in Cluttered Scenes:** The VLM struggled with visual parsing in highly cluttered or occluded environments, contributing to lower TCR in such scenarios (e.g., Exp. III-C).

In summary, the framework consistently maintained robust world-model representations and achieved high task completion rates in most experiments, with failures concentrated in cases of ambiguous linguistic input or increased physical scene complexity not fully modeled at the execution level.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, offering a compelling path toward more adaptable, intelligent, and general-purpose robotic systems. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

- **Bridging the Language-to-Action Gap with Foundation Models:** The primary significance lies in demonstrating a robust framework that effectively bridges the semantic understanding of natural language with precise robotic action. By intelligently integrating diverse, off-the-shelf foundation models—LLMs for reasoning and coordination, VLMs for perception—the system successfully translates high-level human commands into multi-step robot plans and executions.
- **Reducing Data and Training Burden:** A critical impact is the significant reduction in the need for task-specific datasets and fine-tuning. Unlike data-hungry VLA models that require massive amounts of training data, this framework leverages the pre-trained knowledge of foundation models and structured scene representations. This drastically lowers the development cost and time for deploying robots in new environments or for novel tasks, accelerating the pace of robotic adoption and deployment.
- **Enabling Long-Horizon and Semantically Constrained Tasks:** The framework's demonstrated ability to handle long-horizon tasks, such as the Tower of Hanoi, and tasks requiring abstract reasoning like "organize the table," marks a substantial advancement. The use of scene graphs provides the necessary semantic grounding and persistent world model for complex, multi-step planning that traditional symbolic AI often struggles with and end-to-end models find hard to generalize to.
- **Enhanced Adaptability and Generalization:** The modular architecture, coupled with general-purpose foundation models, offers inherent adaptability. The VLM's ability to localize novel objects and affordances without retraining, and the LLM's capacity to interpret varied instructions, highlight the system's potential to generalize to new objects, configurations, and tasks encountered in real-world, unstructured environments.
- **Practical Middle Ground for Robotics Development:** The research establishes a valuable "middle ground" in robotic AI. It is more generalizable and less data-intensive than fully end-to-end VLA models, yet more sophisticated and spatially grounded than purely symbolic LLM planners. This balanced approach provides a practical blueprint for developing robust and versatile robotic systems, offering a viable alternative to existing paradigms.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as sensitivity to linguistic ambiguity and the lack of dynamic obstacle modeling, pave clear pathways for future research. Extending the framework to dexterous manipulation, deformable objects, complex geometries, and improved collision avoidance will be crucial next steps, building upon the strong foundation laid here.
- **Broader Societal Impact:** As robots become more capable of understanding natural language and performing complex tasks with minimal upfront programming, their integration into human environments (e.g., manufacturing, logistics, elder care, home assistance) will become more seamless. This work contributes to a future where robots are more intuitive to interact with and can perform a wider array of useful functions, increasing productivity and improving quality of life.

In conclusion, this paper by Dinesh and Park represents a significant leap forward in robotic manipulation, offering a powerful, adaptable, and resource-efficient framework that leverages the collective intelligence of foundation models. Its methodical approach to integrating language, vision, and action through a dynamically maintained world model positions it as a key reference for the next generation of general-purpose intelligent robots.
