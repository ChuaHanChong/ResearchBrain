# Meta Reinforcement Learning for Sim-to-real Domain Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> A sim-to-real method combining gradient-based meta reinforcement learning (MAML-style, optimized with PPO) with a VAE trajectory generator that supplies a 2D latent action space. The meta-policy is trained across randomized MuJoCo dynamics to adapt in a few policy-gradient steps, then deployed on a KUKA LBR 4+ hitting a hockey puck to targets, giving more stable and consistent adaptation than domain randomization.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning on real robots is sample-hungry and its random exploratory actions risk damaging hardware and the operating environment, making full real-world training infeasible.
- Simulators mismatch reality even after tedious parameter tuning, and zero-shot domain randomization can fail because no single policy performs well across the whole randomized dynamics range.
- Policies pretrained in simulation are used as fine-tuning starting points, but those initial parameters are not optimized for adaptation, so real-world updates are unstable and inconsistent.

## Method

- A ==variational autoencoder== trained on 7371 robot swing trajectories (17 cubic-spline waypoints) decodes a 2D latent vector into safe motor trajectories, giving the policy a smooth low-dimensional action space.
- A **MAML**-style meta-policy over latent actions is trained by backpropagating through N=3 inner adaptation steps of vanilla policy gradient with K=16 rollouts, with the inner step size learned.
- Meta-optimization uses **PPO** across batches of tasks sampled from randomized MuJoCo dynamics: anisotropic linear, torsional and rotational friction, puck mass 50-500g, and noisy initial puck position.

## Results

- In four simulated friction conditions repeated **25 times**, domain randomization wins before adaptation but the meta-policy is consistently better after a few steps, with the baseline even degrading from the first update in the medium-friction case.
- On the real KUKA setup, **3** runs per puck with **16** evaluation targets and K=16 update rollouts (**48** points per step) show meta learning improving or plateauing while the baseline fails to improve on the high-friction red puck.
- Latent-action distributions over repeated runs stay near the origin for the meta-policy through **10** adaptations despite training for only 3, whereas baseline distributions diverge, once forcing a real-world run to be aborted.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly optimizing a policy for adaptability, rather than for average robustness, buys stable low-variance fine-tuning; robustness-first training can be actively harmful to downstream gradient updates.
> - Constraining exploration to a learned generative latent space keeps updates inside the region where the decoder produces valid behaviour, but leaving that region silently produces unusable trajectories.
> - Randomizing an unphysical parameter such as anisotropic friction can absorb unmodeled effects elsewhere in the simulator, substituting for expensive contact-model tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Modern reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated advancements, particularly in areas such as game playing, but its application in robotics faces challenges primarily due to low sample efficiency and potential safety concerns during exploratory training. Training robotic policies directly on physical hardware is often resource-intensive and carries risks of damage. Physics simulators offer a partial solution by reducing training time and mitigating hardware risk. However, a significant discrepancy, known as the "sim-to-real gap," often exists between simulated and real-world dynamics. Directly deploying policies trained in simulation to real hardware frequently results in performance degradation due to these modeling inaccuracies.

Existing approaches to address the sim-to-real gap include:
- **Zero-shot transfer with Domain Randomization:** This method trains a policy to be robust across a variety of randomized conditions in simulation, aiming for direct transfer without real-world adaptation. While effective for some tasks and uncertainties in perception or dynamics, it may be insufficient if a single robust policy for all conditions does not exist.
- **Simulation Parameter Tuning/Interweaving Real Data:** This involves meticulously tuning simulator parameters to match reality or iteratively optimizing the simulation model or policy by incorporating real robot samples. These approaches can be costly and time-consuming, requiring access to physical hardware during the training phase.
- **Memory-based Adaptation:** Embedding memory within the network allows it to identify and respond to various dynamic conditions by encoding past states and actions.
- **Policy Transfer for Adaptation:** Using a policy trained in simulation as an initial starting point for further fine-tuning in the real world. However, this initial policy may not be optimally structured for efficient adaptation.

Meta-learning, or "learning to learn," has emerged as a compelling, yet less explored, alternative within this landscape. Unlike traditional machine learning that aims to optimize for a single task, meta-learning focuses on training models that are proficient at adapting to new tasks or environments efficiently. Within meta-learning, gradient-based methods like Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) explicitly optimize model parameters to serve as a strong initialization for subsequent fast adaptation. Prior work has explored meta-learning for stabilizing model-based RL against minor dynamic uncertainties.

This work contributes to the meta-learning for sim-to-real domain transfer paradigm by directly adapting a model-free policy to a wide range of dynamic conditions. It differentiates itself by demonstrating the consistent and stable nature of gradient-based meta-learning for policy adaptation, contrasting with the potential instability of standard domain randomization approaches during adaptation. Furthermore, it integrates generative models for trajectory generation to provide a low-dimensional, safe, and task-specific action space, addressing challenges related to exploration in complex robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research stem from the limitations encountered when deploying reinforcement learning policies from simulation to real-world robotic systems.

**Motivation:**

1. **Addressing RL's Sample Inefficiency and Unsafe Exploration in Robotics:** Modern reinforcement learning algorithms typically require extensive data, which is expensive and time-consuming to collect on physical robots. Moreover, the trial-and-error nature of RL often involves random, potentially unpredictable, and unsafe exploratory actions that could harm the robot or its environment.
2. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** While physics simulators mitigate some training challenges, policies trained in simulation often perform poorly in reality due to unavoidable discrepancies between simulated and physical dynamics. Fine-tuning simulators to perfectly match reality is often infeasible or impractical, especially for tasks heavily dependent on intricate dynamic parameters.
3. **Limitations of Existing Sim-to-Real Adaptation Methods:**
 - **Domain Randomization:** While making policies robust, a single policy might not achieve optimal performance across the entire range of potential real-world dynamics.
 - **Standard Policy Adaptation:** Directly adapting a policy trained in simulation (even with domain randomization) using conventional RL updates can lead to unstable or inconsistent performance changes in the real world, as the initial policy may not be optimized for efficient subsequent adaptation. This instability was observed to manifest as divergent policy updates and performance deterioration in some cases.
4. **Need for Consistent and Stable Adaptation:** For robust robotic deployment, an adaptation mechanism is required that consistently improves performance in the target domain without erratic policy changes or degradation. The authors hypothesize that explicitly training a policy for adaptation, rather than just training a robust policy, can provide this consistency.

**Key Objectives:**

1. **Develop a Novel Meta-Learning Method for Sim-to-Real Domain Adaptation:** Propose a method that leverages meta-learning principles to enable robotic policies to quickly and effectively adapt to varying dynamic conditions encountered in the real world after initial training in simulation.
2. **Train an Adaptable Policy for Diverse Dynamic Conditions:** Explicitly train a policy to be proficient at adapting to a wide spectrum of randomized dynamics encountered in simulation, with the intention of transferring this adaptability to physical systems.
3. **Integrate Trajectory Generation for Enhanced Exploration:** Combine gradient-based meta-learning with a task-specific trajectory generation model. This is intended to create a low-dimensional, smooth, and inherently safe action space, thereby facilitating more efficient and secure exploration during the adaptation process.
4. **Demonstrate Predictable and Consistent Domain Adaptation:** Provide empirical evidence that gradient-based meta-learning results in more predictable and consistent domain adaptation compared to conventional approaches, thus positioning it as a suitable methodology for sim-to-real transfer under uncertain dynamics.
5. **Evaluate Performance on a Physical Robotic System:** Validate the proposed method's feasibility and effectiveness on a real robotic platform, specifically a KUKA LBR 4+ robot performing a hockey puck hitting task under unknown friction conditions. This evaluation aims to show performance improvements after a small number of trials in the physical environment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method integrates meta-reinforcement learning with a generative model for trajectories to achieve robust sim-to-real domain adaptation. The approach is structured into three main components: trajectory generation, domain adaptation, and meta-policy training.

**A. Preliminaries and Problem Statement**
The work frames the problem as meta-reinforcement learning over a set of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), denoted M. These MDPs share the same reward function, state space (S), and action space (A), but differ in their state transition probabilities, which are characterized by a set of dynamic parameters, referred to as a task τ ∈ τ. The objective is to find a learning algorithm that, after N adaptation steps under a new dynamic condition τ, yields an optimal policy π*θ,τ for that specific condition.

**B. Trajectory Generation**
To address the high dimensionality and safety concerns of robotic action spaces, a generative model is employed to create a low-dimensional, smooth, and task-specific action space.
1. **Variational Autoencoder (VAE):** A VAE is trained on a dataset of robot trajectories (u0:T) that are suitable and safe for the given task.
 - **Encoder:** Maps high-dimensional trajectories to a low-dimensional latent space representation (z).
 - **Decoder (gφ):** Reconstructs the original trajectory from a latent vector. Once trained, the decoder functions as a generative model, mapping latent vectors (z) to robot trajectories (u = gφ(z)).
2. **Action Space Reduction:** The policy then learns to output latent actions π(z|s) instead of raw motor commands. This effectively reduces the action space dimensionality, focusing the policy on terminal rewards and contributing to faster, safer on-policy domain adaptation. For the hockey puck task, the latent space was 2-dimensional, which empirically disentangled hitting angle and hitting strength.

**C. Domain Adaptation (Algorithm 1)**
This step describes how a policy's parameters are adjusted for a specific dynamic condition.
1. **Rollout Collection:** Starting with the current policy πθn, the agent repeatedly (K times) performs the following:
 - Samples a random goal state (sg).
 - Samples a latent action (zk) from the policy's distribution πθn(z|sg).
 - Generates a full trajectory (u0:T) using the pre-trained generative model gφ(zk).
 - Executes the trajectory on the robot/simulator and records the initial state (sg), sampled latent action (zk), and the resulting reward (rk).
2. **Policy Update:** After K rollouts, the collected rewards are normalized. The policy parameters θn are then updated using a vanilla policy gradient method, aiming to maximize the expected cumulative reward for the current dynamic conditions. This process is repeated N times.

**D. Training the Meta-Policy (Algorithm 2)**
The core of the meta-learning approach is to find optimal initial meta-parameters (θm) for the policy that facilitate rapid adaptation.
1. **Initialization:** Meta-parameters θm are randomly initialized.
2. **Iterative Training:** The following steps are repeated until convergence:
 - **Task Sampling:** A batch of distinct tasks (τb) is sampled from a distribution p(τ). Each task represents an environment with different, randomized dynamic parameters (e.g., puck mass, friction coefficients).
 - **Inner-Loop Adaptation:** For each sampled task τb, the agent starts with the current meta-policy parameters θm and performs N adaptation steps as described in Section III-C (Algorithm 1). This results in an adapted policy πθN,b for each specific task.
 - **Outer-Loop Meta-Update:** After obtaining the adapted policies for all tasks in the batch, K additional rollouts are collected using each final adapted policy πθN,b. Critically, the gradients from these final rollouts are backpropagated not just to the adapted policy's parameters, but through all N inner-loop update steps, all the way back to the original meta-policy parameters θm. This meta-update is performed using a model-free RL algorithm, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). This explicit optimization ensures that θm is a starting point that maximizes performance *after* N adaptation steps across the distribution of tasks.

**E. Experimental Setup**
1. **Robotic Platform:** A KUKA LBR 4+ robot equipped with a floorball stick.
2. **Task:** Hitting a hockey puck (two types: low-friction ice hockey puck, higher-friction inline hockey puck) on a flat surface to a target location.
3. **Puck State Measurement:** A ceiling-mounted camera tracks puck position.
4. **Simulation Environment:** MuJoCo, where the puck's mass, five friction parameters (linear x, y; torsional; rotational x, y), and initial position noise were randomized (Table I). Anisotropic friction (μy randomized relative to μx) was specifically introduced to model observed discrepancies between simulation and reality and account for unmodeled physical effects like stick elasticity or surface unevenness.
5. **Reward Function:** r = −d^2 − log(d + b), where d is the distance to the target and b is a small constant.
6. **Policy Architecture:** A neural network with a 128-unit hidden layer.
7. **Training Details:** K = 16 rollouts per update, N = 3 adaptation steps. The adaptation step size (α) was learned during meta-training. The generative trajectory model weights remained fixed during policy training.

**F. Baseline for Comparison**
A standard domain randomization approach was used as a baseline. A policy was trained using PPO across the same range of randomized dynamics as the meta-learning setup, but without the explicit meta-learning objective for adaptation. For a fair comparison of adaptability, this baseline policy was then subjected to the same adaptation procedure (K rollouts, vanilla policy gradient update) as the meta-learned policy, using the same adaptation step size (α) as learned by the meta-model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings from both simulated and real-world experiments, focusing on the efficacy and stability of the proposed meta-learning approach for domain adaptation.

**A. Generative Trajectory Model Analysis**
- The Variational Autoencoder successfully learned a 2-dimensional latent space (z0, z1) to represent hockey puck trajectories.
- Analysis of the latent space (Figure 5) indicated that the model effectively disentangled control parameters: z0 correlated with the hitting angle, and z1 with hitting strength. As z0 increased, trajectories consistently shifted the puck's final position towards the left in a smooth and continuous manner. This disentanglement provided a structured, low-dimensional, and safe action space for the policy.

**B. Simulation Experiments**
- **Performance Comparison (Figure 6):**
 - Initially, without any adaptation (step 0), the domain randomization baseline exhibited superior performance. This was expected, as the baseline was trained to be robust across varied dynamics.
 - However, after a few adaptation steps, the proposed meta-learning method consistently surpassed the baseline in terms of reward across various dynamic conditions (isotropic low, isotropic medium, anisotropic low x, anisotropic low y friction cases).
 - A notable observation was the performance deterioration of the domain randomization baseline during adaptation in two out of four cases (e.g., Isotropic medium friction, Figure 6b), sometimes starting as early as the first update. This suggested that the baseline policy was not optimized for stable adaptation.
- **Latent Action Distribution Analysis (Figure 7):**
 - **Baseline (Domain Randomization):** Before adaptation (step 0), the policy took consistent actions across experimental repetitions. However, after a few adaptation steps, the latent action distributions of the baseline policies diverged significantly across different repetitions of the experiment. This divergence escalated with more updates, pushing policy parameters away from the stable region of the trajectory generator, likely leading to more varied and less predictable trajectories, and consequently, less stable reward gradients. This explained the observed performance deterioration and inconsistency.
 - **Proposed Method (Meta-Learning):** In contrast, the meta-learned policy demonstrated consistent and stable updates. Latent action distributions remained clustered and close to the origin across repetitions, even after 10 adaptation steps (despite the meta-policy being trained for only 3 steps). This indicated that the meta-policy had learned to perform stable and consistent update steps, preventing the policy from drifting into unstable regions of the action space. This behavior was repeatable across multiple experimental runs.

**C. Real-world Experiments**
- **Performance Comparison (Figure 8):**
 - Similar to simulations, the domain randomization baseline showed higher initial rewards (step 0) with both the red (higher friction) and blue (low friction) pucks.
 - **Baseline:** The baseline exhibited inconsistent behavior during real-world adaptation. Some updates improved performance, while others led to deterioration. In one instance with the red puck, the policy diverged significantly, rendering the trajectory model unsuitable and necessitating an early termination of the experiment. This confirmed the instability observed in simulations.
 - **Proposed Method:** The meta-learned policy demonstrated consistent adaptation in the real world. Performance either steadily improved or plateaued without significant deterioration or divergence. This was particularly evident for the higher-friction red puck, where the baseline failed to provide any performance improvement, while the meta-learning approach showed consistent gains. The overall variance in performance was also notably smaller for the meta-learned policy compared to the baseline.
- **Puck-specific Observations:** The blue puck (low friction) initially showed a stronger performance increase with the first adaptation steps. This was hypothesized to be due to a clearer initial policy gradient direction (e.g., reducing hitting strength significantly improved rewards) compared to the more complex dynamics of the red puck.

In summary, the meta-learning approach consistently led to stable and effective domain adaptation, both in simulation and on a physical robot, outperforming a standard domain randomization baseline that often exhibited unstable and inconsistent adaptation behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic reinforcement learning and sim-to-real transfer by demonstrating a stable and repeatable methodology for domain adaptation.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Stable and Consistent Sim-to-Real Domain Adaptation:** The work provides evidence that gradient-based meta-learning can serve as a robust tool for transferring policies from simulation to reality. It mitigates the issue of inconsistent and potentially unstable policy updates that can arise when adapting policies trained with standard domain randomization, making the adaptation process more reliable for real-world deployments.
2. **Addressing Robotic Exploration Challenges:** The integration of a latent variable generative model for motor trajectories effectively creates a low-dimensional, smooth, and inherently safe action space. This facilitates more efficient and safer exploration during policy adaptation, which is crucial for reducing hardware wear and risks in robotic applications.
3. **Enhanced Adaptability for Uncertain Dynamics:** By explicitly training the policy to be good at adapting (learning to learn), the method enables rapid and effective adjustments to new or unknown dynamic conditions in the target environment. This is particularly relevant for robotics where exact physical parameters are often difficult to model or vary over time.
4. **In-depth Analysis of Adaptation Stability:** The detailed analysis of latent action distributions during adaptation provides valuable insight into *why* meta-learning leads to more stable updates compared to domain randomization. This understanding can inform future development of robust adaptation strategies.

**Potential Impact:**

1. **More Robust Robotic Deployments:** The improved stability and consistency of domain adaptation could lead to more reliable and predictable performance of RL-trained robots in real-world scenarios, reducing the need for extensive manual tuning or re-training.
2. **Reduced Real-world Data Requirements:** While the method still requires some real-world samples for adaptation, the efficiency and stability of the adaptation process suggest that fewer real-world interactions may be needed to achieve acceptable performance compared to unstable adaptation methods.
3. **Foundation for Future Research in Adaptive Robotics:**
 - **Compensation for Unmodeled Dynamics:** The observation regarding anisotropic friction compensating for unmodeled physical properties suggests a promising avenue for research into how variations in certain simulation parameters can implicitly account for inaccuracies in others, potentially simplifying simulator design.
 - **Improved Sample Efficiency and Informed Exploration:** The paper highlights opportunities for further investigation into optimizing real-world sample efficiency, possibly through more structured and informed exploration strategies or off-policy adaptation with safety constraints. This could further accelerate deployment and reduce operational costs.
 - **Broader Applications:** The principles demonstrated could be extended to other robotic tasks where dynamic uncertainties are prevalent, such as manipulation, locomotion, or interaction with deformable objects.

Overall, this research represents a step towards making reinforcement learning a more practical and dependable tool for complex robotic applications by addressing fundamental challenges in sim-to-real transfer through a meta-learning approach that prioritizes stable and consistent adaptation.
