# Energy-Regularized Imitation Learning for Force- and Work-Aware Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Energy-regularized imitation learning fine-tunes a pretrained RVT-2 manipulation policy to reduce physical effort. It defines a joint-space mechanical-work proxy from torque times angular displacement, trains a transformer predictor to estimate per-key-action work from robot state and action, then freezes it as a differentiable loss term. On 12 RLBench tasks, average work drops 2.1% (208.8 J to 204.4 J) while success rises from 86.2% to 86.9%.

## Problem

- Imitation-learning policies such as PerAct and RVT-2 optimize only imitation accuracy and task success, ignoring the physical effort, redundant accelerations, or unnecessary contact their motions incur.
- Mechanical work cannot serve directly as a training loss because torques and contacts are computed inside non-differentiable physics engines, outside the policy's computation graph.
- Prior energy-aware methods add torque penalties inside RL or use classical dynamics-based trajectory optimization, whose exact dynamics are hard to obtain for contact-rich learned manipulation.

## Method

- **Joint-space mechanical-work proxy**: per-step work sums absolute ==torque== times absolute ==joint-angle displacement== across joints, accumulated over the simulator steps executing each key action to give action-level work labels.
- **Differentiable energy predictor**: a ==transformer encoder== with ==CLS token== over state, action, and step-count tokens regresses action-level work via ==Huber loss==, trained on ==HDBSCAN==-filtered demonstrations excluding atypical high-torque, low-motion steps.
- **Energy-regularized fine-tuning**: the frozen predictor scores the policy's predicted action; its ==min-max-normalized== output is added to the imitation loss with weight λ=10, updating only policy parameters.

## Results

- On 12 RLBench tasks with RVT-2, average mechanical work falls from **208.8 J** to **204.4 J** (**2.1%**) while mean success rises from **86.2%** to **86.9%**; Shape Sorter work drops **15.8%**.
- Energy predictor reaches **2.40 J** average MAE (**7.02%** relative error); HDBSCAN filtering cuts MAE from **2.80 J**, and the unfiltered predictor actually raises fine-tuned work to **214.8 J**.
- Adding internal RVT-2 features lowers predictor MAE to **2.30 J** yet fine-tuned work worsens to **207.8 J** versus **204.4 J**; work increases on Open Drawer, Stack Blocks, and Stack Cups.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A frozen learned surrogate can turn a non-differentiable simulator quantity into a policy-training regularizer, sidestepping differentiable simulation or backpropagation through contact dynamics.
> - Lower surrogate error does not guarantee a better regularizer; gradients must align with controllable action changes, so evaluate surrogates by downstream policy effect, not MAE.
> - Effort penalties conflict with contact-critical tasks: large weights let policies avoid decisive contact, so insertion and stacking need task-conditioned weights or contact-aware energy terms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Imitation-trained manipulation policies are usually rewarded for choosing the demonstrated action, not for how much physical effort their executed motion takes. As a result, they can finish a task while making redundant movements or unnecessary contact. A direct fix is hard because simulator torques and contact behavior do not provide a differentiable training signal for a high-level visual policy.

Otani, Taketsugu, and Ukita work around this in three steps. They measure joint-space work in simulation. They then teach a separate predictor to estimate that work from robot state and proposed action. Finally, they freeze the predictor and use its estimate as an extra fine-tuning penalty. In their evaluation, the method lowers simulated work without lowering the *average* success score, although the effect is not uniform across tasks.

The authors’ schematic of the motivating contrast. The upper sequence is short and physically efficient. The lower sequence is redundant and involves unnecessary contact. This is an illustration, not a recorded experimental comparison.

### 2. From torque to a training signal

The effort target is built from simulator quantities. At each simulator step, the method sums, across robot joints, the magnitude of joint torque multiplied by the magnitude of that joint’s angular displacement. These step values are summed over the trajectory. For training, the simulator steps used to execute each higher-level key action are aggregated into an action-level label. This absolute joint-space quantity is a proxy for manipulator effort. It is not electrical energy, and it is not net work transferred to the object.

The predictor learns this label from demonstration key actions. Torque is used to make the label and is not supplied as a predictor input. The inputs are robot state, proposed action, and task-step index.

Before predictor training, a clustering step called HDBSCAN removes low-density atypical successful steps. Examples include re-grasps, repeated approaches, accidental contact, and temporary stuck states. The authors’ stated aim is to focus training on representative successful behavior.

During fine-tuning, the predictor is frozen. The policy keeps its original imitation loss against the demonstrated action and receives an added normalized predicted-work loss, weighted by λ. The main setting is λ=10. Only policy parameters are updated. Simulator work supplies the predictor’s labels, and training does not backpropagate through the simulator.

Image and robot-state inputs enter the policy. Its predicted action feeds the energy predictor, which supplies the energy loss. Separately, the predicted and expert actions are compared to form the task loss. The two losses combine into a total loss that backpropagates to the policy.

### 3. What the evaluation establishes

The main comparison fine-tunes RVT-2, a high-precision, multi-task visual imitation policy, at λ=10 on 12 simulated RLBench tasks. Work is the trajectory-level proxy in joules, averaged over successful episodes only. Under that protocol, average work falls from 208.8 J to 204.4 J, a 2.1% reduction. Mean success rises from 86.2% to 86.9%.

The pattern is not uniform. On Place Shape in Shape Sorter, success rises from 41% to 47%, and work falls from 260.69 J to 219.48 J. In contrast, Open Drawer, Stack Blocks, and Stack Cups show increased work, and success declines in some task rows. Because work is averaged over successful episodes only, the table is not a matched-episode comparison.

RVT-2 versus “Ours” at λ=10: success (%), work (J), and work reduction (%) for 12 tasks. Work is averaged over successful episodes only.

A second comparison tests the filtering step. With λ=10, filtering improves predictor MAE from 2.80 J to 2.40 J. Fine-tuned work is 204.4 J with filtering versus 214.8 J without it. Success rates are not reported for the unfiltered arm.

Predictor accuracy alone also does not decide the outcome. Adding RVT-2 internal features improves MAE to 2.30 J, but the resulting policy has 207.8 J mean work. The authors interpret this as evidence that useful gradients matter, not predictor MAE alone.

### 4. The boundary

The contribution is a way to add a simulator-derived effort signal to imitation-policy training without backpropagating through a differentiable simulator. The gain is modest and task-dependent. The result comes from simulation and does not demonstrate energy savings on a physical robot.

The authors also report a trade-off. Excessive λ can harm success, especially in contact-rich insertion and articulated-object tasks, because the policy may avoid decisive contact or become overly conservative.
