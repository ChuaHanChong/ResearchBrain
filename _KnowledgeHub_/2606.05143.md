# HORIZON: Recoverability-Governed Curriculum for Physical-Domain Scaling

> [!summary] Summary
> The HORIZON framework introduces a recoverability-governed curriculum for robot learning, enabling policies to adapt to diverse physical conditions. This approach achieved robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Go2 robot under unseen morphology and payload perturbations, significantly outperforming traditional domain randomization methods.

## Problem

- Robot policies trained with online reinforcement learning struggle to generalize robustly across diverse physical conditions due to the limitations of traditional domain randomization.
- Excessively wide domain randomization ranges often lead to unrecoverable failures, generating poor on-policy training signals and potentially collapsing the learning process.
- Existing methods lack a principled way to expand physical domains incrementally while ensuring that new dynamics remain learnable and generate useful corrective data.

## Method

- **HORIZON** implements a recoverability-governed curriculum that adaptively expands physical domains by iteratively searching near the policy's learnable boundary.
- A two-part recoverability gate (locomotion feasibility and checkpoint-comparison) assesses candidate physical ranges, committing them only if rollouts are sufficiently recoverable and prior performance is maintained.
- The framework incorporates rollback and boundary refinement mechanisms, allowing the system to revert to a stable checkpoint or tighten search brackets when challenges are too difficult, preventing training collapse.

## Results

- HORIZON achieved **28.1%** OOD-all success, significantly outperforming 'Direct wide DR' (**10.3%**) and 'w/o rollback' baselines (**8.8%**) by maintaining higher recoverable rollout signals.
- The identified compact four-domain core curriculum reached **36.8%** OOD-all success, surpassing full-domain HORIZON (**28.1%**) and other methods like multi-expert distillation (**16.5%**), GRAM (**22.7%**), and ADR (**14.7%**) with **2.3x** lower training cost.
- The HORIZON-trained policy demonstrated robust zero-shot transfer on a real-world Go2 robot under unseen morphology (e.g., locked joints, stilts) and dynamic payload perturbations (3kg, 5kg), where baseline policies failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recoverability is an operational requirement for effective on-policy physical-domain expansion, as hard-range exposure is beneficial only when rollouts remain recoverable enough to provide corrective data.
> - Physical-domain expansion is uneven and policy-dependent; some domains become bottlenecks but can be substantially expanded after intermediate recovery phases, while others show high robustness with moderate reachability.
> - A compact 'core-domain' composition (e.g., actuation, mass, disturbance, COM) focusing on critical domain interactions can achieve stronger generalization with less training cost than attempting full-domain coverage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental challenge in online reinforcement learning (RL) for robotics: how to scale policies to achieve robust generalization across diverse physical conditions. Traditional machine learning has benefited from scaling laws that show improved generalization with broader data and compute. However, in online RL for physical control, data is generated by the policy itself, creating a coupling where experience must expand while remaining learnable.

Current approaches to exposing policies to physical variation largely rely on domain randomization (DR), where physical parameters are randomized within fixed ranges. This method faces limitations: too narrow ranges can lead to brittle policies, while excessively wide ranges can cause training collapse due to unrecoverable failures, providing little useful on-policy signal. Prior research in robust locomotion suggests that effective disturbances push robots towards unstable but still recoverable states, and that environment design or controller parameters can act as adaptive learning interfaces. These observations highlight the concept of "recoverability" as a crucial factor.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of domain randomization, curriculum learning, and continual reinforcement learning. While adaptive domain randomization methods exist, they often focus on updating randomization distributions. Curriculum learning orders tasks by difficulty, and continual RL addresses retaining prior knowledge when learning new tasks. Robust quadruped locomotion has seen advancements through sim-to-real training, adaptation modules, and generalist policies (e.g., LocoFormer with aggressive DR). HORIZON distinguishes itself by focusing on the *organization* of physical-domain training—specifically, how to expand physical ranges *on-policy* through a controlled, checkpointed curriculum governed by recoverability, rather than simply scaling architectures or using aggressive, fixed randomization. It aims to develop a principled method for progressive physical-domain expansion that maintains learnability throughout the training process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate how a robot policy can effectively benefit from exposure to increasingly challenging physics within an on-policy training framework. The authors identify "recoverability" as a central constraint in this process. Their core motivation stems from the observation that simply widening the range of randomized physical parameters, a common practice in domain randomization, is often insufficient for robust robot policy scaling. If new dynamics are too dissimilar from the current policy's capabilities, they lead to rapid, unrecoverable failures, generating weak on-policy training signals and potentially collapsing the learning process. Conversely, if the dynamics are too similar, the policy may become brittle and fail to generalize.

Specifically, the paper aims to:

1. **Understand the role of recoverability:** Determine when harder physical dynamics remain "close enough" to the current policy to generate meaningful, corrective on-policy data, rather than causing rollouts to terminate in unrecoverable states.
2. **Develop a principled curriculum for physical-domain expansion:** Design a mechanism, named HORIZON, that enables continuous physical-domain growth by organizing structured physical variations into learnable frontiers. This mechanism should avoid unrecoverable expansions by employing strategies such as rollback and boundary refinement.
3. **Investigate the characteristics of physical-domain expansion:** Through experiments, explore the regularities governing how physical domains can be expanded. This includes examining whether direct domain widening is uniformly beneficial across different physical axes, how domain composition affects robustness, and whether offline distillation of isolated policies can substitute for joint on-policy curriculum learning.
4. **Demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer:** Evaluate the developed curriculum's ability to produce policies that generalize robustly to unseen physical perturbations and morphological changes in real-world quadruped robots without fine-tuning or additional inputs.
5. **Identify effective domain compositions:** Determine if a compact subset of physical domains, when trained together, can yield better overall robustness and efficiency than exposing the policy to the full range of physical variations, by focusing on interactions that demand shared recovery behaviors.

The chosen benchmark, quadruped locomotion, serves as a physically demanding testbed due to its multiple physical interfaces (actuation, mass, disturbances, contact, inertia, center-of-mass shift, initial state) and dense feedback, allowing for rapid assessment of policy failures and recoveries.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HORIZON implements a recoverability-governed curriculum for physical-domain scaling in on-policy reinforcement learning. The core idea is to expand physical domains incrementally, ensuring that new dynamics remain within the current policy's "recoverable boundary."

**Adaptive Domain Expansion:**
Instead of sampling from a fixed distribution, HORIZON iteratively searches near the policy’s learnable boundary. This process involves a manager `M` that, in each phase `k`, selects a domain group `g_k` and proposes a candidate physical range `ξ_k`. The policy is then trained on this range, generating rollouts `τ_k`. An evaluator `E` assesses these rollouts, and an updater `U` modifies the curriculum state `c_k`, which stores the mastered frontier, failed brackets, pending retries, and refinement state for each domain group.

**Frontier States and Phase Updates:**
For each physical domain group, HORIZON maintains several frontier states: mastered, candidate, failed, and pending-retry. A candidate physical range `ξ_k` is "passed" (i.e., committed as a new mastered frontier) only if it satisfies a two-part recoverability gate:

1. **Locomotion Feasibility Gate (L_k):** This gate assesses the quality of rollouts in the candidate environment. It requires the average episode length (`ℓ_k`) to exceed a minimum threshold (`η_ℓ`) and the tracking error (`e_k`) to be below a maximum threshold (`η_e`).
2. **Checkpoint-Comparison Gate (C_k):** This gate ensures that introducing the new dynamics does not degrade the policy’s performance on previously mastered conditions. It compares the current policy’s tracking error (`e_k|*`) and return (`R_k|*`) in the last committed checkpoint environment against the checkpoint’s baseline statistics (`e*`, `R*`), allowing only a small degradation within defined tolerance margins (`δ_e`, `δ_R`).

Both `L_k` and `C_k` must pass for the candidate range to be committed (`S_k = L_k · C_k`). If successful, the mastered frontier is advanced, and the optimizer and curriculum state are saved. If it fails, the system records the failed range, reloads the last committed checkpoint, and either tightens the search bracket, creates a retry, or marks a local learnability boundary.

**Challenge Proposal:**
The manager proposes the next challenge based on the current mastered frontier (`f_k^*`) and any previously failed frontiers (`f_k^-`). Coarse proposals expand outwards towards the curriculum limit (`b`) using a growth factor (`ρ_grow`), while recovery proposals retreat by interpolating (`α`) between the mastered and failed frontiers.

**Domain Groups and Curriculum Ranges:**
The physical state space is factorized into seven distinct domain groups: action authority, morphology scale (mass), external perturbations (disturbance), contact conditions, body inertia, center-of-mass (COM) shift, and initial state. Each group has a "baseline range" for near-nominal randomization and wider "curriculum limits" that define the manager's search region. In "focused mode," only one domain group is actively advanced at a time, while others remain fixed at their latest committed checkpoint ranges to isolate failure attribution.

**Policy and Training Details:**
The policy is a decoder-only transformer, conditioned on proprioceptive observations, commands, and previous actions. It is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in Isaac Lab, leveraging causal attention, RoPE, and a sliding-window key-value cache. Privileged simulation-only signals are used for the critic. The reward function includes terms for linear and yaw velocity, penalizes vertical velocity, roll/pitch angular velocity, action rate, and various deviations from desired states, while rewarding feet air time.

**Evaluation Protocol:**
The effectiveness of HORIZON is evaluated through several comparisons:
- **Rollback ablation:** Compares HORIZON against variants without rollback and a "Direct wide DR" baseline that uses the target hard ranges from the start.
- **Frontier reachability:** Measures how far each physical axis can be expanded.
- **Core-domain composition:** Investigates the efficiency and effectiveness of training on subsets of physical domains.
- **Comparative generalization:** Benchmarks HORIZON against multi-expert distillation, Adaptive Domain Randomization (ADR), and GRAM on a fixed out-of-distribution (OOD) suite, including a demanding 7-domain OOD all test.
- **Real-world evaluation:** Zero-shot deployment on hardware (Go2 robot) under unseen morphology and payload perturbations (e.g., locked joints, stilts, dynamic payloads).
- **Cross-robot simulation:** Tests the policy's ability to control various quadruped morphologies in simulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings concerning physical-domain scaling, supported by simulations and hardware experiments:

1. **Recoverability as an Operational Requirement for On-Policy Expansion:**
 - Experiments demonstrate that direct widening of randomization ranges or training without a rollback mechanism ("Direct wide DR" and "w/o rollback" baselines) leads to low recoverable rollout signal (i.e., unrecoverable failures) and poor Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) generalization (e.g., 10.3% and 8.8% OOD-all success, respectively).
 - HORIZON, by contrast, maintains higher episode lengths and significantly higher OOD-all success (28.1%), indicating that hard-range exposure is beneficial only when rollouts remain recoverable enough to provide corrective on-policy data. The composite recoverability gate, incorporating both locomotion feasibility and checkpoint comparison, was found to be more predictive of future frontier progress than episode length alone.

2. **Policy-Dependent Recoverability Frontiers and Uneven Expansion:**
 - Physical-domain expansion is not uniform across all physical axes. Some domains, such as actuation, disturbance, and COM, initially present bottlenecks but can be expanded substantially after intermediate recovery phases. For instance, HORIZON increased actuation coverage from 4.1% (base randomization) to 46.3%, disturbance from 4.0% to 54.7%, and COM from 3.0% to 42.8%, significantly outperforming an MLP comparison.
 - Other domains, like contact, inertia, and initial state, show high per-group OOD success even with moderate frontier reachability, suggesting they are less critical bottlenecks for generalization when considered in isolation. This implies that direct widening across all domains may not be the most effective strategy.

3. **Core-Domain Composition and Sample Quality over Coverage:**
 - The study reveals that multi-domain physical scaling is more of a "relation problem" (how domains interact) than a "coverage problem" (maximizing the number of domains). A compact "core-domain" curriculum was identified, comprising Actuation, Mass, Disturbance, and COM, through diagnostic analysis of pairwise domain interactions. This core was selected because it generated shared recovery demands and improved transfer for bottleneck axes.
 - **Efficiency and Generalization:** The compact four-domain core curriculum achieved a stronger composed OOD-all success rate (36.8%) with less training cost (2.3x normalized to direct-wide-DR budget) compared to full-domain HORIZON (28.1%) which incurred higher costs by spending phases on easier or saturated axes.
 - **Comparative Performance:** The core curriculum consistently outperformed other methods on the challenging 7-domain OOD-all stress test (36.8% vs. Full-domain HORIZON 28.1%, Multi-expert distillation 16.5%, GRAM 22.7%, and ADR 14.7%). Full-domain training was stronger on certain isolated, easier groups but weaker under simultaneous complex perturbations, suggesting that adding too many domains can dilute the quality of recoverable joint samples. Offline distillation of isolated experts performed substantially worse, indicating that compressing individual behaviors does not replicate the benefits of joint on-policy training under coupled perturbations.

4. **Strong Real-World Generalization and Zero-Shot Transfer:**
 - The HORIZON-trained policy demonstrated robust zero-shot transfer on hardware (Go2 robot) under unseen real-world morphology and payload perturbations. These interventions included scenarios like locked knees, stilt walking, dynamic payloads (3kg, 5kg), and combined morphology-payload changes.
 - In contrast, a baseline policy trained with basic domain randomization (`base-DR`) largely failed these difficult deployment tasks, being unable to maintain balance or sustain commanded locomotion. This highlights HORIZON's ability to learn policies that generalize to realistic, complex physical variations.
 - The final trained model also supported cross-embodiment locomotion control in simulation across additional quadruped bodies (e.g., Go1, A1, Spot, CyberDog, B1, B2), providing supplementary evidence of morphology coverage.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HORIZON framework introduces a novel perspective on physical-domain scaling in robot reinforcement learning, positioning it as a continual growth problem where recoverability serves as the organizing principle for on-policy expansion. This work challenges the conventional wisdom that simply widening randomization ranges or maximizing domain coverage automatically leads to more robust policies.

The primary significance of this research lies in its empirical demonstration and articulation of three key regularities:
1. **Recoverability as a prerequisite:** It establishes that for new dynamics to be useful in on-policy training, they must remain within the policy's recoverable boundary, providing corrective data rather than leading to training collapse. This finding has implications for designing more effective curriculum learning strategies in physically embodied RL.
2. **Uneven and policy-dependent expansion:** The observation that different physical axes become learnable at different rates, and that direct, aggressive widening can be detrimental, suggests that future domain randomization and curriculum methods should incorporate adaptive, fine-grained control over individual physical parameters.
3. **Importance of compact core compositions:** The finding that a compact set of interacting core domains can be more effective than full-domain exposure or offline distillation implies that focusing on "relation problems" and preserving recoverable sample density is crucial. This could lead to more efficient training paradigms that achieve high robustness with reduced computational costs.

The potential impact of HORIZON is substantial for the development of general-purpose robot controllers. By enabling robust zero-shot transfer to real-world hardware under diverse and challenging conditions, the framework contributes to bridging the sim-to-real gap. This could accelerate the deployment of autonomous legged robots in unstructured and unpredictable environments, where real-world variations in morphology, payload, and external disturbances are common.

While the current work focuses on locomotion and relies on a hand-designed physical-domain space, its principles could be extended to more complex tasks like loco-manipulation, where recoverability might depend on coordinating body dynamics with object interaction. Future research could explore automated domain discovery methods, potentially integrating language models, and investigate the analogous concept of recoverability in perceptual-domain curricula, paving the way for more holistic and generalizable robot learning systems.
