# Causal Foundation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Causal foundation models (CFMs) are transformers pretrained on synthetic structural-causal-model priors that estimate treatment effects on entirely new tabular datasets via in-context learning, with no training or tuning. This tutorial unifies their design space, prediction target, prior identifiability and architecture, then benchmarks the three open CFMs against tuned classical estimators, finding competitive accuracy at 1-2 orders of magnitude less wall-clock time.

## Problem

- Classical causal estimation demands a bespoke pipeline per dataset: propose a mechanism, pick an estimator, tune hyperparameters, retrain, with no reuse or transfer across problems.
- Precursor learning-to-learn approaches train on narrow, low-dimensional structural causal models, giving no evidence they scale to real high-dimensional, big-data environments.
- The three first-generation CFMs shipped with incompatible priors, prediction targets and evaluation setups, so no head-to-head comparison or practitioner-facing usage guidance existed.

## Method

- Unified ==prior-data-fitted-network== formalism casting every CFM as ==amortized Bayesian inference== over a ==posterior predictive== of a causal estimand (CID, CEPO or CDTE) trained on synthetic ==SCM priors==.
- Design-space taxonomy contrasting Do-PFN's ==non-identifiable prior==, CausalPFN's ==backdoor strong-ignorability prior==, and CausalFM's three setting-specific models with a five-component ==Gaussian-mixture head==.
- Matched-hardware benchmark protocol on RealCause-Lalonde over 10 seeds, plus an open codebase and Jupyter notebooks reporting PEHE, ATE relative error and end-to-end wall-clock runtime.

## Results

- CausalPFN reaches PEHE **8.97** on Lalonde-CPS versus the tuned T-Learner's **9.04**, with best CFM average rank **1.75** against T-Learner's **1.40**.
- All three CFMs run **1-2 orders of magnitude** faster on CPU, CausalPFN at **18.4s** median versus T-Learner **1803s**, roughly a **100x** amortization speedup.
- Do-PFN and CausalFM sit at ATE relative error **0.88-0.95** on both cohorts with tiny standard errors, indicating systematic shrinkage of the predicted effect toward zero.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Restricting the pretraining prior to identifiable data-generating processes pays off: the backdoor-prior model wins whenever the deployment setting actually satisfies those assumptions.
> - Amortization shifts cost from deployment to pretraining, so the practitioner-relevant metric is inference-only wall-clock time, not the compute the model already spent.
> - Competitive point estimates do not imply calibrated ones; prior-induced bias and effect shrinkage hide behind good ranks, so check magnitude recovery and coverage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of causal inference traditionally requires a customized approach for each new problem, involving the proposition of a causal mechanism, selection of a compatible estimator, and subsequent training. This bespoke pipeline often prevents the reuse of models or the transfer of knowledge across different tasks. Simultaneously, the broader machine learning landscape has seen a paradigm shift towards foundation models, which are networks pretrained at scale and then applied to diverse tasks without requiring fine-tuning or model updates.

This work introduces "Causal Foundation Models" (CFMs) as an approach that integrates the foundation model paradigm into causal inference. CFMs aim to address the limitations of traditional causal inference methods by providing a generalist model capable of estimating causal quantities, such as the average treatment effect (ATE) or conditional average treatment effect (CATE), on new datasets through in-context learning. This positions CFMs as an alternative to existing methods like Bayesian additive regression trees, double machine learning, causal forests, and meta-learners (e.g., S-, T-, and X-learners), which necessitate problem-specific design and training. The paper aims to provide a practical introduction to this emergent area, summarizing background in both causal inference and machine learning before detailing the CFM concept.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to provide a practical and hands-on introduction to Causal Foundation Models (CFMs). The authors aim to equip readers with the foundational knowledge, computational tools, and practical examples necessary to integrate CFMs into their own research or applications. This includes providing example code and Jupyter notebooks, along with a full codebase, to facilitate immediate engagement with these models.

The motivation for developing and introducing CFMs stems from several challenges inherent in traditional causal inference workflows:
- **Bespoke Pipeline:** Conventional causal inference typically requires a tailored pipeline for each new problem. This involves analyzing data, proposing a causal mechanism, selecting an appropriate estimator, tuning hyperparameters, and then training the model. This process is repeated for every new task, leading to duplicated effort and limited knowledge transfer.
- **Efficiency and Speed:** The iterative nature of traditional methods is time-consuming. CFMs are motivated by the potential to significantly increase inference speed by providing pretrained networks that can estimate causal quantities on new datasets without requiring further training or fine-tuning, leveraging in-context learning.
- **Performance Improvement:** Beyond efficiency, CFMs are introduced with the expectation of demonstrating competitive or superior performance on various causal inference tasks compared to existing methods.
- **Accessibility for Non-Experts:** The complexity of designing and executing traditional causal inference pipelines can be a barrier for non-expert users. CFMs aim to compress much of this workflow into a single forward pass of a pretrained network, thereby reducing friction and enabling rapid causal analysis across numerous small datasets.
- **Amortized Bayesian Inference:** CFMs are designed to amortize Bayesian inference for causal quantities. This means that instead of explicitly approximating the posterior distribution for each new problem, CFMs learn a mapping from observational data directly to a Posterior Predictive Distribution (PPD) of the causal quantity, providing uncertainty quantification as part of the prediction process. This is particularly relevant given the inherent uncertainty in causal estimation from observational data.
- **Generalization:** By being trained on tasks sampled from a diverse prior over possible data-generating processes and causal mechanisms, CFMs are designed to generalize to a wide variety of unseen scenarios, a capability that traditional, problem-specific models lack.

The paper ultimately aims to introduce CFMs to a broader audience, compare different design approaches, discuss recent developments, and encourage researchers and practitioners to explore their utility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Causal Foundation Models (CFMs) are conceptualized as adaptations of Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) to the domain of causal inference. PFNs are transformer-based models designed to perform Bayesian inference by mapping a dataset directly to a Posterior Predictive Distribution (PPD) using in-context learning. To apply this paradigm to causal inference, CFMs incorporate specific adaptations concerning the training loss, the design of synthetic data priors, and architectural considerations for handling causal variables.

#### 3.1. Core Principles of CFMs

CFMs are defined as PFNs pretrained on a diverse set of causal tasks to estimate causal quantities from new datasets using in-context learning, without updating model weights at inference time. The workflow for CFMs contrasts with traditional causal estimators by replacing iterative training and tuning with a single forward pass, where the observational dataset serves as context for the pretrained model to make predictions on query data (Figure 4).

#### 3.2. Adaptations for Causal Inference

The transition from predictive PFNs to CFMs necessitates three key adaptations:

##### 3.2.1. The Causal Prior-Data Loss

- **Challenge:** Unlike predictive PFNs where the model predicts labels from features (both available in supervised data), CFMs must predict *causal* quantities (e.g., potential outcomes, treatment effects) from *purely observational* data. Ground-truth causal quantities (e.g., counterfactuals for an individual) are not directly observable in real-world data.
- **Solution:** CFMs are trained using a modified "causal prior-data loss."
 - For the Conditional Expected Potential Outcome Posterior Predictive Distribution (CEPO-PPD), the loss (Equation 27) involves sampling a Data Generating Process (DGP) from a prior, generating an observational dataset, and then computing the ground-truth CEPO value from the sampled DGP to evaluate the model's likelihood.
 - For the Conditional Interventional Distribution Posterior Predictive Distribution (CID-PPD), the loss (Equation 28) similarly involves sampling a DGP, generating observational data, and then simulating an interventional outcome from the true CID to evaluate the model's likelihood.
- **Tractability:** Both losses are tractable if interventional data can be simulated, as they only require evaluating the model at a ground-truth causal value, not knowing the true PPD in closed form.
- **Convergence:** Training with these losses encourages the model's output (qθ) to approximate the true PPD. For CEPO, this convergence to the true CEPO mean is proven conditional on identifiability assumptions holding for the prior's support. Non-identifiability would manifest as uncertainty in the CID.

##### 3.2.2. Tractable Synthetic Priors over DGPs

- **Necessity:** Real observational data lacks the ground-truth counterfactuals or CEPO values needed for the causal prior-data loss. Therefore, synthetic data generation is a fundamental component for training CFMs.
- **Structural Causal Models (SCMs):** CFMs rely on SCMs (Section 2.4) to define Data Generating Processes (DGPs) and generate both observational and interventional data. An SCM consists of a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) representing causal relationships, exogenous variables (noise), and structural equations defining how endogenous variables are generated from their parents and noise.
- **Prior Design:** The diversity and properties of the SCM prior π(ψ) are critical for CFM generalization.
 - **Identifiability:** Different CFMs make different choices regarding identifiability in their priors. Do-PFN uses a non-identifiable prior, allowing it to capture uncertainty from unobserved confounders. CausalPFN uses a "backdoor prior," supported on DGPs satisfying strong ignorability and SUTVA, ensuring identifiability under these conditions. CausalFM uses separate models and priors for backdoor, instrumental variable (IV), and frontdoor settings. The authors emphasize that non-identifiable priors prevent recovery of the true causal effect even with infinite data.
- **Data Generation:**
 - **Observational Data:** Sample exogenous noise variables and propagate them through the SCM's structural equations to generate (X, T, Y) triplets.
 - **Interventional Data:** To obtain potential outcomes Y(t), the structural equation for T is replaced by a fixed value `t*` (do-operator). Noise variables are sampled, and the modified SCM generates an interventional sample (x, `t*`, Y(`t*`)).
 - **CEPO Labels:** For CEPO `μ_t(x)`, an expectation over relevant noise variables is taken (Equation 31). Some models (e.g., CausalPFN) augment the SCM to directly compute `μ_t(x)` in a forward pass, adding a separate noise node for realism.

##### 3.2.3. Transformer Architectures for CFMs

- **In-Context Learning (ICL):** Like TFMs, CFMs are transformer-based. They receive a tabular observational dataset `D_obs` as context and a causal task (e.g., CATE for `x_m`, CEPO for `(x_m, t_m)`) as a query.
- **Input Representation:** Input data (covariates X, treatment T, outcome Y) must be tokenized and embedded.
 - Some CFMs (CausalPFN, Do-PFN) ensure the treatment variable is a specific column. Do-PFN also uses a treatment column indicator.
 - CausalFM uses separate encoders for covariates and treatment before concatenation.
- **Attention Mechanism:** Masked attention is applied, ensuring query tokens attend only to context tokens, making query predictions independent of each other once conditioned on `D_obs`.
- **Output:** The CFM `M_θ` outputs an estimate `q_θ` of the desired causal estimand's PPD. Point estimates (e.g., CATE(x)) are derived from the mean of this PPD. CausalFM, for instance, uses a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) head to represent the CDTE-PPD (Equation 36).

#### 3.3. Training Process Overview (Figure 5)

1. Sample an SCM `S_ψ` from the prior `π`.
2. Generate an observational dataset `D_obs` (size N) from `S_ψ`.
3. Simulate interventions to create an interventional dataset `D_int` (size M) from `S_ψ`, providing ground-truth causal targets.
4. Train the CFM using the causal prior-data loss, predicting the true causal target from `D_obs` as context.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper benchmarks three openly available Causal Foundation Models (CFMs)—Do-PFN, CausalPFN, and CausalFM—against several established traditional causal inference models. The evaluation is conducted on the semi-synthetic RealCause-Lalonde benchmark, specifically the Lalonde-CPS and Lalonde-PSID cohorts, which provides known ground truth for causal effects while preserving empirical covariate distributions.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Benchmark Data:** Lalonde-CPS (16,177 samples) and Lalonde-PSID (2,675 samples). Outcomes are simulated to ensure conditional ignorability, and results are averaged over 10 independent semi-synthetic realizations.
- **Baselines:** A range of classical methods including S-Learner, T-Learner, X-Learner, IPW, DR-Learner, and Debiased ML. These baselines were extensively optimized using FLAML AutoML for nuisance models (LightGBM, XGBoost, Random Forest, etc.) and hyperparameters, with a 900-second budget per nuisance model.
- **CFMs:** Pre-trained checkpoints of Do-PFN, CausalPFN, and CausalFM (backdoor setting) were used directly, without any parameter or hyperparameter tuning.
- **Metrics:**
 - **PEHE (Precision in Estimation of Heterogeneous Effects):** Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of individual-level CATE estimates (`√[1/N Σ (τ(x_n) - ˆτ(x_n))^2]`). Lower values are better.
 - **ATE Relative Error:** Relative absolute error for population-level ATE estimates (`|ˆλ - λ| / |λ|`). Lower values are better.
 - **CATE Runtime:** Wall-clock CPU time for generating predictions, including training, tuning, and inference for classical baselines, but only inference for CFMs.
 - **Average Rank:** Mean rank across methods for each metric and cohort.

#### 4.2. Benchmark Performance (Table 3 and Figure 7)

- **Accuracy:**
 - **CausalPFN** exhibited competitive performance, achieving the lowest average rank among CFMs (1.75 for PEHE, 2.65 for ATE Relative Error). It closely trailed the highly optimized T-Learner and even outperformed it on the Lalonde-CPS cohort for PEHE (8.97 vs. 9.04). For ATE, IPW showed the strongest performance (0.15 on Lalonde-CPS, 0.08 on Lalonde-PSID), while CausalPFN was also strong (0.17 on Lalonde-CPS, 0.24 on Lalonde-PSID).
 - **Do-PFN and CausalFM** were somewhat less performant than CausalPFN but still competitive with tuned X- and S-Learners, particularly in terms of PEHE.
- **Runtime:**
 - CFMs demonstrated a significant runtime advantage. All three CFMs were 1-2 orders of magnitude faster than the traditional models. CausalPFN was the fastest on CPU (18.4 seconds), while traditional methods like T-Learner and Debiased ML required over 1800 seconds due to extensive training and hyperparameter optimization. The paper also notes further speedups for CFMs when run on a GPU. This highlights the "amortized" nature of CFM inference, where the significant computational cost of pretraining is performed once and then reused across many tasks.
- **Average Treatment Effect Magnitude Recovery:**
 - **CausalPFN** successfully recovered the population-level ATE, with relative errors of 0.17 (Lalonde-CPS) and 0.24 (Lalonde-PSID).
 - **Do-PFN and CausalFM** showed a systematic issue in ATE recovery, exhibiting relative errors near 1 (0.88-0.95) across both cohorts. This indicates a consistent shrinkage of the predicted effect towards zero, suggesting a potential systematic bias or limitation in their specific prior designs or estimation approach for the ATE.

In summary, the benchmarking results provide empirical evidence that CFMs can deliver competitive causal effect estimates, particularly CausalPFN, while drastically reducing the wall-clock time required for deployment compared to traditional methods that necessitate extensive training and tuning for each new task.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Causal Foundation Models represents a significant development in the field of causal inference, potentially impacting both research methodology and practical application.

#### 5.1. Core Significance

- **Amortized Bayesian Causal Inference:** The fundamental contribution is demonstrating the feasibility of amortizing Bayesian causal inference across diverse tasks using the foundation model paradigm. This means that once a CFM is pretrained, it can estimate causal quantities for new, unseen datasets without requiring retraining, fine-tuning, or hyperparameter optimization.
- **Efficiency and Scalability:** By replacing the bespoke, iterative design-and-train cycle with a single forward pass inference, CFMs offer substantial gains in computational speed. The benchmark results indicate 1-2 orders of magnitude faster inference times than extensively tuned traditional methods, making causal analysis more efficient and scalable, especially in scenarios involving many small datasets or rapid iterative exploration.
- **Accessibility:** The simplified workflow of CFMs has the potential to lower the barrier to entry for practitioners who may not be causal inference experts. This could democratize access to causal insights across various domains, enabling broader application in fields like economics, marketing, and medicine.
- **Competitive Performance:** CFMs have demonstrated competitive performance on established causal benchmarks (e.g., RealCause-Lalonde), sometimes on par with or even outperforming highly optimized traditional estimators. This suggests that the speed gains do not necessarily come at the expense of accuracy.
- **Uncertainty Quantification:** As PFNs, CFMs inherently provide Posterior Predictive Distributions (PPDs) of causal quantities, offering a direct mechanism for uncertainty quantification in causal estimates, which is crucial for robust decision-making.

#### 5.2. Broader Directions and Potential Impact

The work highlights several burgeoning areas of CFM development that suggest a wider future impact:
- **Expanded Causal Capabilities:**
 - **Beyond Binary Treatments:** Development of CFMs for continuous treatments (e.g., CCPFN) and longitudinal settings with time-dependent confounding (e.g., CausalLongPFN) expands their applicability to more complex real-world scenarios.
 - **Incorporating Structural Knowledge:** Future CFMs are being designed to leverage partial ancestral information or known causal graphs, allowing practitioners to inject domain expertise to improve estimation accuracy.
 - **Partial Identification and Sensitivity Analysis:** Addressing partially identifiable settings (e.g., IV-ICL) and providing tools for causal sensitivity analysis will allow CFMs to operate robustly even in the presence of unobserved confounders or when exact identification is not possible.
- **Causal Discovery Foundation Models:** The prior-fitting methodology extends naturally to causal discovery, with emerging models (e.g., Arrow, TabCausal, DCD-PFN, TabPFN-CFM) learning to infer causal graphs or their posteriors directly from data. This could revolutionize the initial step of understanding causal relationships before intervention.
- **Domain-Specific Applications:** The successful application of the CFM paradigm to specialized scientific domains like single-cell perturbation modeling (e.g., MapPFN, PerturbPFN) and survival analysis (e.g., SIC, SurvivalPFN) demonstrates its versatility and potential to accelerate research in complex scientific fields.
- **Reliability and Calibration:** Ongoing research into the frequentist consistency, bias, and calibration of CFM predictions indicates a commitment to developing robust and trustworthy models, essential for widespread adoption. This includes strategies for correcting prior-induced bias and handling query misspecification.
- **Future Vision for Unified CFMs:** The paper envisions future CFMs that are more general-purpose, supporting multiple treatment regimes, different identification assumptions, and a wider array of causal estimands within a single model. Such unified models, coupled with interfaces that explicitly communicate identifiability and propagate uncertainty, could represent a comprehensive tool for causal analysis.

In conclusion, CFMs represent a paradigm shift in how causal inference can be approached, offering a path toward more efficient, accessible, and potentially more powerful causal analysis across a broad spectrum of scientific and industrial applications.
