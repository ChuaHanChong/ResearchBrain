# Neural Fields as World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Joshua Nunley from Indiana University introduces isomorphic world models implemented via motor-gated neural fields that preserve spatial structure, enabling continuous physical prediction. This framework facilitates robust offline policy learning with effective sim-to-real transfer and spontaneously develops body-linked representations aligning with neurobiological constraints.

## Problem

- Existing computational models for intuitive physics often rely on explicit object graphs or symbolic representations, failing to integrate raw visual input or motor commands directly for real-time interaction.
- Many machine learning world models compress visual input into abstract latent vectors, discarding spatial structure and leading to representational discontinuities like object 'teleportation'.
- There is a need to understand how the brain predicts physics, supports offline task learning, and develops self-representations while adhering to neurobiological constraints such as spatially organized sensory cortices.

## Method

- Developed a **motor-gated neural field** architecture, a spatially organized ==recurrent network== that maintains the ==spatial topology of sensory input (isomorphism)== in its representations via ==Amari-style neural field dynamics== with ==local lateral connectivity==.
- Integrated ==action-conditional prediction== using ==motor gating==, where continuous motor commands multiplicatively modulate activity in specific neural field channels, abstracting ==gain modulation== observed in cortical areas.
- Trained the world model using a ==next-frame visual prediction loss== in 2D physics environments (ballistic trajectories and a musculoskeletal arm) to learn internal dynamics, then used it as a ==frozen differentiable simulator== for offline policy learning.

## Results

- The neural field model achieved a median prediction loss of **9.33 × 10⁻⁴** for ballistic trajectories, significantly outperforming a VAE-LSTM baseline and demonstrating **0% teleportation** compared to **15.4%** for the baseline.
- Policies trained entirely offline using the frozen neural field world model attained an **81.5% catch rate** in a real physics environment for an arm catching task, substantially exceeding VAE-LSTM policies (**46.0%**) and approaching real-physics-trained policies (**89.0%**).
- Motor-gated channels associated with reciprocal joint commands in the arm model exhibited significant body selectivity (e.g., shoulder reciprocal channel had a median selectivity index of **2.18**), indicating emergent representation of the agent's own body.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Preserving spatial topology in world models allows physics prediction to emerge as a geometric problem of continuous activity propagation, preventing unphysical discontinuities often seen in latent-space models.
> - Neurobiologically informed architectural principles, including retinotopic maps, local lateral connectivity, and gain-like motor modulation, are crucial for accurate physical prediction and robust offline learning capabilities.
> - Body-linked representations can spontaneously emerge within motor-gated world models as a byproduct of the visuomotor prediction objective, without explicit supervision for body parts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses foundational questions in artificial intelligence and cognitive neuroscience regarding how intelligent agents, particularly humans, develop and utilize internal models of the world for prediction, planning, and self-awareness. It builds upon the concept of an "intuitive physics engine" (IPE), proposed by Battaglia, Hamrick, and Tenenbaum (2013), which suggests humans possess an internal mechanism for running mental simulations to predict physical outcomes. Previous computational frameworks attempting to model intuitive physics, such as Interaction Networks (Battaglia et al., 2016) and the Neural Physics Engine (Chang et al., 2017), have often relied on explicit object graphs and symbolic representations, thereby not directly integrating raw visual input or motor commands. This constitutes a gap in understanding how the brain predicts physics while actively engaging with the world.

The paper also relates to Grush's (2004) emulation theory, which posits that the brain constructs internal forward models—emulators—that predict the sensory consequences of motor commands and can operate offline, for instance, during mental practice (Jeannerod, 1995). Modern machine learning "world models" (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018) are computational implementations of this idea. However, many of these models compress visual input into latent vectors, discarding the spatial structure inherent in sensory cortex. This compression, followed by recurrent models predicting abstract latent state transitions, can lead to discontinuities, such as objects "teleporting" across representational space, rather than propagating locally as in the physical world.

Neurobiological evidence, such as findings that motion-sensitive area MT activates during trajectory prediction even without visual motion (Ahuja et al., 2022, 2024), suggests that physics simulation may occur within spatially organized visual representations. This contrasts with models that use abstract, spatially unstructured latent spaces.

This research proposes "isomorphic world models" to bridge these gaps. These models are designed to preserve the spatial structure of sensory input, enforcing locality where nearby points in the world map to nearby points in the representation, and information propagates through spatial neighbors. The paper positions neural fields, which are spatially organized recurrent networks with local lateral interactions, as a suitable implementation of this isomorphic principle. While neural fields have a history in modeling cortical dynamics (Amari, 1977; Schöner et al., 2016), their application as world models for planning and control, particularly with motor gating, represents a novel exploration. The work aims to investigate whether preserving spatial topology in world models changes their capacity for prediction, control, and motor-contingent representation, while adhering to observed neurobiological constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether an isomorphic architectural principle, specifically implemented via motor-gated neural fields, can lead to world models that exhibit human-like physical prediction, support offline task learning, and develop body-linked representations. The motivation stems from a critique of standard machine learning world models, which often discard spatial structure by compressing visual input into latent vectors, and a desire to align computational models more closely with neurobiological constraints observed in sensory and sensorimotor cortices.

The specific objectives and underlying motivations are:

1. **Develop a world model architecture that preserves spatial topology:** The core motivation is the "isomorphism hypothesis," which suggests that maintaining the spatial structure of sensory input allows physics prediction to become a geometric problem, where object trajectories unfold through continuous paths in representational space, analogous to their movement in physical space. This addresses the limitation of latent-space models where information can "teleport" discontinuously, violating physical principles.

2. **Integrate action-conditional prediction through motor gating:** The paper aims to enable world models to predict the sensory consequences of an agent's own actions. This is motivated by Grush's emulation theory, emphasizing the role of forward models in action and imagery. The proposed mechanism, motor-gated channels, implements gain modulation, a neurobiologically observed computational principle in cortex (Salinas & Thier, 2000; Andersen et al., 1997) where motor signals multiplicatively scale neural responses in spatially organized sensory representations.

3. **Test the capacity for continuous physical prediction:** A key objective is to demonstrate that enforcing local connectivity in the neural field architecture is sufficient for learning ballistic physics, ensuring that predicted trajectories proceed through continuous paths rather than discontinuous jumps. This aims to validate the hypothesis that local dynamics inherently lead to physically plausible predictions.

4. **Evaluate support for offline task learning and transfer to reality:** The research seeks to determine if a world model learned with these principles can function as a "frozen differentiable simulator." The objective is to train a novel control policy entirely offline, using only the model's internal simulations, and then successfully transfer this policy to a real physics environment without further interaction or fine-tuning. This directly tests the "mental practice" aspect of emulation theory and challenges the "sim-to-real gap" often encountered in model-based reinforcement learning.

5. **Investigate the emergent development of body-linked representations:** A further objective is to explore whether motor-gated channels can spontaneously develop body-selective activity without explicit labels for the body. This is motivated by theories of body schema (Gallagher, 2005) and developmental psychology (Bahrick, 1995; Rochat, 1998) which suggest infants learn about their bodies through sensorimotor contingencies. The aim is to show that a computational precursor to body schema can emerge as a byproduct of the visuomotor prediction objective within the proposed architecture.

In summary, the motivation is to create a world model that is both computationally powerful and neurobiologically plausible, by specifically incorporating spatial structure, local dynamics, and action-dependent modulation, to address key challenges in physical prediction, motor control, and self-representation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a neural field architecture designed to embody the principle of isomorphism, maintaining spatial structure in representations. This architecture is then tested across three distinct experiments.

**Neural Field Architecture:**
The core of the methodology is a spatially organized recurrent network, or neural field. Its state, `h ∈ R^(C × H × W)`, representing `C` channels over `H × W` spatial locations, updates at each timestep through three influences:
1. **Decay:** A negative term `-h_t`
2. **Lateral Input:** A 7×7 convolutional kernel `K` applied to `ReLU(h_t)`, implementing local lateral connectivity where each location influences only its spatial neighbors. This is crucial for enforcing continuous information propagation.
3. **Visual Input:** `W_in * I_t`, where `I_t` is the visual input from the environment.
The update rule follows an Amari-style neural field dynamic:
`h_t+1 = h_t + Δt/τ * (-h_t + K * ReLU(h_t) + W_in * I_t)`
Visual predictions (`I_hat_t`) are reconstructed linearly from the hidden state: `I_hat_t = W_out * h_t`.

**Motor Gating:** To enable action-conditional prediction, the first `M` channels of the neural field are designated as motor-gated. After each dynamics update, the activity in these channels is multiplicatively modulated by continuous motor commands `m_i`:
`h^(i)_t+1 = m_i * h_tilde^(i)_t+1` for `i ∈ {1, ..., M}`
This mechanism is designed to mimic gain modulation, a computational principle observed in cortical areas like posterior parietal cortex for sensorimotor integration.

**Neurobiological Constraints:** The architecture is structurally informed by three neurobiological constraints:
1. **Retinotopic maps:** Maintaining spatial correspondence with visual input.
2. **Local lateral connectivity:** Restricting interactions to spatial neighbors.
3. **Gain-like modulation:** Coupling motor commands to spatial activity.
ReLU activation and convolutional kernels are used as abstractions for neural firing thresholds and local synaptic connectivity, respectively. Learning is implemented via backpropagation, distinct from the architectural design.

**Task Environments:**
1. **Ballistic Trajectory (Experiment 1):** A 32×32 pixel visual field where a ball moves under gravity. The model observes the first three frames, then predicts the remainder autonomously. This setup uses 16 channels and no motor gating.
2. **Musculoskeletal Arm (Experiments 2-3):** A 120×45 pixel visual field containing a planar double-pendulum arm. The arm is controlled by four continuous motor commands (shoulder co-contraction (C), shoulder reciprocal (R), elbow C, elbow R) inspired by the Equilibrium-Point Hypothesis. A ball falls from a random horizontal position for a catching task. This setup uses 32 channels, with 4 motor-gated.

**Training and Evaluation:**
World models are trained using mean squared prediction loss with the Adam optimizer over 10,000 epochs.

**Frozen-Simulator Policy Training (Experiment 2):** This proceeds in three stages:
1. The arm world model is initially trained from random motor-babbling trajectories using next-frame prediction.
2. A small CNN+LSTM "catch predictor" is trained. This predictor acts as a differentiable surrogate for the binary catch event. It receives visual reconstructions from the *frozen* world model replaying real-physics rollouts, with per-timestep caught/missed labels from the corresponding real-physics trajectories.
3. The trained world model and catch predictor are frozen. A policy network is then trained exclusively offline. During simulated rollouts, the policy observes the reconstructed visual field, outputs motor commands, the frozen world model rolls forward, and the frozen catch predictor scores the predicted trajectory. Policy weights are updated by maximizing predicted catch probability through gradients that pass through both frozen modules. No real-environment interaction or additional world-model training occurs during policy learning. The policy's performance is measured by its catch rate when deployed to the real physics environment.

**Body Schema Analysis (Experiment 3):** For the arm model, a selectivity index is calculated to compare motor-gated channel activity over the arm region versus the ball region, normalized by reconstruction activity in those regions:
`Selectivity = (Motor_arm / Motor_ball) / (Recon_arm / Recon_ball)`
Values above 1 indicate body-selective representation.

**Baseline:** The neural field model is compared against a VAE-LSTM architecture inspired by Ha and Schmidhuber (2018). This baseline compresses observations to a 32-dimensional latent space using a convolutional VAE, an LSTM predicts latent dynamics, and a decoder reconstructs predictions. It requires staged training (VAE first, then frozen VAE with LSTM) due to gradient instability. The VAE-LSTM has significantly more parameters (17-67x) than the neural field. The comparison focuses on the architectural contrast between latent-vector and spatially structured world models rather than a strict parameter-matched benchmark.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presented findings from three experiments designed to test the implications of preserving spatial topology in world models.

**Experiment 1: Physics from Lateral Dynamics (Ballistic Trajectory Prediction)**
The neural field model demonstrated the ability to predict ballistic motion accurately. After observing a ball for only three initial frames, the model's internal activity continued to track the parabolic trajectory of the ball even without further visual input.
- **Prediction Loss:** The neural field achieved a median prediction loss of 9.33 × 10^-4 (IQR: [0.000895, 0.000988]) across 10 seeds. This was significantly lower than the VAE-LSTM baseline, which had a median loss of 3.94 × 10^-3 (p < 0.001).
- **Trajectory Continuity:** A key distinction emerged in trajectory coherence. The neural field's maximum frame-to-frame displacement of predicted centroids during blind rollouts was 2.06 pixels, with 0.0% of sequences exhibiting "teleportation" (jumps >3.0 pixels). In contrast, the VAE-LSTM showed a maximum displacement of 21.97 pixels (approximately 10.7 times larger) and displayed teleportation in 15.4% of sequences (p < 0.001). This indicates that the local connectivity constraint of the neural field effectively prevents abrupt, non-physical jumps in predicted motion.

**Experiment 2: Frozen-Simulator Policy Learning (Arm Catching Task)**
This experiment evaluated the capacity of a frozen learned world model to support offline training of a catching policy that transfers to a real physics environment.
- **Catch Rate:** Policies trained entirely within the neural field's simulated environment achieved an 81.5% catch rate (IQR: [79.2%, 84.7%]) when deployed to the real physics environment. This performance approached that of policies trained directly on real physics, which achieved 89.0%.
- **Baseline Comparison:** VAE-LSTM policies, trained under the same frozen-simulator paradigm, achieved a catch rate of only 46.0% (p = 0.003), approximately half the performance of the neural field policies.
- **Prediction Loss vs. Transfer Quality:** Despite achieving comparable pixel-MSE prediction losses with the VAE-LSTM on the arm task, the neural field enabled substantially better policy transfer. The paper suggests this is because the neural field's dynamics act as a spatial integrator, representing position and velocity through the location and movement of activity, allowing the policy to directly read spatial features without needing to decode them from a compressed latent vector. The coherence of long rollouts within the neural field simulator was critical for effective gradient propagation during policy training.

**Experiment 3: Emergent Body-Selective Encoding (Arm Model)**
This experiment investigated whether the motor-gated arm world model developed body-selective representations without explicit supervision.
- **Reciprocal (R) Channel Selectivity:** Motor-gated channels associated with reciprocal (R) commands, which control joint movement direction, showed significant arm selectivity. The shoulder R channel had a median selectivity index of 2.18 (p = 0.002), and the elbow R channel had a median selectivity index of 1.50 (p = 0.002). This indicates that these channels were approximately twice as active over the arm region compared to what was required for visual reconstruction alone.
- **Co-contraction (C) Channel Selectivity:** In contrast, co-contraction (C) channels, which modulate stiffness with minimal immediate visual change, exhibited no significant body selectivity (shoulder C: p = 0.16; elbow C: p = 0.96).
- **Interpretation:** The observed selectivity pattern is linked to the function of each motor command. Reciprocal commands, which produce large arm movements, necessitate a representation of the arm's location for accurate prediction. Co-contraction, with less immediate visual impact, does not impose the same predictive demand for spatial arm representation. This suggests that body selectivity emerges as a consequence of the visuomotor prediction objective, rather than from explicit labels.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research offers several significant contributions and has potential impacts across cognitive science, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence.

**1. Validation of the Isomorphism Hypothesis:** The core significance lies in providing preliminary evidence that "isomorphic world models"—architectures preserving the spatial structure of sensory input—can effectively learn and predict physical dynamics. By demonstrating that local lateral connectivity in neural fields is sufficient for learning continuous ballistic trajectories, the work supports the idea that physics prediction can emerge as a geometric problem of activity propagation rather than abstract state transitions. This validates a key architectural principle that aligns with observed properties of sensory and sensorimotor cortices.

**2. Neurobiological Plausibility and Alignment:** The study introduces motor-gated neural fields that explicitly incorporate three neurobiological constraints: retinotopic maps, local lateral dynamics, and gain-like motor modulation.
 - The continuous trajectory prediction aligns with findings that motion-sensitive areas like MT activate during internal simulations, suggesting that mental physics unfolds within spatially organized representations.
 - The multiplicative motor gating provides a computational implementation of gain modulation, a mechanism prevalent in posterior parietal cortex for integrating motor commands with spatial sensory information.
 This alignment suggests a framework that could model aspects of human cognition with greater biological fidelity.

**3. Enhanced Offline Learning and Transfer for Control:** A notable impact is the demonstration that a frozen, differentiable neural field world model can effectively support offline policy learning for a novel control task (catching a ball), with policies transferring successfully to a real physics environment. This significantly outperforms latent-space baselines and implies a computational mechanism for "mental practice" or "dream-like rehearsal," where internal forward models are used to improve action capabilities without direct environmental interaction. This capability addresses a critical challenge in robotics and reinforcement learning, the "sim-to-real gap," by showing that a structured simulator can provide reliable gradients for policy optimization.

**4. Emergence of Body-Linked Representation:** The finding that motor-gated channels develop body-selective encoding without explicit body labels is significant. It offers a computational precursor to the "body schema," suggesting that the brain might implicitly distinguish its own body from the external world through sensorimotor contingencies. This supports developmental theories (e.g., Bahrick, 1995; Rochat, 1998) which posit that infants learn about their bodies by detecting the contingent relationship between their actions and resulting sensory feedback. It also provides a mechanism related to comparator models of agency (Blakemore et al., 2002), where action-conditional predictions help differentiate self-caused from externally-caused sensations.

**5. Interpretability and Enactivist Perspectives:** The direct interpretability of the neural field, where predicted positions correspond to activity in specific spatial locations, contrasts with opaque latent-space representations. This aligns with enactivist accounts of perception (O'Regan & Noë, 2001), where understanding arises from mastering sensorimotor contingencies rather than constructing internal symbolic representations. The model suggests that the feeling of immediate physical reasoning might stem from this spatial, directly readable representation.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges limitations, including the use of simple 2D environments. Future work could extend the model to handle 3D space, complex object interactions (occlusion, collision), and explore other spatially structured architectures (e.g., ConvLSTMs, cellular automata, graph neural networks on spatial graphs) that embody the isomorphism hypothesis. Integrating such models with symbolic reasoning for more abstract physical understanding remains an open question.

In conclusion, this research provides a computational framework that treats physics, control, and body representation as integrated spatial problems, offering a biologically informed and robust approach to world modeling with implications for both artificial and natural intelligence.
