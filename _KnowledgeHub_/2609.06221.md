# RefGuard: Identity-Aware Language-Guided Robot Manipulation via Joint Target-Anchor-Frame Grounding

> [!summary] Summary
> RefGuard is an identity-aware grounding framework for language-guided manipulation that delays commitment by maintaining a joint posterior over target, anchor, and reference frame. It builds a frame-conditioned two-layer scene graph from RGB-D, scores whole interpretations in a hypothesis table, then executes, clarifies, reobserves, or aborts. On a real UF850 arm it records zero identity switches while executing correctly on 90% of ambiguity-stress trials.

## Problem

- End-to-end VLA policies and LLM pipelines commit early to a single target, anchor, and frame, collapsing residual ambiguity into silent, irreversible wrong-instance actions.
- Tracking and segmentation systems preserve assigned object identities over time but never determine which identity the language instruction intended in the first place.
- Ask-for-help methods choose among candidate plans rather than resolving the coupled target-anchor-frame uncertainty that determines the referent within a single action.

## Method

- A **two-layer object-centric scene graph** separates frame-independent geometry (distance, containment, overlap) from frame-conditioned directional offsets, so directional relations stay uncommitted across candidate reference frames.
- A **joint hypothesis table** scores each complete target-anchor-frame interpretation by summing object, relation, frame-prior, and action-feasibility evidence, then ==softmax-normalises== rows after pruning hard-constraint violations.
- A **posterior-to-decision policy** thresholds top-mass, margin, entropy, and an ==action-equivalence test== to pick EXECUTE, CLARIFY, REOBSERVE, or ABORT, with ==pre-execution contract verification== before motion.

## Results

- Across duplicate-target, duplicate-anchor, and frame-conditioned settings RefGuard had **0%** identity-switch rate versus **28-59%** for GR00T, a 0.5-scale VLA, and an LLM pipeline, retaining **93.3%** clean-scene success.
- On **2,400** solvable procedural episodes correct execution rose from **56.6%** to **80.5%** over the anchor-and-frame-first ablation, while deferral dropped from **43.4%** to **19.5%**.
- Under post-grounding perturbations RefGuard recovered on **86.7%** of trials versus **0%** for the LLM pipeline; giving controls the same scene update lifted them to **22-23/30** but switches persisted.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Ambiguity is a joint property: fixing any one coupled latent variable early both selects wrong instances and prunes otherwise valid hypotheses downstream.
> - Safety gains need not come from abstention; preserving uncertainty raised correct executions and lowered deferrals at the same time.
> - Re-observation alone is insufficient after scene changes: reliable recovery also requires re-binding with joint grounding uncertainty intact, not a locked assignment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of language-guided robot manipulation has experienced substantial advancements, primarily driven by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, Large Language Models (LLMs), and Vision-Language Models (VLMs). These models enable robots to interpret natural language instructions and translate them into physical actions in various environments. Systems such as GR00T, π0.5, and LLM-driven pipelines have demonstrated capabilities in object detection, vision-language reasoning, and grasp planning, leading to increasingly fluent robotic behaviors.

However, a persistent challenge in these systems is reliable execution in complex or cluttered scenes, particularly when dealing with referential ambiguity. Existing approaches often commit prematurely to a single interpretation of the instruction, selecting a most likely target, anchor object, or reference frame early in the process. This early commitment can lead to what the authors term an "identity switch" – a failure mode where the robot executes a geometrically valid action on an object that matches the description but is not the one intended by the user. Such failures are problematic because they are silent and irreversible, without the robot acknowledging the underlying uncertainty. Current systems may lack mechanisms to explicitly model and manage this joint uncertainty, or they might allow robots to ask for clarification but often at the level of selecting between pre-defined plans rather than resolving the fundamental ambiguities in object identity and spatial relations.

This work addresses this specific gap by focusing on the joint nature of referential ambiguity, where the target object, its anchor, and the contextual reference frame are interdependent variables. It positions itself as a complementary approach to existing VLA and LLM/VLM grounders, providing an interface that preserves and verifies this joint uncertainty before action, thereby enhancing the reliability and safety of language-guided manipulation systems. The paper argues that better perception or advanced tracking systems alone are insufficient, as the core issue lies in the interpretation of language and its connection to physical instances under ambiguity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enhance the reliability of language-guided robot manipulation, particularly in cluttered environments where referential ambiguity is common. The authors motivate this work by identifying a critical failure mode called an "identity switch." This occurs when a robot, despite possessing accurate object detection, proficient vision-language reasoning, and reliable grasp planning, acts on a physical object instance that is semantically compatible with the instruction but unintended by the user. These failures are attributed not to a single module's deficiency but to the inherent structure of grounding under ambiguity, where a premature commitment to a single interpretation of an instruction leads to an irreversible error.

The motivation for addressing identity switches stems from the observation that the intended referent in a natural language instruction is often determined by three coupled latent variables: the target object, the anchor object (if any), and the reference frame (e.g., "left of the can" can be from the robot's or user's perspective). Existing systems frequently make hard choices about these variables early in the processing pipeline. This early commitment can convert residual ambiguity into a silent and unrecoverable error, as the robot lacks awareness that its interpretation was uncertain.

The key objectives are structured as follows:

1. **Characterize Identity Switches:** Formally define identity switches as a specific failure mode in language-guided robot manipulation, where a geometrically valid action is performed on a semantically compatible but physically unintended object. The authors frame the underlying ambiguity as a joint inference problem over the target instance, anchor instance, and reference frame.
2. **Propose RefGuard Framework:** Introduce RefGuard, an identity-aware grounding framework designed to explicitly model and preserve referential uncertainty. This framework utilizes a two-layer scene graph that separates frame-independent geometry from frame-dependent directional relations. It employs joint target–anchor–frame posterior inference, delaying commitment to a single referent until the decision stage.
3. **Develop a Decision Policy:** Equip the robot with a decision policy that maps the posterior belief state to appropriate actions:
 - **Execute:** When the belief is sufficiently concentrated and feasible.
 - **Clarify:** When target, anchor, or frame identity is ambiguous, allowing the robot to query the user for more information.
 - **Reobserve:** When perceptual evidence is weak or stale, prompting the robot to gather new observations.
 - **Abort:** When no valid interpretation or feasible action remains.
 This policy aims to prevent confident but incorrect executions and provide mechanisms for handling uncertainty proactively.
4. **Evaluate Performance:** Conduct rigorous evaluations on a real robotic platform and a large-scale procedural suite to demonstrate RefGuard's ability to reduce identity switches. The evaluation also aims to show that these safety benefits do not degrade performance on unambiguous tasks and that the advantages are due to joint, delayed commitment rather than mere abstention or access to scene updates.

In essence, the motivation is to move beyond simply selecting the "most likely" grounding towards building more reliable and trustworthy robot systems that are aware of their own referential uncertainty and can act appropriately when facing ambiguity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RefGuard operates as an identity-aware grounding framework designed to address referential ambiguity in language-guided robot manipulation. The system integrates perception, language parsing, a scene graph, joint inference, and a decision policy.

**System Overview:**
The robot receives a natural-language instruction and RGB-D observations from both an external third-person camera and a wrist-mounted camera.
1. **Perception Module:** Built upon SAM 3, this module fuses RGB-D observations to detect and extract a set of object instances, providing geometric, semantic, and uncertainty information for each.
2. **Language Parser:** OpenAI GPT-4o is employed to decompose the natural-language instruction into an ordered sequence of symbolic primitive queries. These queries specify the action (e.g., PICK, PLACE), target phrase, anchor phrases with relations, anchor-free constraints, a reference-frame cue (e.g., "robot's perspective," "my left," or unspecified), and action constraints (e.g., graspability, containment). Notably, the parser provides symbolic structure but does not perform instance-level grounding.
3. **RefGuard Primitive Processing:** For each primitive query, RefGuard aims to return a task contract, which includes the primitive action, the selected target instance (if executing), a target-conditional hypothesis table, spatial and action-feasibility constraints, a posterior-concentration score, and a reason code for the decision made.

**Object-Centric Scene Graph:**
RefGuard constructs a two-layer scene graph, `G = (V, E_pos, {E_f_dir}_f∈F)`, to represent the scene:
- **Nodes (V):** Each node `o_i` corresponds to a detected object instance, encapsulating its geometry (mask, image center, 3D position, bounding box), semantics (category, attributes, affordances), and uncertainty (detection uncertainty, visibility). Object identities are local to the current scene and rebuilt after each observation, enabling recovery from scene changes.
- **Frame-Independent Layer (E_pos):** This layer stores pairwise geometric relationships that are invariant to the reference frame, such as metric distance, height differences, bounding-box overlap, contact, and containment. These support topological relations like "near" or "on."
- **Frame-Conditioned Layer ({E_f_dir}_f∈F):** This layer stores directional offsets (e.g., "left-of," "in-front-of") computed under each candidate reference frame. This design allows the system to evaluate frame-dependent relations without committing to a single frame upfront, with offsets calculated only for frames relevant to the query.

**Candidate Evidence Scoring:**
Three types of evidence are used to score interpretations:
1. **Soft Object Score `E_obj(o_i, q)`:** Aggregates category match, attribute match, detector confidence, and visibility for a given object and phrase. Missing attributes do not penalize candidates.
2. **Soft Relation Score `E_r(k)(i, j_k; f)`:** Evaluates spatial relations using `E_pos` for topological relations and `E_f_dir` for directional relations. This score retains uncertainty for weak or near-tie evidence.
3. **Action Feasibility `E_act(h)`:** Assesses the feasibility of the proposed action (e.g., graspability, collision avoidance for picking; support height, clearance, containment for placing).

**Joint Hypothesis Table for Grounding:**
RefGuard grounds each primitive query by inferring the target, anchor, and reference frame jointly.
- **Hypothesis Representation:** Each row `h` in a hypothesis table represents a complete interpretation: `(target_candidate, anchor_candidates, reference_frame, row_score, normalized_weight)`. Anchor-free constraints are also integrated.
- **Frame Prior:** Each candidate frame `f` has a prior `π_F(f)` derived from the parsed cue `c_F,ℓ`. Explicit cues concentrate the prior, while unspecified cues spread it. Unobservable frames are pruned.
- **Row Scoring:** The row score `s_h` combines object evidence for target and anchors, the frame prior, relation evidence (using `E_r`), and action feasibility `E_act(h)`.
- **Pruning and Normalization:** Rows violating hard constraints (e.g., target and anchor coinciding if they must differ) are pruned before normalization into weights `w_h`.
- **Uncertainty Diagnosis:** From the hypothesis table, RefGuard computes a target belief `B_T(i)`. It also identifies a `H_top` (smallest set of rows exceeding a mass threshold). Uncertainty is diagnosed by comparing variables within `H_top`: `TARGET-AMBIGUOUS`, `ANCHOR-AMBIGUOUS`, `FRAME-AMBIGUOUS`, `PERCEPTION-WEAK`, or `SCENE-STALE`.

**Posterior-to-Decision Policy:**
The policy uses the target marginal `B_T` and a posterior-concentration score `κ` (derived from `p_1` and `p_2`, the largest and second-largest target beliefs, and normalized target entropy `¯H_T`) to make a decision:
- **ABORT:** If no valid hypotheses exist (`H_valid` is empty).
- **REOBSERVE:** If `p_1 < 0.50` or `κ < 0.40`, or if perception is weak/stale.
- **CLARIFY:** If `p_1 - p_2 < 0.15`, normalized target entropy `H_T > 1.50`, or if top anchor/frame hypotheses are not action-equivalent (NAE).
- **EXECUTE:** Otherwise (belief is concentrated, feasible, and action-equivalent).
If `CLARIFY` is chosen, the user's reply is parsed as a new constraint, inconsistent hypotheses are removed, and the primitive is re-grounded.

**Posterior-Aware Planning and Verification:**
- **Planning:** When executing, the target is committed, but the planner retains high-weight anchor and frame hypotheses. Grasps and placement poses are sampled and checked against *all* retained spatial constraints. If conflicting goals are detected during pose-level checking, the query is rerouted to `CLARIFY` or `REOBSERVE`.
- **Pre-execution Verification:** Before any robot motion, a contract check verifies the visibility and pose of the target and anchors, confirms spatial constraints, and re-evaluates action feasibility. Failure triggers rerouting to `REOBSERVE`, `CLARIFY`, or `ABORT`.
- **Execution-time Scene Update:** After each primitive, the scene graph is rebuilt from new observations. Picked objects become in-gripper memory nodes, and placed objects are matched to expected poses. This allows dynamic re-grounding for subsequent primitives, preventing reliance on stale initial bindings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RefGuard focuses on its ability to mitigate "identity switches" while maintaining robust performance across various conditions, including unambiguous tasks, ambiguous scenes, scene changes, sequential tasks, and large-scale evaluations.

**Real-Robot Stress Suite (UF850 Arm):**
- **Clean Scenes (S1 - Unambiguous):** RefGuard achieved a task success (TS) of 93.3% (28/30 trials), performing comparably to baselines like π0.5 (90.0%) and LLM-based (90.0%). This indicates that RefGuard's safety mechanisms do not hinder performance in straightforward scenarios.
- **Identity-Aware Grounding (S2-S4 - Ambiguity Stress):** This is where RefGuard demonstrated its primary advantage.
 - **S2 (Duplicate Targets):** RefGuard recorded 0/28 (0.0%) identity switches (ISR), meaning no instances of executing on the wrong object among executed trials. Baselines, in contrast, showed significant ISRs (GR00T: 52.0%, π0.5: 42.3%, LLM-based: 41.4%).
 - **S3 (Joint Target-Anchor Grounding):** Similarly, RefGuard had 0/27 (0.0%) identity switches, while baselines exhibited ISRs of 40.0% (GR00T), 36.0% (π0.5), and 44.4% (LLM-based). This specifically validated the joint inference over target and anchor.
 - **S4 (Frame-Conditioned Grounding):** RefGuard again showed 0/26 (0.0%) identity switches, outperforming baselines which had ISRs ranging from 28.0% (GR00T) to 59.3% (π0.5). This highlighted the importance of handling reference frame ambiguity.
 - **Overall S2-S4:** Across 90 ambiguity-stress trials, RefGuard achieved 81 correct executions without a single identity switch, whereas baselines produced 30-41 identity switches.
- **Scene Changes and Hard Negatives:**
 - **S5 (Post-grounding Perturbation):** RefGuard demonstrated strong recovery performance of 86.7% (26/30 trials) after scene changes. In contrast, the LLM-based baseline, which uses early commitment without re-observation, achieved 0% recovery. GR00T and π0.5 showed 40.0% and 46.7% recovery, respectively.
 - **S6 (Hard Negatives):** On instructions with irreconcilable ambiguity, RefGuard correctly deferred (CLARIFY, REOBSERVE, or ABORT) in 100% (30/30) of trials, avoiding arbitrary and potentially incorrect executions. Baselines without an explicit deferral mechanism were not applicable for this metric.

**Ablation Studies and Shared-Update Controls:**
- **Hard-A/F (Early Anchor/Frame Commitment):** This ablation, which commits to anchor and frame before target inference, exhibited an ISR of 4/23 across S2-S4, and lower correct execution (19/30) compared to RefGuard (0/26 ISR, 26/30 CE). This result supports that preserving joint uncertainty is crucial for preventing identity switches.
- **Shared-Update Controls (LLM+Update, Hard-A/F+Update):** Providing baselines with post-change observations and scene-graph updates in S5 improved their recovery, but they still incurred identity switches (5/27 and 3/26 respectively). RefGuard maintained 0/26 identity switches and stronger recovery, indicating that re-observation alone is insufficient; joint uncertainty preservation is also necessary for reliable recovery.

**Sequential Composition and Generalization:**
- **Sequential Tasks (2-6 Primitives):** RefGuard maintained high primitive completion rates (95.0% total) and sequential success (83.3% total) across sequences of increasing length (L=2, 4, 6), with no observed identity switches across 120 primitives.
- **Held-Out Object Categories:** The framework generalized to two new object categories, achieving 94.4% primitive completion and 83.3% sequential success, again with no identity switches.
- **Free-Form Paraphrases:** RefGuard achieved 90.0% programmatic exact match for free-form paraphrases, demonstrating robustness to varied linguistic inputs.
- **Post-Action Checkpoints:** RefGuard significantly outperformed a locked-binding control in dynamic re-grounding accuracy after scene changes (93.3% vs. 33.3%), emphasizing the benefit of dynamic re-grounding rather than fixed initial bindings.

**Procedural LIBERO-Style Evaluation at Scale (3,200 Episodes):**
- **Solvable Episodes:** RefGuard achieved a total correct execution (CE) of 80.5% (1931/2400), substantially higher than the Hard-A/F ablation's 56.6% (1358/2400). This advantage was consistent across varying clutter levels (5-20 objects). RefGuard also showed lower deferral rates on solvable episodes (19.5% vs. 43.4%), indicating more efficient execution. No identity switches were recorded for either method among executed solvable trials in this large-scale evaluation, suggesting that the primary difference was between correct execution and deferral.
- **Hard-Negative Episodes:** RefGuard achieved a 100% safe deferral rate on hard-negative episodes, consistently choosing to defer rather than executing arbitrarily. Hard-A/F deferred in 77.8% of these cases, meaning it committed incorrectly in a notable fraction.

In summary, the findings indicate that RefGuard consistently reduces or eliminates identity switches in ambiguous manipulation scenarios, without compromising performance on unambiguous tasks. Its ability to preserve joint target-anchor-frame uncertainty, combined with a robust decision policy and dynamic re-grounding, contributes to safer and more reliable robot behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RefGuard presents a significant contribution to the field of language-guided robot manipulation by systematically identifying and addressing the critical failure mode of "identity switches." This failure, where a robot executes a geometrically valid action on an unintended object due to referential ambiguity, has been a silent yet persistent challenge in robotic systems.

The potential impact of this work is multi-faceted:

1. **Enhanced Reliability and Safety:** By explicitly modeling and preserving joint uncertainty over the target, anchor, and reference frame, RefGuard enables robots to be aware of the reliability of their grounding. This capability directly translates to increased reliability in task execution, as the robot is less likely to perform unintended actions. The decision policy to execute, clarify, reobserve, or abort provides a robust mechanism for preventing costly and irreversible errors, thereby improving the overall safety of human-robot interaction in shared workspaces.
2. **Principled Approach to Ambiguity:** RefGuard offers a principled, late-commitment approach to referential ambiguity, moving beyond the prevalent practice of early and often implicit commitment to a single interpretation. This method is shown to be superior to simply improving perception or allowing for abstention, as the core benefit stems from the joint inference process itself. This could influence the design of future language-guided systems, encouraging developers to incorporate more sophisticated uncertainty management rather than solely focusing on maximizing single-best predictions.
3. **Complementary to Foundation Models:** The proposed framework is designed to be complementary to advancements in VLA, LLM, and VLM models. Instead of replacing these powerful grounders, RefGuard provides an interface to refine their outputs, verify hypotheses, and handle situations where even highly capable foundation models might struggle with subtle ambiguities. This suggests a pathway for integrating RefGuard's principles into the broader ecosystem of robot learning and control, making existing and future foundation models more robust in real-world applications.
4. **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** The ability of RefGuard to explicitly clarify ambiguities or explain its reasons for deferral (e.g., "target ambiguous," "perception weak") can foster more natural and trustworthy human-robot collaboration. Users can understand why a robot hesitated or asked a question, leading to more efficient and less frustrating interactions compared to systems that fail silently or act incorrectly with confidence.
5. **Scalability and Generalization:** The evaluation across diverse real-robot stress tests, sequential tasks, generalization to held-out categories, and a large-scale procedural suite indicates the framework's robustness and scalability. This suggests that the principles of RefGuard could be applicable to a wide range of manipulation tasks and environments, from industrial settings to domestic assistance, where cluttered scenes and nuanced language are common.

In conclusion, RefGuard addresses a fundamental limitation in current language-guided robot manipulation. By prioritizing identity awareness and carefully managing referential uncertainty, it lays groundwork for developing more intelligent, reliable, and trustworthy autonomous systems that can operate effectively and safely in complex, human-centric environments.
