# Learning Visual Prompts for Guiding the Attention of Vision Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Technical University of Munich, Volkswagen AG, and University of Oxford developed a self-supervised framework to learn visual prompts that effectively guide the attention of pre-trained Vision Transformers to specific image regions. These learned prompts consistently achieved higher attention gain and improved performance on downstream tasks compared to traditional manually designed prompts.

## Problem

- Traditional visual prompts are manually designed (e.g., red circles), relying on human intuition and often suboptimal for specific Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures.
- Existing prompts do not account for architectural differences between ViTs or adapt to specific tasks and data distributions.
- There was a lack of a data-driven, self-supervised method to learn effective visual cues for guiding ViT attention.

## Method

- A ==self-supervised optimization framework== uses a ==U-Net== as a 'neural prior' to generate visual prompts, avoiding direct pixel optimization.
- During training, prompts are randomly placed on input images, and the attention weights of the frozen ViT's ==CLS token== are extracted.
- A ==Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss== minimizes the difference between the extracted attention weights and a target Gaussian distribution centered at the prompt location.

## Results

- Learned prompts consistently achieved higher relative attention gain, particularly in higher transformer layers, compared to manually designed prompts across various ViT models (e.g., CLIP-B, DeiT).
- Different ViT architectures (e.g., DeiT, DINOv2, SigLIP) exhibited unique responses and developed distinct optimal visual prompt patterns.
- The learned prompts improved performance on downstream tasks, including keypoint naming on CUB, referring expression recognition on RefCOCO, and enhancing multimodal large language model accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal visual prompts are model-specific, with different Vision Transformer architectures responding distinctly to learned patterns.
> - A self-supervised framework can effectively learn complex, data-driven visual prompts without requiring labeled data.
> - Guiding attention through learned visual prompts can enhance model interpretability and performance, facilitating adaptation of pre-trained models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Introduction

Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as powerful architectures for computer vision tasks, offering exceptional performance across a range of applications. A key aspect of their operation is the attention mechanism, which determines which parts of an image the model focuses on when making predictions. This paper, authored by researchers from Technical University of Munich, Volkswagen AG, and University of Oxford, introduces a novel method for learning visual prompts that can effectively guide the attention of pre-trained Vision Transformers to specific regions of interest within images.

#### 1.2. Background

Visual prompting has emerged as an effective technique for adapting vision models to new tasks without modifying their parameters. This is particularly valuable with the rise of large, pre-trained vision models that are computationally expensive to fine-tune. Traditional visual prompting approaches typically use hand-crafted visual cues, such as red circles or squares, to direct a model's attention to regions of interest. However, these manually designed prompts have several limitations:

1. They rely on human intuition and may not be optimal for a given model
2. They don't account for differences between model architectures
3. They cannot adapt to specific tasks or data distributions

The concept of visual prompting draws inspiration from adversarial patches, which are designed to manipulate model predictions by adding carefully crafted perturbations to input images. However, while adversarial patches aim to cause misclassification, visual prompts are designed to enhance model performance by guiding its attention to relevant regions.

Vision Transformers process images by dividing them into patches, which are then processed through a series of self-attention layers. The CLS token, which aggregates information from all patches, plays a crucial role in determining the model's final representation of the image. By manipulating the attention patterns associated with this token, visual prompts can influence which regions of the image contribute most to the model's understanding.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Motivation for a Data-Driven, Self-Supervised Approach

Unlike traditional approaches that rely on manually designed visual cues like red circles, which may not be optimal for all ViT architectures, this method learns prompts in a data-driven, self-supervised manner. By optimizing the prompts to guide the model's attention to specific locations, the approach enhances the model's ability to focus on relevant regions, potentially improving performance on downstream vision tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Methodology

The researchers propose a self-supervised optimization framework to learn visual prompts that effectively guide the attention of pre-trained Vision Transformers. The key components of this approach include:

1. **Neural Prior Parameterization**: Instead of directly optimizing the pixel values of the prompt, the authors use a neural network (U-Net) as a "neural prior" to generate the prompt. This approach provides several advantages:
 - Improved optimization dynamics
 - Better generalization to different image contexts
 - More stable training process

2. **Random Prompt Placement**: During training, the prompt is placed at random locations in the input image, which forces the model to learn a prompt that is effective regardless of its position.

3. **Attention Extraction**: After adding the prompt to the input image, the modified image is fed into a pre-trained and frozen Vision Transformer. The attention weights of the CLS token in the last layer are extracted.

4. **Target Gaussian Map**: A target Gaussian map is created, centered at the location of the prompt. This map represents the desired attention distribution.

5. **Self-Supervised Loss**: The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the extracted attention weights and the target Gaussian map is used as the loss function. The optimization objective can be formulated as:

$$\mathcal{L}(P) = \text{KL}\left(A_{\text{CLS}}(I+P_{ij}) \parallel \mathcal{G}(i,j,\sigma)\right)$$

where $P$ is the prompt, $A_{\text{CLS}}$ represents the attention weights of the CLS token, $I$ is the input image, $P_{ij}$ is the prompt placed at position $(i,j)$, and $\mathcal{G}(i,j,\sigma)$ is a Gaussian distribution centered at $(i,j)$ with standard deviation $\sigma$.

The neural prior is initialized with random noise and gradually refined through optimization. This process allows the model to discover complex prompt patterns that are more effective than hand-crafted designs.

#### 3.2. Self-Supervised Learning Framework

The self-supervised learning framework is a crucial aspect of the proposed method. It enables the optimization of visual prompts without requiring labeled data, making it widely applicable across different domains and tasks. The framework works as follows:

```python
# Pseudocode for the self-supervised prompt learning
def train_visual_prompt(image_dataset, vision_encoder, epochs=100):
    # Initialize neural prior (e.g., U-Net)
    neural_prior = initialize_neural_prior()
    optimizer = Adam(neural_prior.parameters())

    for epoch in range(epochs):
        for image in image_dataset:
            # Generate random location for prompt
            i, j = random_location(image.shape)

            # Generate prompt using neural prior
            random_noise = torch.randn(input_size)
            prompt = neural_prior(random_noise)

            # Apply mask if needed
            prompt = apply_mask(prompt)

            # Place prompt at random location
            image_with_prompt = place_prompt(image, prompt, i, j)

            # Get attention from frozen vision encoder
            with torch.no_grad():
                attention_cls = vision_encoder.get_cls_attention(image_with_prompt)

            # Create target Gaussian centered at prompt location
            target_gaussian = create_gaussian(i, j, sigma, image.shape)

            # Compute KL divergence loss
            loss = kl_divergence(attention_cls, target_gaussian)

            # Update neural prior
            optimizer.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            optimizer.step()

    return neural_prior
```

This self-supervised approach has several advantages:

1. **No Need for Labels**: The method doesn't require any task-specific labels, making it applicable to any image dataset.
2. **Model Agnostic**: It can be applied to any Vision Transformer architecture without modification.
3. **Data Efficiency**: The approach can learn effective prompts with relatively small amounts of data.
4. **Adaptability**: The framework can be easily extended to different application scenarios and model architectures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Experimental Results

The researchers conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of their learned visual prompts across different Vision Transformer architectures, including CLIP (Base and Large), DeiT, SigLIP, and DINOv2. They compared the learned prompts with manually designed baselines such as red circles and squares.

The experiments revealed several interesting findings:

1. **Relative Attention Gain**: The learned prompts consistently achieved higher relative attention gain compared to manually designed prompts, especially in the higher layers of the transformer. This is illustrated in Figure 2, which shows the relative gain in attention for different transformer layers.

2. **Model-Specific Behavior**: Different ViT architectures responded differently to the prompts. For example, DeiT showed a much stronger response to the learned prompts compared to the red circle baseline, as shown in Figure 3.

3. **Prompt Size and Shape**: The researchers investigated the impact of prompt size and shape on performance. They found that the optimal prompt characteristics varied depending on the specific ViT architecture. Figure 4 illustrates how prompt size (relative to tile size) affects accuracy.

4. **Visual Prompt Appearance**: The learned prompts often developed distinctive visual patterns that were model-specific. Examples of these learned prompts for different models are shown in Figure 5.

#### 4.2. Applications and Downstream Tasks

The learned visual prompts demonstrated utility in several downstream applications:

1. **Keypoint Naming on CUB Dataset**: By guiding the attention of the vision model to specific keypoints in bird images, the prompts improved the model's ability to identify and name these keypoints accurately.

2. **Referring Expression Recognition**: On the RefCOCO datasets, the prompts helped models better understand and locate objects referred to in natural language expressions.

3. **Improving Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)**: The authors demonstrated that their visual prompts could improve the accuracy of MLLMs in answering questions about specific parts of images, as shown in Figure 6.

The experiments showed that learned prompts significantly outperformed manually designed ones in these tasks, indicating their effectiveness in real-world applications.

#### 4.3. Model-Specific Behaviors

One of the most interesting findings of the study is that different Vision Transformer architectures exhibit distinct behaviors when interacting with visual prompts. The authors observed:

1. **CLIP Models**: CLIP-Base and CLIP-Large showed similar patterns of attention gain, with a substantial increase in the higher layers. However, the magnitude of the gain was larger in CLIP-Large.

2. **DeiT**: The DeiT model demonstrated a dramatic difference between learned prompts and red circle baselines, with learned prompts achieving much higher attention gain in the higher layers.

3. **DINOv2**: DINOv2 showed a unique pattern where both learned and manual prompts had minimal effect in middle layers but significant impact in the final layers.

4. **SigLIP**: SigLIP exhibited a pattern similar to CLIP but with distinctive peaks in specific layers.

These differences highlight the importance of learning model-specific prompts rather than relying on one-size-fits-all manual designs. The authors found that the optimal prompt appearance varied significantly across models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Conclusion and Impact

This research makes several important contributions to the field of visual prompting and Vision Transformers:

1. It introduces a self-supervised framework for learning visual prompts that can effectively guide the attention of pre-trained Vision Transformers.

2. It demonstrates that learned prompts outperform manually designed ones, especially for certain model architectures.

3. It provides insights into how different ViT architectures respond to visual prompts, revealing model-specific behaviors.

4. It shows that learned visual prompts can improve performance on downstream tasks, including keypoint naming and referring expression recognition.

The implications of this work extend beyond the immediate applications demonstrated. The ability to guide model attention through learned visual prompts opens up possibilities for:

- Improving model interpretability by highlighting regions important for decision-making
- Enhancing model performance on tasks requiring fine-grained attention
- Adapting pre-trained models to new domains without extensive fine-tuning
- Developing more effective human-AI interaction methods through visual communication

As the field of Vision Transformers continues to evolve, techniques for guiding model attention will become increasingly important. This research provides a foundation for future work in this direction, offering a data-driven approach to visual prompting that can adapt to different model architectures and application scenarios.
