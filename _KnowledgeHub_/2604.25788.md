# KinDER: A Physical Reasoning Benchmark for Robot Learning and Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> KinDER establishes a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating robot physical reasoning by isolating core kinematic and dynamic challenges across 25 procedurally generated environments. The evaluation of 13 diverse robotic methods reveals persistent gaps in current approaches for handling complex physical interactions and combinatorial constraints.

## Problem

- Robotic systems require robust physical reasoning to interact with the world, but existing benchmarks often entangle this with other complex factors like perception or language.
- A standardized, dedicated evaluation framework was lacking to isolate and systematically compare physical reasoning capabilities across different robotic paradigms.
- Current benchmarks are often application-driven, making it difficult to measure targeted improvements in a robot's understanding of kinematic and dynamic constraints.

## Method

- Introduced **KinDER**, a benchmark with **25 procedurally generated environments** (==KinDERGarden==) categorized into 2D/3D kinematic/dynamic settings isolating ==five core physical reasoning challenges==.
- Provided ==KinDERGym==, an open-source Python library with Gymnasium API compatibility, object-centric states, parameterized skills, and teleoperation interfaces.
- Established ==KinDERBench==, evaluating **13 baselines** (planning, learning, foundation models) via multi-metric analysis across 8 representative environments.

## Results

- Bilevel Planning achieved highest average success rate (**0.57**) due to extensive symbolic knowledge engineering, with higher inference times.
- Foundation Models with in-context examples (LLMCon, VLMCon) reached **0.43** average vs. **0.34** zero-shot.
- Standard RL (PPO, SAC) and MBRL baselines performed poorly with success rates of **0.02 to 0.13**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current LLM and VLM planning approaches struggle to effectively utilize provided skills and reason over complex action sequences, indicating a need for improved symbolic reasoning integration.
> - Model-based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) suffers from unreliable learned transition models, while standard RL methods struggle with long-horizon tasks and sparse rewards, highlighting limitations in current model learning and reward design.
> - Imitation Learning methods, especially VLA, exhibit surprising robustness and out-of-distribution generalization, suggesting their potential for acquiring transferable physical reasoning skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robotic systems capable of interacting with the physical world necessitates advanced physical reasoning capabilities. This involves understanding and predicting the kinematic and dynamic constraints imposed by the robot's own morphology, the environment, and the specific task requirements. These constraints are often complex and intertwined, making tasks that appear simple semantically challenging for robots. For example, storing a book on a shelf requires not only grasping and moving, but also reasoning about potential obstacles, regrasping needs, available space, and collision avoidance.

Despite the recognized importance of physical reasoning, the robotics community lacks a unified consensus on the state of the art in this area. Existing benchmarks for robot learning and planning often address broader challenges such as task diversity, long-horizon decision-making, or language grounding. Others focus on application-specific domains, such as home assistance, where physical reasoning is conflated with other factors like perception and language understanding. This entanglement makes it difficult to isolate and evaluate physical reasoning abilities specifically.

Furthermore, physical reasoning has been approached from diverse perspectives across different subfields of robotics. Classical methods, such as Task and Motion Planning (TAMP), utilize explicit models and optimization for constraint satisfaction. Model-free techniques, including Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL), rely on data to implicitly learn solutions. More recently, Foundation Model (FM)-based approaches, leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), combine explicit symbolic reasoning with implicit understanding from pretraining. The absence of a standardized framework for evaluating these disparate approaches hinders systematic comparison and progress in integrating their complementary strengths.

KinDER addresses these limitations by providing a targeted benchmark specifically designed for Kinematic and Dynamic Embodied Reasoning. It aims to disentangle physical reasoning challenges from other complexities like perception and language, offering a focused environment for evaluation. The benchmark facilitates direct comparisons across classical planning, modern learning, and foundation model paradigms, thereby aiming to clarify the state of the art and accelerate advancements in robot physical reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce KinDER, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating physical reasoning capabilities in robot learning and planning. The motivation stems from the fundamental challenge that robotic systems face when interacting with the physical world: the necessity to reason effectively about kinematic and dynamic constraints. These constraints arise from the robot's own physical embodiment, the physical laws governing its environment, and the specific demands of the task at hand.

The paper identifies several key aspects motivating the development of KinDER:

- **Pervasiveness of Physical Reasoning:** Physical reasoning is a core competency for broadly capable robots. Tasks that appear simple in human terms often require complex physical reasoning to execute in an embodied system. Without this capability, robots struggle with basic manipulation, navigation, and interaction.
- **Difficulty in Measuring Progress:** The current landscape lacks a consensus on how to effectively measure and evaluate physical reasoning in robotics. No single task is sufficient, and even procedurally-generated task variations often fail to capture the full generality of physical reasoning challenges. Existing benchmarks either cover broader robotic challenges or are application-focused, making it difficult to isolate and assess physical reasoning specifically, independent of factors like perception, language understanding, or domain-specific complexities.
- **Disparity Across Methodologies:** Physical reasoning has been studied in different subfields of robotics using distinct approaches, including Task and Motion Planning (TAMP), Reinforcement Learning (RL), Imitation Learning (IL), and Foundation Model (FM)-based methods. The lack of a common evaluation platform makes it challenging to compare the strengths and weaknesses of these diverse paradigms and to foster research into their integration.

To address these motivations, KinDER is designed with three main contributions:

1. **KinDERGarden:** A collection of 25 simulated environments with procedurally generated variations. These environments are specifically designed to isolate and represent five core physical reasoning challenges:
 - **Basic Spatial Relations:** Understanding and actively manipulating objects based on their relative positions (e.g., placing a fork to the left of a plate).
 - **Nonprehensile Multi-Object Manipulation:** Performing actions beyond simple pick-and-place, such as pushing, sweeping, or scooping multiple objects simultaneously, leveraging whole-arm or whole-body contact.
 - **Tool Use:** Utilizing objects as tools to manipulate other objects, understanding common tool affordances, and improvising with abstract mechanisms.
 - **Combinatorial Geometric Constraints:** Navigating and manipulating objects in confined, cluttered spaces while avoiding collisions, where the number of constraints grows polynomially with the number of objects (e.g., packing or retrieving items).
 - **Dynamic Constraints:** Controlling and stabilizing dynamical systems, such as carrying liquids without spilling, or tossing objects, requiring adherence to velocity limits and task-implicit dynamic requirements.
2. **KinDERGym:** A Python library featuring a Gymnasium-compatible API for the environments, parameterized skills and concepts, multiple teleoperation interfaces, and pre-collected demonstration datasets. This component aims to lower the barrier to entry for researchers and facilitate the collection of further data.
3. **KinDERBench:** A standardized evaluation suite that includes 13 pre-implemented baselines from TAMP, RL, IL, and FM-based approaches. This enables systematic and direct comparison of different methods.

Furthermore, the work includes real-to-sim-to-real experiments to assess the correspondence between simulated environments and real-world physical interaction, providing validation for the benchmark's relevance. By open-sourcing all contributions, KinDER aims to establish a clear foundation for advancing research in robot physical reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The KinDER benchmark is structured around three core components: KinDERGarden for environments, KinDERGym for software accessibility, and KinDERBench for evaluation.

#### 3.1. KinDERGarden: Environments

KinDERGarden comprises 25 procedurally generated environments, categorized into Kinematic2D, Dynamic2D, Kinematic3D, and Dynamic3D, designed to cover the five core physical reasoning challenges.

- **General Environment Structure:** All environments adhere to the Gymnasium API, providing observation and action spaces, a `reset` function for procedural generation, and a `step` function returning observations, sparse rewards (negative one per step until success), and termination indicators. A key design choice is the use of object-centric states, mapping object names to real-valued feature vectors, allowing for variation in the number of objects. For learning-based methods, RGB image observations or fixed-dimensionality vector states (for constant-object variants) are also provided.
- **Kinematic2D Environments (6 environments):** These environments focus on tool use and combinatorial geometric constraints at a high abstraction level. They model transitions based on object poses and robot configurations without considering velocities or accelerations. Implemented in pure Python using 2D shapes, they feature a circular base robot, a 1D extendable arm, and a rectangular vacuum end effector that rigidly attaches objects. Actions are constrained to small configuration changes, with state reversion on collision. Examples include Obstruction2D and StickButton2D.
- **Dynamic2D Environments (4 environments):** These environments focus on nonprehensile multi-object manipulation and tool use, incorporating velocities and accelerations using the Pymunk physics backend. They feature a similar robot with a circular base and a 1D extendable arm, but with a two-fingered gripper for contact-rich dynamics studies. An example is DynObstruction2D, where a robot might push obstacles while grasping a target, differing from the kinematic version where only pick-and-place is possible.
- **Kinematic3D Environments (5 environments):** These environments address spatial relations and combinatorial geometric constraints. They are kinematic, similar to Kinematic2D, using PyBullet for object modeling, forward kinematics, and collision checking. All environments use a TidyBot++ mobile base with a 7DOF Kinova Gen3 arm and Robotiq 2F-85 gripper. Gripper closure rigidly attaches objects. Actions are constrained configuration changes, with collision detection reverting states. Examples include BaseMotion3D and Transport3D.
- **Dynamic3D Environments (10 environments):** This category covers all five core physical reasoning challenges, modeling velocities and accelerations using the MuJoCo physics backend. They employ the same TidyBot++ mobile manipulator. Grasping is dynamic, not rigid. Environment configurations are managed via configuration files, drawing inspiration from the BDDL specification language for procedural task generation and leveraging assets from RoboCasa and MimicLabs. Examples include Shelf3D and SweepIntoDrawer3D.

#### 3.2. KinDERGym: Accessible Software

KinDERGym is a pip-installable Python package designed for ease of use, developed with software engineering standards including continuous integration and unit tests.

- **Parameterized Skills and Concepts:** The package provides utilities for defining hierarchical planning components. Skills are implemented as options with PDDL operators and samplers, allowing for object and continuous parameters. Concepts are relational predicates with classifiers that ground in object-centric states, used for skill preconditions and effects, and contributing to a two-level scene graph.
- **Teleoperation Interfaces and Demonstrations:** KinDERGym includes multiple interfaces for human demonstration collection. 2D environments support mouse-and-keyboard or PS5 controller input. 3D environments offer an iPhone web app (leveraging gyroscope/accelerometer) or a Meta Quest 3S virtual reality headset interface. Demonstrations for 10 environments (≥100 each) are provided, generated through a combination of teleoperation and planning.

#### 3.3. KinDERBench: Baselines and Metrics

KinDERBench provides a standardized evaluation suite with 13 baselines tested on 8 representative environments (2 from each KinDERGarden category).

- **Environments:** Motion2D, StickButton2D, DynObstruction2D, DynPushPullHook2D, BaseMotion3D, Transport3D, Shelf3D, and SweepIntoDrawer3D were selected for evaluation, representing varying difficulties and core challenges.
- **Baselines:** The 13 baselines span various paradigms:
 - **Task and Motion Planning:** Bilevel Planning (BP) (search-then-sample TAMP).
 - **Foundation Models:** LLM Planning (LLMPlan), VLM Planning (VLMPlan), LLM In-context (LLMCon), VLM In-context (VLMCon) (all using GPT-5.2).
 - **Model-based Learning/Control:** Model Predictive Control (MPC), Model-based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL).
 - **Imitation Learning:** Generative Diffusion Planning (GSC), Diffusion Policy (DP), DP + Environment States (DPES), Finetuned VLA (π0.5 VLA).
 - **Reinforcement Learning:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Soft Actor-Critic (SAC).
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Success Rate (SR):** Percentage of successful episodes.
 - **Cumulative Rewards (Rwd):** Sum of rewards for successful episodes (rewards are -1 per step).
 - **Inference Time (Inf-Time):** Wall-clock time per episode (seconds).
 - **Engineering Cost:** A qualitative measure of effort required to adapt a method to a new environment.

Evaluations were conducted over 5 random seeds with 50 episodes per seed. Additional experiments explored out-of-distribution generalization for IL baselines and the scaling of bilevel planning. Real-to-sim-to-real experiments were conducted using a TidyBot++ robot in the Shelf3D environment, using an overhead camera for localization and object pose estimation, planning in simulation, and executing in the real world.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The KinDERBench evaluation provided empirical insights into the performance of various physical reasoning approaches across a range of environments. The benchmarks were conducted over 5 random seeds with 50 evaluation episodes per seed, with results indicating notable strengths and weaknesses of current methodologies.

#### 4.1. Benchmark Results Overview

The overall success rates (SR) across the evaluated environments showed varying performance: Bilevel Planning (BP) achieved the highest average SR (0.57), followed by LLM In-context (LLMCon) and VLM In-context (VLMCon) at 0.43 each. LLM Planning (LLMPlan) and VLM Planning (VLMPlan) recorded 0.34. Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Finetuned VLA achieved 0.32. Generative Skill Chaining (GSC) was at 0.26, Diffusion Policy with Environment States (DPES) at 0.25, Diffusion Policy (DP) at 0.24, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) at 0.13, Model-based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) at 0.08, and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) at 0.02. This general trend suggests that methods involving higher engineering costs or longer inference times often correlate with improved success rates.

#### 4.2. Detailed Observations

- **Classical Planning vs. Foundation Models:** A performance gap was observed between Bilevel Planning (BP) and the LLM/VLM planning baselines, particularly in more complex environments. This indicates potential for further development in foundation model-based planning to match the reasoning capabilities of engineered classical planners. The inclusion of in-context examples significantly improved the performance of both LLM and VLM planners (LLMCon/VLMCon vs. LLMPlan/VLMPlan). However, the comparable performance between LLMPlan/VLMPlan suggests that the VLM component, while receiving image input, did not consistently leverage these visual observations to a meaningful degree beyond the object-centric states already provided.

- **Imitation Learning Performance:** Imitation learning baselines, including Diffusion Policy (DP), DPES, and Finetuned VLA, demonstrated notable performance, particularly considering they do not rely on parameterized skills. The Finetuned VLA baseline achieved a non-trivial success rate (0.43) in the DynPushPullHook2D task with 5 obstacles, which requires both tool use and nonprehensile multi-object manipulation. This was unexpected, given that the 2D rendering and physics of the environment differ from the VLA's pretraining data. Similarly, DP (0.14) and DPES (0.04) showed some success in the long-horizon, multi-stage SweepIntoDrawer3D task, which involves opening a drawer, grasping a sweeper, and then sweeping multiple objects. Analysis of subtask success rates revealed that DP outperformed DPES in this environment, suggesting that DPES did not effectively leverage the additional object-centric states provided.

- **Model-based and Reinforcement Learning:** The Model Predictive Control (MPC) baseline performed effectively, especially given its reliance on sparse reward functions, which the authors attribute to its predictive sampling trajectory optimization. In contrast, the Model-based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) baseline, despite using the same planner as MPC, performed worse, indicating that its learned neural transition model was not sufficiently reliable. Standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) baselines (PPO and SAC) generally performed well only in short-horizon tasks. This is consistent with expectations for methods using sparse rewards and lacking strong inductive biases. Additional experiments showed that dense rewards could improve PPO's performance in some cases, but overall RL success rates remained low across many tasks.

#### 4.3. Additional Results

- **Out-of-Distribution Generalization:** Experiments on DynObstruction2D evaluated the generalization capabilities of imitation learning baselines (DP, VLA) to unseen numbers of obstacles. Both baselines, trained with one obstacle, demonstrated robust performance when tested with 0, 2, and 3 obstacles, albeit with some degradation. The Finetuned VLA exhibited particular robustness, potentially due to its pretraining.
- **Scaling Bilevel Planning:** An evaluation of bilevel planning in the StickButton2D environment showed that as the number of buttons increased, the success rate substantially decreased, and planning inference time increased. For instance, increasing from 1 to 10 buttons led to success rate dropping from 0.99 to 0.00 and inference time increasing from 1.51s to 38.39s. This highlights a limitation in scaling classical planning methods and suggests an area for future research involving learning to enhance planning efficiency.
- **Real-World Validation:** A real-to-sim-to-real experiment was conducted using a TidyBot++ mobile manipulator in the Shelf3D environment. Object-centric states were detected from real-world observations using an overhead camera and object localization. Motion plans generated in the KinDER simulator were then executed on the physical robot. This experiment served to demonstrate the correspondence between the simulated environments and real-world physical reasoning challenges, while also indicating the potential for KinDER to support future real-to-sim-to-real transfer research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

KinDER represents a step toward clarifying and advancing the state of the art in robot physical reasoning. By providing a standardized, open-source benchmark, it addresses several critical gaps in the existing research landscape.

The primary significance of KinDER lies in its ability to enable systematic comparison across diverse robotic paradigms. Researchers working with classical Task and Motion Planning (TAMP), modern Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL), and emerging Foundation Model (FM)-based approaches can now evaluate their methods on a common set of environments. This facilitates the identification of the strengths and weaknesses of each paradigm in addressing specific physical reasoning challenges, fostering a more informed development of hybrid or integrated approaches.

The benchmark's design, specifically its focus on disentangling physical reasoning from other complexities like perception and language understanding, allows for targeted evaluation. The 25 procedurally generated environments, covering five core physical reasoning challenges (Basic Spatial Relations, Nonprehensile Multi-Object Manipulation, Tool Use, Combinatorial Geometric Constraints, and Dynamic Constraints), provide a diverse yet focused testbed. The empirical results, showing that existing methods struggle with many environments, indicate substantial gaps in current approaches. This outcome directly highlights areas requiring further research and innovation in physical reasoning for robotics.

KinDERGym, with its Gymnasium-compatible API, parameterized skills, teleoperation interfaces, and demonstration datasets, contributes significantly to accessibility and reproducibility. Its robust software engineering standards lower the barrier for researchers to engage with the benchmark, collect new data, and implement novel methods. The inclusion of demonstrations is particularly valuable for data-driven learning approaches.

The real-to-sim-to-real experiments provide a crucial validation point, demonstrating that KinDER's simulated challenges correspond to actual physical reasoning problems encountered by real robots. This correspondence enhances the benchmark's credibility and suggests its potential as a platform for developing methods that transfer effectively to the physical world. The limitations acknowledged by the authors, such as aspects of real-world physics not fully captured, inherent stochasticity, and limited robot embodiments, also serve as explicit directions for future work in extending the benchmark's scope and fidelity.

In summary, KinDER is positioned to accelerate research by:
- **Standardizing Evaluation:** Providing a common platform for comparing disparate methods.
- **Identifying Gaps:** Clearly demonstrating where current methods fall short in physical reasoning.
- **Facilitating Research:** Offering accessible tools, environments, and data for new developments.
- **Promoting Collaboration:** Being open-source and encouraging community contributions.

The findings, particularly regarding the performance of different methods and their scaling properties, are expected to guide future research directions, such as improving the reliability of learned transition models, developing more robust RL algorithms for sparse reward settings, enhancing the reasoning capabilities of foundation models, and integrating learning with planning for better scalability.
