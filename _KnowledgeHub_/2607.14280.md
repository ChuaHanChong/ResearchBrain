# DiMaS: Distribution Matching for Steering Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A new Distribution-Matching Steering (DiMaS) strategy enables fine-grained control over robot behaviors, such as end-effector speed and vertical displacement, in flow-matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, achieving consistent and statistically significant modulation with robust task success preservation. The research also explains the limitations of linear steering methods in this domain.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, particularly those using flow-matching for continuous actions, lacked effective post-hoc methods for fine-grained behavioral control.
- Classical linear representation steering techniques, successful in language and vision-language models, proved inconsistent and unreliable when applied to complex, continuous behavioral features in visuomotor tasks.
- There was a limited understanding of the internal representation structure of VLA models, hindering the development of effective steering mechanisms.

## Method

- **DiMaS** (Distribution-Matching Steering) learns an ==optimal transport map== to transform internal representations from a "feature-absent" distribution to a "feature-present" distribution.
- The method partitions VLA internal representations based on ==empirical quantiles== of a continuous scalar behavioral feature (e.g., speed) to define source and target distributions.
- During inference, a ==binary classifier== gates the intervention, and an ==interpolation coefficient== (alpha) allows for tunable steering strength, ensuring task success is largely preserved.

## Results

- DiMaS consistently achieved statistically significant modulation of end-effector speed and vertical displacement on SmolVLA and pi_0.5 models across various LIBERO benchmark suites.
- The method preserved task success rates effectively (e.g., a **6%** drop for significant speed reduction on long-horizon tasks), outperforming linear and prompt-based baselines which were often inconsistent.
- Steering effects generalized to unseen tasks within suites and across disjoint suites, particularly for decreasing interventions, demonstrating that the learned transport captures generalized behavioral structure.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Behavioral features in flow-matching VLA models, while linearly separable in representation space, are not effectively steerable via simple linear shifts due to complex, non-linear latent space geometry.
> - Learning an optimal transport map between feature distributions (DiMaS) enables consistent and statistically significant control over continuous behaviors, unlike traditional linear or prompt-based methods.
> - Interventions applied in the action expert's deeper layers and later flow-matching steps are most effective, and training DiMaS on diverse tasks enhances its transferability and robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a developing area within the field of artificial intelligence and robotics, specifically focusing on the control and interpretability of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The advent of foundation models, such as Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), has catalyzed advancements in robotic policies, leading to the formulation of VLA models. These models integrate visual observations and natural language instructions to predict low-level robot actions. Initially, VLA models often employed autoregressive generation of discrete action tokens. More recently, the field has progressed to state-of-the-art systems that pair VLMs with flow-matching networks, enabling the prediction of continuous actions through an iterative denoising process.

Despite progress in action generation, the area of *representation steering* in VLA models remains largely unexplored. Representation steering involves post-hoc methods to control specific attributes of generated outputs by intervening on the internal representations of generative models. This technique is established in LLMs and VLMs for tasks such as alignment or style control. Existing methods typically rely on the "linear representation hypothesis" (LRH), which postulates that semantic features are encoded as linear directions in the model's activation space, allowing for simple additive interventions.

This paper identifies a gap: these classical linear steering methods, effective for language and vision-language tasks, have not been thoroughly evaluated or adapted for flow-matching VLA models, which produce continuous actions and trajectories. Previous work on VLA interpretability and steering has explored autoregressive VLAs (Häon et al., 2025) or proposed linear shifts for feature control (Buurmeijer et al., 2026), but with limitations such as requiring intervention pauses to maintain task success. This research positions itself by explicitly targeting flow-matching VLAs and proposing a novel, non-linear steering approach that aims to overcome the limitations of linear methods in the visuomotor domain, while preserving task success. The paper also contributes to the understanding of the underlying representation structure in VLA models, elucidating why linear steering approaches are insufficient.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate post-hoc methods for fine-grained behavioral control in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, specifically those utilizing flow-matching action experts. This involves the ability to govern *how* a robot performs a task by intervening on its internal representations, rather than solely focusing on *what* the robot does.

The motivation for this objective stems from several factors:

- **Application Value:** For any deployed generative model, the capacity for fine-grained control over its outputs offers substantial practical utility. In robotics, this translates to enabling users to directly shape specific attributes of a robot's predicted trajectory or actions (e.g., speed, height of movement), enhancing flexibility and adaptability in real-world scenarios.
- **Underexplored Capability in VLAs:** While representation steering is a well-established tool in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for controlling abstract features, its application to VLA models, particularly those employing flow-matching for continuous action generation, has been underexplored. The unique characteristics of continuous action spaces and the iterative denoising process of flow-matching networks present distinct challenges compared to steering discrete token generation.
- **Limitations of Classical Steering Methods:** Existing representation steering techniques, largely based on the linear representation hypothesis (LRH), assume that behavioral features are encoded as simple linear directions in the latent space. The authors hypothesize that these classical linear methods may be inadequate for the complex, continuous behavioral features in visuomotor settings. Their empirical analysis and investigation into the internal representation structure serve to validate this hypothesis and motivate the need for a more sophisticated steering strategy.
- **Generalizability and Robustness:** A key motivation is to ensure that any developed steering mechanism not only controls the targeted features but also generalizes robustly to different tasks and scenarios beyond those used for its calibration. This involves understanding the conditions under which behavioral control transfers effectively and where it may weaken, providing insights into the robustness of representation-level interventions in VLAs.
- **Interpretablity and Understanding:** Beyond mere control, the work is motivated by a desire to gain a deeper understanding of how VLA models encode and process behavioral features within their internal representations. By analyzing the structure of the action expert, the research aims to explain the shortcomings of traditional linear steering and inform the design of more effective control mechanisms. This contributes to the broader field of mechanistic interpretability in advanced AI models.

In summary, the research aims to bridge the gap in fine-grained behavioral control for flow-matching VLAs, address the limitations of existing linear steering paradigms, and provide foundational insights into the representation of behavioral features within these complex robotic policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named DiMaS (Distribution-Matching Steering), is specifically designed for flow-matching-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. It intervenes on internal representations by learning a mapping between distributions of these representations.

**VLA Architecture:**
The method considers a generic VLA architecture $f = (f_V, f_A)$, comprising a VLM backbone ($f_V$) and an action expert network ($f_A$). At each timestep $t$, the VLA receives an observation $x_t = (I_t, T, x_{s_t})$ (image, text instruction, proprioceptive state) and predicts low-level robot actions $y_t$. The VLM backbone processes these multimodal inputs and its representations condition the action expert $f_A$. The action expert is trained with a flow-matching objective to iteratively refine noisy action tokens $a_{0,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ across $M$ denoising steps. The final action chunk $y_t$ is derived from $a_{M,t}$. DiMaS primarily intervenes on residual stream representations, denoted $h_p^\ell(x_t)$ for the VLM and $h_{p,m}^\ell(x_t)$ for the action expert (where $m$ is the denoising step).

**DiMaS Learning/Training Phase:**

1. **Representations and Behavioral Feature Extraction:**
 - Residual-stream representations $h_{p,m}^\ell(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are extracted from a specific layer $\ell^*$ and flow-matching step $m$ of the action expert $f_A$ across all rollouts.
 - Each representation $h_i$ is paired with a continuous scalar behavioral feature $\phi_i = \phi(a_i)$, derived from the corresponding predicted action $a_i$. For example, end-effector speed is the magnitude of translational displacement.

2. **Source and Target Distributions ($D^-, D^+$):**
 - The extracted representations are partitioned into two sets based on the scalar feature value using empirical quantiles:
 - $D^-$ (source distribution): representations $h_i$ where $\phi_i \le q_\tau$ (lower quantile).
 - $D^+$ (target distribution): representations $h_i$ where $\phi_i > q_{1-\tau}$ (upper quantile).
 - By restricting to tails ($\tau = 0.25$ and $1-\tau = 0.75$), the method aims to create well-separated populations for feature-absent and feature-present behaviors.

3. **Steering as an Optimal Transport Map:**
 - Instead of a fixed linear shift (as in classical linear steering), DiMaS learns a transport map $T_{(\ell)}^{(m)}$ that moves representations from the source distribution $D^-$ to the target distribution $D^+$.
 - This map is learned by minimizing the Kantorovich optimal transport objective $W_2^2(D^-, D^+)$. This objective finds the most efficient way to move "mass" from one distribution to another, respecting their full geometry.
 - In practice, this is a discrete optimal transport problem solved using the low-rank Sinkhorn algorithm, with entropy regularization, to handle the high dimensionality and finite empirical samples.

**DiMaS Test-time Intervention (Inference):**

1. **Feature Filter (Gating):**
 - For a given test-time representation $h = h_{p,m}^\ell(x_t)$, a binary classifier $g_{(\ell)}^{(m)}: \mathbb{R}^d \to \{0,1\}$ (implemented as a linear SVM trained to separate $D^-$ from $D^+$) is used.
 - Steering is applied only if the classifier predicts that the feature is absent (i.e., $g_{(\ell)}^{(m)}(h) = 1$, indicating $h$ is in $D^-$). If the feature is already present, the representation is left unchanged.

2. **Interpolation Strategy:**
 - To balance feature control and task success, an interpolation coefficient $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ is introduced.
 - First, the input representation $h$ is projected onto the source distribution $D^-$ using a nearest-neighbor projection $P(h) = \arg\min_{z \in D^-} \|z - h\|$.
 - Then, the learned transport map is applied: $T_{(\ell)}^{(m)} \circ P(h)$.
 - The final steered representation is a convex combination: $h \leftarrow (1-\alpha) h + \alpha (T_{(\ell)}^{(m)} \circ P(h))$.
 - $\alpha=0$ yields the original representation, $\alpha=1$ applies full transport, and intermediate values provide continuous modulation. This "interpolation knob" allows users to fine-tune steering strength and minimize potential drops in task success rate.

**Hyperparameters and Implementation Details:**
- **Quantiles:** Lower quantile $q_\tau=0.25$, upper quantile $q_{1-\tau}=0.75$.
- **Classifier:** Linear SVM with regularization $C=0.1$.
- **Optimal Transport:** Low-rank Sinkhorn algorithm, regularization $\epsilon=10^{-4}$, max iterations 5000.
- **Steering Layer:** Applied at the second-to-last layer of the action expert.
- **FM Steps:** Intervention applied at all flow-matching steps.
- **Computational Cost:** Optimal transport map computation is done once offline (e.g., ~85 minutes for a suite). Inference-time intervention is lightweight, adding ~0.62 ms per flow-matching step, which is deemed acceptable for real-time deployment.

**Baselines for Comparison:**
- **Mean-difference steering (VLM Mean):** Applies a constant additive shift ($ \mu_+ - \mu_-$) to VLM representations.
- **Regression-based steering (VLM Regression, FM Regression):** Fits a linear regressor to predict feature value and shifts representation along the regressor's direction to achieve a target feature quantile. Applied to VLM or FM action expert.
- **Prompt injection:** Modulates behavior by rephrasing natural language instructions (e.g., "go faster," "do this slowly").

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates DiMaS on two flow-matching VLA models, SmolVLA and $\pi_{0.5}$, using the LIBERO benchmark (suites: Object, Spatial, Goal, LIBERO-10). The target behavioral features are end-effector motion speed and vertical displacement (z-displacement). Metrics include Success Rate (SR), mean feature value, and statistical significance via paired t-tests.

#### 4.1. Can Representation Steering Control Behavior?

- **Inconsistency of Baselines:** Linear steering (mean-difference, regression-based) and prompt-based baselines generally perform inconsistently and unreliably. They often fail to shift the target feature significantly, or sometimes shift it in the unintended direction (e.g., increasing intervention leading to a decrease).
- **DiMaS Effectiveness:** DiMaS demonstrates consistent and statistically significant modulation of target features in both intended directions (increasing and decreasing).
 - **Speed Control:** DiMaS effectively controls speed on both SmolVLA and $\pi_{0.5}$. Task success rate is largely preserved during speed modulation.
 - **Z-Displacement Control:** DiMaS effectively controls vertical displacement on $\pi_{0.5}$. However, controlling z-displacement often incurs a greater drop in success rate, which is attributed to this feature being more intrinsically tied to task completion (e.g., lifting objects).
 - **Asymmetry:** Decreasing interventions (slowing down, lowering end-effector) tend to produce more statistically significant shifts compared to increasing interventions.
- **Generality Across Models:** DiMaS proves effective across both $\pi_{0.5}$ (larger model, predicts 10 actions) and SmolVLA (compact model, predicts 1 action). $\pi_{0.5}$ generally achieves stronger results, possibly due to its smoother, less dynamic trajectories making it more robust to intervention.

#### 4.2. Understanding Steering Generalization

The study investigates steering transferability across four settings with increasing dissimilarity between training and evaluation tasks:

1. **Setting 1 (Held-out initial states of the same task):** Steering learned and evaluated within a single task's variations.
2. **Setting 2 (Held-out initial states across tasks in a suite):** Steering learned from multiple tasks within a suite, evaluated on held-out initial states.
3. **Setting 3 (Held-out tasks within a suite):** Steering learned from a subset of tasks in a suite, evaluated on unseen tasks within the same suite.
4. **Setting 4 (Transfer across disjoint suites):** Steering learned from one LIBERO suite, evaluated on a different, disjoint suite.

- **Aggregating Tasks Helps (Setting 1 vs. 2):** Learning DiMaS from a diverse set of tasks (Setting 2) leads to more consistent and stable behavioral shifts than learning from a single task (Setting 1). This is attributed to capturing a broader structure of the target behavior across representation space.
- **Persistence on Held-out and Disjoint Tasks (Settings 3-4):** DiMaS's effectiveness does not collapse on unseen tasks (Setting 3) or across disjoint suites (Setting 4). For $\pi_{0.5}$, decreasing speed remained significant across suites in Setting 3, and the effect persisted in the intended direction even for the hardest cross-suite transfer (Setting 4 to LIBERO-Goal), though sometimes with reduced statistical significance. This suggests the learned transport captures feature-related structure rather than task-specific artifacts.
- **Weakness on Diverse Suites:** LIBERO-Goal, being the most behaviorally diverse suite, exhibits weaker steering effects. Shifts are generally smaller and less frequently statistically significant, indicating a challenge for generalization when task heterogeneity is high.

#### 4.3. Steering on Longer-Horizon Tasks

- DiMaS successfully produces a downward shift in speed on LIBERO-10, a suite with substantially longer-horizon tasks. For $\alpha=0.5$, a significant mean speed decrease is observed (p < 0.01) with a manageable drop in success rate (96% to 90%), demonstrating effectiveness over extended horizons.

#### 4.4. Investigating Internal Structure in VLAs (Why Linear Steering Fails)

- **Linear Separability:** An analysis of SmolVLA's internal representations reveals high linear separability between high-speed and low-speed examples, particularly in the action expert's deeper layers and later flow-matching steps (accuracy near 100%). VLM layers show lower separability (max 87%). This finding motivates intervening in the action expert's deeper layers.
- **Linear Shift is Insufficient:** Qualitative visualization using PCA shows that while representations are linearly separable, a simple linear shift (even a large one) only translates the source distribution, failing to match the *shape* and geometry of the target distribution. This inability to match the full distributions explains why linear steering methods are ineffective in controlling complex behavioral features in VLAs, motivating DiMaS's distribution-matching approach.

#### 4.5. Analyzing DiMaS

- **Interpolation Factor ($\alpha$):** Ablation studies confirm that $\alpha$ provides a controllable trade-off. Larger $\alpha$ values lead to stronger feature modulation (e.g., lower speed) but also a greater reduction in task success rate. $\alpha=0.5$ is identified as a favorable operating point, achieving substantial feature control while largely preserving task success. $\pi_{0.5}$ is found to be more robust to $\alpha$ changes than SmolVLA.
- **Qualitative Results:** Example end-effector trajectories demonstrate that DiMaS steering consistently produces the intended changes in behavior, such as visibly smaller vertical displacement. Failure cases are observed when suppressing a behavior crucial for task completion.
- **Steering-Layer Ablation:** The choice of intervention layer within the action expert significantly impacts effectiveness. Later layers (e.g., L15-L17) are most effective at inducing the intended behavioral shifts and preserving success rates. Earlier and middle layers are less effective, sometimes shifting features in the wrong direction or leading to significant success rate drops. This reinforces the decision to intervene in late layers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation and AI interpretability by addressing a crucial gap in fine-grained behavioral control for state-of-the-art Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

- **Advancement in VLA Control:** The paper introduces DiMaS, a novel Distribution-Matching Steering strategy tailored for flow-matching VLAs. This method effectively enables precise and reliable control over specific behavioral attributes, such as end-effector speed and vertical displacement. This represents a substantial advancement over previous approaches that either lacked consistency, failed to preserve task success, or were limited to autoregressive VLA architectures.
- **Improved Interpretability and Understanding:** The comprehensive analysis of internal representation structure within VLAs provides foundational insights into *why* classical linear steering methods, prevalent in LLMs and VLMs, are insufficient for visuomotor tasks. By demonstrating that behavioral features are linearly decodable but not linearly steerable, the research highlights the complex, non-linear geometry of VLA latent spaces, guiding the design of more appropriate intervention strategies like DiMaS. This contributes to the broader effort of understanding and opening up complex foundation models.
- **Enhanced Generalizability and Robustness:** The detailed evaluation of DiMaS's generalizability across diverse task settings (from held-out initial states to transfer across disjoint suites) provides crucial empirical evidence. The findings indicate that the learned transport map captures fundamental behavioral structure rather than task-specific artifacts, suggesting that DiMaS interventions can maintain effectiveness even in out-of-distribution scenarios. This robustness is critical for deploying controllable robotic policies in varied real-world environments.
- **Practical User Interface for Robot Behavior:** The "interpolation knob" ($\alpha$) offered by DiMaS provides a normalized and interpretable mechanism for users to modulate the strength of a behavioral intervention, allowing for a continuous trade-off between control intensity and task success rate. This user-friendly control interface could simplify the adaptation of VLA policies to specific operational requirements or user preferences without requiring complex re-training or prompt engineering.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for future exploration:
 - **Selective Intervention:** Moving beyond applying interventions at all timesteps to learning *when* to intervene (e.g., only during specific phases of a task) could lead to even smoother and more efficient control.
 - **Finer Correspondence in Action Chunking:** For models that output action chunks, a more precise mapping between individual representations and their corresponding output actions could refine the learned transport and improve control accuracy.
 - **Broader Applicability:** The underlying mechanism of DiMaS, applicable to any feature observable from representations, suggests its potential extension to new tasks, robot embodiments, and more abstract behavioral features, establishing representation-level control as a versatile interface for shaping robot behavior.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the state of behavioral control in VLA models, offers critical insights into their internal workings, and provides a robust, generalizable methodology with substantial potential for enhancing the practical utility and adaptability of robotic systems.
