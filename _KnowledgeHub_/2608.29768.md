# SmoothRL: Online Reinforcement Learning During Asynchronous Execution

> [!summary] Summary
> SmoothRL is an online RL framework that fine-tunes pretrained VLA policies inside the asynchronous inference loop. It partitions each action chunk by frame index into committed, execution and discarded regions under a fixed latency budget, backpropagating value gradients only through the executed region while the critic conditions on the full span. On three real-robot tasks success rises from 8-39 percent to 83-94 percent.

## Problem

- Existing online RL fine-tuning assumes synchronous execution, so policies are optimized under trajectory dynamics that differ from the asynchronous inference loop actually used at deployment.
- Offline RL keeps deployment asynchronous but steers policies only via advantage reweighting or conditioning, never backpropagating value gradients into the parameters that generate executed actions.
- Prior asynchronous online RL confines optimization to a frozen diffusion policy's latent noise space, bounding attainable gains by the decoder and blocking direct reuse of human interventions.

## Method

- ==Chunk role partitioning== under a fixed latency budget n: every chunk splits by frame index into committed [0,n), execution [n,2n) and discarded [2n,H) regions, making boundaries deterministic.
- ==Value-gradient truncation== sends grad-a Q only through the execution region, while the critic ingests the committed prefix as concurrent-decision state augmentation under a ==chunk-skip Bellman backup== spanning 2n frames.
- Instantiation on a frozen ==pi-0.5== base with an ==RLT-style RL token==, ==TD3+BC== residual actor, ==REDQ critic ensemble==, velocity/acceleration/jerk ==smoothness penalty==, and raw-action human interventions reused as BC targets.

## Results

- On the 25-DoF Astribot S1 after 250 online rollout episodes, dynamic tossing improved **39 to 94 percent**, pen capping **8 to 83 percent**, box opening **30 to 90 percent**.
- Motion smoothness improved measurably: RMS acceleration and jerk of the right end-effector dropped **52 percent** and **47 percent** respectively during autonomous throwing rollouts versus the base policy.
- Residual teleoperation intervention reached about **80 percent** success on the farthest bins versus about **30 percent** for absolute VR teleoperation; box opening transiently dipped to **20 percent** at 150 episodes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimize a policy under the same execution schedule it will be deployed with; gradients should reach only the actions the environment actually executed.
> - Asynchronous inference turns control into a concurrent decision process, so the in-flight action chunk belongs in the critic's state, not just the observation.
> - Bounded residual corrections on a frozen base cap achievable gains: systematic base-policy bias outside the local trust region stays uncorrectable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards more generalist manipulation capabilities through the development of large robot foundation models, such as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World-Action Models (WAMs). The deployment of these complex models in physical robots necessitates addressing two primary challenges: achieving high task success rates (reliability) and ensuring smooth, real-time execution.

Contemporary VLA and WAM models often exhibit high inference latency due to their scale. To maintain real-time control frequency and avoid pauses in robot motion, modern robot systems commonly employ asynchronous inference combined with action chunking. This approach overlaps policy computation with action execution, effectively hiding latency and enabling continuous control. However, this asynchronous paradigm introduces a discontinuity: newly generated action chunks are based on stale observations, potentially leading to jerky movements or inconsistencies at chunk boundaries. Existing methods to address this include chunk-stitching and trajectory blending techniques, which aim to maintain continuity between successive action chunks during inference or post-processing.

Simultaneously, achieving the precision and robustness required for diverse real-world tasks often relies on online reinforcement learning (RL). Online RL allows policies to adapt and improve from newly generated data. A significant challenge arises because most existing online RL methods, particularly those based on value-gradient paradigms, assume synchronous execution where the policy output directly corresponds to the action executed by the robot. This assumption conflicts with the asynchronous execution strategies employed for deployment. Prior work that attempts online adaptation under asynchronous execution often restricts policy optimization to latent spaces, which can limit the potential for improvement and direct control.

SmoothRL positions itself by directly addressing this mismatch. It aims to integrate gradient-based online RL fine-tuning directly within the asynchronous inference loop used for deployment. This work attempts to reconcile the requirements of online learning (which needs accurate feedback on executed actions) with asynchronous execution (where only a portion of a generated action chunk is actually performed). By doing so, SmoothRL seeks to enable a "Reinforce in Deployment" paradigm, where the policy is optimized under the same dynamic execution conditions it will face during real-world tasks, without compromising real-time performance or the ability to improve through direct value gradients.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of SmoothRL is to develop an online reinforcement learning framework that can fine-tune pretrained robot policies while operating under asynchronous inference, thereby satisfying the simultaneous requirements of reliability (high task success rates) and smooth real-time execution in physical robot deployment.

The motivation for this work stems from several detailed challenges observed in the deployment of state-of-the-art robot foundation models:

1. **Reconciling Online RL with Asynchronous Execution**: Large robot models have increased inference latency. To achieve smooth, continuous robot motion, modern deployment strategies utilize asynchronous inference with action chunking, where policy computation and action execution overlap. However, most online RL methods assume synchronous execution, meaning the policy is optimized under dynamics that differ from those encountered at test time. This fundamental mismatch can lead to "gradient contamination," where policy updates are influenced by actions that were generated but never actually executed by the robot. SmoothRL aims to resolve this by performing online RL directly within the asynchronous deployment loop.

2. **Enabling Direct Value-Gradient Optimization in Asynchronous Settings**: The value-gradient paradigm, where the gradient of the action-value function is directly backpropagated to policy parameters, is effective for adapting pretrained policies. However, existing approaches that allow for online adaptation under asynchronous execution typically restrict policy optimization to latent spaces (e.g., latent steering of diffusion policies). This restriction can limit the achievable improvement, as the policy's expressive capability remains bounded by the frozen base policy's decoder. SmoothRL's objective is to enable direct value-gradient backpropagation to the policy parameters in the raw action space, allowing for more substantial policy improvement.

3. **Ensuring Smoothness and Continuity under Online Adaptation**: While asynchronous execution helps hide latency, it can introduce discontinuities at chunk boundaries because new chunks are generated from stale observations. Existing methods address this through chunk-stitching or blending. When online RL is introduced, the optimization process could potentially disrupt the inherent smoothness of a pretrained policy. SmoothRL aims to integrate online RL without sacrificing the motion continuity necessary for dexterous manipulation.

4. **Facilitating Natural Human Intervention**: Human intervention is a common and often necessary component of real-robot RL for tasks that are costly or risky to explore autonomously. For systems that optimize in a latent action space, incorporating human demonstrations can be complex, often requiring unreliable latent inversion or separate offline data collection. SmoothRL is motivated to operate in the raw action space, allowing human interventions to be directly incorporated as regression targets for the policy and as valid transitions for the critic.

In summary, SmoothRL is motivated by the need for a practical and effective framework that can adapt powerful, yet high-latency, robot foundation models in real-time on physical robots. It specifically targets the technical challenge of correctly propagating learning signals from observed outcomes back to policy parameters when the policy's output and the robot's executed actions are not perfectly aligned due to asynchronous scheduling.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SmoothRL is presented as a general framework for online reinforcement learning that fine-tunes pretrained robot policies during asynchronous inference. Its methodology is built upon a value-gradient paradigm and explicitly models the asynchronous execution process during training.

**A. The Asynchronous Inference Loop and Chunk Partitioning:**
The core of SmoothRL's approach involves explicitly modeling the asynchronous inference process. Under asynchronous execution, a policy-generated action chunk is only partially executed by the environment. To account for this, each action chunk of horizon *H* is partitioned into three regions based on its execution status:
- **Committed Region [0, *d*)**: Contains actions that have already been issued by the *previous* inference cycle. These actions cannot be influenced by the current policy generation. Its length *d* is determined by the current inference time.
- **Execution Region [*d*, *d* + *c*)**: Contains newly generated actions that are *actually executed* by the robot before the next inference completes. Its length *c* is determined by the arrival time of the subsequent chunk. This is the only part of the chunk that directly affects the environment.
- **Discarded Region [*d* + *c*, *H*)**: Contains actions that are superseded by the next inference cycle and are never executed by the robot.

To simplify and stabilize the learning process by eliminating stochasticity in region boundaries, SmoothRL enforces a fixed latency budget, *n*. An upper bound *n* on inference time is estimated, and each chunk is requested *n* control steps before its first frame is due. If inference completes earlier, the system waits. This ensures that the committed region length (*d*) and execution region length (*c*) are consistently fixed to *n*. Thus, for any horizon *H* ≥ 2*n*, the committed region is [0, *n*), the execution region is [*n*, 2*n*), and the discarded region is [2*n*, *H*).

**B. Gradient-based RL in Continuous Action Space with Chunk-Skip Backup:**
SmoothRL formulates reinforcement learning over action chunks, treating each chunk as an atomic action. A chunk-skip Bellman backup is used for the action-value function *Q*, where the discounted return *r* is accumulated over the execution of the chunk, and the successor state is observed after the chunk is fully executed. The formulation is adapted for the fixed execution window of [0, 2*n*), meaning *r* accumulates over these 2*n* frames, the bootstrap state is *s*<sub>*t*+2*n*</sub>, and the discount exponent is 2*n*. This ensures an unbiased backup as the critic is conditioned on the actual action sequence that generated the reward.

**C. Value Gradient Truncation and Critic Input:**
- **Gradient Truncation**: To prevent policy updates from being influenced by actions that were generated but not executed, the value gradient ∇_a *Q* is computed *only* with respect to the execution region *a*<sub>[*n*,2*n*)</sub>. This ensures that the segment receiving the gradient is precisely the policy output that the environment actually observes.
- **Critic Input**: While the gradient is truncated, the critic receives the *full* action span. Specifically, for the current chunk, it receives the committed region `~a`<sub>[0,*n*)</sub> (which represents actions already issued by the preceding chunk) and the execution region *a*<sub>[*n*,2*n*)</sub>. The committed region is provided to the critic for two reasons:
 1. The chunk-skip bootstrap requires conditioning on the action sequence that generated the interval reward, which includes the committed actions.
 2. The committed region serves as state augmentation for the concurrent decision process, providing information about the action currently in flight.

**D. Asynchronous Execution Embedded in the Training Loop:**
Crucially, the asynchronous inference loop, including policy forward passes and action execution overlap, runs during training rollouts, not just at deployment. This ensures that the replay trajectories accurately record the actions the robot actually executed under the same timing and execution schedule that the objective is defined against. This alignment prevents a mismatch between training and deployment dynamics.

**E. Human Intervention and Raw Action Space:**
SmoothRL operates directly in the raw action space, which allows for natural integration of human interventions. Two modes are supported:
- **Absolute Intervention**: The teleoperated chunk is directly issued, replacing the policy output.
- **Residual Intervention**: Human input is added as a correction to the policy-generated chunk.
In both cases, the action actually issued by the operator is recorded and used as the behavioral cloning (BC) target for the execution region. Intervened episodes are stored in the replay buffer as ordinary transitions, and the state augmentation (committed region) faithfully reflects human commands when intervention occurs.

**F. Instantiation Details (RLT Skeleton Adaptation):**
The paper instantiates SmoothRL using an RLT-like skeleton:
- A frozen base policy (e.g., π<sub>0.5</sub>) generates reference action chunks (`¯a`<sub>*t*:*t*+2*n*</sub>) and provides a compressed internal representation (RL token `z_t`).
- A lightweight, trainable attached network (MLP actor-critic) predicts a bounded residual correction to the reference action.
- The critic is an MLP that takes `[z_t, s_t, ~a_[0, n), a_[n, 2n)]` as input. It uses a REDQ-style ensemble of *N* critics for stability and pessimistic estimation.
- The actor is an MLP taking `[z_t, s_t, ¯a_t:t+2n]` as input, outputting a corrected action chunk. Its loss function comprises:
 1. A Q-maximization term (value gradient propagated only through `a_[n, 2n)`).
 2. A behavioral cloning (BC) term against a target action (`¯a` or human-intervened action).
 3. A smoothness regularizer that penalizes high velocity, acceleration, and jerk, implicitly enforcing the "executable set" constraint.
- The replay buffer is initialized with rollouts from the frozen base policy and then continuously updated with online asynchronous rollouts, including human interventions.
- Updates are paced by rollout progress (e.g., *G* critic iterations per trajectory, with actor and target network updates every *D* critic iterations).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

SmoothRL was evaluated on three real-world robotic manipulation tasks using the Astribot S1 mobile bimanual robot: Dynamic Tossing, Pen Capping, and Box Opening. These tasks represent a range of challenges, from high-speed dynamic manipulation to high-precision bimanual interaction. The performance was measured by task success rate, comparing the frozen base policy against the SmoothRL-tuned policy after 150, 200, and 250 online rollout episodes.

**A. Overall Performance Improvement:**
The framework demonstrated substantial improvements in task success rates across all three tasks after 250 online rollout episodes compared to the frozen base policy:
- **Dynamic Tossing**: Improved from 39% to 94%. This task requires accumulating velocity for a precise release, making it sensitive to continuous motion.
- **Pen Capping**: Increased from 8% to 83%. This task demands millimeter-level precision for aligning and inserting the cap.
- **Box Opening**: Rose from 30% to 90%. This task involves stabilizing a box, precisely inserting a blade into a seam, and cutting tape.

**B. Correction of Systematic Failures and Enhanced Precision:**
Analysis of failure modes revealed that the frozen base policy exhibited systematic, consistent errors (e.g., fixed biases, inadequate adaptation to pose variations). SmoothRL's online fine-tuning progressively corrected these biases. For instance, in box opening, the initial leftward bias of the blade was largely rectified. In dynamic tossing, the policy learned to modulate release velocity more effectively with target distance, moving beyond fixed biases. Visual comparisons of failures showed that RL-tuned policies resulted in "near misses" with smaller deviations (e.g., 1-2mm errors for blade insertion), whereas base policy failures were characterized by larger, systematic deviations. This indicates that SmoothRL facilitates "closing the last millimeter" of precision needed for challenging tasks.

**C. Task-Specific Learning Trajectories:**
The learning dynamics varied by task:
- **Dynamic Tossing and Pen Capping**: Showed monotonic improvement, with the most significant gains observed within the first 150 rollout episodes. For pen capping, the success rate increased from 8% to 67% in this initial phase, suggesting rapid correction of dominant systematic offsets.
- **Box Opening**: Exhibited a different trajectory, with an initial drop in performance to 20% after 150 episodes (below the base policy's 30%), followed by a recovery to 40% at 200 episodes and ultimately 90% at 250 episodes. This transient degradation suggests an initial exploration phase where the attached network discovers effective corrections.

**D. Impact of Intervention Modes:**
The study found that residual intervention was more effective for dynamic tasks like tossing. For challenging bin positions, residual intervention achieved approximately 80% success, significantly outperforming absolute chunk-level teleoperation via VR (approximately 30%). Residual intervention reduced the operator's control burden by allowing them to correct deviations while preserving the base policy's velocity profile, leading to better motion continuity across intervention boundaries.

**E. Smoother Motion:**
The research indicated that SmoothRL produces smoother motion. Quantitative measurements (RMS) of the right end-effector's XYZ motion during a real autonomous throwing rollout showed that SmoothRL reduced acceleration by 52% and jerk by 47% compared to the base policy. This supports the framework's ability to maintain smooth real-time execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SmoothRL presents a framework that addresses a critical gap in the deployment of advanced robot policies: the integration of gradient-based online reinforcement learning with asynchronous execution. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Robust Deployment of Large Robot Models**: Modern VLA and WAM models offer generalist capabilities but come with high inference latency, necessitating asynchronous execution. SmoothRL provides a method to fine-tune these models online under their actual deployment conditions, which is crucial for their practical application in real-world, dynamic environments. This bridges the theoretical assumptions of many RL algorithms with the practical realities of robot control systems.

2. **Achieving High Reliability and Smoothness Simultaneously**: The framework demonstrates that it is possible to achieve both high task success rates and smooth, real-time robot motion concurrently. By explicitly modeling asynchronous execution and truncating gradients, SmoothRL ensures that policy optimization aligns with physically executed actions without introducing undesirable discontinuities or pauses. This is particularly impactful for dexterous and dynamic tasks where continuity and precision are paramount.

3. **Enhancing Precision for Fine-Grained Manipulation**: The significant improvements observed in high-precision tasks like pen capping and box opening indicate that SmoothRL can effectively correct systematic biases of pretrained policies, enabling robots to achieve millimeter-level accuracy. This capability is vital for many complex industrial and service robotics applications.

4. **Practical Online Adaptation with Human-in-the-Loop Learning**: By operating in the raw action space, SmoothRL naturally accommodates human interventions. This allows operators to guide policy learning directly, without complex latent space inversions, making human-in-the-loop online RL more practical and efficient for real robots, especially for challenging or unsafe exploration scenarios. The effectiveness of residual intervention also highlights the potential for intuitive human-robot collaboration in refining skills.

5. **Foundational Work for Future RL Architectures**: The methodology of chunk role partitioning and gradient truncation offers a general principle for handling action-execution mismatches. This could influence the design of future online RL algorithms and policy architectures, extending beyond the specific instantiation presented. It opens avenues for investigating how generative policies or more complex blending operators could be integrated into such a framework.

6. **Insights into Learning Dynamics under Asynchrony**: The observed learning trajectories, including initial performance drops for some tasks, provide insights into the exploration dynamics and adaptation requirements when an RL module interacts with a high-latency base policy in an asynchronous loop. This contributes to the understanding of how to manage transient behaviors during online robot learning.

In conclusion, SmoothRL contributes a principled approach to a fundamental problem in real-world robot learning, demonstrating how to effectively and reliably adapt complex robot policies in challenging, high-precision, and dynamic tasks while maintaining operational smoothness. Its principles lay groundwork for more robust and capable robot systems in the future.
