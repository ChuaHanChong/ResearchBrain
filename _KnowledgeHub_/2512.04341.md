# Long-Horizon Model-Based Offline Reinforcement Learning Without Explicit Conservatism

> [!summary] Summary
> NEUBAY, a model-based offline reinforcement learning algorithm, utilizes a Bayesian principle without explicit conservatism for policy learning. It enables long-horizon planning via adaptive rollout truncation and architectural stability, demonstrating competitive performance across 33 benchmarks and achieving new state-of-the-art results on 7 D4RL and NeoRL datasets.

## Problem

- Most offline reinforcement learning algorithms rely on explicit conservatism (e.g., uncertainty penalties, short planning horizons) to mitigate value overestimation and unsafe extrapolation due to limited data coverage.
- While Bayesian RL offers a framework to optimize average-case performance under epistemic uncertainty, scaling it to complex tasks without reintroducing conservatism has been challenging due to compounding model errors and value overestimation.
- Explicit conservatism can limit a policy's ability to explore potentially high-reward but underrepresented actions, thereby hindering average-case performance and adaptation, especially in low-quality datasets.

## Method

- **NEUBAY** implements a ==Bayesian principle== by optimizing the expected return under a ==posterior distribution over world models==, using a large ensemble to quantify epistemic uncertainty and aiming for ambiguity-neutral average-case performance.
- The algorithm enables long-horizon rollouts by ==adaptively truncating trajectories== based on the model's ==epistemic uncertainty==, utilizing a ==quantile-based threshold== derived from the offline dataset.
- It incorporates design choices like a large ==world model ensemble (N=100)==, ==Layer Normalization== within the world model to stabilize predictions and bound errors, and ==history-dependent recurrent actor-critic networks (LRU)== with a small learning rate for context encoders to support adaptation and prevent overestimation.

## Results

- **NEUBAY** achieved competitive performance across **33** D4RL and NeoRL locomotion, Adroit, and AntMaze datasets, establishing new state-of-the-art mean performance on **7 tasks** (e.g., increasing walker-medium-v2 from **95.5 to 106.4** and pen-cloned-v1 from **74.1 to 91.3**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that adaptive long-horizon rollouts (with **ζ=1.0**) were critical, with shorter horizons leading to performance collapse due to severe value overestimation, and Layer Normalization was essential for stabilizing long rollouts.
- Introducing explicit uncertainty penalties generally degraded NEUBAY's performance, particularly on low-quality datasets, and replacing the history-dependent agent with a Markovian one severely reduced performance in complex domains like AntMaze.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit conservatism is not universally necessary for offline RL, as a non-conservative Bayesian approach can achieve competitive or superior performance through test-time adaptation, especially on low-quality datasets.
> - Long-horizon rollouts, when effectively managed with adaptive truncation and architectural stability (e.g., Layer Normalization), can actively mitigate value overestimation by reducing the reliance on biased bootstrapped terms.
> - Bayesian offline RL framed as an epistemic POMDP necessitates history-dependent recurrent agents to maintain long-term memory, enabling test-time adaptation and Bayes-optimal policies in uncertain environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) typically involves online interaction with an environment. However, real-world applications often face constraints such as high costs or safety risks associated with data collection. Offline RL addresses this by learning policies solely from pre-collected static datasets, without further environment interaction. This approach allows for safe and scalable training and the potential to develop policies that surpass the performance of the data-generating behavior policies.

The majority of existing offline RL algorithms operate under an explicit conservatism principle. This principle typically involves penalizing actions that are out-of-distribution (i.e., not well-represented in the dataset) or restricting the planning horizon of learned models to a small number of steps. This conservatism is intended to mitigate issues like value overestimation and unsafe extrapolation into uncertain regions of the state-action space, which are common challenges in offline learning due to limited data coverage. While these methods aim for robustness, they can also limit a policy's ability to explore potentially high-reward but underrepresented actions, thereby suppressing average-case performance and adaptation.

An alternative perspective is offered by Bayesian RL, which aims to optimize average-case performance under epistemic uncertainty. Prior work has formalized offline RL from a Bayesian viewpoint as an epistemic Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), where partial observability arises from the limited data coverage. This framework allows for test-time adaptation through history-dependent policies. However, scaling this Bayesian principle to complex, realistic tasks without reintroducing explicit conservatism has presented challenges. These challenges include managing value overestimation, mitigating compounding errors inherent in learned world models over longer horizons, and effectively training agents with long-term memory required for adaptation in POMDPs. Existing Bayesian-inspired offline RL algorithms often revert to explicit conservatism through uncertainty penalties or short rollouts, or they simplify to model-free approaches.

This work contributes to the offline RL landscape by challenging the universal necessity of explicit conservatism and developing a practical model-based Bayesian approach. It aims to demonstrate that a Bayesian framework, by directly addressing epistemic uncertainty, can achieve competitive or superior performance on certain types of datasets without explicit penalties or short horizon restrictions. It particularly focuses on enabling and leveraging long-horizon rollouts, which is a departure from conventional model-based offline RL practices that favor short horizons.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a practical and effective offline reinforcement learning algorithm that adheres to a Bayesian principle, explicitly without relying on commonly employed mechanisms of explicit conservatism. The authors aim to demonstrate that this "neutral Bayesian" approach can offer advantages, particularly on datasets of varying quality and coverage, by enabling test-time adaptation.

The motivation for this work stems from several points:

1. **Questioning the Universality of Explicit Conservatism:** The paper questions whether explicit conservatism, which penalizes out-of-dataset actions or limits rollout horizons, is universally optimal or necessary for offline RL. While conservatism helps in mitigating extrapolation errors and value overestimation, it can also restrict a policy from exploring potentially superior actions that are underrepresented in the dataset, thus hindering average-case performance and adaptation.

2. **Revisiting the Bayesian Perspective:** The authors propose to revisit a complementary Bayesian perspective. This approach models a posterior distribution over possible world models and trains a history-dependent agent to maximize expected return under this posterior. This formulation is argued to directly address epistemic uncertainty, which arises from limited data coverage, without requiring explicit penalties for out-of-distribution actions.

3. **Demonstrating Advantages in Low-Quality Datasets:** Through an illustrative bandit setting, the paper intends to show that a Bayesian approach can adaptively explore and commit to better, unseen actions in low-quality datasets where conservative methods might fail by rigidly adhering to observed data. This highlights a potential strength of Bayesianism in scenarios where optimal actions might be scarce or underrepresented in the training data.

4. **Addressing Challenges for Scalability:** Scaling the Bayesian principle to realistic, high-dimensional tasks presents several technical challenges:
 - **Value Overestimation:** When explicit conservatism (e.g., uncertainty penalties) is removed, policies are more susceptible to overestimating the value of actions, especially those in unvisited state-action regions. This challenge becomes central to address in a non-conservative Bayesian framework.
 - **Compounding Model Errors:** Learned world models inevitably contain inaccuracies. When these models are used to generate long trajectories (long-horizon rollouts), these errors can accumulate rapidly, leading to unreliable predictions and hindering policy learning.
 - **Training Agents with Long-Term Memory:** Bayesian RL, framed as an epistemic POMDP, necessitates agents that can maintain and update an implicit belief about the true environment based on observed history. This requires robust long-term memory mechanisms, which standard recurrent neural networks often struggle with over extended sequences.

To overcome these challenges, the authors are motivated to introduce specific design choices that enable long-horizon model-based planning and effective policy learning from such generated data within a Bayesian framework. The ultimate goal is to develop an algorithm, NEUBAY, that can be competitive with or outperform leading conservative methods, particularly on datasets where conservatism might be detrimental.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces NEUBAY (NEUtral BAYesian), an algorithm grounded in the Bayesian principle for long-horizon model-based offline reinforcement learning, explicitly without relying on conservatism. The methodology can be broken down into the following key aspects:

#### 3.1. Core Principle and Objective

NEUBAY operates on the Bayesian principle, which optimizes the expected return under a posterior distribution over plausible world models, $P_D(m)$, rather than the worst-case model as in conservative methods. This corresponds to solving a Bayes-adaptive MDP or an epistemic POMDP, where the true environment is partially observed due to data limitations. The approach is "ambiguity-neutral," aiming for average-case performance. The posterior $P_D(m)$ is approximated using a finite ensemble of learned world models. Epistemic uncertainty is quantified by the disagreement (standard deviation) among the mean predictions of these ensemble members.

#### 3.2. Illustrative Bandit Example

The paper first demonstrates the principle in a two-armed bandit setting with a skewed dataset, where one arm (arm 1) is completely unobserved, while the other (arm 0) has a known reward distribution.
- **Conservative Agent:** With an uncertainty penalty, a conservative agent is forced to always select the observed arm (arm 0) due to high uncertainty on the unobserved arm.
- **Bayesian Agent:** Without a penalty, the Bayesian agent, trained on a posterior that spans various possible reward parameters for arm 1, adapts by briefly exploring the unseen arm. If arm 1 is better than arm 0, the Bayesian agent identifies and exploits it, outperforming the conservative agent. If arm 1 is worse, the Bayesian agent's return is slightly lower due to exploration cost. This illustrates that Bayesianism excels on low-quality datasets by enabling test-time adaptation.

#### 3.3. Enabling Long-Horizon Rollouts in Realistic MDPs

A core tenet of NEUBAY is that long-horizon rollouts are essential for non-conservative Bayesian agents to mitigate value overestimation. By relying more on model-generated rewards over a longer horizon and less on the bootstrapped term, the algorithm can reduce the bias in value estimation. To make long-horizon rollouts feasible despite compounding model errors, several design choices are introduced:

- **Design Choice 1: Adaptive Rollout Truncation using Epistemic Uncertainty.**
 - Instead of fixed short horizons, rollouts are truncated adaptively based on the model's epistemic uncertainty. A quantile-based threshold, $U(\zeta)$, is used, where $\zeta \in$. $U(\zeta)$ is defined as the $\zeta$-quantile of the uncertainty values of state-action pairs in the offline dataset.
 - Rollouts continue as long as the uncertainty $U_\theta(\hat{s}_t, \hat{a}_t)$ remains below $U(\zeta)$. The most aggressive choice, $\zeta = 1.0$, is found to yield the best performance, allowing rollouts to be as long as the model is reliable within the observed data distribution.
 - Planning starts by sampling initial histories $h_t$ from any timestep in the offline dataset (MBPO-style) to maintain coverage.
 - Rollouts stop if the uncertainty threshold is exceeded, the maximum episode length $T$ is reached, or a ground-truth terminal state is encountered. Only ground-truth termination disables bootstrapping; other truncations preserve it.

- **Design Choice 2: Larger Ensemble Size (N=100).**
 - A larger ensemble (e.g., N=100) is used to better approximate the Bayesian posterior over world models and enhance member diversity, which is crucial for long-horizon rollouts where compounding errors amplify posterior inaccuracies.

- **Design Choice 3: Layer Normalization (LN) in the World Model.**
 - LN is applied within the hidden layers of the world model's neural network architecture. This is shown to significantly stabilize predicted state norms, bound compounding errors, and prevent state predictions from diverging, thereby supporting longer, more stable rollouts.

#### 3.4. Stable Recurrent RL for Learning from Long-Horizon Rollouts

To train policies from the mixture of real and model-generated long-horizon data, NEUBAY employs recurrent off-policy RL.
- **History-Dependent Agent:** Both the actor and critic are recurrent networks (RNNs) equipped with LRU (Linear Recurrent Unit) encoders. This enables them to maintain long-term memory of past observations, which is necessary for the history-dependent Bayes-optimal policies in epistemic POMDPs and for test-time adaptation.
- **Design Choice 4: Balancing Real and Imagined Data ($\kappa$).**
 - A mixing ratio $\kappa \in (0, 1)$ is used in the RL loss to balance contributions from real transitions (prefix of trajectories) and model-generated transitions (rollout portion). This acts as a regularizer, preventing over-reliance on the model.
- **Design Choice 5: Small Context Encoder Learning Rate ($\eta_\phi$).**
 - The learning rate for the recurrent encoders ($\eta_\phi$) is kept significantly smaller than that for the MLP heads. This is motivated by insights from online recurrent RL, where history representations can diverge rapidly with small parameter changes, and in this context, helps to curb overestimation by slowing learning.

The overall algorithm alternates between generating rollouts using the world model ensemble and training the recurrent actor-critic agent using a mixture of real and imagined data. Each rollout fixes a single model from the ensemble, adhering to the Bayesian objective.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of NEUBAY was conducted on various offline RL benchmarks, including D4RL locomotion, NeoRL locomotion, D4RL Adroit, and D4RL AntMaze, covering 33 datasets.

#### 4.1. Benchmarking Performance

- **Competitiveness:** NEUBAY demonstrated competitive performance compared to leading conservative model-free and model-based algorithms. For D4RL locomotion, its average normalized score was 80.1 versus 83.6 for the best baseline (VIPO). For NeoRL locomotion, it achieved 64.7 versus 73.3 (VIPO). For D4RL Adroit, it scored 21.1 versus 28.1 (MoMo), and for AntMaze, 28.8 versus 64.9 (LEQ).
- **State-of-the-Art (SOTA) Results:** NEUBAY achieved new SOTA mean performance on 7 of the 33 datasets, with 3 statistically significant gains. Notable advancements include walker-random-v2 (27.9 $\rightarrow$ 34.1), walker-medium-v2 (95.5 $\rightarrow$ 106.4), pen-cloned-v1 (74.1 $\rightarrow$ 91.3), and hammer-cloned-v1 (5.0 $\rightarrow$ 14.4).
- **Performance on Bayesian-inspired Methods:** NEUBAY generally outperformed other Bayesian-inspired methods, many of which still incorporate explicit conservatism.
- **Dataset Quality and Coverage:**
 - **Low-Quality Datasets (11 tasks):** NEUBAY ranked among the best in 5 tasks and showed reasonable performance in 10 tasks, consistent with the bandit example illustrating Bayesianism's advantage in adaptive exploration.
 - **Medium-Quality, Moderate Coverage Datasets (7 tasks):** NEUBAY ranked among the best in 5 tasks with no failures.
 - **Medium-Quality, Narrow Coverage Datasets (15 tasks):** NEUBAY was weaker, ranking among the best in only 3 tasks. This indicates limitations in posterior modeling under highly restricted data, especially in complex environments like AntMaze where contact-rich dynamics and sparse rewards make world modeling challenging.

#### 4.2. Ablation and Sensitivity Studies

- **Adaptive Long-Horizon Rollouts (Uncertainty Quantile $\zeta$):** This was identified as a decisive factor. The most aggressive choice, $\zeta = 1.0$, yielded the best performance on almost all datasets, resulting in routine planning horizons of 64-512 steps (75th-percentile) and 256-1000 steps (maximum) in environments with T=1000. In contrast, smaller $\zeta$ values (e.g., 0.9 or 0.99), which result in shorter effective horizons, often led to performance collapse due to severe value overestimation. This supports the paper's central insight that long horizons reduce overestimation by shifting reliance from bootstrapping to lower-biased imagined rewards.
- **Layer Normalization in World Models and Uncertainty Truncation:** LayerNorm was shown to prevent the explosion of predicted state norms and stabilize reward predictions, thus mitigating compounding errors. This architectural choice, combined with uncertainty-based truncation, enabled the effective use of long-horizon rollouts.
- **Ensemble Size:** Reducing the ensemble size from 100 to 20 or 5 generally degraded performance, indicating that a larger ensemble contributes to better posterior approximation and model diversity.
- **Context Encoder Learning Rate ($\eta_\phi$) and Real Data Ratio ($\kappa$):** These hyperparameters required per-dataset tuning. Optimal encoder learning rates were generally smaller, which helps to curb overestimation. $\kappa$ values beyond the commonly used 0.05 were often beneficial, acting as a soft regularizer.
- **Explicit Conservatism (Uncertainty Penalty $\lambda$):** Introducing an uncertainty penalty ($\lambda > 0$) into NEUBAY generally hurt performance, especially on low-quality datasets, consistent with the bandit intuition. While some specific tasks saw benefits with certain $\lambda$ values, this highlights the need for careful tuning and confirms that penalties are not a universal solution, especially in AntMaze where they consistently harmed performance.
- **Markov Agent Ablation:** Replacing the history-dependent agent with a Markovian one showed similar performance on many locomotion tasks but led to severe degradation in complex domains like AntMaze. This suggests that memory and test-time adaptation are crucial in environments with high epistemic uncertainty, such as unknown maze layouts.

#### 4.3. Computation Costs

NEUBAY's training process involves world-model training (one-time cost) and agent training. World model training for an ensemble of N=100 models on `halfcheetah-medium-expert-v2` cost approximately 6 hours, while recurrent agent training cost 4.4 hours. The rollout inference was highly efficient due to vectorization, with negligible time costs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of offline reinforcement learning by challenging the prevailing reliance on explicit conservatism and offering a detailed exploration of a Bayesian alternative. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Re-evaluation of Explicit Conservatism:** The paper provides empirical and theoretical arguments against the universal optimality of explicit conservatism. By demonstrating that a non-conservative Bayesian approach can achieve competitive or superior performance, especially on low-quality and moderate-coverage datasets, it suggests that penalizing out-of-dataset actions might not always be the most effective strategy. This opens a discussion on the appropriate balance between pessimism and adaptability in offline RL.

2. **Advancement of Bayesian Offline RL:** This work introduces NEUBAY as a practical and effective algorithm for model-based Bayesian offline RL. By addressing critical challenges such as value overestimation, compounding model errors, and the need for long-term memory, NEUBAY makes the theoretical advantages of Bayesianism more accessible in real-world benchmarks. It establishes a strong baseline for future research in this under-explored area.

3. **Novel Role for Long-Horizon Rollouts:** The research highlights a previously underexplored role for long-horizon rollouts in non-conservative offline RL: actively reducing value overestimation. This finding contrasts with conventional model-based RL practice, which typically restricts rollouts to short horizons to mitigate compounding errors. By successfully integrating adaptive long-horizon planning with architectural innovations like Layer Normalization, NEUBAY demonstrates that long horizons, when properly managed, can be a powerful tool.

4. **Emphasis on Adaptive Agents and Test-Time Adaptation:** NEUBAY's use of history-dependent recurrent agents for Bayes-optimal policy learning underscores the importance of adaptation in offline RL. By maintaining an implicit belief about the environment, the agent can adapt its behavior during test-time interaction, even without online parameter updates. This aligns with a broader vision of RL agents that can refine behavior as uncertainty resolves.

5. **Practical Guidelines and Future Directions:** The paper offers practical guidelines for applying NEUBAY, identifying scenarios where it is preferable (low-quality, moderate-coverage data) and key hyperparameters to tune. It also outlines promising avenues for future work, including improving world models (e.g., multi-step prediction, generative models), enhancing uncertainty quantification, and developing offline policy selection methods that leverage the learned Bayesian world model. These suggestions can guide subsequent research efforts to build upon NEUBAY's foundation.

Overall, this work contributes to a shift in perspective within offline RL, moving towards methods that prioritize adaptive decision-making under uncertainty rather than strict conservatism. This could lead to more robust and generalizable policies in diverse real-world applications where data quality and coverage are variable.
