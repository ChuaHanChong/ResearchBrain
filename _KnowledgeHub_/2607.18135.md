# Isaac Sim-to-Real: Reinforcement Learning based Locomotion for Quadrupeds

> [!summary] Summary
> An end-to-end reinforcement learning policy was developed at the University of Louisville for quadruped locomotion, achieving zero-shot transfer from Isaac Sim to the Unitree Go1 robot. This policy demonstrated robust performance, including higher velocity tracking and superior disturbance rejection, when compared to the robot's integrated controller.

## Problem

- The persistent "sim-to-real gap" in reinforcement learning (RL) for robotics, where policies trained in simulation often fail on physical hardware.
- The need for robust whole-body control strategies for quadrupedal robots to achieve agile and adaptive locomotion across diverse and challenging terrains.
- Limitations of traditional model-based controllers in handling complex, nonlinear dynamics and recovering from unexpected disturbances in unstructured environments.

## Method

- Trained an RL policy using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== within NVIDIA's ==Isaac Sim==, leveraging 4096 parallel environments for efficient training.
- Implemented extensive ==domain randomization== techniques, including randomization of physical parameters (e.g., friction, mass), ==terrain curriculum==, system disturbances (random pushes), and command velocities.
- Utilized an "==actuator-net==" (==Multilayer Perceptron==) to accurately model the non-linear dynamics of the Unitree Go1's actuators, which was critical for successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- Achieved successful zero-shot transfer of the RL policy to the Unitree Go1, demonstrating stable and effective locomotion across diverse physical terrains (e.g., gravel, sand, inclines).
- The RL policy exhibited superior disturbance rejection, recovering from strong external kicks without falling, and matched or surpassed the velocity tracking performance of the Unitree Go1's integrated controller.
- The policy achieved higher stable linear velocities of **2.0 m/s** and angular velocities of **1.8 rad/s**, which exceeded the stable operating range of the integrated controller under dynamic command.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Comprehensive domain randomization, encompassing both physical parameters and environmental conditions, coupled with accurate actuator modeling, is crucial for bridging the sim-to-real gap in complex robotic systems.
> - Meticulous reward function shaping, balancing terms for desired velocities, gait periodicity, and penalty terms for joint limits and stability, effectively guides RL agents to learn robust and natural locomotion gaits.
> - High-fidelity, GPU-accelerated simulation platforms like Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab enable the rapid training (e.g., 3 hours for 5000 iterations) of complex, end-to-end locomotion policies that can be directly transferred to physical hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics, particularly quadrupedal locomotion, aims to achieve agile and robust mobility across diverse and challenging environments. Traditionally, locomotion control systems have relied on a hybrid approach, combining high-level gait generation and stability control schemes (such as Zero-Moment Point or Model Predictive Control) with low-level actuator controllers for joint position tracking. While these methods provide basic mobility, they often encounter difficulties with the complex, nonlinear dynamics inherent in irregular terrains.

In recent years, there has been a significant shift towards learning-based strategies, predominantly Reinforcement Learning (RL), for developing more adaptive and robust control policies. These RL approaches increasingly focus on whole-body control, moving beyond high-level gait generation to encompass all necessary components of locomotion. This paradigm shift has led to the development of high-performance simulation environments by industry leaders such as OpenAI (OpenAI Gym), NVIDIA (Isaac Sim), and Google (MuJoCo), which provide controlled and efficient platforms for training complex RL policies.

However, a persistent challenge in RL-based robotics is the "sim-to-real gap," where policies that perform effectively in simulation often face unexpected issues when deployed on physical hardware. Previous research has explored various methods to bridge this gap, including real-world training and fine-tuning, or simulation training enhanced with techniques like domain randomization, noise injection, and system disturbances. Actuator modeling has also been identified as a critical factor for achieving robust real-world performance.

This work contributes to this landscape by leveraging NVIDIA's Isaac Sim and its companion RL framework, Isaac Lab, which are noted for their advanced physics solvers and capabilities. The paper positions itself within the context of recent RL advancements, acknowledging approaches that function as high-level controllers, those integrating high-bandwidth sensors, and those dependent on pre-trained models within teacher-student architectures. Distinctively, this research aims to achieve whole-body control without relying on pre-acquired data or teacher-student frameworks, focusing instead on comprehensive reward shaping and robust sim-to-real techniques to enable a zero-shot transfer of a low-level position-control policy to physical hardware. The validation against a commercial quadruped's integrated controller further benchmarks its performance against established model-based methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a robust Reinforcement Learning (RL) locomotion framework that enables whole-body control for quadrupedal robots, specifically aiming for a zero-shot sim-to-real policy transfer. This objective is motivated by several key challenges and opportunities in the field of legged robotics:

First, despite the effectiveness of RL in simulation for complex locomotion tasks, the **sim-to-real gap** remains a significant hurdle. Policies trained solely in simulation frequently fail to perform adequately when transferred to physical robots due to discrepancies between simulated and real-world physics, sensor noise, and actuator characteristics. The authors are motivated to address this by implementing specific techniques to achieve direct, zero-shot transfer without real-world fine-tuning.

Second, the desire for **whole-body control** in quadrupedal locomotion is a central motivation. Traditional methods often compartmentalize control into high-level gait planning and low-level joint control. This work aims to learn an end-to-end policy that manages all required components of locomotion, producing a low-level position-control policy that can coordinate the entire robot's body for agile and robust movement, particularly in irregular terrains where traditional controllers struggle. The authors explicitly state their policy does not rely on pre-acquired data or teacher-student frameworks, emphasizing a purely learned, end-to-end approach based on reward shaping.

Third, the motivation includes exploring the capabilities of **NVIDIA's Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab**. These platforms offer high-performance GPU-accelerated physics simulation and integrated RL tools. The authors aim to demonstrate the potential of these new tools for developing advanced locomotion policies and evaluating their effectiveness in bridging the sim-to-real gap.

Finally, the research is motivated by the need to develop **adaptive and resilient controllers** that can operate in dynamic, unstructured environments and recover from disturbances. By benchmarking their RL policy against the Unitree Go1's integrated model-based controller, the authors seek to demonstrate that learning-based approaches can not only match but potentially surpass the performance of established methods in terms of velocity tracking, achieved velocities, and disturbance rejection.

In summary, the key objectives articulated through the paper's contributions are:
- To present an end-to-end RL-based locomotion policy capable of zero-shot transfer to the Unitree Go1.
- To detail the specific domain randomization and actuator modeling techniques employed to overcome the sim-to-real gap.
- To validate the policy's performance on physical hardware, including comparisons with the Unitree Go1's integrated controller regarding velocity tracking, achievable velocities, and disturbance rejection capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on training a reinforcement learning (RL) policy within a high-fidelity simulation environment, Isaac Sim, with a specific focus on techniques to facilitate zero-shot transfer to a physical quadruped, the Unitree Go1.

**A. Problem Definition (Markov Decision Process)**
The locomotion problem for a quadruped is framed as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), characterized by:
- **State Space (S):** The agent's current configuration and relevant environmental information.
- **Action Space (A):** The set of possible actions the agent can take.
- **Transition Probability (P):** The likelihood of moving from one state to another after taking an action.
- **Reward Function (R):** A scalar value received after taking an action in a given state.
- **Discount Factor (γ):** Determines the importance of future rewards.
The agent's objective is to learn an optimal policy, π*(A|S), that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward over time.

**B. Sim-to-Real Techniques**
To bridge the sim-to-real gap and achieve zero-shot transfer, two primary techniques were implemented:

1. **Domain Randomization:**
 - **Parallel Environments:** The policy was trained across 4096 parallel simulation environments simultaneously on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU.
 - **Parameter Randomization:** Key physical parameters of the robot and environment were randomized within specified ranges (±% of initial values) to increase policy robustness. These included:
 - Foot friction.
 - Foot restitution.
 - Body mass (link mass scaled from 0.8 to 1.3).
 - Center of mass offset (-0.025 to 0.025).
 - Initial joint positions (scaled -0.3 to 0.3).
 - Initial velocities (body velocity add -0.25 to 0.25, joint velocities scaled -2.5 to 2.5).
 - Body position and orientation offsets.
 - **Terrain Curriculum:** The difficulty of the simulated terrain was gradually increased during training. This involved generating terrains with slight height variations, bumpy patches (via noise), and ramps.
 - **System Disturbances:** Random pushing events were applied to the robot's base every 10-15 seconds (linear velocity [-1.0, 1.0] m/s) to simulate external forces and improve disturbance rejection.
 - **Command Randomization:** Desired command velocities (linear X, Y and angular Z) were sampled from specified ranges ([-2, 3] m/s for X, [-1.5, 1.5] m/s for Y, and [-1.5, 1.5] rad/s for Z) every 0.3 to 10.0 seconds.

2. **Actuator Modeling (Actuator-Net):**
 - A Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) network, termed an "actuator-net," was employed to model the non-linear dynamics of the Unitree Go1's actuators.
 - This network was trained using recorded joint information from policy deployments to minimize the error between predicted and actual torque outputs based on historical joint positions and velocities.
 - The actuator-net architecture consisted of three layers of 64 neurons, utilizing the soft-sign activation function.
 - It was configured with proportional (k_p = 20) and derivative (k_d = 0.5) gains to balance joint stiffness and compliance. The authors noted that training without this actuator model significantly hindered sim-to-real transfer.

**C. Action Space**
The policy's action space is a 12x1 vector representing the target joint positions for the Unitree Go1's 12 actuated joints. These values are scaled by a factor of 0.2 and offset by the robot's nominal standing configuration to promote initial stability. The resulting scaled and offset joint positions are then fed into the actuator-net for torque calculation.

**D. Observation Space**
The policy utilizes a 48x1 observation vector comprising minimal state information, without explicit scaling:
- **IMU-related:** Linear velocity, angular velocity (yaw), and projected gravity (X, Y components) – aiding in velocity tracking and disturbance modeling. Noise was injected into these states (0.02 for linear velocity, 0.01 for angular velocity, 0.05 for projected gravity).
- **User Command:** Desired linear (X, Y) and angular (Z) velocities.
- **Joint Information:** Relative joint positions (difference from nominal stance), joint velocities. Noise was injected into joint positions (0.05) and joint velocities (0.1).
- **Previous Action:** The unscaled action taken in the previous timestep, enabling the policy to learn the impact of its actions.

**E. Reward Function Shaping**
Extensive reward shaping was used to guide the policy towards stable and periodic gaits while maintaining desired velocities. The reward function includes:
- **Reward Terms (Positive Weights):**
 - **Feet Air Time:** Encourages proper foot lift-off (weight 5.0).
 - **Linear/Angular Velocity Error:** Rewards tracking desired velocities (weight 5.0 for both).
 - **Gait:** Promotes natural, synchronous, and asynchronous foot contact (weight 10.0).
 - **Foot Clearance:** Ensures feet are lifted sufficiently off the ground (weight 5.0).
- **Penalty Terms (Negative Weights):**
 - **Action Smoothness:** Penalizes large changes in consecutive actions (-1.5).
 - **Air Time Variance:** Reduces variations in air time between feet (-1.0).
 - **Base Motion/Orientation:** Penalizes excessive roll/pitch angular velocities and deviations from upright orientation (-2.0 and -5.0, respectively).
 - **Foot Slippage:** Discourages slipping contact (-1.0).
 - **Foot Force:** Penalizes excessive force during foot contact, aiming for softer landings (-0.00002).
 - **Joint Torque/Acceleration/Velocity/Deviation:** Penalizes high values to prevent strenuous or unnatural joint movements (-0.0005, -0.0002, -0.015, -0.75 respectively).
 - **Joint Position/Velocity Limits:** Applies large penalties for exceeding joint limits for safety and stability (-100.0 and -10.0 respectively).
 - **Thigh/Calf Contact:** Penalizes undesired contact of robot limbs with the ground (-10.0 and -1.0, respectively), crucial for stability when moving backward.

**F. Network Architecture, Training, and Apparatus**
- **Network Architecture:** A straightforward actor-critic architecture using Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs). Both actor and critic networks have three layers with 512, 256, and 128 neurons, respectively, using Exponential Linear Units (ELUs) as activation functions.
- **Training Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was used. Hyperparameters included an adaptive learning rate, an entropy coefficient of 0.0025, a target KL-Divergence of 0.01, and discount/advantage estimator factors (γ, λ) of 0.99 and 0.95 respectively, with a learning rate of 0.001.
- **Training Duration:** The policy was trained for 5,000 iterations, taking approximately 3 hours.
- **Simulation Parameters:** Physics simulation timestep (∆t) was 0.002s, and the policy control frequency (∆t) was 0.02s (50Hz).
- **Hardware Apparatus:**
 - The Unitree Go1 quadruped, an 18-DoF robot with 12 actuated joints, was used for physical validation.
 - **Deployment:** The trained policy was compiled to ONNX format. Inference was performed on an Nvidia Jetson Nano, running within a Docker container.
 - **State Estimation:** Linear velocity was estimated using a Kalman Filter. This filter was implemented in C++ and integrated via Pybind11 to achieve low latency, allowing state collection and policy inference to run at up to 400Hz without GPU acceleration, significantly reducing latency compared to the trained 50Hz and improving real-world performance at 100Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides experimental results validating the developed RL locomotion framework on the Unitree Go1 quadruped, comparing its performance against the robot's integrated model-based controller and assessing its disturbance rejection capabilities.

**A. Locomotion Capabilities across Terrains:**
The policy demonstrated the ability to walk effectively on various challenging terrains. This was validated by deploying the Go1 on rugged surfaces such as gravel, sand, and incline/decline plains. A visual example showed the quadruped successfully navigating a steep incline pallet (Figure 1), indicating the policy's robustness to terrain variations. Simulated environments (Figure 2) further depicted the policy's capacity to handle bumpy floors and ramps.

**B. Velocity Tracking Performance:**
The velocity tracking performance was evaluated using a sinusoidal command for linear (X, Y) and angular (Z) velocities over 35 seconds.
- **RL Policy (Figure 3):** The RL policy exhibited a small phase lag between the desired sinusoidal command velocities and the measured velocities. The tracking performance indicated that the policy was capable of closely following dynamic velocity commands.
- **Unitree's Integrated Controller (Figure 4):** When subjected to the same sinusoidal command, Unitree’s model-based controller produced similar tracking results but encountered instabilities. Specifically, at higher frequencies and amplitudes (implying larger accelerations and velocities), the integrated controller experienced foot slippage, necessitating a reduction in maximum and minimum command velocities to maintain stability.
- **Comparison:** The RL-based approach was observed to match or produce comparable velocity tracking performance to the integrated model-based controller, while concurrently achieving higher linear velocities of 2.0 m/s and angular velocities of 1.8 rad/s, which were beyond the stable operating range of the integrated controller under dynamic commands.

**C. Disturbance Rejection Capabilities:**
A disturbance rejection test was conducted by applying three separate, progressively stronger kicks to the trunk of the quadruped.
- **Policy Response (Figure 5):** The policy successfully recovered from all three kicking events. Despite the increasing force applied during each kick, the quadruped only stumbled and did not fall to the ground. The robot's height (blue line) showed deviation from its nominal configuration (green line) during the kicks, but it consistently returned to the nominal height, indicating effective recovery. The linear velocity plot (red curve) provided an approximation of the force applied by each kick.
- **Implication:** These results indicate a high degree of robustness of the RL policy against external disturbances such as collisions or unexpected foot slippage during locomotion, a critical aspect for real-world deployment.

**D. Computational Efficiency:**
The policy, after being compiled into ONNX format, was shown to run efficiently on an Nvidia Jetson Nano. The inference, combined with necessary state collection and estimation (including a C++ implemented Kalman Filter for linear velocity), could achieve loop rates of up to 400Hz without requiring GPU acceleration. This indicated that the deployed policy has low computational overhead for real-time operation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the development of robust and adaptive locomotion for quadrupedal robots using reinforcement learning, with several significant implications:

**A. Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful demonstration of a zero-shot transfer of an end-to-end RL locomotion policy from Isaac Sim to a physical Unitree Go1 quadruped is a primary contribution. This directly addresses the long-standing challenge of the "sim-to-real gap," which often necessitates extensive real-world fine-tuning. Achieving effective real-world performance without additional real-world training reduces development time, hardware wear, and safety concerns, making RL-based robotics more practical for commercial and research applications.

**B. Enhanced Robustness and Performance:** The policy's ability to match or exceed the velocity tracking performance of the Unitree Go1's integrated model-based controller, coupled with its capacity to achieve higher linear (2.0 m/s) and angular (1.8 rad/s) velocities, indicates a tangible improvement in locomotion capabilities. Crucially, the superior disturbance rejection demonstrated through repeated physical kicks suggests that the learned policy is more resilient to unexpected external forces and environmental perturbations than conventional controllers. This robustness is vital for robots operating in unstructured and dynamic real-world environments.

**C. Validation of Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab:** The work serves as a practical validation of NVIDIA's Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab as effective platforms for developing advanced RL policies for robotics. The utilization of high-performance GPU-accelerated simulation with massively parallel environments is shown to be instrumental in training robust policies within a reasonable timeframe (3 hours for 5,000 iterations). This may encourage wider adoption of these tools within the robotics research community.

**D. Methodological Contributions for Sim-to-Real:** The detailed implementation of domain randomization (including varied physical parameters, terrain curriculum, and random pushes) and the use of an actuator-net for accurate actuator modeling are critical methodological contributions. These techniques provide a blueprint for other researchers aiming to achieve zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with complex robotic systems, highlighting the importance of comprehensive simulation fidelity and variability. The specific reward shaping strategy employed also offers insights into guiding RL agents to develop natural and stable gaits.

**E. Computational Efficiency and Deployment:** The finding that the trained policy, once converted to ONNX format, can be efficiently executed on a low-power edge device like an Nvidia Jetson Nano at high frequencies (up to 400Hz inference loop rate without GPU acceleration) has significant implications for embedded systems. This demonstrates that sophisticated RL controllers are becoming deployable on resource-constrained hardware, expanding the potential applications for autonomous legged robots in real-world scenarios without reliance on high-performance on-board computing.

In conclusion, this research advances the state of the art in quadrupedal locomotion by presenting a framework that effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap using a data-efficient RL approach. The demonstrated performance in terms of speed, agility, and disturbance rejection positions this work as a foundational step towards the development of highly capable, autonomous legged robots that can navigate and interact with complex physical environments.
