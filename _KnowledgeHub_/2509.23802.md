# STAIR: Addressing Stage Misalignment through Temporal-Aligned Preference Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> STAIR introduces a Preference-based Reinforcement Learning framework that addresses "stage misalignment" in multi-stage tasks by dynamically approximating task stages using temporal distance and prioritizing stage-aligned human preference queries. This approach significantly enhances feedback efficiency, achieving faster convergence and near 100% success rates in complex robotic manipulation tasks while aligning with human cognitive processes.

## Problem

- Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) faces "stage misalignment" in multi-stage tasks, where comparing behaviors from different stages leads to ambiguous and uninformative human feedback.
- Human labelers experience increased cognitive load and provide less reliable feedback when asked to compare behaviors across natural task stage boundaries.
- A "stage reward bias" exists in human preferences, favoring segments closer to task completion, which theoretically leads to a quadratic increase in query complexity for conventional PbRL methods.

## Method

- Develops **STAIR**, a framework that learns a dynamic ==stage approximation== based on *temporal distance* (successor distance) between states, adapting to evolving policies without predefined, task-specific stage definitions.
- Employs contrastive learning to train an energy function that approximates the temporal distance, enabling on-policy updates as the agent's policy evolves.
- Introduces a "quadrilateral distance" to measure stage difference between *segments* and combines it with reward model uncertainty to prioritize informative, stage-aligned preference queries for human labeling.

## Results

- STAIR achieved near **100%** success rates and significantly faster convergence in multi-stage MetaWorld and RoboDesk robotic manipulation tasks, consistently outperforming several state-of-the-art PbRL baselines.
- Human studies confirmed that STAIR-generated queries were perceived as stage-aligned at a significantly higher ratio than queries from baseline methods, validating its temporal distance-based stage approximation.
- Demonstrated enhanced feedback efficiency, achieving a **52.01%** success rate on 'door-open' with 500 queries compared to **20.00%** for baselines, and exhibited robustness to hyperparameter choices and noisy human feedback.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Stage misalignment is a tangible problem in multi-stage PbRL, exacerbated by a measurable "stage reward bias" in human preferences, which can theoretically lead to inefficient learning and increased query complexity.
> - Dynamically approximating task stages using temporal distance effectively captures human cognitive perception of task stages, reducing cognitive load and improving the quality of human feedback.
> - Prioritizing preference queries that are both stage-aligned and where the reward model is uncertain significantly improves feedback efficiency and overall learning performance in PbRL for multi-stage tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in diverse applications such as robotics, gaming, and autonomous systems. A core challenge in RL is the design of effective reward functions, which often necessitates significant manual effort and expert knowledge. Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) offers an alternative by learning reward functions directly from human preferences over agent behaviors, thereby aiming to align agent objectives with human intentions more effectively.

However, a gap exists in the application of PbRL to multi-stage tasks, which are common in real-world sequential problems. Existing PbRL methodologies frequently overlook the sequential, multi-stage nature of these tasks. This oversight can lead to "stage misalignment" during the preference collection process, where human labelers are asked to compare behaviors originating from distinct stages of a task. Cognitive science, particularly event segmentation theory, suggests that humans naturally segment continuous action sequences into discrete events or stages. Comparisons that span these natural event boundaries impose higher cognitive loads on labelers and can result in ambiguous or uninformative feedback.

This research addresses the stage misalignment issue, which has implications for the efficiency and effectiveness of PbRL algorithms in complex, multi-stage environments. It integrates concepts from temporal distance learning, a technique previously utilized in goal-conditioned RL, skill discovery, and state representation, to facilitate stage approximation within the PbRL framework. The work aims to refine preference query selection by incorporating an understanding of task stages, thereby improving the efficiency and robustness of reward learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the issue of "stage misalignment" in Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) when applied to multi-stage tasks. The authors identify and systematically analyze several motivations and challenges that underpin this objective:

- **Inefficiency in Multi-Stage Tasks:** Many real-world tasks, such as robotic "go-fetch-return," involve a sequence of distinct sub-tasks (e.g., navigation, grasping, returning). Traditional PbRL methods often present human labelers with segments of behavior from mismatched stages (e.g., a navigation segment versus a manipulation segment). This leads to uninformative or ambiguous feedback, as humans struggle to make meaningful comparisons between qualitatively different behaviors. Such ambiguity hinders efficient policy learning.
- **Cognitive Load on Human Labelers:** Drawing upon event segmentation theory from cognitive science, the paper highlights that humans naturally segment continuous action sequences into discrete event boundaries. Queries that require comparisons across these natural event boundaries (i.e., from different stages) increase the cognitive load on labelers, potentially leading to less reliable and more inconsistent preference judgments.
- **Reduced Information Gain from Misaligned Queries:** Differences in state distributions across distinct stages can diminish the informational value of stage-misaligned preference queries. When the reward model receives less informative feedback, it requires a larger volume of queries to learn an effective reward function, thereby reducing feedback efficiency.
- **Impact of Stage Reward Bias:** The research empirically demonstrates that human preferences often exhibit a "stage reward bias," where certain stages (e.g., those closer to task completion) are inherently preferred over others. When such bias exists, conventional PbRL methods face a significantly increased demand for feedback, exhibiting quadratic growth in query complexity compared to a linear growth for stage-aligned approaches, as theoretically analyzed in Proposition 3. This bias exacerbates the inefficiency caused by stage misalignment.
- **Challenges in Stage Identification:** Addressing stage misalignment requires identifying the distinct stages within a task. However, two significant challenges arise:
 - **Subjectivity and Lack of Generalizability:** Predefined stage definitions often rely on subjective expert knowledge specific to a particular task, limiting their transferability and generalizability across different tasks or even variations of the same task.
 - **Adaptability to Evolving Policies:** As an RL agent's policy evolves during training, the nature and progression of its behaviors change. A static stage definition (e.g., based on timestep) might become incompatible or irrelevant with an improved, more advanced policy. Therefore, stage measurement needs to be dynamically adaptive to on-policy changes.

In summary, the motivation is to enhance the feedback efficiency and overall performance of PbRL in complex multi-stage environments by proactively mitigating stage misalignment. This involves developing a method to automatically identify and prioritize informative, stage-aligned preference queries without requiring explicit, task-specific stage definitions or relying on static approximations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, STage-AlIgned Reward learning (STAIR), addresses stage misalignment by learning a stage approximation and using it to prioritize stage-aligned preference queries. The methodology is divided into two main phases: learning a temporal distance model for stage approximation and using this model for stage-aligned query selection.

**Phase 1: Learning a Stage Approximation based on Temporal Distance**

STAIR utilizes *temporal distance* as a measure for approximating stage differences between states. This approach aims to circumvent the challenges of predefined, subjective stage definitions and to adapt to evolving policies.

1. **Temporal Distance Definition:** Temporal distance quantifies the transition probability between states under a given policy. States that are easily reachable from each other (i.e., temporally close) are considered to belong to similar stages, while those that are difficult to reach (temporally distant) are indicative of distinct stages. The specific measure employed is the *successor distance*, defined based on the discounted state occupancy measure $p^\pi_\gamma(s' = y | s_0 = x)$, which represents the discounted probability of reaching state $y$ from state $x$ under policy $\pi$. The successor distance $d^\pi_{SD}(x, y) = \log \left( \frac{p^\pi_\gamma(s' = y | s_0 = y)}{p^\pi_\gamma(s' = y | s_0 = x)} \right)$ is a quasi-metric, providing a reliable measure of state similarity.

2. **Learning Temporal Distance via Contrastive Learning:** The successor distance $d^\pi_{SD}(x, y)$ is learned using *contrastive learning*.
 - An energy function $f_\theta(x, y)$ is trained to assign higher scores to state pairs $(x, y)$ that occur within the same trajectory and are temporally close (positive pairs, sampled from $p^\pi_\gamma(s' = y | s_0 = x)$).
 - Conversely, it assigns lower scores to state pairs from different trajectories or that are temporally distant (negative pairs, sampled from marginal distributions $p_s(x)$ and $p_+(y)$).
 - The optimization is performed by minimizing the symmetrized InfoNCE loss (Equation 6), which encourages the energy function to discriminate between positive and negative pairs.
 - The energy function is parameterized as $f_\theta(x, y) = c_{\theta_c}(y) - d_{\theta_d}(x, y)$. After training, the $d_{\theta_d}(x, y)$ component directly approximates the successor distance, $d^\pi_{SD}(x, y)$.
 - This learning process is performed in an "on-policy" manner, meaning the temporal distance model is periodically updated using data generated by the current policy, allowing it to adapt dynamically as the policy evolves.

**Phase 2: Stage-Aligned Query Selection**

Once the temporal distance model is learned, STAIR uses it to select informative, stage-aligned segment pairs for human labeling.

1. **Measuring Stage Difference between Segments (Quadrilateral Distance):** The temporal distance is inherently a state-level metric. To apply it to segments (sequences of state-action pairs), the authors propose a *quadrilateral distance* $d_{stage}(\sigma_0, \sigma_1)$. This metric calculates the average of four temporal distances between the initial and final states of two segments $\sigma_0 = (s^0_0, \dots, s^0_{H-1})$ and $\sigma_1 = (s^1_0, \dots, s^1_{H-1})$:
 $d_{stage}(\sigma_0, \sigma_1) = \frac{1}{4} \cdot (d^\pi_{SD}(s^0_0, s^1_0) + d^\pi_{SD}(s^0_{H-1}, s^1_{H-1}) + d^\pi_{SD}(s^0_0, s^1_{H-1}) + d^\pi_{SD}(s^0_{H-1}, s^1_0))$.
 The "side terms" ($d^\pi_{SD}(s^0_0, s^1_0)$ and $d^\pi_{SD}(s^0_{H-1}, s^1_{H-1})$) favor segments with closely aligned starting and ending points, indicating straightforward stage alignment. The "diagonal terms" ($d^\pi_{SD}(s^0_0, s^1_{H-1})$ and $d^\pi_{SD}(s^0_{H-1}, s^1_0)$) implicitly encourage the selection of segments that are temporally compact and likely concentrated within a single stage.

2. **Query Selection Score Function:** A score function $I(\sigma_0, \sigma_1)$ is used to rank candidate segment pairs. This function combines two criteria:
 - **Stage Alignment:** Measured by $d_{stage}(\sigma_0, \sigma_1)$, normalized to $$. Lower $d_{stage}$ indicates better alignment.
 - **Reward Model Uncertainty:** Measured by $d_{state}(\sigma_0, \sigma_1)$, which quantifies the variance of preference predictions across an ensemble of reward models, a common indicator of informativeness in PbRL. Higher uncertainty indicates greater informativeness.
 The combined score is defined as $I(\sigma_0, \sigma_1) = (c_{stage} - d_{stage}(\sigma_0, \sigma_1))(c_{state} + d_{state}(\sigma_0, \sigma_1))$, where $c_{stage}$ and $c_{state}$ are hyperparameters. The first term incentivizes stage alignment, while the second term encourages queries where the reward model is uncertain.
 The algorithm then selects $M$ queries with the largest scores from a pool of $N_c$ candidate pairs.

**Overall STAIR Framework (Algorithm 1):**
STAIR integrates these components into an iterative RL loop:
1. Initialize replay and feedback buffers.
2. Pre-train the policy (e.g., using unsupervised methods).
3. In each iteration:
 - Rollout trajectories with the current policy and store data.
 - Periodically, if total feedback budget is not met, select $M$ stage-aligned queries using the described method, query the teacher, and store preferences.
 - Update the reward model using collected preferences.
 - Periodically, update the temporal distance model using the latest collected data.
 - Relabel the replay buffer with the updated reward model.
 - Update the policy using the relabeled data.

This approach ensures that preferences are collected efficiently by focusing human feedback on comparisons that are both stage-aligned and informative according to the current reward model's uncertainty.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both theoretical analyses and empirical evidence to support its claims regarding stage misalignment and the effectiveness of STAIR.

#### 4.1. Impact of Stage Misalignment

- **Existence of Multi-Stage Property:** The authors empirically validated the multi-stage property in MetaWorld robotic manipulation tasks. A classifier trained to predict the timestep of a given state from expert trajectories achieved 81% accuracy, indicating distinct state distributions across different timesteps and thus supporting the inherent multi-stage structure of these tasks (Proposition 1).
- **Stage Reward Bias:** Human preference experiments on MetaWorld tasks (e.g., window-open) revealed a clear bias: labelers consistently preferred segments occurring later in the task trajectory. This "stage reward bias" suggests that humans implicitly assign higher value to behaviors closer to task completion, further highlighting the problem of cross-stage comparisons.
- **Theoretical Inefficiency of Conventional PbRL:**
 - Proposition 2: In a general multi-stage abstract MDP, conventional PbRL (sampling queries across all stages) requires $O(|\Omega||\Upsilon|\log(|\Omega||\Upsilon|))$ additional queries to learn an optimal policy compared to stage-aligned PbRL, where $|\Omega|$ is the number of stages and $|\Upsilon|$ is the number of actions per stage.
 - Proposition 3: When stage reward bias is sufficiently large (i.e., reward ordering between different stages is dominated by the stage itself), conventional PbRL's query complexity worsens to $O(|\Omega|^2|\Upsilon|\log(|\Upsilon|))$ additional queries compared to stage-aligned PbRL. Empirical tests in an abstract MDP model confirmed that stage-aligned selection yields better learning efficiency, especially with increasing stage reward bias (Figure 3, Right).

#### 4.2. Performance on Benchmark Tasks

- **Multi-stage Tasks (MetaWorld and RoboDesk):** STAIR consistently outperformed several state-of-the-art PbRL baselines (PEBBLE, RUNE, MRN, QPA, RIME) across a range of complex robotic manipulation tasks from MetaWorld (e.g., door-open, sweep-into, window-open, faucet-open) and RoboDesk (push-green). STAIR achieved success rates near 100% in most cases and demonstrated faster convergence speeds, indicating superior feedback efficiency.
- **Single-stage Tasks (DMControl):** In locomotion tasks from DMControl (e.g., walker-run, quadruped-walk, quadruped-run), which are typically considered single-stage, STAIR maintained competitive performance. It outperformed PEBBLE and was comparable to other baselines. The authors suggest this might be due to an implicit curriculum learning effect induced by STAIR, where the method progressively focuses on different aspects of the task as the policy evolves.

#### 4.3. Human Studies

- A human labeling experiment was conducted to assess the alignment of STAIR's approximated stages with human cognition. Labelers were asked to identify if two segments in a query belonged to the same stage. STAIR-generated queries were perceived by humans as stage-aligned at a significantly higher ratio compared to queries generated by PEBBLE (Figure 8, Figure 13 in Appendix). This indicates that the temporal distance-based stage approximation effectively captures human-perceived task stages.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Impact of Stage Difference Approximator:**
 - Variants using 'Timestep' (approximating stage by collected timestep) or 'STAIR (ISR)' (using temporal distance to initial state and Interval Span Ratio for segment difference) performed less effectively than the full STAIR model.
 - This suggests that the proposed quadrilateral distance, which evaluates stage differences in a two-dimensional space, is more effective than one-dimensional approximations.
- **Impact of Temporal Distance Update Frequency:** A lower frequency of temporal distance updates (larger $K_{SD}$) resulted in decreased performance. This finding emphasizes the importance of training the temporal distance approximator in an on-policy manner to ensure it adapts to the evolving policy.
- **Robustness to Hyperparameters:** STAIR's performance remained consistent across different values of hyperparameters $c_{stage}$ and $c_{state}$ in the query selection score function, indicating robustness (Table 1).
- **Enhanced Feedback Efficiency:** When comparing performance with varying numbers of total queries ($N_{total}$), STAIR consistently outperformed PEBBLE, especially under limited feedback conditions. For example, with 500 queries on 'door-open', STAIR achieved 52.01% success rate compared to PEBBLE's 20.00% (Table 2). This demonstrates STAIR's ability to extract more information from fewer human preferences.
- **Robustness to Noisy Feedback (Appendix F.3):** Experiments with error-prone (10% incorrect feedback) and inconsistent (mixture of myopic and error-prone) teachers showed that STAIR consistently outperformed baselines under both noisy conditions (Table 15).

In summary, the results demonstrate that stage misalignment is a tangible problem in PbRL, exacerbated by stage reward bias. STAIR effectively mitigates this issue through its temporal distance-based stage approximation and stage-aligned query selection, leading to improved performance, faster convergence, higher feedback efficiency, and alignment with human cognitive processes, while also exhibiting robustness to hyperparameter choices and noisy feedback.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Preference-based Reinforcement Learning by identifying and providing a solution for the problem of "stage misalignment" in multi-stage tasks. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Improved Efficiency and Effectiveness of PbRL:** By focusing on stage-aligned preference queries, STAIR significantly enhances the feedback efficiency of PbRL. This reduces the number of human labels required to train effective reward functions, making PbRL more practical for complex real-world applications where human feedback is costly or time-consuming to obtain. The demonstrated faster convergence and higher success rates in multi-stage tasks illustrate this improvement.
- **Enhanced Human-AI Collaboration:** The methodology directly addresses the cognitive burden on human labelers. By presenting comparisons between segments from similar stages, STAIR makes the preference-labeling task more intuitive and less ambiguous for humans, potentially leading to more consistent and higher-quality feedback. The human studies confirm that STAIR's approximated stages align with human cognition, suggesting a better human-AI interface for reward learning.
- **Generalizability and Adaptability:** A notable contribution is the development of an on-policy stage approximator based on temporal distance. This approach eliminates the need for predefined, task-specific stage knowledge, which can be subjective and difficult to generalize. The dynamic adaptation of temporal distance to evolving policies ensures that the stage approximation remains relevant throughout the learning process, addressing a critical challenge in dynamic environments.
- **Robustness to Diverse Task Structures:** STAIR's strong performance in multi-stage robotic manipulation tasks, coupled with its competitive performance in single-stage locomotion tasks, suggests a broad applicability. The hypothesized implicit curriculum learning in single-stage scenarios indicates a potentially broader utility of stage-aware learning beyond explicit multi-stage problems.
- **Theoretical and Empirical Validation:** The paper provides a systematic analysis of stage misalignment, including theoretical propositions on query complexity and empirical validation through diverse experiments, human studies, and ablation studies. This comprehensive investigation solidifies the understanding of the problem and the advantages of the proposed solution.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for future research in several areas:
 - **Alternative Preference Formats:** The current limitation regarding the applicability of quadrilateral distance to other preference formats (beyond pairwise segment comparisons) presents an area for further exploration.
 - **Advanced Curriculum Learning:** The observed implicit curriculum learning in single-stage tasks could be further investigated to develop more explicit and robust automatic curriculum generation methods in PbRL.
 - **Real-world Deployment:** By making PbRL more efficient and robust, STAIR moves closer to enabling the deployment of RL agents in complex, human-aligned real-world scenarios where tasks inherently possess multi-stage structures.

In summary, STAIR represents an advance in PbRL by tackling a fundamental challenge in multi-stage environments. Its novel combination of temporal distance learning and stage-aligned query selection offers a more efficient, user-friendly, and robust approach to learning from human preferences, with the potential to significantly impact the development of more capable and aligned AI systems.
