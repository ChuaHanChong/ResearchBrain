# On the Design of Safe Continual RL Methods for Control of Nonlinear Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Vanderbilt University developed Safe EWC, a method that combines Elastic Weight Consolidation with reward shaping, to enable reinforcement learning agents to continuously adapt to changing system dynamics while adhering to safety constraints. Empirical evaluations in MuJoCo environments with simulated faults showed that Safe EWC reduces catastrophic forgetting and maintains lower safety violation costs compared to existing safe or continual reinforcement learning approaches.

## Problem

- Safe Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms, like CPO, often suffer from catastrophic forgetting when operating in non-stationary environments where system dynamics change over time.
- Continual RL algorithms, while designed to adapt to new conditions and retain past knowledge, generally lack mechanisms to inherently guarantee system safety.
- Real-world, safety-critical control systems require both continuous adaptation to dynamic environments (e.g., component faults) and robust safety assurance, a challenge not fully addressed by current RL methods.

## Method

- The authors proposed **Safe EWC**, an approach that integrates a simple ==reward-shaping== mechanism into the ==Proximal Policy Optimization with Elastic Weight Consolidation (PPO+EWC)== framework.
- This reward shaping modifies the task reward by subtracting a ==penalty proportional to safety violations==, encouraging the agent to prioritize both reward maximization and safety constraint satisfaction.
- The ==Elastic Weight Consolidation== component aims to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by penalizing changes to neural network weights important for previously learned tasks, ensuring knowledge retention across dynamic shifts.

## Results

- **Safe EWC** agents consistently incurred significantly lower total costs (safety violations) than PPO+EWC and, in several tasks, outperformed CPO in safety performance, particularly in later stages of learning.
- **Safe EWC** demonstrated substantially lower Task Forget Percentages and improved backward transfer compared to CPO, indicating better retention of learned policies and reduced catastrophic forgetting in non-stationary settings.
- While CPO initially showed higher sample efficiency in some tasks, **Safe EWC** successfully balanced safety and reward, achieving competitive task rewards while maintaining safety, unlike PPO+EWC which often maximized rewards at the expense of high safety costs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Existing Safe RL algorithms are indeed prone to catastrophic forgetting, and standard Continual RL algorithms often disregard safety constraints, empirically validating a critical gap in the field.
> - A relatively simple reward-shaping modification can effectively enable a continual learning algorithm to balance safety and adaptation, showing promise for integrating these two objectives.
> - The interplay between safety, task performance, and adaptation depends on the specific environment and task sequence, highlighting trade-offs that require careful consideration in algorithm design.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated successes across various control applications, including unmanned aerial vehicles and robotics. However, a significant challenge for real-world deployment, especially in industrial and mission-critical systems, is ensuring the safety of the controlled system. This challenge has led to the emergence of the field of Safe Reinforcement Learning (Safe RL).

Safe RL approaches are broadly categorized into model-based and model-free methods. Model-based techniques often leverage known physical models or learned data-driven models, incorporating tools such as Model Predictive Control, Lyapunov functions, Control Barrier Functions, or formal verification to ensure safety. Model-free Safe RL, suitable when accurate dynamic models are unavailable, typically employs constrained optimization, with methods like Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) and Lagrangian Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO-Lag) being prominent examples. While these methods aim to satisfy safety constraints, their performance in non-stationary environments, where system operating conditions change over time, remains an area requiring further investigation.

Systems operating over extended periods inevitably encounter changes, such as component degradation, sudden faults, or environmental shifts. Addressing this dynamic nature is the focus of Continual Reinforcement Learning (Continual RL) or Lifelong Reinforcement Learning. The primary objective of Continual RL algorithms is to adapt to new conditions without "catastrophic forgetting"—the loss of knowledge about previously learned tasks. Common Continual RL strategies include regularization-based methods (e.g., Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)), experience replay, knowledge bases, and network expansion techniques. While Continual RL aims to maintain performance across changing tasks, these approaches generally do not inherently account for system safety.

This research specifically addresses the intersection of Safe RL and Continual RL, a domain identified as understudied and critical for deploying RL in real-world, safety-critical, and adaptive systems. The paper investigates the implications of continual adaptation on system safety, particularly in the context of abrupt and significant changes in system dynamics, such as those caused by equipment faults. It aims to bridge the gap between algorithms designed for safety and those designed for continuous adaptation, considering scenarios where both objectives must be met simultaneously.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the limitations of current RL methodologies when applied to real-world, safety-critical systems that operate in dynamic, non-stationary environments. While Safe RL algorithms aim to ensure safety, they often exhibit catastrophic forgetting when confronted with changing system conditions. Conversely, Continual RL algorithms are designed to adapt to new conditions and retain past knowledge, but they typically do not incorporate mechanisms to guarantee system safety. The paper identifies this intersection as a critical yet understudied problem.

The key objectives of this research are:

1. **Empirically Demonstrate the Need for Safe Continual RL:** The initial objective is to provide empirical evidence that existing Safe RL algorithms struggle with catastrophic forgetting in continual learning settings, and that existing Continual RL algorithms often fail to satisfy safety constraints in non-linear, non-stationary systems. This involves:
 - Evaluating a popular continual RL algorithm, Online Elastic Weight Consolidation (PPO+EWC), to show its inability to satisfy safety constraints in non-linear systems subject to varying operating conditions (e.g., MuJoCo HalfCheetah and Ant environments with velocity constraints and sudden joint loss non-stationarity).
 - Demonstrating that an agent trained with Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO), a Safe RL algorithm, experiences catastrophic forgetting in these continual learning scenarios.

2. **Explore a Reward-Shaping Method for Safe Continual Learning:** Following the demonstration of the existing gaps, a subsequent objective is to investigate a straightforward approach to integrate safety considerations into a continual learning framework. Specifically, the researchers aim to:
 - Propose a simple reward-shaping method to modify the PPO+EWC algorithm, referred to as "Safe EWC." This method penalizes the agent for safety violations, thereby encouraging it to prioritize both safety and task performance.
 - Evaluate whether this modified algorithm can effectively ensure safety constraint satisfaction while simultaneously reducing catastrophic forgetting and improving backward transfer compared to CPO and PPO+EWC in the specified non-linear, non-stationary dynamical systems.

In essence, the research seeks to highlight the practical challenges of applying current RL techniques to real-world control problems with simultaneous safety and adaptivity requirements, and to propose and evaluate a foundational approach to address these integrated challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

**Problem Definitions and Assumptions:**
The paper frames the problem within the context of a Non-stationary Constrained Markov Decision Process (NSC-MDP). An NSMDP extends a standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) by allowing the transition function and reward function to change over time, characterizing environment changes. An NSC-MDP further adds cost functions, `C: S×A → R^m`, which are constrained by maximum cost values `d = (d_1..., d_m)`. The objective is to maximize expected discounted reward while ensuring the expected discounted sum of costs for each constraint `i` remains below `d_i`. The research focuses on "soft" safety constraints, where the goal is to minimize violations rather than prevent them entirely. Task changes are assumed to be sudden and drastic, and the occurrence of a task change is known, simplifying the problem by isolating the continual learning aspect from task detection.

**Control Tasks:**
Empirical studies were conducted using modified MuJoCo locomotion environments, HalfCheetah and Ant, from the Safety Gymnasium Python library.
- **Safety Constraint:** Both environments were given a maximum velocity constraint. Exceeding this threshold constituted a safety violation, creating a conflict between maximizing forward progress (reward) and respecting the velocity limit (cost).
- **Non-Stationarity:** To simulate faults or equipment damage, joints were periodically removed. Every 1 million training timesteps, a "fault" occurred, changing the system dynamics. Maintenance was simulated by returning the system to a nominal state.
 - **HalfCheetah:** Tasks involved removing the front or back leg.
 - **Ant:** Tasks involved removing both front or both back legs.
- **Task Sequence:** A cyclical sequence was designed to ensure each task was revisited, with the nominal task appearing most frequently. This design aimed to force distinct optimal policies for each task (e.g., crawling vs. walking).

**Algorithm Details:**
Three algorithms were compared:

1. **Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO):**
 - **Purpose:** Served as the representative Safe RL algorithm.
 - **Mechanism:** CPO directly handles constraints in the policy optimization process, performing trust-region updates to guarantee both cost satisfaction and reward increases. It aims to find a policy that maximizes expected return subject to constraints on expected costs and a KL-divergence trust region on policy updates.
 - **Implementation:** The CPO implementation from the SafePO Python library was used, leveraging its validated performance and optimized hyperparameters on MuJoCo safe velocity tasks.

2. **Proximal Policy Optimization with Elastic Weight Consolidation (PPO+EWC):**
 - **Purpose:** Served as the representative Continual RL algorithm.
 - **Mechanism:** EWC prevents catastrophic forgetting by adding a penalty to the neural network's loss function. This penalty discourages drastic changes to "important" weights for previously learned tasks. The importance of weights is approximated by the Fisher information matrix. The total EWC loss sums penalties from all previous tasks.
 - **Integration with PPO:** The EWC loss was applied to the actor network within PPO. The Fisher information matrix was calculated using observations from the final 20 episodes of each task.
 - **Hyperparameter Tuning:** A grid search for the EWC hyperparameter `λ` was performed on the HalfCheetah environment. `λ=10` was selected as it demonstrated stable nominal performance while learning new tasks and reduced forgetting compared to pure PPO.

3. **Safe EWC (PPO+EWC with Reward Shaping):**
 - **Purpose:** The proposed simple safe continual RL algorithm.
 - **Mechanism:** This approach modifies the original task reward by subtracting a penalty proportional to safety violations: `r_Safe EWC(s, a) = r(s, a) - βC(s, a)`, where `C(s, a)` is the cost and `β` is a cost weight hyperparameter. This effectively incorporates a safety objective into the PPO+EWC loss.
 - **Hyperparameter Selection:** The `β` coefficient was set by dividing the maximum reward achieved by PPO+EWC by the maximum cost, aiming to make the cost as important as the reward. This resulted in `β=5`.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Training:** Agents were trained for 8 million total interaction timesteps across the defined task sequences.
- **Replication:** Each algorithm was run with 5 different random seeds to account for stochasticity.
- **Parallelization:** Training was parallelized across 10 threads per seed, resulting in 50 simultaneous environment instances.
- **Hardware:** An AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3960X 24-Core CPU was used.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
Performance was assessed using both qualitative analysis of training curves and quantitative metrics:
- **Total Cost:** The average total cost incurred across all visits to each individual task, indicating safety performance (lower is better).
- **Task Forget Percentage:** Quantifies catastrophic forgetting by measuring the percentage drop in performance from the final reward of a previous task visit to the immediate (first episodic) reward of the next visit to the same task (lower or negative (backward transfer) is better).
- **Final Task Reward:** The asymptotic reward achieved across all visits to each task, indicating overall task performance (higher is better).
- **Qualitative Analysis:** Visual inspection of training reward and cost curves, and immediate reward plots upon task re-visits.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study conducted empirical evaluations on the HalfCheetah and Ant MuJoCo environments subjected to non-stationary conditions (joint removal faults) and velocity safety constraints.

**A. Case Study 1: HalfCheetah**
- **Reward Performance (Catastrophic Forgetting):**
 - CPO agents exhibited clear catastrophic forgetting, with rewards on revisited tasks often lower than the last time the task was experienced.
 - PPO+EWC and Safe EWC agents initially showed some forgetting but demonstrated improved retention on subsequent revisits, indicating their continual learning capabilities. The PPO+EWC agents, in particular, had the highest positive slope in immediate reward on nominal tasks, suggesting better memory recall.
 - Quantitatively, EWC-based methods (PPO+EWC and Safe EWC) had significantly lower Task Forget Percentages (e.g., CPO: 46.6% on Nominal; PPO+EWC: 26.1%; Safe EWC: 19.6%), confirming their advantage in remembering past tasks.
 - Final Task Reward for Safe EWC (2692.6 ± 161.5) was comparable to CPO (2034.3 ± 190.1) in the Nominal task and competitive in others. PPO+EWC achieved the highest rewards but at the expense of safety.

- **Cost Performance (Safety Constraint Satisfaction):**
 - PPO+EWC agents consistently incurred very high costs, indicating they largely ignored the velocity safety constraint in pursuit of maximum reward.
 - CPO agents maintained low costs throughout the training process, demonstrating their effectiveness in satisfying safety constraints.
 - Safe EWC agents, while initially having slightly higher costs than CPO in the nominal task, showed significantly lower costs than PPO+EWC. Importantly, by the fourth visit to the nominal task, Safe EWC achieved lower cost violations than CPO, suggesting it learned to remember safe actions across task visits.
 - Quantitatively, Safe EWC achieved the lowest Total Cost in Nominal (680.1 ± 34) and Front (17.4 ± 19.9) tasks, outperforming CPO in these specific contexts (CPO: 787.4 ± 112.1 Nominal; 629.7 ± 298.7 Front).

**B. Case Study 2: Ant**
- **Reward and Cost Dynamics:**
 - For the initial 4-5 million timesteps, CPO demonstrated higher sample efficiency, converging to a reasonable policy and maintaining lower costs faster than PPO+EWC and Safe EWC.
 - However, CPO still exhibited catastrophic forgetting after each task switch.
 - Both PPO+EWC and Safe EWC agents showed evidence of positive backward transfer in some tasks (indicated by negative Task Forget Percentages), meaning learning on subsequent tasks improved performance on previously seen ones.
 - Similar to HalfCheetah, PPO+EWC disregarded safety constraints, leading to high costs, whereas Safe EWC maintained significantly lower costs than CPO once it had learned reasonable policies.

- **Quantitative Metrics:**
 - **Total Cost:** Safe EWC achieved the lowest average total costs across all tasks (e.g., Nominal: 323 ± 30), significantly outperforming CPO (Nominal: 873.6 ± 78) and PPO+EWC (Nominal: 11543.4 ± 4563). This suggests Safe EWC was more effective in maintaining safety in this environment.
 - **Task Forget Percentage:** PPO+EWC and Safe EWC demonstrated strong backward transfer for the Nominal and Back tasks (e.g., Safe EWC: -507.2% Nominal, -16.7% Back). CPO still experienced forgetting, although less pronounced for the Nominal Ant task (15.1%) compared to HalfCheetah.
 - **Final Task Reward:** Due to its aggressive focus on safety, Safe EWC's final task rewards were generally lower than CPO's (e.g., Safe EWC: 2880.8 ± 53 Nominal; CPO: 2901.4 ± 59 Nominal) and PPO+EWC's (PPO+EWC: 3972.8 ± 487 Nominal), reflecting the trade-off.

**Overall Findings:**
The empirical results consistently demonstrated that CPO agents maintained safety but suffered from catastrophic forgetting in non-stationary environments. PPO+EWC agents showed strong continual learning capabilities (less forgetting, positive backward transfer) but critically failed to uphold safety constraints. The proposed Safe EWC algorithm, through its simple reward-shaping mechanism, was able to balance these competing objectives. It exhibited less catastrophic forgetting than CPO and comparable or superior safety constraint satisfaction to CPO, especially in later stages of training or for specific tasks. The specific performance trade-offs and sample efficiency varied between environments (e.g., CPO's initial sample efficiency in Ant). The study indicated that the ability of Safe RL algorithms to avoid catastrophic forgetting can be task-dependent, warranting further investigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the development of robust and adaptable autonomous systems, particularly in safety-critical domains. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Empirical Validation of a Critical Research Gap:** The paper empirically demonstrates that current state-of-the-art Safe RL algorithms (like CPO) are prone to catastrophic forgetting when faced with non-stationary conditions, while leading Continual RL algorithms (like PPO+EWC) disregard safety constraints. This validation clearly highlights the necessity for dedicated research at the intersection of these two fields, thereby defining an important problem space for future investigation. This is crucial for advancing RL beyond static laboratory settings to real-world applications where systems inevitably encounter changing operating conditions and component degradation.

2. **Introduction of a Foundational Safe Continual RL Approach:** By proposing "Safe EWC," a simple reward-shaping modification to PPO+EWC, the paper offers an initial, pragmatic solution that balances safety and continual learning. The results suggest that even a basic modification can significantly improve safety performance in continual learning scenarios while retaining the benefits of reduced catastrophic forgetting and improved backward transfer. This serves as a foundational step, providing a baseline and proof-of-concept for integrating safety into continual learning paradigms.

3. **Guidance for Future Research Directions:** The study identifies several avenues for further exploration. It suggests that more sophisticated mechanisms from Continual RL (e.g., experience replay buffers, knowledge bases, network expansion) could be adapted to explicitly remember safety constraints. Furthermore, it highlights the need for a deeper understanding of how different types of non-stationarity influence the performance and safety properties of RL algorithms, and what characteristics of tasks or task sequences necessitate specific continual learning mechanisms. This paves the way for the development of more robust, generalizable, and theoretically grounded safe continual RL algorithms.

4. **Enabling Real-World Deployment of RL:** The ability of an RL agent to both maintain safety and adapt to dynamic, unpredictable environments is paramount for its deployment in real-world industrial and mission-critical applications such as autonomous vehicles, robotics in unstructured environments, and complex control systems with potential for component failures. This work contributes to bridging the gap between theoretical RL advancements and practical deployment requirements, moving closer to autonomous systems that can operate safely and reliably over their lifetime despite changes. The concept of managing system faults through continual learning while preserving safety is directly applicable to prognostics and health management (PHM) for intelligent systems.
