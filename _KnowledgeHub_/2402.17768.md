# Diffusion Meets DAgger: Supercharging Eye-in-hand Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign introduce "Diffusion Meets DAgger (DMD)," a framework that enhances eye-in-hand imitation learning by using conditional diffusion models to synthesize realistic out-of-distribution observations with corrective action labels. This approach significantly boosts policy robustness and sample efficiency, consistently outperforming vanilla Behavior Cloning and NeRF-based augmentation across diverse manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Compounding execution errors in imitation learning cause policies to diverge from expert trajectories in out-of-distribution (OOD) states, leading to cascading failures in real-world robotic applications.
- The Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) method effectively addresses compounding errors but is prohibitively expensive due to its requirement for continuous, iterative human supervision for data collection.
- Prior generative methods for synthesizing OOD states, such as those based on NeRFs, often assume static scenes, making them unsuitable for eye-in-hand manipulation where the robot frequently interacts with and deforms objects.

## Method

- Introduces "Diffusion Meets **DAgger** (DMD)," a framework that uses a conditional diffusion model to synthesize out-of-distribution (OOD) image-action pairs to augment expert demonstrations.
- The conditional diffusion model is fine-tuned on expert data and/or task-agnostic "play data" to generate realistic perturbed views from expert images, simulating off-trajectory states.
- Corrective action labels for the synthetic images are derived by computing the relative end-effector pose to a future expert frame (typically 3 steps ahead), ensuring that the synthetic action always makes progress towards the goal.

## Results

- Achieved up to **100%** success rate on non-prehensile pushing, significantly outperforming vanilla Behavior Cloning (**30%** success) and a NeRF-based baseline (**50%** success).
- Demonstrated strong sample efficiency, achieving **80%** success with as few as 8 expert demonstrations for pushing, and showed improved generalization to new objects and environments.
- Successfully applied to complex 6-DoF tasks like pouring coffee beans (**80%** success vs. BC's **0%**) and hanging a shirt (**90%** success vs. BC's **20%**), indicating improved robustness and precision across diverse tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conditional diffusion models can effectively synthesize realistic out-of-distribution observations in dynamic, eye-in-hand robot manipulation scenes, overcoming limitations of static-scene methods like NeRFs.
> - Using a sufficiently distant future expert frame (e.g., k=3) to compute action labels for synthesized OOD images is crucial for providing consistent corrective supervision and avoiding an "overshooting problem" where the generated action might move backward.
> - Training the diffusion model with diverse "play data" (task-agnostic robot interactions) can further enhance the quality and diversity of synthetic OOD views, leading to more robust policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a fundamental challenge in **imitation learning (IL)** for robotics: the problem of **compounding execution errors**. In IL, a robot policy is trained to mimic expert demonstrations. However, even small deviations from the expert's trajectory at test time can lead the robot into **out-of-distribution (OOD) states**, where the learned policy performs poorly, leading to cascading errors and task failure. This is a well-known limitation that significantly hinders the real-world deployment of IL policies, especially when training data is limited.

Historically, a prominent solution to this problem has been **Dataset Aggregation (DAgger)**. DAgger iteratively collects additional expert data by having a human expert provide corrective actions when the robot encounters OOD states during execution. While theoretically effective, DAgger is often prohibitively expensive and time-consuming in practice, as it requires continuous human supervision and intervention in a closed-loop system. This practical bottleneck has spurred research into more sample-efficient and autonomous ways to achieve the benefits of DAgger.

The broader research landscape has explored several avenues to mitigate compounding errors and improve IL robustness:
- **More Efficient Data Collection:** Developing interfaces or methods to ease and scale up expert data collection (e.g., VR teleoperation).
- **Multi-Modal Expert Demonstrations:** Modeling diverse expert behaviors rather than a single trajectory.
- **Representation Learning:** Utilizing pre-trained visual representations to enhance generalization.
- **Synthetic Data Augmentation:** This is the most direct lineage to the current paper. Prior work has used various methods to synthesize OOD states for augmentation, either in low-dimensional state spaces or using generative models. Notably, **Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs)** have been explored for view synthesis to generate OOD observations. However, NeRFs typically assume static scenes, which becomes a limitation when the robot manipulates objects, causing scene deformations.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of imitation learning robustness and advanced generative models. It revisits the idea of "data creation" (as opposed to data collection) for off-trajectory states, drawing inspiration from early work by Pomerleau. Crucially, it leverages recent advancements in **diffusion models** for image synthesis. Diffusion models have shown exceptional capabilities in generating high-quality, diverse images, even in complex scenarios. By using diffusion models instead of NeRFs, the authors aim to overcome the static scene assumption, which is critical for eye-in-hand manipulation tasks where the gripper frequently interacts with and deforms the scene. Thus, "Diffusion Meets DAgger (DMD)" aims to provide DAgger-like benefits (robustness to OOD states) by replacing costly human data collection with automated data synthesis using cutting-edge generative AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are driven by the limitations of current imitation learning paradigms:

1. **Address Compounding Execution Errors:** The core motivation is to overcome the prevalent issue of small errors accumulating and leading to catastrophic failures in policies trained via imitation learning, particularly when the policy encounters states not present in the expert demonstrations.
2. **Achieve DAgger-like Benefits Without the Cost:** DAgger is known to mitigate compounding errors effectively, but its requirement for repeated, expensive human supervision for data collection makes it impractical for many real-world robotic applications. The paper aims to develop a method that reaps these benefits without the prohibitive data collection cost.
3. **Improve Sample Efficiency for Imitation Learning:** A significant goal is to enable robust policy learning from "few demonstrations." If synthetic data can effectively augment scarce expert data, it dramatically reduces the human effort required to train capable robot policies.
4. **Leverage Modern Generative Models for OOD Data Synthesis:** The authors aim to explore and validate the utility of conditional diffusion models for synthesizing realistic, off-trajectory observations in eye-in-hand manipulation setups. This is motivated by the recognized limitations of prior view synthesis methods (like NeRFs) when scenes undergo deformation due to robot interaction.
5. **Develop a Framework for Eye-in-Hand Imitation Learning:** The work specifically targets "eye-in-hand" setups, where the camera is mounted on the robot's hand, as these are increasingly popular. The goal is to provide a practical and effective framework for generating augmented datasets for such scenarios.
6. **Validate Design Choices and Efficacy Across Diverse Tasks:** The research seeks to systematically evaluate different aspects of their data synthesis approach (e.g., choice of target frame for action labeling, utility of different training data for the diffusion model) and demonstrate its effectiveness across a variety of manipulation tasks with different complexities (e.g., 3DoF vs. 6DoF actions, precision requirements, generalization to new objects).

In essence, the paper is motivated by the desire to make imitation learning more robust and practical for real-world robotic deployment by autonomously generating the "corrective" data that DAgger traditionally requires human experts to provide.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Diffusion Meets DAgger (DMD)" approach operates in three main stages: diffusion model training, augmented dataset generation, and policy training. The core idea is to synthesize out-of-distribution (OOD) observations and their corresponding corrective action labels, then use this augmented dataset to train a more robust policy.

#### 3.1. Overview

Given a small set of expert demonstrations (task data, D), DMD generates an augmented dataset ($\tilde{D}$) consisting of OOD image-action pairs. The final policy ($\pi$) is then trained on the combined dataset ($D \cup \tilde{D}$). The OOD images ($\tilde{I}_t$) are synthesized by a conditional diffusion model that takes an expert image ($I_t$) and a desired perturbation ($\Delta p$) as input. The action labels ($\tilde{a}_t$) for these synthetic images are derived from the original expert trajectory.

#### 3.2. Diffusion Model for Synthesizing $\Delta p$ Perturbed Views

The function $f(I_t, \Delta p)$ responsible for image synthesis is implemented using a **conditional diffusion model**.
- **Base Model:** The authors fine-tune a pre-trained diffusion model from Yu et al., which is designed for long-term photometrically consistent novel view synthesis. This choice leverages large-scale pre-training while adapting to specific robotic scenes.
- **Conditioning:** The model generates image $I_b$ by conditioning on a reference image $I_a$ and a transformation matrix $_aT_b$ (representing $\Delta p$) that maps points from camera $b$'s frame to camera $a$'s frame. For DMD, $I_a$ is an expert observation $I_t$, and $_aT_b$ is the desired $\Delta p$.
- **Architecture:** The model uses a U-Net architecture and performs denoising in the latent space of a VQ-GAN autoencoder for efficiency. Pose conditioning ($_aT_b$) is injected into the U-Net via cross-attention layers.
- **Model Training:**
 - **Data Triples:** The diffusion model requires triplets of (reference image $I_a$, target image $I_b$, relative pose $_aT_b$).
 - **Pose Extraction:** Structure from Motion (SfM) algorithms (COLMAP or ORB-SLAM3) are used to extract camera poses ($_tT_w$) for images within expert trajectories. Relative poses $_aT_b$ are then computed from these world poses.
 - **Finetuning Strategy:** The diffusion model is finetuned on these extracted triplets. The VQ-GAN codebooks are kept fixed.
 - **Data Sources for Finetuning:** The diffusion model can be finetuned using task-specific expert data (D), task-agnostic "play data" (random robot interactions), or a combination of both. Play data, involving varied interactions without task completion, can provide richer diversity for view synthesis.

#### 3.3. Generating Out-of-Distribution Images and Labels

- **Sampling OOD Images:** For each image $I_t$ from an expert trajectory, $\Delta p$ is sampled. This typically involves a small translation (e.g., 2-4cm) in a random direction and/or small rotations (e.g., $\pm 10-15^\circ$ for yaw, pitch, roll). The diffusion model $f(I_t, \Delta p)$ then synthesizes the perturbed image $\tilde{I}_t$.
- **Labeling Generated Images:** This is a critical step. For a synthesized image $\tilde{I}_t$, the corresponding action label $\tilde{a}_t$ is computed as the relative end-effector pose that would take the robot from $\tilde{I}_t$'s pose to the pose of a future expert image $I_{t+k}$.
 - **Addressing the "Overshooting Problem":** A key insight is that using $I_{t+1}$ as the target for labeling $\tilde{I}_t$ can lead to "overshooting," where the synthesized image $\tilde{I}_t$ has already moved past $I_{t+1}$ in the direction of expert progress. This can result in a corrective action that moves *backward* from the desired goal, leading to conflicting supervision. To mitigate this, the authors propose using a future frame $I_{t+k}$ with $k > 1$ (empirically, $k=3$ was found optimal) to compute $\tilde{a}_t$. This ensures the synthetic action always makes progress towards the goal, even if the perturbation is significant.
 - **Action Calculation:** Using the SfM-derived camera poses ($_tT_w$ for $I_t$ and $_{t+k}T_w$ for $I_{t+k}$), and treating $\tilde{I}_t$'s pose as $_tT_{\tilde{t}}$ relative to $I_t$, the action label $\tilde{a}_t$ is computed as $_tT_{t+k} = {_{\tilde{t}}T_t} \cdot {_tT_w} \cdot (_{t+k}T_w)^{-1}$.

#### 3.4. Policy Training and Execution

- **Policy Architecture:** The policy network uses an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 backbone followed by MLPs to predict actions.
- **Loss Function:** L1 regression loss is used to train the policy to predict the relative end-effector poses.
- **Action Space:** Tasks involve either 3DoF relative translations (pushing, stacking) or full 6DoF relative poses (pouring, shirt hanging).
- **Baselines:** The primary baseline is vanilla Behavior Cloning (BC) trained only on expert demonstrations. Comparison is also made against SPARTN, a NeRF-based augmentation method.
- **Evaluation:** Performance is measured by success rate in real-world robot experiments, with randomized A/B testing to minimize environmental factors. Offline metrics like median angle error are also used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that Diffusion Meets DAgger (DMD) consistently and significantly outperforms vanilla Behavior Cloning (BC) and a NeRF-based augmentation baseline across a diverse set of eye-in-hand manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Non-prehensile Pushing Task

- **DMD vs. BC (High Data):** With 24 expert demonstrations, DMD achieved a 100% success rate for pushing an apple, while BC only reached 30%. This highlights DMD's ability to create robust policies.
- **DMD vs. BC (Low Data):** DMD demonstrated strong sample efficiency, achieving an 80% success rate with as few as 8 expert demonstrations (with diffusion model trained on play data), whereas BC only managed 20%.
- **DMD vs. SPARTN (NeRF-based):** DMD achieved 100% success compared to SPARTN's 50%. Qualitatively, diffusion-generated images (Figure 9) were superior, faithfully synthesizing views even when the gripper manipulated the object, a scenario where NeRFs (which assume a static scene) produced artifacts or failed (e.g., blurred or missing objects). This is a crucial validation of using diffusion models over NeRFs for dynamic scenes.
- **Utility of Play Data:** Training the diffusion model on a combination of task and play data (100% success) further improved performance compared to using task data alone (80% success), suggesting the value of diverse, even if task-agnostic, interactions for the generative model.
- **Generalization to New Objects:** DMD maintained its advantage when tested on a new object (a white cup), achieving 90% success compared to BC's 80%.

#### 4.2. Stacking Task

- DMD significantly outperformed BC across both seen and unseen cup instances. For training cups, DMD achieved an average success rate of 97% (100%, 90%, 100% for different cups), compared to BC's average of 37%.
- For two unseen cups (a larger red cup and a Sprite can), DMD achieved 85% success (80% and 90%), demonstrating better generalization to novel objects compared to BC's 45%.

#### 4.3. Pouring Coffee Beans Task

- DMD achieved an 80% success rate, whereas BC completely failed (0%). This highlights DMD's ability to handle complex 6-DoF tasks requiring precision and continuous adjustment.
- Even when the diffusion model was trained only on task data (without play data), DMD still achieved a 70% success rate, significantly better than BC's 30%.

#### 4.4. Hanging a Shirt Task

- For this complex 6-DoF task involving multiple sub-tasks and contact, DMD attained a 90% success rate, while BC only succeeded in 20% of trials. DMD learned to correctly position the hanger on the rack, overcoming common BC failures like raising the shirt too high.

#### 4.5. In-the-Wild Cup Arrangement Task (with Diffusion Policy)

- This experiment tested DMD's compatibility with an advanced policy learning framework (Diffusion Policy) and its ability to improve generalization with a large, diverse dataset (1447 demos) and novel environments.
- DMD improved overall success rate from 16% (vanilla Diffusion Policy) to 64% when tested on 5 novel cups in a novel lab environment. This shows that DMD can enhance even state-of-the-art imitation learning methods and improve out-of-distribution generalization.

#### 4.6. Offline Validations (Pushing Task)

- **Comparison to Standard Augmentations:** DMD's diffusion-generated augmentations led to larger improvements in policy performance (lower median angle error) compared to standard image augmentation techniques like color jitter and horizontal flip, both independently and in combination.
- **Effect of Future Frame (k) for Labeling:** Using a future frame $I_{t+k}$ for labeling synthetic images was crucial. The median angle error decreased as $k$ increased, plateauing around $k=3$. This confirmed the "overshooting problem" and the effectiveness of using a sufficiently distant future frame to ensure consistent progress toward the goal.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that DMD significantly boosts the robustness, sample efficiency, and generalization capabilities of imitation learning policies by effectively synthesizing high-quality, corrective out-of-distribution data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Diffusion Meets DAgger (DMD)" framework represents a significant step forward in making imitation learning more practical and robust for real-world robotic applications. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

1. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization of Robotic Policies:** The most direct impact is the remarkable improvement in policy robustness to off-trajectory states and better generalization to novel objects and environments. By synthetically generating corrective data, DMD addresses the fundamental limitation of compounding errors that plagues vanilla behavior cloning. This directly translates to more reliable and deployable robotic systems.
2. **Increased Sample Efficiency and Reduced Human Labor:** DMD effectively reaps the benefits of DAgger—providing corrective feedback for OOD states—without the prohibitive cost of continuous human supervision and data collection. By showing strong performance even with few expert demonstrations (e.g., 8 demos for pushing), DMD significantly reduces the human effort and time required to train capable robot skills. This makes imitation learning accessible for a wider range of tasks and users.
3. **Demonstration of Generative AI's Power in Robotics:** The work showcases a powerful application of advanced conditional diffusion models in robotic learning. It successfully overcomes the limitations of prior view synthesis methods (like NeRFs) that struggle with dynamic, deforming scenes common in manipulation tasks. This validation opens doors for diffusion models to be integrated into other aspects of robotic perception and planning, especially where realistic scene generation under manipulation is needed.
4. **Broad Applicability Across Diverse Tasks:** The framework's effectiveness across four distinct and challenging manipulation tasks (pushing, stacking, pouring, shirt hanging) with varying action spaces (3DoF, 6DoF), precision requirements, and object interactions demonstrates its versatility. Its compatibility with advanced IL frameworks like Diffusion Policy further broadens its potential use cases.
5. **Practical Insights for Data Augmentation:** The empirical findings regarding the importance of selecting an appropriate future frame for labeling synthetic data (the "overshooting problem") and the utility of "play data" for training the diffusion model offer valuable practical guidance for researchers and practitioners in the field. The result that task data alone can effectively finetune the diffusion model is particularly useful for rapid deployment.
6. **Catalyst for Future Research:** DMD's limitations (e.g., assumptions about state recoverability, challenges in non-visual modality synthesis, complex action label derivation) naturally pave the way for exciting future research directions. This includes exploring diffusion models for predicting discontinuous object dynamics, generating multi-modal OOD states, and developing more sophisticated methods for assigning action labels in highly unstructured scenarios.

By publicly releasing code, data, and models, the authors foster reproducibility and further research, ensuring a broader impact on the robotics and machine learning communities. DMD brings imitation learning closer to real-world applicability by making it more robust and less data-intensive.
