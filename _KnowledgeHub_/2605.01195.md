# TAIL-Safe: Task-Agnostic Safety Monitoring for Imitation Learning Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> TAIL-Safe introduces a framework for runtime safety monitoring and recovery for imitation learning policies, enhancing their robustness in manipulation tasks. This system improved task success rates from 20-25% for vanilla policies to a consistent 100% in both simulation and real-world experiments.

## Problem

- Imitation Learning (IL) policies are susceptible to failure even within their training distribution due to sensitivity to initial conditions, compounding approximation errors, and runtime variations.
- Existing safety approaches in robotics often require explicit knowledge of system dynamics, struggle with high-dimensional visuomotor tasks, or lack mechanisms for active recovery.
- A general, task-agnostic solution for runtime safety monitoring and recovery for pre-trained visuomotor IL policies was missing, hindering their deployment in safety-critical scenarios.

## Method

- **TAIL-Safe** proposes a runtime monitoring and recovery system that defines task-agnostic safety criteria (visibility, recognizability, graspability) for manipulation tasks, fused by a Lipschitz-continuous ==WeightNet==.
- A Safety Q-value function, learned as a ==reach-avoid Bellman operator== with an energy-shaped Lipschitz-continuous landscape, empirically characterizes a control invariant set of safe state-action pairs.
- A gradient-based recovery controller, inspired by ==Nagumo's tangentiality condition==, iteratively adjusts policy actions to steer the system back into the safe operating set when violations occur.

## Results

- The TAIL-Safe framework achieved a **100%** task success rate on Franka Emika robot experiments for 'Candy Picking' and 'Pick-and-Place' tasks, compared to **20-25%** for vanilla IL policies under runtime perturbations.
- Recovery actions were minimal, averaging less than one per episode (**0.63-0.85**), with an inference latency of **2.8 ms**, demonstrating practical efficiency.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the WeightNet was essential for context-aware criterion fusion, and the combination of Lipschitz continuity and energy shaping in the Q-function increased mean gradients **20-fold**, enabling **100%** recovery within an average of **2 steps**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Context-aware fusion of task-agnostic safety criteria via a Lipschitz-continuous WeightNet is crucial for dynamic failure detection across different phases of a manipulation task.
> - Learning a Q-function with a 'Bounded Hill' energy landscape and enforcing Lipschitz continuity provides stable, predictable gradients essential for effective and rapid gradient-based recovery.
> - A high-fidelity digital twin built with Gaussian Splatting enables the systematic and safe collection of failure data, which is vital for training robust safety monitors without risking physical hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation Learning (IL) policies, particularly recent advancements like flow-matching and diffusion policies, have demonstrated proficiency in learning complex manipulation tasks. However, a significant challenge for their practical deployment is their susceptibility to failure even within their training distribution. These failures can stem from sensitivity to initial conditions, compounding approximation errors, and various runtime variations such as perceptual noise or environmental perturbations. Such fragility prevents widespread application in field robotics, especially in safety-critical domains where policy failure could result in damage or endanger individuals.

Existing approaches to safety in robotics include constraint-based training, uncertainty quantification via deep ensembles, and human intervention. Classical control theory offers formal guarantees for safety through methods like Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) and Hamilton-Jacobi reachability. However, these methods typically require explicit knowledge of system dynamics and struggle with the high-dimensional, visuomotor nature of many IL tasks. Learning-based extensions of these control-theoretic methods exist but often still necessitate dynamics models or latent dynamics learning, and have not widely scaled to visual observations. Uncertainty-based methods can detect risky states but generally do not provide mechanisms for recovery.

This paper addresses a gap in the current IL literature by proposing a framework that enables a trained IL policy to assess its own task success assurance at runtime, identify states likely to lead to failure, and execute recovery actions. Unlike many prior works, it aims for a task-agnostic solution that does not require explicit failure demonstrations or detailed dynamics models, making it more directly applicable to pre-trained visuomotor policies. The work integrates concepts from control theory (invariant sets, Nagumo’s tangentiality condition) with modern machine learning techniques (Gaussian Splatting for high-fidelity simulation, Lipschitz-continuous neural networks) to provide an empirical safety assurance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to enable the safe and reliable deployment of imitation learning policies in real-world scenarios. Despite their capabilities in learning complex tasks, current IL policies frequently fail due to their inherent sensitivity to minor changes and the accumulation of errors. This lack of robustness makes them unsuitable for applications where failure carries significant risks, such as industrial settings or human-robot interaction.

The key objectives of the TAIL-Safe framework are:
- **Identify Policy Failure Modes**: To establish a set of task-agnostic criteria that can predict an imitation learning policy's imminent failure in any manipulation task involving grasping an object. This moves beyond task-specific definitions of failure, aiming for broader applicability.
- **Characterize a Safe Operating Set**: To learn a Lipschitz-continuous Q-value function that maps state-action pairs to a long-term safety score. The zero-superlevel set of this function is designed to empirically characterize a control invariant set of state-action pairs from which the policy can reliably complete the learned task. This allows the policy to "know" where in the state space it is assured to succeed.
- **Enable Gradient-Based Recovery**: To design a recovery mechanism that, when the policy proposes an action outside the identified safe set (i.e., when the Q-value approaches zero or becomes negative), can steer the system back to a safe region. This mechanism is inspired by Nagumo’s tangentiality condition, using the gradient of the Q-function to guide the policy.
- **Systematically Collect Failure Data**: To develop a method for collecting necessary failure data without risking physical hardware. This is crucial for training a safety monitor that learns from situations where the policy deviates from expert demonstrations. This objective is addressed through the creation of a high-fidelity digital twin using Gaussian Splatting.
- **Demonstrate Practical Effectiveness**: To empirically validate that the proposed TAIL-Safe framework can significantly improve the task success rate of fragile imitation learning policies when subjected to runtime perturbations in both simulated and real-world environments.

Ultimately, the work aims to bridge the gap between high-performance but fragile IL policies and the practical requirement for safety assurance in deployed robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TAIL-Safe (Task-Agnostic Imitation Learning Safety Watchdog) operates as a runtime monitoring and recovery system for pre-trained deterministic visuomotor IL policies. The framework conceptualizes safety as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where a Q-value function evaluates the long-term task success assurance of state-action pairs.

**A. Task-Agnostic Reward Function (R)**
To define what constitutes a safe state, TAIL-Safe proposes three task-agnostic criteria for manipulation tasks involving grasping, encoded into a scalar safety heuristic function `h(s, a) ∈ [−1, 1]`. This function serves as the immediate reward `R(s, a)`. A positive `h(s, a) > 0` indicates that all criteria are met, while `h(s, a) < 0` signifies a violation.
- **Visibility (s_fov_)**: Measures if the target object is within the sensor's field of view. It's calculated by projecting the object's point cloud into camera coordinates and evaluating point density and distance from the image center.
- **Recognizability (s_rec_)**: Assesses how well the current visual observation aligns with the training data distribution. It uses 128-dimensional embeddings from the policy's visual encoder, reduced by PCA, and scores based on the inverse Mahalanobis distance from a Gaussian model fitted over expert demonstrations.
- **Graspability (s_grasp_)**: Quantifies the geometric quality of potential contact with the target object. It involves semantic segmentation (using SAMv2) to isolate the object's point cloud, sampling antipodal grasp candidates, and scoring the alignment between the current end-effector pose and the nearest high-quality grasp pose.

These three scores are fused into a single scalar `h(s, a)` by a **WeightNet**, a multi-layer perceptron. Unlike a fixed weighted sum, WeightNet learns dynamic weights that adapt to the current task phase (e.g., visibility dominates during approach, graspability near contact). The network is trained via binary cross-entropy on trajectory-level labels, and Lipschitz continuity is enforced via $\ell_{\infty}$-norm row-wise weight normalization.

**B. Q-Function for Control Invariant Set Identification**
To capture long-term safety, TAIL-Safe defines a Safety Q-value function `Q(s, a)`. Instead of cumulative discounted rewards, it models `Q(s, a)` as the minimum safety margin encountered along the future trajectory induced by the policy $\pi$:
`Q(s, a) = E_{\pi} [min_{k≥0} γ^k R(s_{t+k}, a_{t+k}) | s_t = s, a_t = a]`
A non-negative `Q(s, a) ≥ 0` empirically indicates task success assurance based on training data. The zero-superlevel set `C_safe_ = {(s, a) ∈ S × A | Q(s, a) ≥ 0}` defines an empirical control invariant set for the policy.

**1. Digital Twin for Data Collection:**
A core challenge is safely acquiring failure data. TAIL-Safe addresses this by constructing a high-fidelity digital twin using Gaussian Splatting (from ∼100 RGB images). This twin allows systematic collection of perturbed rollouts (`∼500 per task, with ∼40% failures`) in a risk-free environment. Object Gaussians are updated online from the wrist-mounted RGB-D stream, ensuring the twin mirrors the real-world state. Perturbations include object displacement (±5cm), rotation (±30°), and Gaussian noise injection.

**2. Reach-Avoid Bellman Operator:**
The Q-function is trained using a modified Bellman recursion that propagates the minimum reward:
`Q_π(s_t, a_t) = min(h(s_t, a_t), γ ⋅ Q_π(s_{t+1}, π(s_{t+1})))`
Targets `y_t` are computed via backward recursion from terminal outcomes (+1 for success, -1 for failure). The Q-network is then regressed to these targets using `L_anchor = E[(Q_ϕ(s_t, a_t) - y_t)^2]`.

**3. Learning Q as an Energy Function:**
To ensure informative gradients for recovery, `Q(s, a)` is shaped as an "energy function" with a "Bounded Hill" geometry. This means `Q` peaks at demonstrated `(s, a)` pairs and decays smoothly as actions deviate. This is enforced via a hinge loss `L_hill = E_{(s,a*)~D} [max(0, Q(s, a) - (1 - α∥a - a*∥^2))]`. The complete objective combines `L_anchor` and `L_hill`. This shaping is applied only at states from successful trajectories to prevent artificial safe regions.

**4. Lipschitz Continuity of Q:**
To ensure stable and predictable gradient-based recovery, the Q-function is implemented as a 4-layer MLP with spectral normalization on each layer and Softplus activations. This enforces Lipschitz continuity, bounding the global Lipschitz constant `L_Q ≤ 2.5`, which in turn ensures predictable step magnitudes and guaranteed progress towards `C_safe_`.

**C. Recovery Controller**
At deployment, TAIL-Safe monitors `Q(s, a)` in real-time. If `Q(s, a) ≥ 0`, the policy's action is executed directly. If `Q(s, a) < 0`, a gradient-based recovery mechanism is initiated, inspired by Nagumo’s tangentiality condition. It performs projected gradient ascent on `Q`:
`a^{(k+1)} = Proj_A [a^{(k)} + η ⋅ ∇_a Q(s_t, a^{(k)}) / ∥∇_a Q(s_t, a^{(k)})∥_2]`
This iteratively adjusts the action until `Q ≥ 0` or after `k_max = 10` iterations. The Lipschitz constraint `L_Q` guarantees a bounded recovery step `∥∆a∥_2 = η` and a lower-bounded safety improvement per step.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Policy Failure vs. TAIL-Safe Guided Success**
Experiments were conducted on a Franka Emika robot for two tabletop manipulation tasks: "Candy Picking" and "Pick-and-Place," under object displacements (±5cm) and rotations (±30°).
- **Vanilla Policy Performance**: Without TAIL-Safe, the vanilla flow-matching policy achieved 20.0-25.0% success rates in simulation and 20.0-25.0% on the real robot for both tasks. Common failure modes included wrong orientation, failure to reach goals, incorrect object selection, and getting lost after grasping.
- **TAIL-Safe Guided Performance**: When guided by TAIL-Safe, the policy achieved a consistent 100% success rate across all conditions in both simulation and real-world experiments (Table I). This was accompanied by more efficient episodes (74-81 steps with TAIL-Safe vs. 92-95 without) and an average of less than one recovery action per episode (0.63-0.70 in simulation, 0.80-0.85 in real robot).

**B. Resilience to Runtime Perturbations**
TAIL-Safe demonstrated resilience to tested perturbations. When the Q-value approached zero or became negative, the recovery controller successfully steered the robot back to a safe region, ensuring task completion. However, limitations were observed:
- **Extreme Proprioceptive Deviation**: If the robot's configuration deviates significantly from its training distribution, no bounded safe set might exist.
- **Extreme Object Displacement**: Beyond training bounds (>5cm, >30° rotation), the Q-function encountered unseen embeddings, leading to unreliable predictions and potential failure.

**C. Baseline Comparison**
TAIL-Safe was compared against three baseline safety monitors on a 480-episode dataset (59,779 state-action pairs):
- **Ensemble Disagreement**: Achieved an AUROC of 0.525, indicating near-chance detection. Ensemble mean actions were actively harmful for recovery (mean Q-value change of -0.86). Inference latency was 15.0 ms.
- **Learned CBF**: Showed good detection (AUROC 0.987) but suffered from flat gradients. 97.4% of its gradients in the unsafe region were near-zero, resulting in only 6.9% successful gradient-based recoveries against an oracle Q. Inference latency was ∼3 ms.
- **Instantaneous h(s, a)**: Achieved high per-step AUROC (0.999), but lacked temporal aggregation. On "deceptive" states (where `h` was positive but the episode later failed), TAIL-Safe's trajectory-level Q provided 3.2× better separation between safe and unsafe states (Cohen’s d: 0.93 vs. 0.29).

TAIL-Safe Q(s, a) achieved an AUROC of 0.999, 100% oracle-true recovery, and only 1.1% flat gradients, with an inference latency of 2.8 ms (Table III). These results indicate that the combination of reach-avoid Q, energy-shaped Lipschitz landscape, and WeightNet fusion is crucial for effective recovery.

**D. Ablation Study**
1. **WeightNet for Context-Aware Criterion Fusion**: WeightNet achieved 100.0% safe trajectory recall and 98.4% safe state recall (Table II). In contrast, a fixed equal-weights fusion method failed to identify any safe trajectories (0.0% recall), demonstrating the necessity of context-aware weighting (e.g., visibility importance during approach vs. graspability near contact). It provided a detection lead time of 8.2 timesteps (410ms) before failure.
2. **Lipschitz Constraint and Energy Shaping for Recovery**:
 - **No Constraints**: Led to a flat Q-landscape (mean gradient 0.036), resulting in only 20% recovery success over 170 steps.
 - **+ Lipschitz**: Improved stability, increasing recovery success to 35% in 67 steps, but gradients remained weak.
 - **+ Energy Shaping**: With both Lipschitz and energy shaping, mean gradients increased 20-fold (to 0.802), enabling 100% recovery success within an average of 2 steps (Table IV). This confirms their synergistic contribution to stable and effective gradient-based recovery.
3. **Q-Function Calibration Analysis**: The Q-function exhibited good discriminative power (AUROC 99.3%, AUPRC 99.7%). Critical safety metrics were favorable: false safe rate of 0.84% and false unsafe rate of 1.58%. An Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.37 indicated reasonable calibration, supporting its reliability for safety classification (Table V).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TAIL-Safe framework represents a significant step towards enabling the safe and reliable deployment of imitation learning policies in real-world robotic applications. By providing an empirical task success assurance at runtime, it addresses a fundamental limitation of current IL approaches: their fragility to unmodeled variations and compounding errors.

The key contributions and their potential impact include:
- **Runtime Safety Assurance for IL**: TAIL-Safe provides a practical mechanism for IL policies to monitor their own safety and recover from impending failures. This is crucial for expanding the applicability of IL from controlled laboratory settings to diverse, less predictable environments, particularly in safety-critical domains where policy failure can have severe consequences.
- **Bridging Control Theory and Visuomotor IL**: The framework intelligently adapts concepts from classical control theory (e.g., control invariant sets, Nagumo’s tangentiality) to the high-dimensional, visuomotor domain of IL, circumventing the need for explicit dynamics models. This offers a novel approach to formalizing safety in learning-based systems.
- **Task-Agnostic Failure Identification**: The use of task-agnostic criteria (visibility, recognizability, graspability) makes the system broadly applicable across various manipulation tasks, reducing the burden of task-specific safety engineering.
- **Safe Failure Data Collection**: The innovative use of a high-fidelity Gaussian Splatting digital twin for systematically generating failure data without risking physical hardware is a practical solution to a common bottleneck in training robust safety monitors. This approach accelerates the development and validation of safety systems.
- **Robust Recovery Mechanism**: The Lipschitz-continuous, energy-shaped Q-function, combined with a gradient-based recovery controller, ensures that corrective actions are stable, predictable, and effective in steering the robot back to a safe operating region.

While TAIL-Safe provides empirical rather than formal guarantees, contingent on the runtime environment matching the training distribution, the observed substantial improvement in task success (from 20-25% to 100%) demonstrates its practical value. This work lays groundwork for more robust and trustworthy autonomous systems, pushing IL policies closer to being deployed in scenarios where safety is paramount. Future work extending to stochastic policies, adaptive safe sets, and more complex environments could further broaden its impact.
