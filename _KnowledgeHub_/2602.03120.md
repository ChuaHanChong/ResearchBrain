# Quantized Evolution Strategies: High-precision Fine-tuning of Quantized LLMs at Low-precision Cost

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UCLA and Cognizant AI Lab introduce Quantized Evolution Strategies (QES), a method for high-precision, full-parameter fine-tuning of quantized Large Language Models directly within their low-precision discrete parameter space. QES achieves significant performance improvements (e.g., up to 31.85% accuracy on Qwen2.5-3B INT4) over prior zeroth-order methods while maintaining an inference-level memory footprint.

## Problem

- Fine-tuning quantized Large Language Models (LLMs) is difficult because traditional backpropagation methods require high-precision, differentiable operations incompatible with discrete, low-precision weights.
- Existing zeroth-order (ZO) optimization and Evolution Strategies (ES) for quantized models suffer from "stagnation" where small gradient signals vanish or become inaccurate in discrete parameter spaces.
- Storing high-precision optimizer states (e.g., error vectors or Adam states) for quantized models can negate the memory benefits of quantization, hindering deployment on resource-constrained devices.

## Method

- QES combines Evolution Strategies with a ==stochastic discrete perturbation strategy and boundary gating== to operate directly within the quantized parameter space.
- It implements an ==accumulated error feedback mechanism==, inspired by Delta-Sigma modulation, which accumulates fractional updates in a high-precision residual vector to prevent gradient stagnation.
- A ==Stateless Seed Replay mechanism== is used to reconstruct the high-precision error vector on-the-fly from a limited history of random seeds and rewards, drastically reducing memory overhead.

## Results

- QES significantly improved arithmetic reasoning accuracy on the Qwen2.5-3B INT4 model from a base of **2.80%** and QuZO's **14.25%** to **31.85%**.
- The stateless seed replay method achieved performance remarkably close to storing the full high-precision residual (e.g., **31.85%** vs. **33.50%** for Qwen2.5-3B INT4), demonstrating minimal approximation impact.
- Analysis showed that parameter updates are sparse (~**10^-2** of parameters change per step), and updates hitting quantization boundaries are extremely rare (**<**1×**10^-5** for INT4), ensuring high fidelity for stateless reconstruction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accumulating fractional update errors over time allows for precise gradient ascent in discrete parameter spaces, enabling effective optimization despite the integer-based parameter updates.
> - Maintaining a high-precision error state is crucial for optimization in quantized spaces, and this state can be efficiently reconstructed without being stored, trading computation for memory.
> - Parameter updates in quantized models are sparse, and instances where updates hit quantization boundaries are rare, which validates the high fidelity and low approximation error of the stateless reconstruction method.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to advancements in capabilities across various domains, including reasoning, coding, and problem-solving. However, these models typically require substantial computational resources, particularly in terms of memory, for both deployment and fine-tuning. Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) has become a standard practice to mitigate the memory bottleneck for inference, enabling the deployment of LLMs on memory-constrained devices by reducing their precision to 3-4 bits (e.g., using techniques like SmoothQuant, GPTQ, and AWQ).

While PTQ facilitates efficient inference, it generally renders the model as a static artifact. Fine-tuning conventionally occurs prior to quantization and necessitates significant computational infrastructure. Direct fine-tuning of already quantized models presents several challenges. Standard fine-tuning paradigms, such as Reinforcement Learning (RL), rely on backpropagation and continuous weight spaces to compute gradients. This dependence makes them incompatible with quantized models, where parameters reside in a discrete, non-differentiable space. Existing approaches like the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) for backpropagation in quantized networks suffer from bias and require storing full-precision latent weights and optimizer states, which undermines the memory benefits of quantization. Similarly, methods like QLoRA are memory-efficient by freezing quantized weights and optimizing only small, high-precision adapters, but they do not allow for full-parameter updates of the quantized model itself.

Zeroth-Order (ZO) optimization and Evolution Strategies (ES) have emerged as backpropagation-free alternatives for fine-tuning LLMs, demonstrated in methods like MeZO and prior ES work. These methods offer memory efficiency by estimating gradients through parameter perturbations and fitness evaluations. However, these techniques were initially designed for continuous, high-precision parameter spaces. When applied directly to discrete quantized spaces, prior ZO/ES methods (e.g., QuZO, QZO) have exhibited limitations, particularly in complex reasoning tasks. The paper identifies a "stagnation problem," where the discrete nature of the parameter space causes gradient signals to vanish or become inaccurate, impeding effective optimization.

This research addresses the gap in the landscape by introducing a method for full-parameter fine-tuning of quantized LLMs directly within their discrete parameter space, while simultaneously overcoming the issues of gradient stagnation, inaccuracy, and high memory overhead associated with optimizer states. It draws inspiration from signal processing principles, specifically Delta-Sigma modulation and error feedback mechanisms historically used in communication-efficient training (e.g., 1-bit Stochastic Gradient Descent), to adapt these concepts for backpropagation-free quantized optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable the effective and memory-efficient fine-tuning of Large Language Models (LLMs) directly within their low-precision, quantized parameter space. This capability is critical for deploying and adapting LLMs on resource-constrained hardware where Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) is a necessity.

The motivation behind Quantized Evolution Strategies (QES) arises from several specific challenges inherent in the existing LLM fine-tuning and quantization landscape:

1. **Overcoming Optimization Stagnation in Discrete Spaces**: Quantized models operate on a discrete lattice of integer parameters. Standard fine-tuning methods, even backpropagation-free approaches like Evolution Strategies (ES) or Zeroth-Order (ZO) optimization, struggle to make progress when the estimated update steps are smaller than the minimum discretization gap (e.g., one integer unit). Naively rounding these small updates often results in a zero parameter change, leading to "stagnation" where the optimization process halts or becomes highly inefficient due to vanishing or inaccurate gradient signals. The paper aims to develop a mechanism that can effectively utilize these minuscule gradient signals to ensure continuous and precise optimization.

2. **Reducing Memory Footprint to Inference Levels**: While quantization significantly reduces the memory footprint of model weights, the optimizer states (e.g., momentum buffers, accumulated gradients) used in standard fine-tuning methods typically require high-precision (e.g., FP16 or FP32) storage. For large LLMs, these optimizer states can consume gigabytes of VRAM, often negating the memory benefits of quantization and rendering full-parameter fine-tuning impractical on consumer-grade hardware. A key motivation is to design an optimization framework that maintains an optimizer state with a memory footprint comparable to that of low-precision inference itself.

3. **Preserving High-Precision Learning Dynamics**: The discrete nature of quantized parameters introduces discretization errors that can reduce the accuracy of parameter updates, leading to suboptimal or unstable learning trajectories. The research aims to develop a method that can maintain the fidelity of high-precision learning dynamics, effectively tracking the continuous optimization trajectory even when operating solely in a discrete parameter space. This is motivated by the need for fine-tuning performance that approaches that of full-precision models, without their associated computational burden.

By addressing these challenges, QES aims to democratize access to LLM fine-tuning, making it feasible in low-resource environments and potentially enabling a new paradigm for scaling LLMs by allowing more parameters to be included within the same memory budget through aggressive quantization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Quantized Evolution Strategies (QES) is an optimization paradigm designed to perform full-parameter fine-tuning directly on quantized LLMs in their discrete integer space (W ∈ {0..., 2^B - 1}^d). The methodology centers on two innovations: accumulated error feedback and stateless seed replay.

#### 3.1. Evolution Strategies for Quantized Models

QES utilizes Evolution Strategies (ES) to estimate gradient directions without backpropagation. The objective is to maximize a reward function J(W).
- **Discrete Perturbations**: Unlike standard ES that uses continuous Gaussian perturbations (σϵ), QES generates discrete perturbations δ. This is achieved by stochastically rounding scaled Gaussian noise: δ = ⌊σϵ⌋ + b, where ϵ ∼ N(0, I) and b is a Bernoulli random variable ensuring unbiased rounding.
- **Boundary Gating**: To ensure that updates remain within the quantized codebook limits, a boundary gating mechanism is applied: W' = Gate(W + δ), which masks invalid updates.
- **Gradient Estimation**: The gradient direction ˆg is estimated by aggregating discrete search directions weighted by their rewards (F_i): ˆg = (1/Nσ) Σ F_i ⋅ δ_i.

#### 3.2. Accumulated Error Feedback

This mechanism addresses the problem of optimization stagnation and inaccurate updates in discrete spaces.
- **Challenge**: The desired update step (αˆg) is often smaller than the minimum discretization gap (e.g., one integer unit). Naive rounding would result in ∆W = 0, halting optimization, or introducing significant errors.
- **Inspiration**: This challenge is analogous to problems in signal processing, specifically Delta-Sigma modulation, where quantization error is accumulated over time to preserve signal fidelity. This principle has also been applied in communication-efficient training (e.g., 1-bit SGD) to carry forward uncompressed errors.
- **QES Implementation**: QES maintains a high-precision error vector e_t (typically FP16) that accumulates the quantization error from previous steps. The update dynamics are defined as:
 1. **High-precision update (u_t)**: u_t = αˆg_t + γe_{t-1}. This sums the current scaled gradient estimate with a decayed version of the accumulated error from the previous step. γ ∈ (0, 1] is a decay factor to stabilize history.
 2. **Discrete weight update (∆W_t)**: ∆W_t = Round(u_t). The desired high-precision update is rounded to the nearest integer.
 3. **Error accumulation (e_t)**: e_t = u_t - ∆W_t. The fractional component (the difference between the desired update and the actual discrete update) is stored as the new error to be carried forward.
- **Effect**: This mechanism allows infinitesimal gradient signals to accumulate within e_t over multiple iterations. An actual discrete weight change (∆W ≠ 0) is triggered only when the accumulated signal crosses the rounding threshold (|u| ≥ 0.5). This ensures that the quantized model's physical weights (W_t) never deviate by more than half a grid step from an ideal, underlying continuous optimization trajectory (Θ_t = W_t + e_t), thus maintaining high-precision learning dynamics.

#### 3.3. Stateless Seed Replay

This component addresses the significant memory overhead incurred by storing the dense, high-precision error vector e_t, which can negate the memory benefits of quantization.
- **Challenge**: The error vector e_t is a dense FP16 tensor with the same dimension as the model parameters (d), making its storage prohibitive for large LLMs.
- **Solution**: Instead of explicitly storing e_t, it is reconstructed on-the-fly when needed. This is possible because e_t is deterministically derivable from the initial state and the history of optimization steps.
- **Mechanism**:
 1. **Lightweight History Buffer**: QES maintains a circular buffer H_t = {(S_τ, F_τ)} for a limited window of the past K generations. This buffer stores only the random seeds (S) used to generate perturbations and the scalar rewards (F) for each population.
 2. **On-the-fly Reconstruction**: To compute e_t at any step, the error accumulation process (Equations 6-8) is re-simulated for the past K steps, starting from an assumed zero error state at step t-K. The decay factor γ ensures that contributions from steps beyond K steps ago are negligible (γ^K ≈ 0).
 3. **Approximation**: During this replay, boundary constraints are checked using the *current* weights W_t, rather than reconstructing historical weights W_τ. This is considered a minimal discrepancy because discrete updates are sparse, meaning W_τ ≈ W_t.
- **Effect**: This approach trades computational cost (K additional reconstruction operations per update) for memory. It reduces the optimizer state memory complexity from O(d) to O(K), where K is orders of magnitude smaller than d (e.g., K ≈ 50 for d > 10^9). This brings the GPU memory requirements for QES down to the level of low-precision inference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates QES across various LLM architectures, quantization formats, and task families, comparing its performance against a set of baselines.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Tasks**:
 - **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: RoBERTa-large on SNLI, MNLI, RTE, and SST-5 (classification accuracy).
 - **Reasoning Tasks**: Countdown and GSM8K (binary correctness). Qwen2.5-1.5B and Qwen2.5-3B were used as backbones, with an additional scaling case study on Llama-3.1-8B.
- **Quantization Formats**: INT4, INT8 (weights only via GPTQ), and W8A8 (weights and activations via LLM-Compressor).
- **Baselines**: Base Model (quantized, no fine-tuning), QuZO (state-of-the-art quantized zeroth-order method), MeZO (full-precision zeroth-order SGD), First-Order (FP32 and W8 with Straight-Through Estimator), and Full Residual (an oracle QES variant that stores FP16 residuals explicitly, used for ablation).

#### 4.2. Supervised Fine-Tuning Results

- QES significantly improved performance on the SFT tasks (Table 1). On RoBERTa-large with 8-bit weights (W8), QES achieved an average accuracy of 44.4%, substantially outperforming QuZO (34.2%) and the W8 First-Order baseline with STE (41.0%).
- Notably, QES even surpassed the full-precision MeZO baseline (36.5%), which operates in FP32, suggesting that the accumulated error feedback mechanism in QES effectively recovers more useful update signals than smoothed zeroth-order estimation even when the latter has access to continuous parameters.
- While a gap remained to the full-precision First-Order method (57.3%), QES closed a significant portion of this gap without requiring backpropagation or large optimizer states.

#### 4.3. Reasoning Task Results

- QES demonstrated consistent and often substantial gains across all reasoning tasks, model sizes, and quantization formats (Table 2).
- QuZO exhibited brittleness, particularly on the coarser INT4 lattice and smaller 1.5B model (e.g., on Qwen2.5-1.5B INT4, QuZO improved by only 1.75 points on Countdown and showed no improvement on GSM8K).
- In contrast, QES achieved large gains. For example, on Qwen2.5-3B W8A8, QuZO collapsed to 4.40% accuracy on GSM8K, while QES reached 80.82%.
- The performance gap between QuZO and QES generally widened with increasing task difficulty (Countdown to GSM8K) and narrowed with larger model scale, consistent with the hypothesis that larger models present smoother optimization landscapes.
- A scaling case study with Llama-3.1-8B INT4 (Appendix C, Table 5) showed QES improving accuracy from a 64.14% base to 82.64% on GSM8K, demonstrating its generalizability across architectures and larger scales without specific hyperparameter tuning.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies of Stateless Seed Replay

- **Residual Storage (Stateless Replay vs. Full Residual)**: Comparing QES with Stateless Seed Replay against the Full Residual oracle (which stores FP16 residuals explicitly) showed that the two variants performed within a few percentage points across most configurations (Table 6). In several cases, the seed-replay variant matched or slightly exceeded the oracle, indicating that the approximation introduced by replaying historical states with current weights is generally accurate and its practical impact is often on the scale of seed variance.
- **Window Size and Decay (K and γ)**: Evaluation on Qwen2.5-1.5B INT4 (Countdown) (Appendix D, Table 7 Top) demonstrated that performance is sensitive to the decay factor γ. When γ was scaled to ensure historical errors vanished within the window (γ^K ≈ 0), shrinking K from 50 to 10 significantly reduced accuracy. However, with a fixed γ=0.90, even a smaller window K=10 retained substantial performance (13.05% vs 16.00% for K=50), indicating that the collapse was due to aggressive decay rather than merely truncated history. This suggests K can be tuned to trade accuracy for reduced computational cost.
- **Fidelity of Stateless Seed Replay**: The analysis confirmed that reconstruction errors arising from using current weights (W_t) during replay are minimal (Appendix D, Table 7 Bottom). The update ratio (fraction of parameters changed per step) was consistently low (~10^-2), and the boundary hit ratio (fraction of changes landing on a quantization boundary) was negligible (<10^-5 for INT4). The simultaneous occurrence required for a reconstruction error is therefore exceedingly rare.

#### 4.5. Computational Cost Analysis

- **Memory Footprint**: QES's optimizer state memory was consistently around 29.7 KB across all configurations (Table 8), matching the inference-only footprint of QuZO. This is significantly lower than the gigabytes required by the Full Residual variant or typical Quantization-Aware Training (QAT).
- **Wall-clock Time**: Stateless Seed Replay introduces a computational overhead proportional to K (Table 9). For K=50, it added approximately 16.7% overhead for 1.5B models and 12.5% for 3B models over the no-replay oracle in a naive sequential implementation. This overhead can be mitigated by tuning K and parallelizing reconstruction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Quantized Evolution Strategies (QES) represents a notable advancement in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuning, particularly for resource-constrained environments.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
1. **Enabling Direct Fine-tuning of Quantized LLMs**: QES successfully addresses the fundamental challenge of performing full-parameter fine-tuning directly on models with discrete, low-precision quantized weights. This capability was previously limited by the incompatibility of standard gradient-based methods with non-differentiable spaces and the inherent limitations of prior zeroth-order optimization techniques.
2. **Overcoming Optimization Stagnation and Inaccuracy**: The accumulated error feedback mechanism, inspired by signal processing principles, effectively resolves the "stagnation problem" where minute gradient signals in discrete spaces would otherwise lead to stalled optimization. By integrating these signals, QES ensures consistent progress and accurate parameter updates, enabling effective learning even in ultra-low bit settings.
3. **Achieving Inference-Level Memory Footprint**: The Stateless Seed Replay mechanism dramatically reduces the memory overhead associated with optimizer states from gigabytes to kilobytes. This innovation allows for full-parameter learning on hardware that previously could only accommodate quantized inference, thereby democratizing access to LLM fine-tuning for a broader range of users and devices.
4. **Demonstrated Performance Gains**: QES consistently outperformed state-of-the-art quantized fine-tuning methods like QuZO and, in several instances, surpassed even full-precision zeroth-order baselines (MeZO) across various supervised fine-tuning and complex reasoning tasks. Its robust performance across different model scales (1.5B to 8B) and quantization formats (INT4, INT8, W8A8) indicates its generalizability and practical utility.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Democratization of LLM Adaptation**: By enabling high-precision learning dynamics at the memory cost of low-precision inference, QES allows individuals and organizations with consumer-grade hardware (e.g., a single GPU with limited VRAM) to fine-tune large LLMs. This can significantly broaden access to and customization of powerful AI models, fostering innovation in diverse applications.
- **Resource Efficiency and Sustainability**: The substantial reduction in memory and computational requirements for fine-tuning contributes to more resource-efficient AI development. This could lead to a lower energy footprint and reduced environmental impact associated with scaling and deploying large models.
- **A New Scaling Paradigm for LLMs**: The research suggests a novel pathway for future LLM scaling. By trading precision for parameter count and requiring only low-precision inference during training, it might be possible to train models with one or two orders of magnitude more parameters on the same hardware. This could unlock emergent capabilities in even larger, more aggressively quantized architectures.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: QES opens several avenues for further research, including extending the framework to more aggressive or non-uniform quantization schemes (e.g., binary networks, FP4), developing adaptive algorithms for managing replay window size and decay rates, and exploring its application in other deep learning domains beyond LLMs.

In conclusion, QES represents a significant technical advancement that makes fine-tuning large language models on memory-constrained devices a practical reality, potentially transforming how LLMs are developed, deployed, and scaled.
