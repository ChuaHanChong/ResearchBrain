# PolySim: Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Humanoid Control via Multi-Simulator Dynamics Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> PolySim introduces a framework for training humanoid control policies by simultaneously leveraging multiple heterogeneous physics simulators, effectively addressing the sim-to-real gap through dynamics-level randomization. This approach enables policies to generalize robustly to unseen simulator dynamics and facilitates zero-shot deployment on physical robots, increasing success rates by up to 52.8% on an unseen MuJoCo domain compared to single-simulator baselines.

## Problem

- Policies trained in a single simulator often overfit to its specific dynamics and inductive biases, leading to a significant "sim-to-real gap" when deployed on real robots.
- Existing domain randomization methods are limited to perturbing parameters within a single simulator's fixed physics model, failing to address fundamental model-class mismatches.
- Training complex, high-dimensional humanoid whole-body control policies directly on physical robots is impractical due to safety concerns, cost, and data collection difficulties.

## Method

- **PolySim** trains a single reinforcement learning policy concurrently across multiple heterogeneous physics simulators to achieve dynamics-level domain randomization.
- A distributed client-server architecture isolates the `TrainClient` from multiple `SimServers` for elastic distributed execution and fault isolation, using a GPU-direct communication pipeline (PyTorch RPC over NCCL) for high-throughput, low-latency data exchange.
- A `==Simulator Router==` harmonizes diverse simulator characteristics, providing a unified API through physics harmonization, API translation, and numerical normalization.

## Results

- PolySim achieved superior zero-shot generalization to an unseen MuJoCo simulator (+**52.8%** over IsaacSim, +**44.3%** over IsaacGym) while enhancing in-distribution robustness and precision on seen simulators, reducing tracking error while maintaining high success rates.
- The framework proved experimentally efficient, maintaining high training throughput with only a marginal overhead of +**0.1** to +**0.3** seconds per iteration compared to the slowest single simulator.
- Policies trained using PolySim were successfully deployed zero-shot onto a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, confirming its effectiveness in bridging the sim-to-real gap for complex whole-body motions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training across multiple simulators randomizes the underlying dynamics models themselves, not just parameters; the resulting mixed transition kernel is theoretically closer (Wasserstein distance) to real dynamics than any single simulator, lowering the sim-to-real gap.
> - Multi-simulator training offers synergistic benefits, improving generalization to unseen domains and enhancing in-distribution robustness and precision as a regularization effect, rather than just additive gains.
> - PolySim's parallel training avoids the "catastrophic forgetting" issues observed in sequential multi-simulator training approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid whole-body control (WBC) has experienced significant advancements, largely driven by progress in reinforcement learning (RL) and high-fidelity physics simulators. Training complex, high-dimensional, and contact-rich humanoid control policies in the real world is often impractical due to safety concerns, cost, and the difficulty of data collection. Consequently, simulator-based training has become a standard practice, allowing for the generation of extensive interaction data.

However, a fundamental challenge in robot learning, known as the "sim-to-real gap," persists. This gap arises because simulators provide only approximate models of real-world physics, each incorporating inherent assumptions and limitations, collectively termed "simulator inductive bias." Policies trained within a single simulator often overfit to its specific dynamics, leading to suboptimal performance when deployed on different simulators or, more critically, on physical robots.

Existing approaches to mitigate the sim-to-real gap primarily fall into two categories:
1. **Domain Randomization (DR):** This method involves perturbing observation, action, latent states, or a limited set of physical parameters within a *single* simulator. While it can enhance robustness to minor parameter variations, it does not address the fundamental model-class mismatch stemming from the simulator's core transition model, contact solver, time integrator, or actuator models. Randomized rollouts, therefore, remain confined to a narrow neighborhood of that simulator's dynamics.
2. **System Identification (SysID):** This involves tuning simulator parameters to match real-world hardware data. While effective in improving transfer, SysID often demands substantial real-world robot data, can be device-specific, and still operates within the inductive bias of a single physics engine.

More recent work has explored cross-simulator frameworks like HumanoidVerse and RoboVerse (MetaSim), which aim to improve interoperability and ease of switching between different simulation engines. These platforms facilitate sim-to-sim and sim-to-real studies by providing unified interfaces or datasets. However, they generally emphasize modularity and switching between engines rather than enabling *parallel, concurrent optimization* of a single policy across multiple heterogeneous simulators within one training loop.

PolySim differentiates itself by directly addressing the simulator inductive bias through "dynamics-level domain randomization." Instead of merely randomizing parameters within one simulator, it proposes to train policies simultaneously across *multiple* distinct simulators. This approach aims to expose the policy to a broader and more diverse set of transition dynamics during training, thereby encouraging the learned controller to generalize beyond the specific assumptions of any single simulator and reduce the sim-to-real gap more effectively than previous methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust whole-body control (WBC) policy for humanoid robots that exhibits strong generalization capabilities, particularly for zero-shot deployment on real hardware. The central motivation stems from the inherent challenge of the "sim-to-real gap," which significantly impedes the practical application of reinforcement learning policies trained solely in simulation.

The detailed motivations and objectives are as follows:

- **Mitigate Simulator Inductive Bias:** The core problem identified is the "simulator inductive bias," which refers to the inherent modeling assumptions, numerical solvers, and physical approximations embedded within any single physics simulator. These biases lead to substantial discrepancies both between different simulators and between simulation and the real world. A policy trained in one simulator tends to learn dynamics specific to that simulator, making it brittle when transferred to environments with different underlying physics. The objective is to design a training methodology that reduces this bias.
- **Overcome Limitations of Parameter-Level Domain Randomization:** While parameter-level domain randomization (DR) is a common strategy to improve sim-to-real transfer, its effectiveness is limited. DR perturbs specific physical parameters (e.g., friction, mass) around a single simulator's nominal dynamics. However, the underlying physics engine, with its unique contact solvers, time integrators, and actuator models, remains unchanged. This means the randomized dynamics still occupy a narrow "neighborhood" defined by that specific simulator's inductive bias (as illustrated in Figure 2). The motivation is to move beyond this limitation by randomizing the *dynamics model itself* rather than just its parameters.
- **Achieve Generalization Beyond Single Simulators:** The goal is to encourage the learned controller to capture dynamic behaviors that are robust and generalizable across a wider spectrum of physical realities, rather than being optimized for the idiosyncrasies of a single simulation environment. This implies learning a policy that is less sensitive to the specific modeling choices of any one simulator.
- **Enable Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A significant practical objective is to enable policies trained in simulation to be deployed directly onto a real humanoid robot (zero-shot) without requiring extensive fine-tuning or adaptation with real-world data. This would dramatically reduce the cost, time, and safety risks associated with robot development.
- **Develop an Efficient and Scalable Multi-Simulator Training Platform:** To achieve the above, a key objective is to build a robust and efficient training framework capable of integrating multiple heterogeneous simulators. This framework must support parallel execution, handle discrepancies in simulator APIs and physical parameters, and maintain high training throughput.

By addressing these motivations, PolySim aims to bridge the sim-to-real gap more effectively, enabling the development of more robust and transferable humanoid control policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PolySim's methodology is centered on the principle of dynamics-level domain randomization, achieved by training a single policy concurrently across multiple heterogeneous simulators. This approach is realized through three key system designs: Training–Simulation Isolation, a Simulator Router, and GPU-Direct Communication. A theoretical framework also underpins its claimed superiority.

#### 3.1. System Architecture

PolySim employs a distributed client-server architecture to manage the training and simulation processes:
- **Training–Simulation Isolation:** Unlike monolithic designs where the RL learner and simulators run within a single process, PolySim decouples these components into a `TrainClient` and multiple `SimServers`. Each `SimServer` is responsible for advancing its local physics simulation and returning trajectory data (observations, rewards, done signals, info). The `TrainClient` aggregates these trajectories and updates the policy. This isolation provides several benefits:
 - **Elastic Distributed Execution:** Allows training across multiple GPUs and machines.
 - **Toolchain Independence:** The learner's software stack is not constrained by simulator-specific dependencies.
 - **Fault Isolation:** Failures or slowdowns in one simulator do not affect or block others.

#### 3.2. Simulator Router (Unified Interface Virtualization)

The `Simulator Router` is a critical component that harmonizes the diverse characteristics of heterogeneous simulators, presenting a unified, simulator-agnostic interface to the `TrainClient`. This is achieved through three mechanisms:
- **Physics Harmonization:** At initialization, the router takes a unified physical specification and instantiates scenes in each simulator to be as consistent as possible. This involves mapping parameters such as friction, contact settings, actuator models, controller gains, gravity, integration steps, rigid-body properties, and robot descriptions to the corresponding options of each simulator. Residual mismatches due to inherent differences in contact solvers or integrators are treated as controlled dynamics variations.
- **API Translation:** The router translates the heterogeneous APIs of different simulators into a common observation–action–reward contract. It aligns state layouts, reference frames, and ensures that only the physical quantities required by the policy and reward function are materialized. This results in identically shaped tensors with consistent semantic meaning, regardless of the underlying simulation engine.
- **Numerical Normalization:** The router reconciles simulator-specific numerical conventions to ensure that actions output by the neural network are correctly interpreted by each engine. This includes unit conversions (e.g., standardizing angular quantities to radians), range mapping from a normalized action space to engine-specific joint limits or position-target ranges, and enforcing clipping consistent with actuator and controller limits to maintain safety and correctness.

Through these steps, the Simulator Router abstracts away backend idiosyncrasies, enabling stable and high-throughput parallel rollouts from diverse simulators within a single training loop.

#### 3.3. GPU-Direct Communication Pipeline

To maintain high throughput for parallel rollouts, PolySim employs a high-performance communication pipeline.
- **RPC over NCCL:** Observations, actions, rewards, and masks are kept as device tensors end-to-end. Cross-process exchanges between `TrainClient` and `SimServers` utilize PyTorch RPC over NCCL, leveraging NVLink or high-speed PCIe. This approach avoids CPU round trips and redundant data serialization/deserialization, enabling direct tensor transmission from local GPU memory to remote GPU memory. This capability preserves bandwidth and minimizes latency, which is essential for scaling RL training.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Framework

The paper provides a theoretical analysis to demonstrate PolySim's superiority in mitigating simulator inductive bias.
- **Setup and Notation:** The real environment is denoted as $M^* = (S_0, A, T_0, R_0, \gamma)$, and each simulator as $M_i = (S_i, A, T_i, R_i, \gamma)$. State spaces are mapped via projection functions $f_i$ and lifted back to a common space $S_0$ via $g_i$, allowing all transition kernels $\tilde{T}_i$ to operate on $S_0$. The 1-Wasserstein distance $W_1$ is used to measure the difference between transition kernels.
- **Definition of Sim-to-Real Gap:** The sim-to-real gap for a simulator $P_i$ (with lifted kernel $\tilde{T}_i$) is defined as $G_{S2R}(P_i; \Pi) = \sup_{\pi \in \Pi} |\eta_{\tilde{T}_i}( \pi) - \eta_{T_0}(\pi)|$, where $\eta$ is the discounted return and $\Pi$ is the policy class.
- **Upper-bound of Sim-to-Real Gap (Lemma 1):** The sim-to-real gap for a single simulator $P_i$ is bounded by $G_{S2R}(P_i; \Pi) \leq \frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma} L_V \Delta_i$, where $\Delta_i = \sup_{s,a} W_1(T_0(\cdot|s,a), \tilde{T}_i(\cdot|s,a))$. This links the gap to the Wasserstein distance between the real and simulated transition kernels.
- **Definition of PolySim Sim-to-Real Gap (Definition 2):** PolySim, denoted $Q$, is defined as a mixture of $N$ simulators, with a transition kernel $T_Q(\cdot|s,a) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \tilde{T}_i(\cdot|s,a)$, where $w_i$ are weights.
- **Superiority Theorem (Theorem 1):** This theorem states that PolySim achieves a lower sim-to-real gap than any single simulator: $G_{S2R}(Q; \Pi) \leq \min_i G_{S2R}(P_i; \Pi)$. The proof demonstrates that by forming a convex mixture of simulator kernels, PolySim's effective dynamics $Q$ can be strictly closer (in terms of $W_1$ distance) to the true real-world dynamics $T_0$ than any individual simulator's dynamics $\tilde{T}_i$. This geometric intuition, where $T_0$ is closer to the convex hull of simulator dynamics than to any single vertex, is central to the theoretical argument.

In summary, PolySim's methodology combines a robust distributed system design with a principled approach to dynamics randomization, theoretically justified by its ability to reduce the sim-to-real gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate PolySim's effectiveness in mitigating dynamics mismatch, comparing it against single-simulator and sequential multi-simulator training, and assessing its real-world transfer capabilities.

#### 4.1. Superior Generalization to Unseen Domains (Q1)

PolySim demonstrated superior generalization performance, particularly in zero-shot transfer to an entirely unseen simulator, MuJoCo, which was not used during training.
- **Unseen MuJoCo Domain:** The configuration using IsaacSim + IsaacGym + Genesis (Sim+Gym+Genesis) for training achieved the best overall performance in MuJoCo. It showed robustness with success rates increasing by 44.3% over the IsaacGym baseline, 6.4% over Genesis, and 52.8% over the IsaacSim baseline (Table I). It also exhibited superior precision, achieving the lowest global body position tracking error ($E_{g-mpjpe}$), root-relative mean per-joint position error ($E_{mpjpe}$), and root velocity error ($E_{vel}$).
- **Beyond Additive Effects:** PolySim's benefits were not merely additive. For example, when testing on IsaacGym, training with Genesis alone yielded a 0.043 success rate, and IsaacSim alone yielded 0.143. However, joint training on IsaacSim + Genesis increased the success rate to 0.400, surpassing both individual baselines and their simple sum. This suggests a synergistic gain from parallel multi-simulator training.
- **Enhanced In-Distribution Robustness and Precision:** Even when evaluated on a simulator included in its training set, PolySim improved performance. For instance, a policy trained solely on IsaacGym achieved a 1.000 success rate on IsaacGym, but with relatively high error metrics. The IsaacGym + Genesis policy, while maintaining a 1.000 success rate, substantially reduced these error metrics, indicating that incorporating diverse dynamics acts as a regularization effect, leading to smoother and more generalizable control strategies, even in the source domain.

#### 4.2. Parallel vs. Sequential Multi-Simulator Training (Q2)

Comparison between PolySim's parallel training and sequential multi-simulator training revealed distinct differences in generalization.
- **Limited Improvement from Sequential Training:** For unseen evaluation simulators, sequential training showed only marginal improvements (e.g., 3.5% and 4.9% increases in success rates on IsaacSim and Genesis, respectively, compared to single-simulator training). Performance on IsaacGym and MuJoCo even decreased with sequential training.
- **Performance Degradation in Seen Domains:** In seen evaluation simulators, sequential training, particularly "3-serial" training, sometimes degraded performance compared to "2-serial" training (e.g., 10.7% worse on IsaacGym and 0.6% worse on Genesis). This behavior was attributed to a "catastrophic forgetting" issue (Figure 5), where subsequent training stages on new simulators might erase previously learned knowledge.
- **PolySim's Superiority:** PolySim, with its fully parallel training design, consistently mitigated these issues and significantly improved performance across both seen and unseen domains compared to sequential approaches (Figure 4).

#### 4.3. Experimental Efficiency Analysis

PolySim demonstrated efficiency in its multi-simulator training approach.
- **Marginal Overhead:** PolySim's iteration time remained close to that of the slowest simulator in any given recipe, incurring only a marginal overhead of +0.1 to +0.3 seconds (Table II). This indicates that the framework effectively leverages parallel processing and GPU-direct communication to manage diverse physics engines without significant additional computational or communication costs.

#### 4.4. Sim-to-Real Deployment (Q3)

Policies trained with PolySim, combining multiple simulators with parameter-based domain randomization, were successfully deployed zero-shot onto a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. This indicates that PolySim effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap, allowing complex whole-body motions to be executed on physical hardware without further fine-tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PolySim introduces a novel and theoretically supported approach to addressing the persistent sim-to-real gap in robot control, particularly for complex humanoid whole-body control tasks. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer:** By directly confronting simulator inductive bias through dynamics-level randomization across multiple heterogeneous simulators, PolySim demonstrates a more robust method for learning policies that generalize to the real world. This represents an advancement over traditional parameter-level domain randomization, which is limited by the underlying simulator's fixed physics model. The successful zero-shot deployment on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot validates this capability, paving the way for more practical and rapid robot development.
- **Development of More Robust Policies:** The empirical results show that PolySim not only improves generalization to unseen simulators but also enhances the robustness and precision of policies even within environments similar to those used in training. This regularization effect, achieved by exposing the policy to diverse dynamics, leads to more stable and performant controllers.
- **Scalable and Efficient Training Framework:** The proposed client-server architecture, simulator router, and GPU-direct communication pipeline establish a scalable and efficient platform for multi-simulator reinforcement learning. This design allows for concurrent, high-throughput rollouts across diverse engines without incurring substantial computational overhead, thereby making the approach practical for complex RL tasks.
- **Theoretical Justification for Multi-Simulator Training:** The theoretical proof that PolySim achieves a tighter upper bound on the sim-to-real gap provides a foundational understanding for why dynamics-level randomization is superior. This theoretical underpinning strengthens the methodology and offers insights for future research in simulator generalization.
- **Reduced Development Costs and Time:** The ability to achieve zero-shot sim-to-real transfer significantly reduces the need for extensive real-world data collection and fine-tuning, which are typically costly, time-consuming, and potentially hazardous. This enables faster iteration cycles in robot learning and deployment.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** PolySim establishes a framework that can be extended in several directions. Future research can explore integrating an even wider array of simulators, incorporating more diverse and complex tasks (such as manipulation), and potentially adapting the framework for other robotic platforms beyond humanoids. The findings also open avenues for investigating optimal weighting schemes for simulator mixtures and further refining the theoretical bounds.

In summary, PolySim represents a substantial contribution to closing the sim-to-real gap, offering a robust, efficient, and theoretically grounded approach that holds significant promise for advancing humanoid robot control and broader robot learning applications.
