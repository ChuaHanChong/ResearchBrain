# Agile-WAM: An Agile Tactile World Action Model for Contact-Rich Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Agile-WAM is a lightweight tactile World Action Model that fuses camera, tactile force-field and proprioceptive features into one latent, used directly as the source of a flow-matching network transporting it to joint action, future-visual and future-tactile latents. Supervising vision at a longer horizon and touch at the next frame, it beats the strongest baselines overall on nine simulated and five real contact-rich tasks at about 10 ms latency.

## Problem

- Recent tactile world action models extend large pretrained video-generation backbones, incurring heavy compute and memory costs that block high-frequency control and deployment on resource-constrained robot platforms.
- Most tactile policies use touch only as an extra input or reactive feedback signal, rather than predicting how contact will evolve alongside vision and actions.
- Applying one prediction horizon to all modalities ignores their timescales: adjacent visual frames are nearly redundant, giving trivial supervision, while long-horizon tactile targets blur abrupt contact changes.

## Method

- ==Vision-tactile-to-action flow matching==: ResNet-18 visual and tactile encoders plus proprioception fuse into a source latent; an MLP ==velocity field== transports it to targets without cross-attention conditioning, integrated with 6 Euler steps.
- **VITA**-style ==latent action autoencoder==: an MLP encoder-decoder defines the action latent space, with a reconstruction loss plus a loss decoding the ODE-generated latent, backpropagated through integration to close the train-inference gap.
- ==Multi-horizon multimodal prediction==: the flow target concatenates the action latent, a visual latent 8 steps ahead (the executed chunk length) and a next-frame tactile latent, each supervised with ==latent-space L2 losses==.

## Results

- On ManiFeel simulation, Agile-WAM leads eight of nine tasks, e.g. Bulb Screw **92.7%** versus **87.3%** for VITA-VT and **4.0%** for Tactile-WAM; it trails only on Power Plug Insertion (**59.3%** vs **63.3%**).
- Across five real Flexiv tasks, Agile-WAM totals **63/100** successes versus **48/100** for VITA-VT and **33/100** for VITA, leading every task except Peg Insertion (**55%** vs **60%**).
- Inference takes **10.35 ms** (**97 Hz**) versus **408.59 ms** for Diffusion Policy and **636.34 ms** for reimplemented Tactile-WAM; ablations credit gains to prediction losses, not enlarged latent capacity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prediction horizons should match each modality's rate of change: slow, redundant streams need distant targets to be informative, while abrupt contact signals need next-step targets.
> - Large video backbones may not be necessary for world-action benefits in contact-rich tasks: latent future prediction on a small from-scratch flow policy already improves success at millisecond latency.
> - Predicted modalities should match input modalities: with vision and touch as inputs, forecasting only one gives incomplete dynamics supervision and underperforms jointly forecasting both.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A gear or peg can look nearly aligned while the robot's contact state—force, slip, or misalignment—has already changed abruptly, and the wrist camera becomes obstructed precisely when contact determines whether insertion or engagement will succeed. Agile-WAM's distinguishing move is to jointly generate an action chunk and predicted future visual and tactile latents from a single encoded observation, giving vision a longer horizon to change while limiting tactile prediction to the next frame for fast contact reactions. Agile-WAM uses modality-specific horizons to align prediction with the different temporal dynamics of vision and touch. The payoff is visible in corrective behaviors: during peg insertion, the robot slides along the surface toward the hole; during gear assembly, it twists when the middle gear sticks.

Agile-WAM maintaining contact and performing corrective twisting during gear assembly recovery.

### 2. A compact predictive control loop

Current RGB, TacFF (tactile force-field), and proprioception pass through separate encoders and fuse into one shared source latent $z_0$. A lightweight flow-matching network—learning a velocity field that transports a source representation to a target—carries $z_0$ directly to a joint target containing a latent action, a future visual latent, and a future tactile latent. Because observations are encoded once and the flow starts from $z_0$ rather than noise, the repeated observation injection required by conventional diffusion or conditioned flow policies is removed. This direct-flow design removes explicit conditioning during generation. An action autoencoder makes action chunks structurally compatible with the flow and decodes the generated action latent into executable actions; only the first 8 of the 16-action chunk are executed before replanning.

Left-to-right: camera RGB and TacFF are encoded into shared latent $z_0$, with proprioception fused in; the central flow produces a latent action, a latent vision output, and a latent tactile output; the action output is decoded into an action chunk; future visual and TacFF targets are re-encoded for latent losses; the right side shows flow-matching, action-generation, and action-autoencoder losses.

### 3. Why the horizons matter

Adjacent visual frames can be nearly identical, so next-frame visual prediction may offer weak supervision; tactile force can change abruptly at contact, so a long tactile target can blur or skip the transition. Agile-WAM therefore sets $h_{\mathrm{vis}}=h$, matching the eight executed actions in the reported setup, and $h_{\mathrm{tac}}=1$.

Two ablations supply the decisive evidence. The prediction-head ablation holds latent capacity constant: joint action–future modeling reaches 92.7 ± 2.5%, compared with 87.3 ± 0.9% without the prediction head and 86.7 ± 4.1% with the enlarged prediction head but no prediction loss. This isolates predictive supervision from enlarged latent capacity as the source of the gain. The horizon ablation then compares the proposed pair $+8, +1$ against $+1, +1$ and $+8, +8$: success rates are 92.7 ± 2.5%, 90.7 ± 2.5%, and 88.7 ± 2.5% respectively. The authors report consistent degradation when both modalities share one horizon.

Real-world Gear Assembly results put the mechanism in task terms: Agile-WAM succeeds in 16/20 trials (0.80), compared with 11/20 (0.55) for VITA-VT and 15/20 (0.75) for vision-only VITA, though VITA-VT is slightly better on Peg Insertion. These are 20-episode evaluations on a Flexiv Rizon 4 with a 30-FPS wrist camera and a 30-Hz 32×32 pressure sensor, using 50 teleoperated demonstrations per task. In the controlled RTX 4090 latency benchmark over 50 runs with batch size 1, Agile-WAM takes 10.35 ± 0.14 ms per inference (96.62 Hz), compared with 9.71 ms for VITA and 408.59 ± 0.47 ms for visual Diffusion Policy. Robot-interface and sensor-sampling rates can further limit deployed control frequency.

The ablations and recovery examples together support the authors' interpretation that jointly predicting future multimodal states helps actions remain compatible with contact dynamics. The benefit is not tactile input alone; the intervention combines predictive world modeling, horizon supervision, and architecture. The evidence reflects fixed-task, 20-episode evaluations rather than a guarantee of generality.
