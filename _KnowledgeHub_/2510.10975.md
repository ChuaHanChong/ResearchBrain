# RoVer: Robot Reward Model as Test-Time Verifier for Vision-Language-Action Model

> [!summary] Summary
> The ROVER framework enhances frozen Vision-Language-Action (VLA) robot policies by integrating a compact Process Reward Model (PRM) at inference time. This approach, which reallocates computation from training to inference, achieves an 18.4% relative improvement in 5-consecutive-task success rate on the CALVIN benchmark and boosts average success rates on real robot tasks from 72.9% to 88.6%.

## Problem

- VLA models for embodied AI face limitations due to the prohibitive cost and inherent scarcity of collecting diverse, high-quality robotic experience data for traditional "training-time scaling."
- Even well-trained VLA policies exhibit performance fluctuations and brittleness in real-world manipulation tasks, especially long-horizon ones, due to stochastic decoding.
- The continuous scaling of model size and training data for VLA models is economically unfeasible and fundamentally restricted compared to the vast data available for LLMs/VLMs.

## Method

- **ROVER** introduces an external ==test-time scaling== framework that augments frozen VLA policies with a compact ==Process Reward Model (PRM)== without modifying the base policy's weights or requiring additional data.
- The PRM concurrently predicts a scalar ==reliability score== for candidate actions and a ==6D refinement direction==, which guides the generation and selection of optimal actions.
- A ==shared perception cache== amortizes computational costs by reusing extracted features across all candidate actions, enabling efficient evaluation of numerous candidates per control step.

## Results

- ROVER improved the success rates of various VLA backbones on the CALVIN benchmark, with Dita achieving an **18.4%** relative increase in SR@5 (from **50.0%** to **59.2%**) and GR-1 seeing a **17.4%** relative gain (from **43.4%** to **48.7%**).
- Direction-guided sampling, leveraging the PRM's predicted directions, consistently outperformed unguided random expansion for the same candidate budget, particularly for GR-1 and Dita.
- On real robot tasks, ROVER increased the average success rate for Diffusion Policy from **72.9%** to **88.6%**, showing substantial improvements in generalization for unseen scenarios (e.g., pick and place improved from **90%** to **100%**, push button from **50%** to **80%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Performance gains for VLA policies can be reliably achieved by reallocating computational effort from training to sophisticated inference-time verification and refinement, rather than solely through continuous data collection and model retraining.
> - A relatively compact PRM (0.2B parameters, 40M trainable) can effectively provide both scalar preferences and precise 6D action-space directions, significantly improving action selection.
> - Test-time scaling, when combined with efficient caching of perceptual features, allows for evaluating a large number of candidate actions with minimal latency, making the approach practical for real-time robotics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied AI has made significant strides, with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models emerging as a dominant paradigm for controlling robots using visual and linguistic inputs. Historically, progress in VLA models, much like in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), has largely been driven by "training-time scaling"—i.e., using larger model backbones, diversifying demonstration data, and refining data curation processes. This approach, exemplified by systems like RT-X, OpenVLA, and RDT, has achieved impressive results.

However, the paper identifies a critical bottleneck: the prohibitive cost and inherent limitations of collecting diverse, high-quality robotic experience data at internet scale, unlike the vast text and image corpora available for LLMs/VLMs. This data scarcity makes continuous training-time scaling economically unfeasible and fundamentally restricted for real-world robotics. Moreover, VLA success rates can fluctuate due to stochastic decoding and the inherent brittleness of manipulation tasks, particularly in long-horizon scenarios.

This challenge has motivated a shift towards "test-time scaling" – reallocating computational effort from extensive pre-training to more sophisticated inference-time computation. This concept draws inspiration directly from the success of test-time scaling techniques in LLMs, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, self-reflection, and the use of Process Reward Models (PRMs) for strategies like Best-of-N (BoN) or Beam Search. These LLM techniques have demonstrated that even smaller models can achieve performance comparable to much larger ones by increasing "thinking" at inference.

In the embodied AI landscape, test-time scaling efforts can be broadly categorized:
- **Internal Test-Time Scaling:** These methods involve the VLA model generating and refining its own reasoning steps, often requiring expanded datasets with chain-of-thought annotations. Examples include Embodied Chain-of-Thought (e.g., Zawalski et al., Ji et al., Zhou et al., 2025) and approaches that incorporate future-frame prediction (e.g., CoT-VLA, UniVLA). While promising, these still typically necessitate additional data annotation and sometimes architectural modifications.
- **External Test-Time Scaling:** This approach decouples the search and scoring mechanisms from the policy by introducing a separate reward or value verifier that evaluates candidate actions at inference time. This guides candidate generation without modifying the backbone policy's weights. Concurrent works like Hume (Song et al., 2025) and RoboMonkey (Kwok et al., 2025) have begun exploring this direction, often involving dual-system VLAs or reward modeling with large backbones and synthetic data.

RoVer distinctively positions itself within the external test-time scaling paradigm. Its core innovation lies in enhancing *frozen* VLA policies purely at inference time using a *compact* Process Reward Model (PRM). Unlike many internal TTS methods, RoVer explicitly avoids requiring additional data annotations or retraining of the base VLA model. Compared to concurrent external TTS works, RoVer emphasizes a more lightweight PRM (0.2B total parameters, 40M trainable, versus 7B for RoboMonkey), a systematic examination of scaling with candidate budgets, and a focus on broad compatibility across diverse VLA backbones. By turning available test-time computational resources into improved decision-making, RoVer offers a practical and efficient solution to unlock latent capabilities of existing VLA models, circumventing the data-intensive and computationally expensive cycles of traditional training-time scaling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind RoVer stems from the inherent limitations of current VLA models in embodied AI, particularly their heavy reliance on training-time scaling. While scaling up training data and model size has yielded performance gains, this approach is prohibitively expensive for robotics due to the resource-intensive and time-consuming nature of collecting diverse, high-quality robotic experience data. This creates a significant bottleneck for further advancements. Furthermore, even well-trained VLA policies often exhibit performance fluctuations due to stochastic decoding and the delicate nature of real-world manipulation, especially on complex, long-horizon tasks.

Against this backdrop, the authors pose a fundamental question: Can VLA performance be enhanced using test-time scaling mechanisms *without* requiring additional data or model retraining? This question forms the core objective and motivation for the RoVer framework.

Specifically, the key objectives for RoVer are:

1. **Enhance Frozen VLA Policies at Inference Time:** To develop a framework that can "plug and play" with existing, pre-trained VLA models without modifying their architectures or weights. This allows RoVer to improve performance by maximizing the utility of already-learned policies, rather than continuously building larger ones.
2. **Develop a Compact and Powerful Process Reward Model (PRM):** To design an external verifier that not only assigns scalar reliability scores to candidate actions but also predicts precise 6D refinement directions in the action space. This dual functionality is crucial for both intelligent candidate evaluation and guided exploration.
3. **Enable Efficient Direction-Guided Sampling:** To implement a strategy where the PRM's predicted directions can effectively guide the generation and refinement of candidate actions. This aims to concentrate computational effort on promising regions of the action space, improving sample efficiency compared to unguided random exploration.
4. **Amortize Perception Costs for Scalability:** To design the inference process such that shared perceptual features (from observations, language, and robot state) are computed only once per control step and then cached for reuse across all candidate actions. This amortization is vital for ensuring that increased decision-making capability (evaluating many candidates) comes at a reasonable computational cost, making the approach practical for real-world robotic applications.
5. **Transform Compute into Better Decisions:** Ultimately, the overarching goal is to effectively transform available test-time computational resources into improved action decision-making, thereby circumventing the traditional constraints of data collection and model retraining that limit VLA performance.

By achieving these objectives, RoVer aims to offer a new, more sustainable paradigm for enhancing robot capabilities, pushing the boundaries of what can be achieved with existing models and limited additional training data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RoVer introduces an external test-time scaling framework that augments a frozen Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy with a compact Process Reward Model (PRM). The core idea is to leverage the PRM during inference to score multiple candidate actions and guide their refinement, ultimately selecting the most optimal action for execution without altering the base VLA policy's architecture or weights.

#### 3.1. Overview of the Inference Process

At each control step during inference, RoVer operates as follows:
1. **Policy Proposals:** The frozen base VLA policy generates an initial set of $N$ candidate actions.
2. **Direction Prediction and Expansion:** For each policy-generated action, the PRM is queried to predict both a scalar reliability score and a 6D refinement direction in the action space. New candidates are then generated by expanding the initial proposals along these PRM-predicted directions within a specified angular bound.
3. **Candidate Scoring:** All generated candidates (initial proposals + expanded ones) are then scored by the PRM, which assigns a scalar process reward to each.
4. **Optimal Action Selection:** The action with the highest PRM score is selected and executed by the robot.

#### 3.2. Efficiency through Shared Perception Cache

A crucial aspect of RoVer's efficiency is its ability to amortize computational costs. Observations, language instructions, and robot state features are computed only once per control step. These "perceptual features" are then cached and reused across all candidate actions evaluated by the PRM, significantly reducing redundant computation and enabling the evaluation of a larger number of candidates within the same test-time budget.

#### 3.3. Model Architecture of the Process Reward Model (PRM)

The PRM ($R_\phi$) is a lightweight, GPT-2-style transformer.
- **Initialization:** It is initialized with pre-trained weights from GR-1, which itself uses a MAE-initialized image encoder and a CLIP-initialized text encoder. This leverages powerful pre-trained vision-language representations.
- **Inputs:** It takes multi-modal inputs: observations (e.g., multi-view RGB, robot proprioception), natural language goals, and a specific candidate action.
- **Outputs:** It simultaneously outputs a scalar process reward ($r_i$) and an action-space direction ($d_i$). The direction is typically 6D for arm poses (e.g., delta x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw) and excludes discrete gripper commands.
- **Action Amplifier:** To ensure the PRM can discern subtle differences between candidate actions, a compact MLP (multi-layer perceptron) called an "Action Amplifier" is applied to the action embedding before it's fused with observation/language tokens. This reconditions the action channel, enhancing the saliency of fine-grained action deltas and stabilizing training.
- **Parameter Size:** The PRM is compact, with a total of 0.2B parameters, of which only 40M are trainable (the rest are frozen from MAE and CLIP encoders). This contrasts with much larger models used in related work.

#### 3.4. Model Training of the PRM

The PRM is trained to distinguish "better" actions from "worse" ones and to predict the direction towards an expert action.
- **Data Preparation: Direction-Guided and Anchor-Centered Sampling:** Instead of simple noisy expert sampling, RoVer uses an "anchor-centered dynamic scheme" to create informative training pairs:
 1. **Anchor Action:** Given an expert action ($a_e$) from a demonstration, an "anchor action" ($a_{anc}$) is generated by adding base Gaussian noise to the 6D pose subspace of $a_e$.
 2. **Ground-Truth Direction:** The ground-truth unit vector ($u_{gt}$) from $a_{anc}$ pointing towards $a_e$ is computed.
 3. **Better/Worse Actions:** An orthogonal hyperplane to $u_{gt}$ passing through $a_{anc}$ is defined. "Better" actions are sampled in the half-space closer to $a_e$, and "worse" actions in the half-space farther from $a_e$, using an adaptive noise scale. This ensures informative preference labels and direction supervision.
- **Supervision and Objective:** The training objective combines two losses:
 1. **Direction Supervision ($L_{dir}$):** A cosine misalignment loss between the PRM's predicted direction ($\hat{u}$) and the ground-truth direction ($u_{gt}$) is minimized.
 2. **Reward Supervision ($L_{rew}$):** The Bradley–Terry preference loss is applied. For any ordered pair (i.e., action $a_i$ is closer to $a_e$ than $a_j$), the PRM is trained to predict a higher score for $a_i$ than $a_j$.
 3. **Total Loss ($L_{total}$):** A weighted sum of $L_{dir}$ and $L_{rew}$.

#### 3.5. Test-Time Scaling Strategies

RoVer explores two strategies for generating candidate actions from the base policy's proposal ($a_p$):
1. **Random Sampling:** Perturbing $a_p$ with isotropic Gaussian noise. This explores the action space broadly.
2. **Direction-Guided Sampling:** Expanding $a_p$ by sampling along the PRM-predicted direction. This exploits the PRM's guidance to focus exploration on more promising regions.

This detailed methodology enables RoVer to be a general, plug-and-play framework, enhancing VLA policies by intelligently leveraging inference-time computation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate RoVer's effectiveness in both simulation (CALVIN benchmark) and real-world robotics, addressing three key questions: backbone-agnostic gains, scaling and sampling efficiency, and inference efficiency.

**Q1: Backbone-Agnostic Gains (CALVIN Benchmark)**
RoVer consistently improved the performance of diverse, pre-trained VLA baselines (GR-1, Dita, MoDE) on the challenging CALVIN ABC→D split without requiring any retraining of these base policies.
- **GR-1:** Average chain length improved from 3.19 to 3.33, and the success rate for 5 consecutive tasks (SR@5) increased significantly from 43.4% to 48.7% (a 17.4% relative improvement).
- **Dita:** Average chain length increased from 3.61 to 3.84, and SR@5 jumped from 50.0% to 59.2% (an 18.4% relative improvement).
- **MoDE:** Average chain length improved from 4.01 to 4.12, and SR@5 from 63.5% to 66.6%.
These results affirm RoVer's plug-and-play nature and its ability to enhance policies of varying capacities across different stages of VLA research.

**Q2: Scaling and Sampling Efficiency (CALVIN Benchmark)**
The study examined how performance scales with the number of policy proposals ($N$) and direction-guided expansions ($M$), given a total candidate budget ($K=N+M$).
- **General Improvement:** Performance generally increased with a larger total candidate budget $K$.
- **Direction Guidance Effectiveness:** Direction-guided (DG) sampling consistently outperformed unguided Gaussian expansion, particularly for GR-1 and Dita. This shows that the PRM's predicted directions effectively concentrate candidates in promising regions, leading to better performance for the same computational budget.
- **MoDE Specifics:** For MoDE, which outputs short action chunks rather than single actions, the gains were less consistently monotonic across all $N+M$ settings. This was attributed to a "chunk-step mismatch," where the PRM scores only the first action of a chunk, limiting intervention within the chunk. Nevertheless, most $N+M$ configurations still showed positive improvements.

**Q3: Inference Efficiency with a Shared Perception Cache**
The impact of caching perceptual features was evaluated by measuring wall-clock latency per control step.
- **Substantial Speedups:** Caching significantly reduced per-step latency, with speedup increasing with the number of candidates. For 10 candidates, latency dropped from 0.414s to 0.0929s (4.46x speedup). For 1000 candidates, the speedup reached 7.23x.
- **Linear Scaling:** With caching, the per-action cost stabilized around 5.7–6.2 ms, enabling near-linear scaling in the number of candidates for a single perception encoding. This validates that amortizing perception costs via a shared cache is critical for practical test-time scaling.

**Real Robot Experiments (Dual-Arm Dobot Platform)**
RoVer was also evaluated on a real dual-arm Dobot robot using Diffusion Policy (DP) as the base policy for three manipulation tasks: pick and place, push button, and stack bowls, under both seen and unseen conditions.
- **Generalization Gains:** RoVer (DP†) significantly improved generalization capabilities. While matching DP's 100% success on seen cases, it showed substantial gains in unseen scenarios:
 - Pick and place (unseen object): DP improved from 90% to 100%.
 - Push button (unseen position): DP improved from 50% to 80%.
 - Stack bowls (unseen position): DP improved from 40% to 70%.
- **Overall Improvement:** The average success rate across all tasks and conditions increased from 72.9% for DP to 88.6% for DP† (RoVer-enhanced DP).

These comprehensive results strongly support RoVer's premise that reallocating computational effort from training to inference, guided by a compact and efficient PRM, can reliably unlock additional capabilities in embodied policies, leading to improved performance and generalization in both simulated and real-world environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RoVer presents a highly significant contribution to the field of embodied AI, offering a practical and impactful paradigm shift in how robot learning systems can be improved. Its potential implications are multifaceted:

1. **New Paradigm for VLA Improvement:** RoVer establishes a compelling new approach to enhance Vision-Language-Action models. By effectively "reallocating compute from training to inference," it demonstrates that substantial performance gains can be achieved without the traditional, resource-intensive cycles of collecting massive new datasets or retraining gargantuan VLA backbones. This fundamentally challenges the prevailing scale-up-training-data-and-model-size mentality in robotics.

2. **Increased Accessibility and Sustainability:** The framework's "plug-and-play" nature allows it to augment existing, frozen VLA policies. This means that researchers and practitioners can significantly improve the capabilities of their current models without needing to retrain them, which is a major barrier for many due to computational costs and data acquisition challenges. It makes advanced robotic capabilities more accessible and the development process more sustainable.

3. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The consistent improvements in success rates across various VLA backbones and especially in unseen conditions (CALVIN ABC→D split, real robot unseen object/position tasks) highlight RoVer's ability to boost the robustness and generalization capabilities of robot policies. This is crucial for deploying robots in dynamic and unstructured real-world environments.

4. **Computational Efficiency and Practicality:** The design of the compact Process Reward Model (PRM), which has only 40M trainable parameters, and the ingenious use of a shared perception cache are critical for RoVer's efficiency. The caching mechanism amortizes expensive perception computations, enabling the evaluation of many candidates with minimal latency overhead, thus making the test-time scaling framework practical for real-time robotic control. This demonstrates a thoughtful balance between performance enhancement and computational feasibility.

5. **Broad Applicability:** By demonstrating improvements across diverse base policies like GR-1 (GPT-style trajectory model), Dita (Diffusion Transformer), MoDE (Mixture-of-Expert denoisers), and Diffusion Policy, RoVer proves its versatility. This wide compatibility ensures that the benefits of external test-time verification can be leveraged by a broad spectrum of VLA architectures.

6. **Guidance for Future Research:** RoVer opens several promising avenues for future research. While it addresses the "chunk-step mismatch" for chunked policies, further work could explore more integrated solutions. Developing more sophisticated supervision proxies beyond simple expert proximity, or even incorporating human feedback directly into PRM training, could further refine the verifier. Furthermore, exploring how RoVer can be combined with other test-time scaling techniques (internal or external) could lead to even more powerful embodied AI systems.

In essence, RoVer offers a cost-effective, adaptable, and powerful method for unlocking the latent capabilities of embodied AI policies. By strategically leveraging inference-time computation, it provides a viable path towards more capable, robust, and generalizable robots, moving beyond the limitations of purely training-centric scaling.
