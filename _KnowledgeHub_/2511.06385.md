# From Demonstrations to Safe Deployment: Path-Consistent Safety Filtering for Diffusion Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed Path-Consistent Safety filtering (PACS), a method to safely deploy Diffusion Policies and Vision-Language-Action models in dynamic human-robot interaction environments. PACS achieves formal safety guarantees by modifying robot speed and acceleration while maintaining the intended path, resulting in task success rates comparable to unsafe policies and outperforming reactive safety filters with an average safety step time of 0.20 ms.

## Problem

- Diffusion Policies (DPs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models lack formal safety guarantees, preventing their use in safety-critical human-robot interaction (HRI) environments.
- Existing reactive safety filters often cause DPs to enter "out-of-distribution" (OOD) states by altering intended paths, leading to unpredictable behavior and task failures.
- Prior path-consistent safety methods were incompatible with the chunked action outputs and low update frequencies of modern DPs, limiting their real-time application in dynamic environments.

## Method

- Developed Path-Consistent Safety filtering (PACS) that modifies the robot's speed and acceleration while strictly adhering to its intended path, thereby avoiding out-of-distribution states.
- Generates kinematically and dynamically feasible "intended trajectories" from the entire chunked action outputs of Diffusion Policies using a time-optimized planning module.
- Implements a real-time, high-frequency reachability-based safety filter that continuously monitors these trajectories against task-specific safety constraints (Speed and Separation, Power and Force Limiting) and executes failsafe stops if necessary.

## Results

- Path-Consistent Safety (PACS) achieved up to 68% higher task success rates than reactive Control Barrier Function (CBF) baselines in simulation, demonstrating superior performance in avoiding out-of-distribution states.
- PACS maintained task success rates comparable to unprotected policies (e.g., 79% vs. 80% in real-world HRI) while ensuring 0% safety violations and 80% "Safe Success" in dynamic human-robot interaction.
- The system demonstrated real-time capability with an average safety step computation time of 0.20 ms, and improved task execution speed by 14% through the generation of kinematically feasible trajectories from action chunks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Maintaining path consistency is crucial for safeguarding Diffusion Policies, as it prevents out-of-distribution states and preserves task performance, unlike reactive safety interventions.
> - Generating smooth, kinematically and dynamically feasible trajectories from entire action chunks significantly enhances task success and execution speed compared to processing individual actions.
> - Provable real-time safety guarantees can be effectively integrated into high-performance, generative robotic policies for dynamic human-robot interaction, bridging a critical deployment gap.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing towards automating complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks, particularly through advancements in imitation learning. Recent developments, such as diffusion models and flow matching, alongside the availability of large-scale demonstration datasets and vision-language-action models, have enabled robotic policies—referred to as Diffusion Policies (DPs)—to achieve state-of-the-art performance in dynamic environments. These policies learn by approximating conditional action distributions from expert demonstrations.

However, a fundamental challenge with these learning-based, black-box models is their inherent lack of formal safety guarantees. This limitation prevents their deployment in safety-critical applications, especially human-centric environments where collisions or unsafe interactions must be rigorously avoided.

Existing research has explored various safety mechanisms for learning-based policies. These include reactive approaches like Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), and predictive safety filters, as well as methods based on reachability analysis. While these methods aim to ensure safety, they often intervene by altering the robot's intended path. For DPs, which are highly sensitive to deviations from their training data distribution, such interventions can drive the system into "out-of-distribution" (OOD) states. In these OOD regions, DPs tend to exhibit unpredictable behavior, leading to performance degradation and task failures.

Some efforts have integrated safety constraints directly into the iterative action generation process of diffusion policies. However, these often involve high computational costs, limiting their real-time applicability, or they modify the policy itself, which can still influence the learned behavior. Path-consistent safety filters, exist, but previous implementations for learning-based policies primarily handle single goal positions or static constraints, which are not directly compatible with the chunked action outputs of modern DPs or dynamic environments.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing the specific problem of integrating provable, real-time safety guarantees with Diffusion Policies and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in dynamic environments, particularly human-robot interaction (HRI) scenarios. It aims to overcome the limitations of reactive safety filters that cause OOD behavior and the constraints of prior path-consistent methods, thus enabling the practical and safe deployment of high-performing, learned robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to ensure the safe deployment of Diffusion Policies (DPs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models when operating in close proximity to or interacting with dynamic objects, including humans. This overarching goal is motivated by several critical challenges and specific shortcomings of existing approaches:

1. **Lack of Formal Safety Guarantees for DPs:** While DPs achieve high performance on complex manipulation tasks, their black-box nature prevents them from inherently guaranteeing safe behavior, such as collision avoidance or safe interaction with dynamic objects. This limitation is a significant barrier to their adoption in real-world human-centric environments, where formal safety assurances are a prerequisite.

2. **Performance Degradation Due to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) States:** Current reactive safety mechanisms (e.g., Control Barrier Functions) ensure safety by altering the robot's planned actions. However, the large-scale demonstration datasets used to train DPs do not typically include such safety interventions. Consequently, when a reactive safety filter intervenes, it can steer the robot into states or trajectories that are "out-of-distribution" (OOD) relative to the training data. DPs are known to be particularly susceptible to errors and unpredictable behavior in such unseen states, leading to increased risk of task failure and decreased overall performance. The motivation is to prevent this degradation by keeping the robot's motion consistent with the policy's learned behavior.

3. **Limitations of Existing Path-Consistent Approaches:** Path-consistent safety filters aim to maintain the robot's intended path while enforcing safety, typically by slowing down or stopping the robot. However, prior path-consistent methods for learning-based policies that utilize reachability analysis are often designed for single-action inputs or static constraints. Diffusion Policies and VLAs, in contrast, generate "action chunks" (sequences of multiple consecutive actions) and operate at relatively low update frequencies. Directly applying single-action approaches would either result in overly slow execution (stopping at each waypoint) or a loss of fine-grained control and deviation from the desired behavior, neither of which is acceptable for complex tasks.

4. **Real-Time Deployment in Dynamic Environments:** Safeguarding mechanisms must operate in real-time, especially in dynamic environments where objects (or humans) are constantly moving. Some integrated safety approaches for DPs face high computational costs, limiting their applicability to low-dimensional systems or offline planning, thus preventing real-time deployment in complex scenarios.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to achieve the following specific objectives and validate corresponding hypotheses:
- **Provide formal safety guarantees:** Develop a mechanism that offers provable safety for DPs, particularly in challenging human-robot interaction (HRI) tasks involving dynamic objects.
- **Preserve task success rates:** Design a safety filter that intervenes minimally and path-consistently to avoid OOD states, thereby maintaining task success rates close to those of an unprotected, unsafe policy (quantitatively hypothesized as within ±5%).
- **Enhance performance through action chunk processing:** Demonstrate that generating intended trajectories from entire action chunks, rather than processing actions individually, leads to higher task success rates.
- **Ensure real-time capability and efficiency:** Develop a safety mechanism that is computationally efficient, allowing for real-time deployment (e.g., at 1 kHz), and that does not reduce task execution speed unless safety interventions are explicitly required.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to bridge the gap between the high performance of generative robot policies and the strict safety requirements for their deployment in human-centered environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, Path-Consistent Safety filtering (PACS), is designed to provide formal safety guarantees for Diffusion Policies (DPs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in dynamic environments without compromising task performance. The core principle is to ensure safety by modifying the robot's speed and acceleration while maintaining its intended path, thereby avoiding out-of-distribution (OOD) states.

The PACS framework integrates several components:

**A. Safety-Permitting Diffusion Policy Design:**
To facilitate path-consistent interventions, the policy is designed such that slowing down or stopping the robot does not inherently constitute an OOD situation. This is achieved by limiting the observations to RGB images and joint angles, excluding explicit velocity information. The actions generated by the policy are defined as delta joint positions (∆ˆq), representing desired changes in joint configurations over a short time interval.

**B. Intermediate Trajectory Generation from Action Chunks:**
DPs and VLAs typically generate a chunk of *H* consecutive actions, rather than a single action. PACS leverages this by first converting the received action chunk A(t) = (∆ˆq₀..., ∆ˆq_{H-1}) into a sequence of desired waypoints (ˆq₀..., ˆq_h) by integrating the delta joint positions forward in time from the current robot configuration q(0).
Next, an "intended trajectory planning module" (Algorithm 1, line 7) computes a kinematically and dynamically feasible trajectory, denoted χ_I([0, t_h]), that passes through these waypoints. This involves solving a time-optimized optimization problem (Equation 5):
- **Objective:** Minimize the total time *t_h* to traverse the trajectory.
- **Constraints:**
 - The robot's state x(τ) (including joint positions, velocities, accelerations, and jerks) must remain within the robot's kinematic and dynamic limits (X).
 - The trajectory must pass through the desired waypoints at specific time instances (q(t_k) = ˆq_k).
This module uses a trajectory generation library (Ruckig in experiments) to compute smooth trajectories. This intermediate planning step allows for fine-grained control over the execution speed of the action chunk and ensures that the planned path is dynamically feasible, which is crucial for maintaining consistency with the policy's learned behavior.

**C. Real-Time Reachability-Based Safety Filtering:**
The computed intended trajectory χ_I is then continuously monitored by a high-frequency safety filter (Algorithm 1, lines 9-14). This filter operates at a small safety step size α_s (e.g., 1 kHz).
1. **Reachable Occupancy Computation:**
 - **Robot:** The reachable occupancy of the robot C_robot(t) is computed by considering the desired trajectory and a bounded tracking error.
 - **Dynamic Objects:** For dynamic objects (e.g., humans), their reachable occupancies C_object(t) are computed based on measurements of their positions (m_o(t)), bounded measurement errors, velocity and acceleration limits, and sensor delays, following principles from prior work.
2. **Safety Constraints:** PACS supports two types of safety constraints, consistent with ISO/TS 15066:2016:
 - **Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM):** No contact is permitted. If a collision c_coll(t):= C_object(t) ∩ C_robot(t) ≠ ∅ is predicted, the robot must come to a complete stop (T_safe(t) = 0). This is suitable for coexistence tasks.
 - **Power and Force Limiting (PFL):** Allows contact, but only if the robot's kinetic energy T_robot(t) remains below a predefined safe energy threshold T_safe(t) (Equation 3). This is applicable for close human-robot collaboration.
3. **Monitored Trajectory and Verification:** At each safety timestep, the filter constructs a "monitored trajectory" χ_M(t). This trajectory concatenates the intended trajectory (or a recovery motion if already in a failsafe state) with a "failsafe trajectory" χ_F. The failsafe trajectory is a pre-defined stopping motion that brings the robot to an invariable safe set.
 The safety filter then formally verifies the monitored trajectory against the task-specific safety constraints (SSM or PFL) using set-based reachability analysis (implemented using the SaRA framework).
4. **Action Selection:**
 - If the verification is successful (i.e., the robot can complete the intended segment and still reach a safe stopping state), the controller executes the intended control input u_safe(t) = u_I(t).
 - If the verification fails (i.e., safety cannot be guaranteed by continuing on the intended path and then stopping), the controller switches to execute the last successfully verified failsafe trajectory, u_safe(t) = u_F(t), to bring the system to a safe stop.
This process continues at high frequency until a new action chunk is received from the DP, at which point a new intended trajectory is planned and monitored. This inductive safety guarantee ensures constraint satisfaction at all times.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PACS was conducted through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world human-robot interaction (HRI) tasks, validating several key hypotheses.

**A. Simulation Experiments (RoboMimic Benchmarks)**
Experiments were performed on three RoboMimic benchmark tasks (LIFT, CAN, SQUARE) augmented with a dynamic spherical object.
- **Superiority over Reactive Safety Filters:** PACS achieved significantly higher task success rates compared to a reactive Control Barrier Function (CBF) baseline, outperforming it by up to 68% (average 69% for PACS vs. 4% for CBF). This finding indicates that reactive methods frequently drive the policy into out-of-distribution (OOD) states, leading to task failure.
- **Preservation of Task Success (H2 Confirmation):** Comparing PACS (SSM/PFL modes) to the policy running in "OFF" mode (without active safety interventions but with the inverse kinematics translation), PACS showed comparable task success rates (e.g., 0.69/0.72 for PACS vs. 0.70 for OFF average). This supports the hypothesis that path-consistent braking maintains task performance.
- **Benefit of Action Chunk Trajectory Generation (H3 Confirmation):** PACS, which generates trajectories from entire action chunks, achieved a 28% improvement in task success rates compared to a "single-action" version of the safety filter that sequentially processed individual actions within a chunk (0.69/0.72 for PACS vs. 0.41/0.44 for single-action average). This highlights the importance of smooth, kinematically-feasible intermediate trajectory planning.

**B. Real-World HRI Experiments**
Three challenging HRI tasks (SORTING, HANDOVER, FEEDING) were conducted with a Franka FR3 manipulator, using either a Diffusion Policy (DP) or SmolVLA model. Safety constraints included Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM) for SORTING and Power and Force Limiting (PFL) for HANDOVER and FEEDING, with task-specific safe energy thresholds.
- **Necessity and Effectiveness of Safeguarding (H1 Confirmation):** When policies ran in "OFF" mode, they achieved an average task success rate of 79%, but with 0% "Safe Success" and an average of 56% of timesteps exhibiting safety violations. In contrast, with PACS active, the policies achieved an average task success rate of 80% and a "Safe Success" rate of 80%, with 0% safety violations. This clearly confirms that DPs alone are unsafe in dynamic HRI environments and that PACS effectively provides formal safety guarantees.
- **Task Success Preservation (H2 Further Confirmation):** The near-identical average task success rates between "OFF" mode (79%) and "PACS (ours)" mode (80%) across all real-world tasks further supports the hypothesis that PACS preserves task performance while ensuring safety.
- **Performance vs. Reactive Methods in HRI:** For the SORTING task, PACS demonstrated a 37% higher success rate (0.80) than a control barrier function (CBF) based safety filter (0.43). Visualizations (Figure 4) illustrated that the CBF often pushed the robot into OOD states, leading to policy recovery issues and task failures, whereas PACS maintained the robot within the learned distribution by reducing velocity along the intended path.
- **Real-Time Capability (H4 Confirmation):** PACS exhibited efficient computation times. The average safety step for PACS was 0.20 ms, compared to 0.64 ms for the CBF. Recalculating the intended trajectory took approximately 5 ms. These low latencies confirm that PACS is suitable for real-time deployment in highly dynamic environments.
- **Improved Execution Speed with Trajectory Generation (H4 Further Confirmation):** An ablation study on a modified SORTING task (without human interaction) showed that PACS's intermediate trajectory generation reduced the average task execution time by 14% (from 25.2 s to 21.7 s) and increased the average Cartesian end-effector speed by 13% compared to directly sending generated actions to a low-level controller. This demonstrates that ensuring kinematic and dynamic feasibility through trajectory generation can optimize task execution speed.
- **Qualitative HRI Validation:** Qualitative tests for the FEEDING task with a real human demonstrated that the robot slowed down appropriately when approaching the human, and the safeguarded policy performed the task carefully and precisely.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that PACS provides formal safety guarantees for DPs in dynamic, human-centric environments without compromising task success. It achieves this by maintaining path consistency, which avoids driving the policy into OOD states, and by efficiently generating and monitoring kinematically feasible trajectories from action chunks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, Path-Consistent Safety filtering (PACS), that holds significant implications for the deployment of advanced robotic policies in real-world, human-centric environments.

**Addresses a Critical Safety-Performance Gap:** The primary significance lies in its ability to bridge a critical gap between the high performance of modern generative policies (such as Diffusion Policies and Vision-Language-Action models) and the fundamental requirement for formal safety guarantees in human-robot interaction. Prior to this work, deploying these expressive, data-driven policies in safety-critical settings was largely hindered by their black-box nature and susceptibility to unsafe behavior, especially in dynamic and unseen situations. PACS directly addresses this, enabling the application of state-of-the-art imitation learning in scenarios where safety cannot be compromised.

**Preserves Learned Behavior and Task Success:** A key innovation is the path-consistent approach to safety. By modifying the robot's speed and acceleration while staying on its intended path, PACS avoids driving the policy into "out-of-distribution" states. This is crucial because DPs are highly sensitive to such deviations, which typically lead to unpredictable behavior and task failure with reactive safety mechanisms. The ability of PACS to maintain high task success rates, often on par with an unprotected policy, while simultaneously guaranteeing safety, is a substantial advancement over reactive filters which often degrade performance significantly.

**Enables Real-Time Safe HRI with Complex Policies:** The methodology is specifically designed to handle the chunked action outputs of modern DPs and VLAs and to operate efficiently in real-time. This capability, demonstrated through low computation times (0.20 ms per safety step) and successful deployment on challenging real-world HRI tasks (SORTING, HANDOVER, FEEDING), signifies a step towards practical, safe collaboration and coexistence between humans and advanced robots. The ability to guarantee safety against dynamic obstacles, including human movement, is vital for applications in manufacturing, healthcare, and service robotics.

**Generalizability and Applicability:** The framework is presented as a general approach for safeguarding imitation learning policies that use action chunks. Its validation across different generative models (Diffusion and Flow Matching) and diverse manipulation tasks suggests broad applicability beyond the specific examples presented. This generalizability increases the potential impact on a wider range of robotic systems and applications.

**Foundation for Future Development:** This work lays a foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages further exploration into integrating safety mechanisms that are consistent with the policy's learned behavior, rather than conflicting with it. The identified area of future work—handling (semi-)static obstacles via constraint-aware online replanning—indicates potential for extending the framework's robustness and versatility in complex environments.

In conclusion, PACS represents an important development towards the practical and safe deployment of powerful, learned robot behaviors in dynamic, human-centered environments. By providing formal safety guarantees without sacrificing task performance or computational efficiency, it removes a significant barrier to the widespread adoption of generative policies in safety-critical applications.
