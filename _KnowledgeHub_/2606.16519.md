# BadWorld: Adversarial Attacks on World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The Hong Kong Polytechnic University developed BADWORLD, an adversarial attack framework demonstrating that visual world models (VWMs) are vulnerable to imperceptible perturbations that cause catastrophic degradation in generated future video rollouts. The framework's 'Velocity-Min' objective consistently reduced aesthetic and imaging quality metrics (e.g., from 0.501 to 0.405 aesthetic quality on Astra) and increased geometric inconsistency (e.g., MEt3R by 74.8% on Astra).

## Problem

- Existing adversarial attack methods are unsuitable for autoregressive visual world models (VWMs) due to their interactive, history-dependent, and control-conditioned nature.
- Adversaries lack access to ground-truth future videos (missing future supervision), making reference-based attack loss functions inapplicable.
- VWM rollouts depend on unknown future control signals, requiring attacks to be robust across diverse and unpredictable user actions.

## Method

- BADWORLD introduces a ==label-free velocity attack== objective, which disrupts the model's internal velocity predictions at early denoising timesteps and uses a context-based history proxy to address missing future supervision.
- It employs trajectory-adaptive bi-level optimization to generalize attacks across unpredictable user actions by alternately discovering hard-to-attack control trajectories and optimizing the perturbation against them.
- Four self-supervised objectives (Velocity-Min/Max, Drift-Min/Max) were proposed to manipulate predicted velocity magnitude or direction, with 'Velocity-Min' proving most effective.

## Results

- The 'Velocity-Min' objective was the most effective attack across both Astra and Matrix-Game **2.0** VWMs, causing significant declines in video quality metrics and increased geometric inconsistency.
- Ablation studies confirmed the criticality of early-denoising approximation and the efficiency of the context-based history proxy for attack efficacy.
- Trajectory-adaptive bi-level optimization enhanced attack generalizability, particularly against the most resilient samples, ensuring rollout collapse regardless of sample difficulty or camera path.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current VWMs exhibit significant structural fragility, where visually imperceptible perturbations can lead to catastrophic degradation in generated video rollouts.
> - Targeting the velocity prediction at early denoising timesteps is a potent strategy for disrupting the autoregressive generation process, leading to amplified errors.
> - Bi-level optimization against 'hard' trajectories enhances the generalizability of adversarial attacks, ensuring effectiveness even in challenging or previously resilient scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual World Models (VWMs) represent a significant advancement in artificial intelligence, transitioning from passive video generation to interactive simulation capabilities. These models can synthesize action-conditioned future videos based on a single context image and user-defined actions. This functionality has led to their application in diverse fields such as interactive games, robotics, and autonomous driving. A prevalent hypothesis within the research community is that VWMs implicitly learn underlying physical and geometric rules of the world due to their ability to generate coherent rollouts.

Despite these capabilities, a fundamental question regarding the robustness of VWMs to small input perturbations remains largely unexplored. This question is particularly pertinent for safety-critical applications, where fragile or unreliable rollouts could undermine critical functions such as simulation, prediction, or planning. The current work positions adversarial perturbations as a stress test to assess this temporal robustness.

Existing research on adversarial attacks against generative models primarily focuses on domains such as text-to-image personalization, image-to-image editing, and conventional image-to-video (I2V) or video generation pipelines. These attacks are typically designed for scenarios with fixed generation conditions, predefined reference outputs, or objectives targeting localized spatio-temporal degradation. Examples include training data poisoning for text-to-image models or perturbing input context images in image-to-image systems. Recent work has extended adversarial paradigms to video generation, exploring threats like backdoor triggers in text prompts or adversarial trajectories in drag-based I2V systems. However, these prior approaches are generally not directly applicable to autoregressive VWMs due to their interactive, history-dependent, and control-conditioned nature.

A critical gap exists in evaluating the adversarial robustness of autoregressive VWMs, especially those that are conditioned on interactive signals such as camera trajectories or explicit action sequences. Previous I2V attack frameworks, while effective on standard diffusion-based backbones like SVD or Wan2.1, do not address the unique architectural and operational characteristics of autoregressive world models. This paper addresses this gap by introducing an adversarial attack framework specifically tailored for these interactive, autoregressive VWMs, marking a novel contribution to the field of adversarial machine learning for generative models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to evaluate the stability of the learned dynamics within current visual world models (VWMs) when subjected to minor input perturbations. The authors employ adversarial perturbations as a stress test to probe the temporal robustness of these models.

More specifically, the research aims to construct an adversarial context image, denoted as `x_adv = x + δ`, where `x` is the original image and `δ` is an imperceptible perturbation with a bounded L-infinity norm (`||δ||_∞ ≤ η`). The goal is for this perturbed image to cause the generated video rollouts, under the same control sequence, to deviate substantially from the rollouts produced by the clean input image. A significant difference indicates undesirable out-of-distribution behaviors, such as visual artifacts, temporal inconsistency, motion collapse, or semantic drift.

The motivation for this work stems from two central challenges inherent in attacking autoregressive VWMs, which render standard adversarial attack methodologies unsuitable:

- **C1: Missing future supervision.** An adversary targeting a VWM typically has access only to a single context image `x`. Unlike supervised settings, there are no paired ground-truth future videos, predefined correct rollouts, or reference trajectories available. Consequently, reference-based adversarial loss functions, which compare model outputs against a known correct output, cannot be directly applied. This necessitates the development of a label-free and self-supervised attack mechanism.

- **C2: Unknown future controls.** The interactive nature of VWMs means that their generated rollouts are highly dependent on future control signals, which could include camera paths, navigation commands, or discrete user actions. These control signals are generally unknown to the attacker at the time of perturbation generation and can vary unpredictably across users or interactions. An adversarial perturbation optimized for a single, fixed control trajectory might therefore fail to be effective if the user's action sequence changes. This highlights the need for a robust attack that maintains its efficacy across diverse and unpredictable future control signals.

By addressing these two challenges, the research seeks to provide a comprehensive assessment of VWM robustness, with implications for both model safety and potential privacy applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named BADWORLD, is designed to generate label-free adversarial attacks against autoregressive Visual World Models (VWMs). It addresses the challenges of missing future supervision and unknown future controls through two main components: a self-supervised velocity attack and trajectory-adaptive bi-level optimization.

#### 3.1. Label-Free Velocity Attack Objective

To overcome the absence of ground-truth future video supervision (C1), BADWORLD avoids optimizing for a discrepancy in the final rollout. Instead, the attack is formulated at the velocity level, exploiting the fact that corrupted velocity predictions can locally disrupt the generation process, with errors compounding over autoregressive steps.

The attack objective is to find an optimal perturbation `δ*` that minimizes an adversarial loss `L_atk` based on the model's predicted velocity `ˆv_δ`. The generation context `ξ` is defined by `(τ, p, n, t, ϵ)`, where `τ` is the control signal, `p` an optional prompt, `n` the target chunk, `t` a denoising timestep, and `ϵ` Gaussian noise. The perturbation is updated using Projected Gradient Descent (PGD).

To realize this label-free attack, two practical approximations are introduced:
- **Early-denoising approximation**: When the denoising timestep `t` is close to 1, the interpolated latent `z_n_t = (1-t)z_n + tϵ` is predominantly noise-dominated (`≈ ϵ`). By restricting velocity queries to these early denoising timesteps (e.g., 950-1000), the model can be queried directly with `ϵ`, bypassing the need for `z_n`. Disruptions at these early stages are potent as they alter the global structure, leading to amplified errors across the autoregressive rollout.
- **Context-based history proxy**: For the historical condition `z_<n` required by autoregressive generation, a differentiable proxy is constructed by repeating the encoded adversarial context: `h_δ_n = Repeat(E(x + δ), L_n)`, where `L_n` is the required history length. This method enables the perturbation `δ` to influence velocity prediction without requiring knowledge of true future history.

Four self-supervised objectives for `L_atk` are proposed:
- **Velocity magnitude objectives**: These objectives manipulate the magnitude of the predicted velocity `ˆv_δ`.
 - `L_VMax = -||ˆv_δ||^2_2`: Maximizes velocity magnitude, forcing aggressive denoising, which can introduce unnatural contrast and severe distortions.
 - `L_VMin = ||ˆv_δ||^2_2`: Minimizes velocity magnitude, suppressing the update norm, leading to incomplete denoising and retention of initial noise, causing structural degradation.
- **Velocity direction objectives**: These objectives manipulate the direction of `ˆv_δ` relative to a context-anchored reference velocity `v_ref = ϵ - x_ctx`, which represents a static video preserving the initial frame.
 - `L_DMax = -||ˆv_δ - v_ref||^2_2`: Maximizes the divergence from `v_ref`, encouraging semantic drift and temporal inconsistency.
 - `L_DMin = ||ˆv_δ - v_ref||^2_2`: Minimizes the divergence from `v_ref`, pulling the velocity towards the static reference, suppressing scene evolution, and inducing motion collapse.

#### 3.2. Trajectory-Adaptive Bi-Level Optimization

To ensure the attack generalizes across unpredictable user actions (C2), BADWORLD employs a trajectory-adaptive bi-level optimization. The core idea is to find a perturbation `δ` that remains effective even against "hard" trajectories—those control sequences that currently best resist the attack.

This is formulated as a min-max optimization problem: `min_{||δ||_∞<=η} max_{τ ∈T} F (τ ; δ)`, where `T` is the feasible trajectory space, and `F(τ; δ)` is the expected adversarial loss under a given trajectory `τ`.
- The **outer loop** aims to discover hard trajectories `τ` that maximize the expected loss `F(τ; δ)`.
- The **inner loop** updates the image perturbation `δ` to minimize `F(τ; δ)` under these identified hard trajectories.

Trajectory `τ` is parameterized as a vector of control triplets `(ψ_t, f_t, s_t)` for each frame `t`, representing yaw angle, forward displacement, and lateral shift, respectively. Physical plausibility and adherence to the model's in-distribution control space are maintained through bound constraints on absolute magnitudes and temporal derivatives (e.g., `Δψ`, `Δf`, `Δs`).

For the outer loop, a derivative-free method called **Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES)** is used to search for hard trajectories. CMA-ES adapts a multivariate Gaussian distribution over the trajectory vector `τ`. It samples candidate trajectories, evaluates their "fitness" (i.e., the adversarial loss `F(τ; δ)`), and updates its distribution parameters (mean, step-size, covariance matrix) to shift the search towards harder trajectories and capture coherent motion patterns. The top-K hardest trajectories are collected into a pool `P_hard` for each autoregressive chunk.

In the inner loop, for each PGD step, a trajectory `τ` is sampled from the hard trajectory pool `P_hard` (or a random-walk sampler for broad robustness in standard short-horizon steps). The perturbation `δ` is then updated using the gradient of `L_atk` with respect to `δ`, projected back into the `ℓ_∞` bounds.

By alternating between hard-trajectory mining and perturbation updates, BADWORLD generates an adversarial context image designed to induce unstable autoregressive rollouts across diverse and challenging control scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on two open-source autoregressive VWMs: Astra, which uses continuous camera pose conditioning, and Matrix-Game 2.0, which relies on discrete action signals. Two 100-image datasets were compiled: SpatialVID for Astra and Grand Theft Auto (GTA) gameplay frames (preprocessed with Flux Kontext) for Matrix-Game 2.0. A random noise baseline was used for comparison. Attack efficacy was assessed using VBench, VBench++, CLIP-I (for semantic preservation), and MEt3R (for geometric consistency), evaluating aspects like contextual consistency, video quality, and geometric reliance. All attacks were executed on an A800 80GB GPU, restricting optimization to early denoising timesteps (950-1000) and using a context-based history proxy, with a default noise budget `η = 0.05`.

#### 4.1. Main Results on Different Attack Objectives

Comparison of the four self-supervised objectives showed varying degrees of effectiveness across models:

- **On Astra**: The `Velocity-Min` objective consistently demonstrated the most pronounced degradation. It caused substantial declines in aesthetic quality (from 0.501 to 0.405) and imaging quality (from 0.690 to 0.513), while MEt3R, indicating geometric inconsistency, increased by 74.8% (from 0.151 to 0.264) compared to the clean baseline. `Drift-Max` was the second most effective. In contrast, `Velocity-Max`, `Random Noise`, and `Drift-Min` showed negligible impact. Qualitatively, `Velocity-Min` triggered incomplete denoising and grayish artifacts, whereas other objectives primarily altered color temperature.

- **On Matrix-Game-2.0**: This model exhibited broader vulnerabilities. `Velocity-Min` remained the most potent, significantly reducing background consistency and imaging quality, and achieving the highest MEt3R score. `Drift-Max` and `Velocity-Max` also demonstrated effectiveness by degrading imaging quality and CLIP-I scores. Qualitatively, `Velocity-Min` induced severe structural disintegration and noise, `Drift-Max` and `Velocity-Max` resulted in unnaturally high color saturation, and `Drift-Min` produced nearly static videos.

In summary, `Velocity-Min` was identified as the most effective objective across both benchmarks for degrading video quality and was subsequently adopted for further experiments.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies (on Astra with Velocity-Min)

- **Attack Budget**: Increasing the perturbation budget `η` from 0.03 to 0.10 consistently improved attack performance (lower quality metrics, higher MEt3R), indicating greater disruption. A budget of `η = 0.05` was chosen as a balance between attack potency and visual imperceptibility.
- **Diffusion Timesteps**: The early-denoising strategy was critical. Without this specific focus (i.e., using uniform timestep sampling), attack efficacy collapsed, with aesthetic quality rising and MEt3R scores dropping, indicating minimal disruption. This suggests that targeting initial structural formation is essential.
- **History Simulation**: The context-based history proxy (repeating the adversarial context frame) proved efficient and effective. A more computationally intensive "self-rollout" approach yielded only negligible improvements, supporting the efficiency of the proxy.

#### 4.3. Performance of Trajectory-Adaptive Bi-level Optimization

While bi-level optimization showed moderate overall gains on the full Astra benchmark compared to standard `Velocity-Min`, its efficacy was more evident in challenging scenarios. When evaluated on the 10 most resilient samples against `Velocity-Min`, the bi-level approach consistently degraded all metrics, for example, reducing Aesthetic Quality to 0.425. This confirmed its superior generalizability, ensuring rollout collapse regardless of sample difficulty or camera path, and producing more pronounced noise and geometric distortion in cases where the baseline struggled.

#### 4.4. Robustness and Transferability (Supplementary Material)

- **Robustness to Image Preprocessing**: `Velocity-Min` perturbations demonstrated robustness against light Gaussian noise. More aggressive preprocessing (JPEG compression, Total Variation denoising) partially mitigated the attack but still allowed for measurable degradation compared to clean baselines, indicating resilience to standard image-level filters.
- **Black-box Transfer across Models**: Transferability was observed from Matrix-Game to Astra, with noticeable declines in video quality metrics. However, transfer from Astra to Matrix-Game was limited, suggesting that significant input resolution discrepancies can disrupt pixel-level perturbations and degrade black-box transferability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research expose significant structural fragility in current visual world models (VWMs). The BADWORLD framework demonstrates that visually imperceptible adversarial perturbations can reliably trigger catastrophic degradation in future rollouts, manifesting as incomplete denoising, structural collapse, and inconsistency in control responses.

These results highlight critical risks associated with deploying VWMs in safety-critical applications, such as autonomous driving, robotics, or interactive simulation environments. The observed vulnerabilities suggest that VWMs, despite their advanced capabilities, might be susceptible to subtle manipulations that could lead to unpredictable or erroneous behavior in real-world scenarios, potentially undermining reliability and safety.

Conversely, the study also identifies a practical mechanism for privacy protection. The ability to introduce imperceptible perturbations that disrupt unauthorized interactive generation offers a method to safeguard images from misuse by VWMs. This could enable individuals to protect their visual data from being repurposed or manipulated without consent in generative AI applications.

Overall, the work contributes to a deeper understanding of the robustness limitations of contemporary VWMs, providing valuable insights for both the development of more resilient models and the design of privacy-enhancing technologies.
