# Traversing Narrow Paths: A Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning Framework for Robust and Safe Humanoid Walking

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Science and Technology of China developed a two-stage reinforcement learning framework that enables humanoid robots to robustly and safely traverse narrow paths by combining a physics-based foothold planner with learned low-level tracking and high-level modification policies. The framework achieved a 100% success rate on 0.2m wide beams on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, outperforming a state-of-the-art baseline.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with robust and safe locomotion on narrow paths due to limited footholds and high sensitivity to perception inaccuracies or control delays.
- Traditional model-based control methods are susceptible to modeling discrepancies and contact uncertainties, leading to imprecise foot placement on constrained terrains.
- Purely end-to-end reinforcement learning methods for locomotion can overfit simulator assumptions, engage in unsafe exploration, and often lack interpretability in safety-critical applications.

## Method

- A two-stage reinforcement learning framework is proposed, integrating a 3D-LIPM foothold planner, a high-level foothold modifier, and a low-level foothold tracker, all utilizing minimal sensing (proprioception and a compact anterior terrain height map).
- Stage-I trains a robust low-level foothold tracker on flat ground, intentionally perturbing target footholds to enhance its ability to adapt to small variations.
- ==Stage-II== trains a high-level foothold modifier to refine the physics-based foothold targets on narrow paths, prioritizing safety and precision using terrain perception and specific reward terms for 'foothold safety' and 'beam balance'.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved a **100%** success rate in simulation across varying narrow beam widths and lengths, significantly outperforming a 'No-Modifier' baseline (**15%** success) and an 'RL-Only' baseline (**0%** success).
- On a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot, the framework achieved a **100%** success rate and **100%** traversal rate on a 0.2m wide, 3m long beam over 20 trials, surpassing the BeamDojo baseline (**80%** success, **88.16%** traversal rate).
- An ablation study demonstrated that removing intentional disturbances during Stage-I training reduced success to **50%** and increased centerline deviation, confirming the importance of this robustness-building step.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining an interpretable physics-based planner with learned residual adjustments (a hybrid approach) is more effective and safer for narrow path traversal than purely model-based or end-to-end RL methods.
> - Introducing intentional target perturbations during low-level training (Stage-I) is crucial for building a robust foothold tracker capable of handling the dynamic, modified targets generated by the high-level policy on challenging terrains.
> - Minimal exteroceptive sensing (a compact anterior terrain height map) can be effectively integrated into a high-level modifier to provide sufficient information for precise and safe foot placement without overly complex vision pipelines, aiding sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots traversing narrow paths presents a significant challenge in robotics, primarily due to the limited and safety-critical footholds required for stable locomotion. This task necessitates a robust integration of efficient terrain perception, precise foothold selection, and stable locomotion control. The existing research landscape for bipedal locomotion generally follows two distinct paradigms: model-based control and model-free reinforcement learning (RL).

Model-based approaches leverage compact template models, such as the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM), Zero-Moment Point (ZMP), or Instantaneous Capture Point (ICP), to generate stable locomotion patterns and guide foot placement. These methods, often integrated with Model Predictive Control (MPC), provide interpretability and adherence to physical principles, allowing for real-time adaptation to disturbances. However, they are susceptible to modeling discrepancies, contact uncertainty, and control system latency, which can lead to inaccuracies in foot placement, particularly on highly constrained terrains.

Conversely, model-free reinforcement learning methods learn end-to-end policies for foothold selection and locomotion control directly from data. Approaches like attention-based terrain map encoders have demonstrated generalized legged locomotion, and multi-stage RL pipelines with sparse rewards and exteroception (e.g., BeamDojo, which uses LiDAR for terrain mapping) have enabled hardware-validated traversal of narrow beams and stepping stones. While RL offers advantages in generalization and adaptation to complex dynamics, purely end-to-end methods can be prone to overfitting simulator-specific assumptions, engaging in unsafe exploration during training, and may lack interpretability in safety-critical applications.

The limitations of these individual paradigms have motivated the development of hybrid or synthesized methods, often termed "residual learning." These approaches aim to combine the strengths of both by augmenting a physics-based nominal controller with learned data-driven refinements. This allows for improved robustness without discarding the interpretability and stability offered by physical models. Previous work has explored residual policy learning in manipulation and dynamic legged locomotion, demonstrating data-efficient behaviors on hardware. However, applying residual learning to safety-critical, sparse-support tasks like narrow path traversal remains challenging due to the potential for reward collapse and failure sensitivity in training.

This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by proposing a two-stage RL framework that specifically addresses the challenges of narrow path traversal using a residual learning approach. It integrates an explicit physics-based foothold planner (3D-LIPM) with a lightweight, learned foothold modifier, contrasting with purely model-based methods by adding data-driven adaptation and differentiating from purely end-to-end RL by maintaining model interpretability and stability priors. The use of a staged training curriculum and minimal exteroceptive sensing for foothold adjustment further connects this work to advancements in curriculum-driven RL and robust perceptive locomotion for quadrupedal robots. The research aims to bridge the gap in developing an efficient, robust, and safe narrow path traversing control framework for humanoids that combines the benefits of interpretable physical models with the adaptability of reinforcement learning, addressing a persistent challenge in humanoid robot navigation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and safe locomotion control framework for humanoid robots to effectively traverse narrow paths. This objective is driven by the inherent difficulties of such terrains, where the available path width for feasible footholds can shrink to the size of a single foot, leaving minimal to no recovery margins for errors. Even minor perception inaccuracies or control delays can lead to catastrophic failure.

The specific motivations behind the proposed framework stem from the limitations identified in existing locomotion control paradigms:
1. **Challenges with Purely Template-Based Methods:** Traditional physics-based template models, while providing interpretable guidance for balanced locomotion, are often vulnerable to real-world complexities such as modeling discrepancies, uncertainties in contact dynamics, and control system latencies. These factors can lead to inaccuracies in foot placement, which are critically detrimental on narrow support surfaces. The goal is to retain the interpretability of these models while mitigating their susceptibility to real-world imperfections.
2. **Challenges with Purely End-to-End Reinforcement Learning (RL) Methods:** While end-to-end RL approaches offer the potential for generalized and adaptive locomotion, they can struggle in safety-critical tasks. Such methods may overfit to simulator-specific assumptions, potentially leading to unsafe exploration during training. Furthermore, the learned policies often lack interpretability, making it difficult to diagnose and rectify issues, especially when precise and safe foot placement is paramount. The motivation here is to harness the generalization capability of RL without sacrificing safety or interpretability.
3. **Need for Synthesized Approach:** The identified shortcomings of both model-based and model-free methods highlight a need for a synthesized approach. The authors are motivated to develop a framework that preserves the interpretability and stability priors from physics-based models while incorporating data-driven refinements to improve robustness and precision. This residual learning philosophy is central to the design.
4. **Robustness and Safety on Sparse Footholds:** For tasks involving sparse footholds, like narrow beam traversal, the training process for RL can be highly sensitive to failures, making residual learning brittle without careful structuring. A key motivation is to design a training framework with a curriculum that explicitly fosters robustness against disturbances and prioritizes safety-critical foot placement on challenging terrains.
5. **Sim-to-Real Transferability with Minimal Sensing:** Practical deployment of humanoid robots requires efficient training and straightforward transfer from simulation to the real world. Heavy-weight vision pipelines can complicate this process. Therefore, a motivation is to develop a control framework that relies on minimal and consistent sensing information across simulation and hardware, reducing the complexity and increasing the feasibility of real-world application.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Develop a two-stage RL training framework that effectively combines a physics-based foothold planner with learned control components.
- Train a low-level foothold tracker to robustly follow foothold targets despite small disturbances.
- Train a high-level foothold modifier to safely and precisely refine footholds on narrow paths using minimal terrain perception.
- Ensure the resulting controller exhibits high success rates, precise centerline adherence, and adequate safety margins on narrow paths.
- Validate the framework on a physical humanoid robot with a focus on sim-to-real transferability and minimal sensing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named "Traversing Narrow Paths," employs a two-stage reinforcement learning curriculum to achieve robust and safe humanoid walking on narrow terrains. It integrates a physics-based foothold planner with learned low-level tracking and high-level modification policies.

**A. Framework Overview**
The architecture consists of three main components:
1. **3D-LIPM Foothold Planner:** This component first generates an initial swing-leg foothold target ($u_{init}$) based on the robot’s current state (CoM position, velocity) and commanded gait parameters. This planner embodies the explicit, interpretable stepping physics.
2. **High-Level Foothold Modifier (Stage-II Policy):** This policy is event-driven, operating when a step transition occurs. It predicts a body-frame residual ($\Delta u = (\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta \psi)$) for the swing foot. This residual refines the initial foothold target to produce the final target ($u_{final} = u_{init} \oplus \Delta u$). The modifier's output is held constant until the next step transition.
3. **Low-Level Foothold Tracker (Stage-I Policy):** This policy runs at 100 Hz, consuming proprioceptive information (IMU, joint states) and step phase. It outputs desired joint positions to a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller, which operates at 1 kHz, to ensure the robot’s swing foot reaches the $u_{final}$ target.

The framework emphasizes minimal sensing, utilizing only onboard IMU signals, joint states, and a compact anterior terrain height map. This design choice aims to ease sim-to-real transfer by ensuring consistency between simulated and physical sensor representations.

**B. Stage-I Training for Robust Foothold Tracker**
The objective of Stage-I training is to develop a robust low-level foothold tracker capable of adapting to small disturbances in foothold targets. This prepares the policy for tracking modified footholds on narrow paths in Stage-II.

1. **LIPM-based Foothold Planner:** A standard Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM) is used, assuming a constant Center of Mass (CoM) height. The Instantaneous Capture Point (ICP) is computed, and a function $\Pi(\cdot)$ maps the ICP, commanded velocity, and gait phase to generate the initial foothold target $u_{init}$.
2. **Foothold Tracker Training on Flat Ground:** The low-level policy is trained on flat ground. To instill robustness, a bounded random perturbation $\epsilon = (\delta x, \delta y, \delta \psi)$ is added to $u_{init}$ to form the perturbed target $\tilde{u}_{init} = u_{init} + \epsilon$. The perturbation ranges are $\delta x = \delta y = 0.05$ m and $\delta \psi = 20^\circ$. This perturbation is applied only to the swing foot and remains constant until touchdown.
 - **Observations:** The policy observes proprioception (IMU signals, joint states) and current step phase.
 - **Actions:** Desired joint position commands.
 - **Reward Function:** The reward function encourages accurate foothold realization, stable contact scheduling, and overall stable locomotion. Key terms include:
 - `step tracking`: Rewards correct stance leg alternation and precise swing leg foot placement at touchdown (position and yaw accuracy).
 - `tracking lin vel world`: Penalizes errors between commanded and measured base linear velocities.
 - `base heading & base z orientation`: Aligns base heading to commanded value and penalizes base tilt.
 - `joint regularization`: Keeps hip/waist yaw and leg abduction/adduction angles near neutral to avoid extreme poses.
 - Additional penalties for linear velocity in z, angular velocity in xy, DOF velocity, torques, actuation rate, and DOF/torque limits.

**C. Stage-II Training for Safe Foothold Modifier**
Stage-II training focuses on developing a high-level, exteroception-based foothold modifier that refines the template-based foothold targets on narrow paths. The goal is to prioritize safe and precise foot placement without manual lateral locking.

1. **Anterior Terrain Height Map:** To maintain lightweight and deployment-friendly sensing, the modifier consumes a compact anterior terrain height map. This map covers a fixed area in front of the robot (x ∈ [0.1, 1.1] m, y ∈ [−0.8, 0.8] m) with a uniform resolution of 0.1 m, resulting in an 11 × 17 grid. In simulation, heights are queried from the terrain field; in hardware, a LiDAR-based mapper generates this map for consistency.
2. **Foothold Modifier Training on Narrow Paths:** At each step transition, the modifier outputs a body-frame residual $\Delta u$. The final foothold target $u_{final}$ for the swing foot is computed by composing $u_{init}$ with a saturated version of $\Delta u$, `sat_S(∆u)`, where S sets component-wise adjustment bounds.
 - **Observations:** The high-level modifier policy observes proprioception (IMU, joint states), step phase features, the current $u_{init}$ from the planner, and the flattened anterior terrain height map.
 - **Actions:** The body-frame residual $\Delta u = (\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta \psi)$.
 - **Reward Function:** The reward function explicitly emphasizes locomotion safety and precision on narrow paths. Key terms include:
 - `foothold safety`: Penalizes footholds landing in "abyss" regions (below a safety threshold) or on non-flat areas.
 - `beam balance`: Encourages centerline adherence by applying a Gaussian penalty for lateral deviation from the beam's centerline.
 - `forward progress`: Rewards positive forward movement.
 - `face forward`: Encourages alignment of foot orientation with the forward direction.
 - `feet proximity`: Penalizes excessively small inter-foot distance along the forward direction.
 - `action magnitude & action smoothness`: Penalizes the magnitude of the residual and its step-to-step variation, encouraging minimal and smooth adjustments.

**D. Domain Randomization and Hyperparameters**
Both training stages incorporate domain randomization for physical properties (e.g., payload mass, external pushes, friction coefficients) and observation noise (e.g., angular velocity, projected gravity, joint states, height measurements). This aids in policy robustness and sim-to-real transfer. Standard PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) is used for training, with specific hyperparameters detailed in the appendices of the paper.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed two-stage reinforcement learning framework was evaluated through simulation studies and hardware validation on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating its performance in traversing narrow paths.

**A. Simulation Evaluation**
The simulation evaluation compared the proposed method against two baselines and conducted an ablation study using three metrics: success rate (%), centerline deviation (m), and foot-placement Root Mean Square Error (FP-RMSE) (m). Each evaluation involved 20 episodes on straight beams of varying widths (0.15 m, 0.20 m, 0.25 m) and lengths (3-5 m).

- **Baselines:**
 - **No-Modifier:** This baseline used only the Stage-I policy, tracking the LIPM-based foothold targets without any residual refinement.
 - **RL-Only:** This baseline represented an end-to-end RL approach, learning directly from the same observations as the proposed method to output joint positions, without a reduced-order model foothold planner.
- **Ablation Study:** This study investigated the effect of "w/o Stage-I disturbances," meaning the Stage-I policy was trained without the intentional random foothold perturbations.

**Simulation Results Summary:**

| Method | Success rate (%) | Centerline dev. (m) | FP-RMSE (m) |
|:--------------------------- |:--------------- |:------------------ |:-------------------- |
| No-Modifier | 15 | 0.04690 ± 0.00057 | 0.01962 ± 0.00079 |
| RL-Only | 0 | 0.18192 ± 0.07075 | — |
| **Ours** | **100** | **0.01639 ± 0.00117** | **0.02633 ± 0.00083** |

| Configuration | Success (%) | Centerline dev. (m) | FP-RMSE (m) |
|:--------------------------- |:---------- |:------------------ |:-------------------- |
| w/o Stage-I disturbances | 50 | 0.09696 ± 0.07876 | 0.05467 ± 0.05467 |
| **Ours** | **100** | **0.01639 ± 0.00117** | **0.02633 ± 0.00083** |

- **Performance Comparison:** The proposed method demonstrated a 100% success rate across all simulated beam widths and lengths, significantly outperforming both baselines. The No-Modifier baseline achieved only 15% success, while the RL-Only baseline failed in all trials (0% success). The proposed method also exhibited superior centerline adherence, with a centerline deviation of 0.01639 m, considerably lower than No-Modifier (0.04690 m) and RL-Only (0.18192 m). The FP-RMSE for the proposed method was 0.02633 m. While slightly higher than the No-Modifier baseline's 0.01962 m (which tracked unperturbed targets), this is attributed to the proposed method accurately tracking a *modified* foothold target ($u_{final}$) that includes a non-zero residual, effectively making a more challenging target.
- **Ablation Study Findings:** The ablation study showed that removing the random disturbances during Stage-I training significantly degraded performance. The "w/o Stage-I disturbances" configuration achieved only 50% success, a substantially higher centerline deviation (0.09696 m), and a larger FP-RMSE (0.05467 m). This indicates that the intentional target perturbations in Stage-I are critical for developing a robust foothold tracker capable of tolerating variations, which directly benefits narrow path traversal.
- **Failure Mode Observation:** A dominant failure mode observed during training was pronounced heading angle oscillation, which could shrink the feasible foothold set in the local terrain map. This sometimes caused the initial foothold target to drift towards the path edge, pushing the modifier's residual to its bounds without successfully returning the final foothold target onto the narrow path, leading to off-path footholds.

**B. Hardware Validation**
The full proposed method was validated on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot traversing a 0.2 m wide, 3 m long wooden beam. 20 independent trials were conducted.

- **LiDAR-based Height Map:** An onboard LiDAR stream was used to construct a body-centric anterior terrain height map online, consistent with the simulation setup (11x17 grid, 0.1m resolution). The process involved transforming raw data to the robot’s body frame, cropping to a region of interest, gridding, applying adaptive binning with maximum height estimation, clamping heights to a range ([0.7, 1.4] m), and flattening the map.
- **Metrics:** Hardware performance was evaluated using success rate (%) and traversal rate (fraction of beam length completed before failure).
- **Hardware Comparison:** The results were compared with reported state-of-the-art results from BeamDojo.

| Setting | Success rate (%) | Traversal rate (%) |
|:---------------------------- |:--------------- |:----------------- |
| BeamDojo (G1, real beam) | 80 | 88.16 |
| **Ours (G1, real beam)** | **100** | **100** |

- **Performance on Hardware:** The proposed method achieved a 100% success rate and 100% traversal rate over 20 trials, outperforming the BeamDojo baseline, which reported 80% success and 88.16% traversal rate over 5 trials.
- **Mitigation of Bias:** During pilot runs, occasional height-estimation bias near the path boundary was observed, which could nudge foothold targets off-path. This was mitigated by applying robust per-grid statistics (median with outlier rejection), mild temporal smoothing of the height map, and conservative residual bounds. These measures prevented such failures in the reported evaluation runs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for humanoid locomotion that addresses the challenging problem of robust and safe traversal over narrow paths. The findings indicate several areas of significance and potential impact.

**Methodological Advancement:**
The development of a two-stage reinforcement learning framework that couples a physics-based foothold planner with a learned low-level tracker and a high-level modifier represents a methodological contribution to the field of legged locomotion. This approach leverages the interpretability and inherent stability of physics-based models while incorporating the adaptability and generalization capabilities of reinforcement learning. The structured curriculum, progressing from flat ground training with target disturbances to narrow path training with terrain-aware objectives, is demonstrated to be effective for learning critical behaviors in safety-critical environments.

**Enhanced Robustness and Safety:**
The empirical results, particularly the 100% success rate and low centerline deviation in simulation and hardware, indicate that the framework significantly enhances the robot's ability to maintain balance and precisely place its feet on constrained surfaces. The ablation study specifically highlights the importance of incorporating target disturbances in Stage-I training, suggesting a mechanism for fostering a more robust foothold tracker that can better handle variations in commanded foot placements. This robustness is crucial for real-world deployment where perfect foothold planning or tracking is often unattainable. The explicit reward terms for "foothold safety" and "beam balance" in Stage-II contribute directly to the safety-critical aspects of the task, guiding the robot to autonomously select stable and centered footholds.

**Practicality and Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
The framework's minimal sensing requirements (relying on proprioception and a compact anterior terrain height map from LiDAR, rather than complex vision pipelines) contributes to its practicality. This simplifies the observation space and reduces the computational overhead, which is beneficial for real-time operation on robotic hardware. The consistent representation of sensing information between simulation and physical experiments facilitates the sim-to-real transfer process, as validated by successful hardware deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. This aspect has implications for accelerating the development and deployment of humanoid robots in real-world unstructured environments.

**Competitive Performance:**
The demonstrated performance improvement over both purely template-based and end-to-end RL baselines in simulation, as well as outperforming a state-of-the-art method (BeamDojo) on a physical robot, suggests that the proposed hybrid architecture offers a competitive approach to narrow path traversal. The framework provides a balanced solution that mitigates the weaknesses of previous paradigms.

**Future Research Directions:**
The paper outlines future work including extending the framework to more complex sparse-support terrains such as stepping stones, gaps, and curved beams, and expanding the foothold representation to 3D. This indicates the foundational nature of this work, which could serve as a basis for developing more general and versatile humanoid locomotion controllers capable of navigating a wider range of challenging and uneven environments. This could pave the way for humanoids to operate in construction sites, disaster zones, or even household environments with diverse obstacles.
