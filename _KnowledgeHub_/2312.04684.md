# LaRS: Latent Reasoning Skills for Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> LaRS introduces an unsupervised method that discovers and leverages latent reasoning skills from Chain-of-Thought rationales using a Conditional Variational Autoencoder. This approach consistently improves LLM performance on complex reasoning tasks, achieving up to 4x faster pre-processing and significantly enhanced robustness to noisy example banks compared to prior skill-based selection methods.

## Problem

- Existing Chain-of-Thought (CoT) demonstration selection methods, based on question similarity, often lead to 'skill mismatches' as similar questions may require different reasoning processes.
- Prior skill-based CoT selection methods, like Skill-KNN, rely on expensive LLM inferences to generate explicit textual skill descriptions, leading to high computational costs, poor scalability, and subjective skill representations.
- The quality and consistency of CoT prompting can be severely degraded by suboptimal examples, highlighting a need for more robust selection strategies.

## Method

- LaRS proposes a ==latent skill model== that formulates rationale generation as conditioned on both the question and a latent reasoning skill, providing a theoretical framework for skill-based CoT.
- It implements this model using a ==Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE)== with lightweight MLPs and pre-trained embedding models to unsupervisedly discover latent reasoning skills from question-rationale pairs.
- Demonstration selection for a new query involves inferring its desired ==latent skill==, computing demonstrated skills for examples in the bank, and selecting top-k examples based on ==cosine similarity in the latent skill space==.

## Results

- LaRS consistently outperformed the state-of-the-art `Skill-KNN-zero` in **18 out of 20** experiments across four reasoning benchmarks (TabMWP, GSM8K, Spider, COGS), and matched oracle performance in nearly half of the experiments.
- It achieved a **4x faster** pre-processing time for example banks and eliminated LLM inference costs for skill discovery, saving approximately **$30 per 1k** examples compared to `Skill-KNN-zero`.
- The method demonstrated superior robustness to sub-optimal example banks, showing minimal performance degradation (**0.**1%** to 1.9%**) with **30%** random rationales, whereas `Skill-KNN-zero` experienced drops up to **11.7%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reasoning rationales are not solely dependent on the question but are also governed by underlying 'latent reasoning skills' that can be automatically discovered without explicit textual descriptions.
> - Unsupervised learning with a CVAE can effectively capture abstract reasoning concepts in a continuous latent space, which aligns with human understanding of distinct skills.
> - Representing skills as continuous latent variables and using lightweight models for inference dramatically improves computational efficiency and scalability compared to methods relying on LLM-generated skill descriptions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "LaRS: Latent Reasoning Skills for Chain-of-Thought Reasoning" is situated within the rapidly evolving landscape of Large Language Models (LLMs) and their application to complex reasoning tasks, particularly through "in-context learning" (ICL) and "chain-of-thought" (CoT) prompting.

**Evolution of ICL and CoT:**
Initially, ICL demonstrated LLMs' ability to perform tasks by conditioning on a few input-output examples in the prompt, without explicit fine-tuning. CoT prompting extended this by including intermediate reasoning steps (rationales) within these examples, significantly boosting LLM performance on complex tasks requiring multi-step reasoning, such as arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning.

**The Challenge of Demonstration Selection:**
A critical factor for the success of ICL and CoT is the quality and relevance of the "demonstrations" (examples) included in the prompt. Suboptimal examples can severely degrade performance. This has led to extensive research on "demonstration selection" methods.

**Limitations of Prior Approaches:**
- **Question-Similarity-Based Selection:** Many early ICL selection methods focused on finding examples whose *questions* were semantically similar to the input query. However, for CoT, where the *reasoning process* is paramount, similar questions might require different reasoning *skills*, leading to suboptimal or incorrect rationales (as illustrated in Figure 1 of the paper).
- **Existing Skill-Based Selection (e.g., Skill-KNN):** Recognizing the need to align reasoning skills, recent methods like Skill-KNN have emerged. These methods attempt to describe the "skill" of an example's rationale and then select examples with skills similar to the input question's required skill. However, this approach has significant drawbacks:
 - **Reliance on LLM-Generated Skill Descriptions:** It typically requires powerful pre-trained LLMs to *generate textual descriptions* of skills for each example, which is resource-intensive (costly LLM inferences), slow, and can produce arbitrary or inconsistent descriptions based on manual prompt engineering.
 - **Scalability Issues:** Generating unique skill descriptions for every example in a large bank becomes impractical.

**LaRS's Contribution to the Landscape:**
LaRS directly addresses these limitations by introducing an **unsupervised skill-based demonstration selection method**. Instead of explicitly describing skills using LLMs, LaRS learns to represent these "reasoning skills" as latent variables in a continuous space. This paradigm shift offers several advantages:

- **Automation and Efficiency:** It automates skill discovery, bypassing the need for expensive LLM inference or human-designed skill prompts, making it significantly more compute-efficient and scalable than SOTA skill-based methods like Skill-KNN.
- **Theoretical Grounding:** The paper introduces a "latent skill model" formulation, which provides a deeper theoretical understanding of CoT reasoning, positing that rationales are generated conditioned on both the question and a latent reasoning skill. This offers a principled basis for the selection process.
- **Robustness:** By considering the full rationale during skill learning, LaRS develops a more robust understanding of the underlying skills, leading to better performance even with noisy or sub-optimal example banks.

In essence, LaRS pushes the frontier of CoT prompting by providing a method that is not only empirically superior but also theoretically grounded, computationally efficient, and robust, moving beyond heuristic or LLM-dependent skill annotation to a learned, latent representation. This positions it as a significant step towards more autonomous and effective LLM reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the development of Latent Reasoning Skills (LaRS) stem from identified shortcomings in existing Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting and demonstration selection methodologies for Large Language Models (LLMs).

#### 2.1. Primary Objectives

1. **Develop a novel unsupervised method for discovering and leveraging latent reasoning skills:** The core objective is to move beyond explicit, human- or LLM-generated skill descriptions to an automatic, unsupervised discovery of these skills as latent representations within the rationales of CoT examples.
2. **Enable effective skill-based demonstration selection for CoT prompting:** Based on these discovered latent skills, the method should efficiently select the most relevant in-context examples to guide LLMs in solving complex reasoning tasks.
3. **Enhance computational efficiency and scalability:** The proposed solution must significantly reduce the computational overhead and resource intensity associated with current skill-based selection methods, particularly by minimizing reliance on expensive LLM inferences for skill description.
4. **Improve robustness to sub-optimal example banks:** The method should perform well even when the example bank contains noisy or imperfect rationales, reflecting real-world data imperfections.
5. **Provide theoretical grounding for skill-based CoT reasoning:** Introduce a formal model that explains how reasoning skills influence rationale generation in CoT, thereby offering a deeper understanding of the mechanism.

#### 2.2. Motivation

The motivations for pursuing these objectives are multifaceted and directly address critical limitations and challenges in the current state of LLM reasoning:

1. **Ineffectiveness of Question-Similarity-Based Selection for CoT:** Traditional in-context learning often selects examples based on the similarity of the *input question*. However, for CoT, the *reasoning process* (rationale) is crucial. As illustrated in Figure 1, questions that appear similar might require fundamentally different underlying reasoning *skills*. Selecting examples based solely on question similarity can lead to "skill mismatches," where the LLM is prompted with a rationale demonstrating an irrelevant skill, resulting in incorrect answers. This highlights the need for skill-aware selection.
2. **Cost and Scalability Issues of Explicit Skill Description:** While the importance of skill-based selection for CoT is acknowledged (e.g., An et al., 2023b), existing methods like Skill-KNN rely on pre-trained LLMs to *generate textual descriptions* of skills. This approach is inherently problematic:
 - **High Cost:** Generating these descriptions for every example in a bank, and for every test question, requires numerous LLM inferences, which are computationally expensive and incur significant monetary costs.
 - **Lack of Scalability:** The overhead grows linearly with the size of the example bank, making it impractical for very large datasets.
 - **Arbitrary and Subjective Skill Descriptions:** The quality and consistency of LLM-generated skill descriptions are highly dependent on manually crafted prompts, which can be arbitrary, difficult to standardize across diverse tasks, and may not capture the true underlying reasoning logic.
3. **Need for a Principled and Automatic Skill Discovery:** The reliance on external LLM inference or human annotation for skill identification is a bottleneck. There is a strong motivation to develop a method that can automatically and efficiently *discover* these reasoning skills from the raw question-rationale pairs within an example bank, without external guidance or costly auxiliary models.
4. **Desire for Theoretical Clarity:** Much of CoT prompting has been empirical. A motivation is to provide a theoretical framework (the latent skill model) that formally describes how reasoning skills interact with questions to produce rationales. This framework helps to understand *why* skill-based selection is effective and provides a solid foundation for the proposed method.
5. **Practical Robustness and Efficiency:** Beyond academic objectives, there is a practical motivation to create a more robust and cost-effective solution for deploying CoT prompting in real-world applications. Reducing inference costs and improving performance with imperfect data are key industry considerations.

In summary, LaRS is motivated by the critical need to enhance the effectiveness, efficiency, and robustness of CoT prompting by fundamentally rethinking how reasoning skills are identified and utilized in demonstration selection, moving from explicit, costly, and potentially arbitrary descriptions to implicit, unsupervised, and theoretically grounded latent representations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LaRS introduces a novel, unsupervised approach to discover and utilize "latent reasoning skills" for selecting in-context examples in Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. The methodology is structured around a theoretical formulation called the "latent skill model" and its practical implementation using a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE).

#### 3.1. The Latent Skill Model (Theoretical Formulation)

The paper posits that the generation of a rationale (R) for a given question (Q) can be understood through a "latent skill model." This model assumes that a rationale is generated not just from the question, but also conditioned on a latent variable, `z`, which represents a "reasoning skill."

- **Human Rationale Generation:** The model formalizes the human conditional distribution of R given Q as `P_H(R | Q) = ∫ P_H(R | z, Q)P_H(z | Q)dz`. Here, `P_H(z | Q)` is termed the *reasoning policy*, indicating the selection of a reasoning skill `z` for a question `Q`, and `P_H(R | z, Q)` is the distribution of rationales given `Q` and `z`. This formulation suggests that rationales exhibit flexibility (diverse formats, topics, knowledge) which can be abstracted into `z`, and that skill selection is stochastic, reflecting diverse correct reasoning paths.
- **CoT Prompting and LLMs:** The model extends to LLMs, viewing them as implicit skill models that also infer reasoning skills `z`. In CoT prompting, demonstrations (either prefixes or few-shot examples) are seen to influence `P_M(z | pt)`, thereby shaping the LLM's reasoning skill distribution towards more optimal ones. The core idea is that few-shot examples effectively "point" the LLM to retrieve learned knowledge corresponding to a specific reasoning skill.
- **Skill-Based Demonstration Selection:** Building on this, the optimal demonstration selection strategy, `g_skill(Q, R | Q_test)`, is defined to maximize the probability of selecting a demonstration that showcases a reasoning skill aligned with the optimal skill for `Q_test`, i.e., `∫ P_E(Q, R | z)P_E(z | Q_test)dz`. This means selecting examples where the observed skill in the rationale matches the skill predicted as needed for the test question.

#### 3.2. Latent Reasoning Skill Discovery (LaRS Implementation)

To practically realize the skill-based selection, LaRS employs a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) trained on the example bank `D_E = {(Q, R)}`. The CVAE learns to approximate the probabilistic models laid out in the latent skill model formulation.

- **CVAE Components:**
 - **Reasoning Policy Model (π_ϕ(z | Q)):** This model learns to predict the distribution of latent reasoning skills `z` required for a given question `Q`. It approximates `P_E(z | Q)`.
 - **Encoder Model (q_ω(z | Q, R)):** This model learns to map a question-rationale pair `(Q, R)` from the example bank to its corresponding latent reasoning skill `z`. It approximates `P_E(z | Q, R)`.
 - **Decoder Model (p_ψ(R | z, Q)):** This is an auxiliary model that attempts to reconstruct the rationale `R` given `Q` and `z`. Its primary purpose is to facilitate the training of the encoder and reasoning policy, not for direct generation in downstream tasks.
- **Training Process:** The CVAE is trained by minimizing a standard loss function comprising a reconstruction loss and a KL divergence term. This process ensures that `q_ω` and `π_ϕ` effectively capture the conditional distributions of reasoning skills.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Crucially, LaRS avoids the computational expense of training or extensively querying large language models for these components. Instead:
 - Pre-trained *embedding models* (e.g., Deberta-v2-xlarge) are used to map raw text (questions and rationales) into a continuous embedding space.
 - The CVAE's encoder, decoder, and reasoning policy are represented by lightweight, two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) that operate on these embeddings, predicting the mean and variance of Gaussian distributions for latent skills. This makes the training of LaRS models cost-efficient and fast.

#### 3.3. Demonstration Selection Process (Algorithm 1)

Once the LaRS CVAE models (specifically `π_ϕ` and `q_ω`) are trained, the demonstration selection for a new test question `Q_test` proceeds as follows:

1. **Infer Desired Skill for Test Question:** The reasoning policy `π_ϕ(z | f(Q_test))` is used to compute the desired latent reasoning skill `z_test` for the `Q_test`. This is typically the mean of the predicted Gaussian distribution.
2. **Infer Demonstrated Skill for Each Example:** For every example `(Q_j, R_j)` in the example bank, the encoder `q_ω(z | f(Q_j, R_j))` is used to compute its *demonstrated* latent reasoning skill `z_j_post`.
3. **Score Examples:** Each example `(Q_j, R_j)` is scored based on the cosine similarity between `z_test` and `z_j_post`.
4. **Select Top-k Examples:** The top-k examples with the highest similarity scores are selected to form the CoT prompt, ordered appropriately.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Analysis

The paper provides theoretical analysis (Section 4.3 and Appendix E) to prove the optimality of this skill-based selection method under certain ideal assumptions:
- **Assumption 1: Optimal Example Bank:** The example bank `D_E` is sampled from the optimal conditional distribution of rationales, `P_E(R|Q) = P*(R|Q)`, implying it contains "good" rationales.
- **Assumption 2: Expert LLMs/Humans:** Humans and LLMs are expert rationale generators given skills and questions, meaning `P_H(R|z,Q) = P_E(R|z,Q) = P_M(R|z,Q)`.

Under these assumptions, the theory shows that if an LLM is prompted with an infinite number of in-context examples (`k → ∞`) selected by `g_skill`, the LLM will generate rationales according to the optimal conditional distribution `P*(R|Q)`. This provides a strong theoretical justification for the LaRS approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of LaRS across multiple benchmarks and LLMs demonstrates its effectiveness, efficiency, and robustness.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance on Reasoning Tasks

- **Consistent Outperformance:** LaRS consistently outperforms `Skill-KNN-zero` (a state-of-the-art zero-shot skill-based selection method that uses LLMs for skill descriptions) in 18 out of 20 experiments across four challenging reasoning benchmarks: TabMWP (Math Word Problems), GSM8K (Grade School Math), Spider (Text-to-SQL), and COGS (Semantic Parsing).
- **Matches Oracle Performance:** In almost half of the experiments, LaRS achieves performance comparable to `Retrieval-R` (oracle), which selects examples based on the similarity of *ground-truth rationales*. This indicates that LaRS's unsupervised latent skill discovery is highly effective at identifying relevant reasoning patterns.
- **Performance Across LLMs:** LaRS shows improvements across a range of backbone LLMs, including powerful commercial models (gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4o, claude-3-sonnet, claude-3-haiku) and smaller, open-source models (Falcon-40B-Instruct). This demonstrates its broad applicability and effectiveness regardless of the underlying LLM's scale.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** LaRS significantly outperforms `Random` selection and `Retrieval-Q` (which selects based on question similarity), reaffirming the importance of skill-based selection over simple question matching for CoT.

#### 4.2. Significant Computational Efficiency Gains

- **Reduced Pre-processing Time:** LaRS processes example banks four times faster than `Skill-KNN-zero`. For a 1k example bank, LaRS takes approximately 0.5 hours compared to 2 hours for `Skill-KNN-zero`.
- **Elimination of LLM Inference Costs:** LaRS requires no LLM inferences during the pre-processing stage for skill discovery. This leads to substantial cost savings (approximately $30 per 1k examples compared to `Skill-KNN-zero`, which relies on LLMs like gpt-3.5-turbo for skill generation).
- **Cost-Efficient Per-Query Selection:** The cost per input query during the selection stage for LaRS is comparable to `Retrieval-Q` and significantly lower (more than 100% less) than `Skill-KNN-zero`, as LaRS uses lightweight MLPs on pre-computed embeddings.
- **Scalability:** The lightweight nature of LaRS's CVAE (MLPs operating on embeddings) makes it highly scalable to larger example banks, addressing a key limitation of prior skill-based methods.

#### 4.3. Enhanced Robustness to Sub-optimal Example Banks

- LaRS demonstrates superior robustness when the example bank contains noisy or sub-optimal rationales. In experiments where 10%, 20%, and 30% of rationales were replaced with random rationales:
 - `Skill-KNN-zero` experienced performance drops of up to 3% (TabMWP) and 11.7% (COGS) at a 30% replacement rate.
 - LaRS, in contrast, showed remarkable resilience, with only minimal performance degradation (0.1% for TabMWP and 1.9% for COGS) under the same conditions.
- This robustness is attributed to LaRS considering both the question and the rationale when learning skill embeddings, allowing it to better discern useful reasoning patterns even amidst noise.

#### 4.4. Alignment with Human Understanding of Skills

- Visualizations (t-SNE projections in Figure 5) show that the latent reasoning skills discovered by LaRS, even through unsupervised learning, exhibit clear separation corresponding to human-annotated skill labels (e.g., "compute statistics," "compute rate of change").
- This indicates that LaRS effectively captures abstract reasoning concepts that align with human intuition, unlike raw question or rationale embeddings which fail to separate these skills.

#### 4.5. Consistency Across Varying Conditions

- LaRS consistently improves performance over `Retrieval-Q` regardless of the choice of pre-trained embedding models (Sentence-BERT, Deberta-v2-xlarge, text-embedding-ada-02).
- Its performance gains are also consistent across different numbers of in-context examples (k=2, 4, 8), indicating stable benefits.

In summary, LaRS not only delivers state-of-the-art performance in CoT demonstration selection but does so with unparalleled computational efficiency and robustness, addressing key practical challenges in scaling and deploying LLM reasoning systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of LaRS represents a significant advancement in the field of large language models (LLMs) and their application to complex reasoning tasks, with profound implications for both research and practical deployment.

#### 5.1. Advancement in Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting

LaRS offers a more effective and principled method for demonstration selection in CoT prompting. By focusing on *latent reasoning skills* rather than surface-level question similarity or costly explicit skill descriptions, it helps LLMs leverage the most relevant reasoning paths from an example bank. This directly translates to improved accuracy and reliability of LLMs on challenging tasks that require multi-step reasoning.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Efficiency and Scalability of LLM Deployment

One of the most immediate and impactful contributions of LaRS is its dramatic improvement in computational efficiency. By eliminating the need for expensive LLM inferences to generate skill descriptions during pre-processing and selection, LaRS significantly reduces monetary costs and processing time. This makes sophisticated skill-based CoT prompting more accessible and scalable for:
- **Enterprises:** Companies can deploy more robust reasoning LLM applications without incurring prohibitive costs for example selection.
- **Researchers:** Academics can experiment with larger example banks and more diverse tasks without being limited by computational budgets.
- **Smaller Models:** LaRS's ability to boost performance even for smaller backbone LLMs like Falcon-40B-Instruct, without requiring powerful LLMs for skill discovery, democratizes access to effective CoT prompting.

#### 5.3. Robustness to Imperfect Data

The demonstrated robustness of LaRS to sub-optimal example banks is crucial for real-world applications. In practical scenarios, example banks are often curated with human effort and may contain errors, noise, or sub-optimal rationales. LaRS's ability to maintain high performance despite such imperfections means it can be more reliably deployed in diverse and less-than-perfect data environments, reducing the need for painstaking manual curation.

#### 5.4. Deeper Theoretical Understanding of CoT

The latent skill model formulation provides a valuable theoretical framework for understanding the underlying mechanisms of CoT reasoning. By positing that rationales are generated conditioned on latent skills and questions, it moves beyond purely empirical observations and offers a principled way to analyze how LLMs process and apply reasoning. The theoretical proof of optimality under certain assumptions strengthens this understanding and provides a solid foundation for future research.

#### 5.5. Paving the Way for Autonomous Prompt Engineering

LaRS takes a significant step towards automating prompt engineering, a field currently heavily reliant on human intuition and trial-and-error. By automatically discovering and leveraging reasoning skills, LaRS reduces the "human in the loop" requirement for designing effective CoT prompts, making LLMs more self-sufficient in adapting to new tasks and datasets.

#### 5.6. Broad Applicability Across Diverse Tasks

The empirical validation across a variety of tasks—math word problems, text-to-SQL, and semantic parsing—demonstrates the versatility of LaRS. This suggests that the concept of latent reasoning skills and their unsupervised discovery is generalizable across different domains requiring structured or multi-step reasoning, expanding the potential impact of LLMs in various applications.

#### 5.7. Future Research Directions

The paper also openly discusses limitations, suggesting clear avenues for future research, such as exploring the impact of example order, representing decoders as prompt-tuning modules, and learning fine-grained skills for individual reasoning steps. This forward-looking perspective indicates that LaRS is not an endpoint but a strong foundation for continued innovation in LLM reasoning and prompt optimization.

In conclusion, LaRS contributes a highly practical, efficient, and theoretically grounded methodology for optimizing Chain-of-Thought prompting. Its impact will likely be felt in improving the performance of LLMs on complex tasks, reducing the cost and complexity of their deployment, and deepening our scientific understanding of how these powerful models reason.
