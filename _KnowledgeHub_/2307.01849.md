# Crossway Diffusion: Improving Diffusion-based Visuomotor Policy via Self-supervised Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The "Crossway Diffusion" framework from Stony Brook University enhances diffusion-based visuomotor policies by integrating a self-supervised reconstruction objective that regularizes shared intermediate representations. This method achieved up to a 15.7% improvement in success rate on challenging Robomimic datasets and demonstrated improved robustness in real-world robot manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Existing diffusion models for visuomotor control often lack explicit regularization for the quality of their learned visual representations.
- Intermediate representations within these policies may not robustly capture rich visual and state information, potentially limiting overall performance.
- Diffusion-based policies can struggle with generalization and robustness in diverse, visually complex, or noisy environments.

## Method

- Introduces a state decoder capable of reconstructing raw image pixels and other state information from an intermediate representation deep within the action encoder.
- Integrates an auxiliary self-supervised learning (SSL) objective, specifically a reconstruction loss, which jointly optimizes the entire model alongside the primary diffusion loss.
- Leverages a shared "intersection" representation where both the action denoising path and the state reconstruction path converge, ensuring explicit regularization of features critical for both tasks.

## Results

- Achieved up to a **15.7%** improvement in success rate on the challenging ‘Transport, mh’ dataset from Robomimic and consistent gains across other simulated tasks.
- Demonstrated superior performance in real-world robot manipulation, with a **0.95** success rate on 'Duck Lift' and **0.80** on 'Duck Collect', showing improved robustness to novel obstructions and occlusions.
- Produced high-quality image reconstructions from intermediate representations, qualitatively confirming that the 'intersection' effectively encodes significant state information.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly regularizing shared intermediate representations through an auxiliary reconstruction task significantly improves visuomotor policy performance, outperforming approaches that disentangle SSL objectives.
> - Reconstruction of the *current* state as an SSL task is generally more beneficial for diffusion-based policies than predicting future states, which often leads to performance degradation.
> - The choice of SSL objective is crucial; a reconstruction loss was effective, while contrastive learning in this specific setup proved detrimental to policy performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields within artificial intelligence and robotics: behavioral cloning, sequence modeling for robot control, diffusion models, and self-supervised learning.

**Behavioral Cloning (BC)** has long been a foundational method for robot action policy learning, where a robot learns to imitate expert demonstrations. Traditionally, BC trains a policy to predict single actions from states. However, the rise of powerful sequence modeling architectures has shifted the paradigm.

**Sequence Modeling Approaches for Robot Control** have gained prominence by formulating robot policy learning as a task of modeling multi-step state-action trajectories. Inspired by their success in natural language processing and image generation, **Transformers** (e.g., Decision Transformer) have been widely adopted, enabling policies to consider longer histories and predict future actions. This allows for more contextual and coherent behaviors compared to single-step action prediction.

Building upon this, **Diffusion Models** have recently emerged as particularly effective generative models for complex data distributions, excelling in tasks like image and language generation. Their ability to iteratively denoise random noise into structured data makes them highly suitable for generating diverse and coherent trajectories for imitation learning. Earlier works applied diffusion models to low-dimensional states, but the challenge of high-dimensional visual observations for visuomotor control remained. **Diffusion Policy** specifically addressed this by conditioning action sequence generation on visual and other states, establishing a strong baseline for diffusion-based visuomotor policies. Crossway Diffusion directly builds upon and aims to improve this Diffusion Policy framework.

Finally, **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL)** plays a crucial role in the paper. SSL is a paradigm for learning powerful data representations without requiring explicit, human-annotated labels, by creating auxiliary tasks from the data itself. It has been instrumental in training large-scale foundation models in computer vision and has shown promise in enhancing reinforcement learning and imitation learning from pixels. This work specifically explores SSL as an *auxiliary objective* trained jointly with the primary policy learning task, aiming to improve the learned intermediate representations. This positions Crossway Diffusion as a significant contribution to the growing body of research that seeks to integrate advanced generative models with effective representation learning techniques for more robust and capable robot policies, particularly in visually complex environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enhance the performance of diffusion-based visuomotor policies**, specifically in the context of robot behavioral cloning. The authors identify a key area for improvement in existing diffusion policy frameworks, such as Diffusion Policy: the **quality of visual representations** learned by these models.

The motivation stems from the observation that while diffusion models are exceptionally capable of modeling complex data distributions and generating action sequences, the intermediate representations formed during their denoising process are not explicitly regularized to robustly capture information about the input states. The standard Diffusion Policy generates action sequences conditioned on input states, but there isn't a direct mechanism to ensure that the internal features effectively encode the rich visual and other state information. Without such regularization, these intermediate representations might not be as informative or robust as they could be, potentially limiting the policy's overall performance, especially in challenging, visually-driven tasks with diverse or noisy input data.

To address this, the authors propose **Crossway Diffusion**, which introduces two main components:
1. A **carefully designed state decoder** capable of reconstructing raw image pixels and other state information.
2. An **auxiliary self-supervised learning (SSL) objective** (specifically, a reconstruction loss) that jointly optimizes the entire model alongside the original diffusion loss.

By forcing the model's intermediate representations (dubbed the "intersection") to reconstruct the input states, the core motivation is to compel these representations to learn richer, more semantically meaningful, and robust features. This explicit regularization is hypothesized to benefit both the auxiliary reconstruction task and, crucially, the primary task of accurate action sequence generation, leading to a more effective visuomotor policy. The ultimate goal is to achieve consistent and substantial performance improvements over existing diffusion-based policies across a variety of robot tasks, including complex real-world scenarios, without incurring additional computational costs during inference.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Crossway Diffusion extends the Diffusion Policy framework by integrating a state decoder and an auxiliary self-supervised learning objective. The core idea is to leverage the intermediate representations generated during the action denoising process for an additional task: reconstructing the input states.

The Diffusion Policy baseline consists of a state encoder ($E_S$), an action encoder ($E_A$), and an action decoder ($D_A$). The state encoder processes visual and low-dimensional states to produce an observation condition ($h_t$). The action encoder and decoder form the diffusion model, iteratively denoising a noisy action sequence ($A^k_t$) conditioned on $h_t$ to predict the noise ($\epsilon$).

Crossway Diffusion introduces the following key modifications:

1. **State Decoder ($D_S$):** A new module designed to reconstruct the input states ($S_t$) from an intermediate representation of the action encoder, denoted as $X^k_t$. This representation, located deep within the action encoder ($E_A$), is referred to as the "intersection" because both the action denoising path and the new state reconstruction path pass through it.
 - **Design for Visual States:** Visual state decoders are implemented as a sequence of 2D residual convolutional blocks and transposed convolutional layers (ConvTranspose) for upsampling. Positional embeddings are incorporated by concatenating them with the ConvTranspose output along the channel axis, inspired by and.
 - **Design for Low-dimensional States:** These are reconstructed using simpler three-layer Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs).
 - Crucially, the state decoder ($D_S$) is **only active during training** to provide supervisory signals and is discarded during inference, ensuring no additional computational overhead at deployment.

2. **Intersection Transformation ($g(\cdot)$):** This component adapts the intermediate representation $X^k_t$ to be suitable for the state decoder. By default (Design A), only the first vector ($X^k_{t,1}$) along the time axis of $X^k_t$ is used for reconstruction.
 - For low-dimensional states, $g_{\text{low-dim}}(\cdot)$ is an identity function.
 - For visual states, $g_{\text{img}}(\cdot)$ involves splitting the channels of $X^k_{t,1}$ into four folds, reshaping them into a $C/4 \times 2 \times 2$ block, repeating this block spatially to reach a quarter of the desired image resolution, and finally concatenating positional embeddings. This transformation helps bridge the significant dimensionality gap between the action encoder's internal features and raw image pixels.

3. **Crossway Diffusion Loss:** The model is optimized jointly by two objectives:
 - **Original Diffusion Loss ($L_{DDPM}$):** This is the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss used in Diffusion Policy for predicting the noise in the action sequences.
 - **Reconstruction Loss ($L_{Recon.}$):** This is an MSE loss calculated between the reconstructed states ($\hat{S}_t$) from $D_S$ and the original input states ($S_t$). This auxiliary loss supervises $D_S$, $E_S$, and $E_A$.
 - The total loss is a weighted sum: $L_{Crossway} = L_{DDPM} + \alpha L_{Recon.}$, where $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter (set to 0.1). This joint optimization ensures that all components (state encoder, action encoder/decoder, and state decoder) learn collaboratively, with the "intersection" representation being explicitly regularized to capture state information.

The authors conducted extensive ablation studies to evaluate different design choices, including variations in the intersection transformation, state decoder architectures (shallower, ViT-based), and the nature of the auxiliary objective (reconstructing current vs. future states, or using contrastive learning instead of reconstruction). These ablations were critical for confirming the effectiveness of their specific design choices and understanding the underlying mechanisms of improvement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors demonstrate the consistent and substantial advantages of Crossway Diffusion over existing methods across a wide array of simulated and real-world robot tasks.

**1. Superior Performance Across Tasks:**
- **Simulated Environments:** Crossway Diffusion consistently outperformed the baseline Diffusion Policy CNN, as well as other baselines like LSTM-GMM and Implicit Behavioral Cloning (IBC). Notably, it achieved a 15.7% improvement in success rate on the challenging ‘Transport, mh’ dataset from Robomimic, a multi-human demonstration dataset known for its varied proficiency, indicating its robustness to diverse expert data. Similar improvements were observed in tasks like ‘Can’, ‘Lift’, ‘Square’, ‘Tool Hang’, and ‘Push-T’.
- **Real-world Environments:** The method also showed strong performance in real-world robot manipulation tasks, achieving higher success rates on ‘Duck Lift’ (0.95 vs 0.80 for baseline) and ‘Duck Collect’ (0.80 vs 0.70 for baseline). This real-world validation is crucial for demonstrating practical applicability.

**2. Robustness to Novel Conditions:**
- Qualitative tests in real-world scenarios showcased Crossway Diffusion's robustness to various obstructions, including unseen objects, partial occlusions, and even novel object colors (e.g., yellow ducks in 'Duck Collect' task, which were not in training). This highlights the improved generalization capabilities stemming from better visual representations.

**3. High-Quality Image Reconstruction:**
- The state decoder produced "surprisingly well-reconstructed images" for simulated environments. For real-world tasks, reconstructions preserved major robot and object structures, even if finer details were sometimes missed. This qualitatively confirms that the intermediate "intersection" representations effectively encode significant state information.

**4. Insights from Ablation Studies:**
- **Importance of "Intersection":** Ablations on the intersection transformation (`g(.)`) revealed that designs forcing the action denoising and state reconstruction flows to *share* the intermediate representation ($X^k_t$) (Designs A, B, C) consistently outperformed the baseline. In contrast, Design D, which used the state encoder output ($h_t$) and thus disentangled the two flows, showed only marginal or no improvement over the baseline. This strongly validates the "Crossway" concept—explicitly regularizing the shared intermediate representation is key.
- **Reconstruction of All States:** Reconstructing both visual and low-dimensional states (default setting) yielded better performance than reconstructing visual states only, indicating the benefit of a comprehensive reconstruction objective.
- **Current vs. Future State Prediction:** A crucial finding was that reconstructing the *current* state (N=0) was consistently the most beneficial strategy. Predicting future states (N > 0) actually led to performance degradation, often below the baseline, with the notable exception of 'Transport,ph' where N=6 performed best. This suggests that for this specific diffusion-based policy architecture, focusing on robust representations of the immediate past/present is more effective than speculative future state prediction as an SSL task.
- **Reconstruction vs. Contrastive Learning:** Replacing the reconstruction loss with a contrastive learning objective (Crossway-CURL) resulted in significantly worse performance compared to both Crossway Diffusion's default and the Diffusion Policy baseline. This indicates that not all self-supervised learning objectives are beneficial in this context, reinforcing the importance of carefully chosen auxiliary tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Crossway Diffusion paper makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of robot learning:

1. **Advancing Diffusion-based Visuomotor Policy:** The work provides a simple yet highly effective method to consistently improve the performance of state-of-the-art diffusion models for visuomotor control. By integrating a carefully designed state decoder and a reconstruction-based SSL objective, Crossway Diffusion addresses a critical limitation of previous approaches by enhancing the quality of learned visual representations.

2. **Efficient Representation Learning:** The core innovation lies in the "Crossway" design, which effectively leverages existing intermediate representations within the diffusion model for an auxiliary self-supervised task. This approach compels the model to learn richer, more robust, and more semantically meaningful features without modifying the core diffusion mechanism or introducing additional computational cost during inference. This efficiency is crucial for real-world robot deployment where low latency is paramount.

3. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The demonstrated improvements in diverse simulated tasks and, crucially, in real-world robot manipulation scenarios highlight the practical value of Crossway Diffusion. Its robustness to distractions, unseen objects, and occlusions suggests better generalization capabilities, a key challenge in deploying robot policies outside controlled environments. This directly contributes to making robots more reliable and adaptable in complex, unstructured settings.

4. **Guiding Principles for Self-Supervised Learning in Robotics:** The extensive ablation studies offer critical insights into the effective integration of SSL with policy learning, particularly for diffusion models:
 - The explicit regularization of shared intermediate representations (the "intersection") is shown to be more beneficial than disentangled SSL objectives.
 - Reconstruction of the current state outperforms future state prediction as an auxiliary task in this context, providing important empirical guidance for future SSL designs in robotics.
 - Not all SSL paradigms, such as contrastive learning, are universally effective for improving policy learning in this specific setup, urging careful selection and adaptation of SSL techniques. These findings can guide researchers in designing more effective SSL strategies tailored for robot control.

5. **Catalyst for Future Research:** Crossway Diffusion opens new avenues for exploring the interplay between generative models, representation learning, and robot control. Future work could investigate more sophisticated "intersection" designs, explore other forms of reconstruction (e.g., semantic segmentation, depth prediction), or integrate this approach with advanced diffusion acceleration techniques (like Progressive Distillation or Consistency Models) to further improve inference speed without sacrificing performance. The paper's insights into the effectiveness of current-state reconstruction also challenge some common assumptions about the benefits of predictive objectives in robot learning.

In summary, Crossway Diffusion represents a significant step forward in developing more capable and robust visuomotor policies by intelligently integrating self-supervised representation learning into the powerful framework of diffusion models. Its demonstrated performance and detailed analysis provide valuable guidance for the continued evolution of robot learning.
