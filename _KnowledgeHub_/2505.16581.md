# How Ensembles of Distilled Policies Improve Generalisation in Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research theoretically analyzes how policy distillation enhances reinforcement learning agent generalization, identifying that increasing the ensemble size and the diversity of distillation data improves performance. Ensembles of distilled policies, particularly when trained on diverse datasets, consistently generalized better than single policies and even surpassed the original teacher agent in complex environments like Minigrid Four Rooms.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning agents frequently struggle to generalize effectively to new, unseen environments, leading to poor performance in zero-shot policy transfer (ZSPT) settings.
- The observed generalization benefits of policy distillation after training were empirically noted but lacked a comprehensive theoretical explanation for their underlying mechanisms.
- The optimal strategies for data utilization during distillation, especially concerning the role of data diversity and its impact on generalization, remained unclear.

## Method

- The study introduces a theoretical framework, ==Generalisation Through Invariance== ZSPT (GTI-ZSPT), to formally analyze how learning invariances to data symmetries improves generalization in distilled policies.
- A generalization bound is derived that explicitly links the performance of a distilled policy ensemble to its size (N) and the diversity of the distillation data (κ).
- Empirical validation is conducted in both a controlled environment (Reacher with rotational symmetry) and a more complex, less theoretically constrained environment (Minigrid Four Rooms) to test the theoretical insights.

## Results

- Empirical tests in the Reacher environment demonstrated that increasing ensemble size from N=1 to N=100 improved test returns from **0.75** ± **0.147** to 1.05 ± **0.117**, and increasing data diversity (e.g., from C2 to C8 subgroups) improved returns from **0.39** ± **0.0805** to 1.11 ± **0.072**.
- In the Minigrid Four Rooms environment, an N=10 ensemble distilled on 'Explore-Go' data achieved a test performance of **0.88** ± **0.036**, outperforming the original PPO+Explore-Go teacher agent's **0.74** ± **0.040**.
- The empirical performance across both environments consistently followed the predicted 'a + b/√N' relationship with ensemble size, and increased data diversity led to a reduction in non-invariance, confirming the theoretical insights.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generalization performance of distilled policies is theoretically bounded, showing direct improvement with larger ensemble sizes (following a 1/√N relationship) and increased diversity of distillation data.
> - Ensembles of distilled policies implicitly leverage underlying data symmetries, becoming more invariant to these symmetries, which is a key mechanism for generalizing to unseen contexts.
> - Distilling on more diverse datasets, even if collected from existing training contexts, is crucial for fostering better generalization capabilities in student policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) frequently grapples with the challenge of generalization, specifically the ability of an agent to perform effectively in new scenarios not encountered during its initial training. This challenge is formally tested in the zero-shot policy transfer (ZSPT) setting, where an agent trained on a fixed set of training environments (contexts) must generalize to similar, yet unseen, testing contexts.

Previous research has identified policy distillation after training—the process of transferring knowledge from an existing policy (teacher) to a newly initialized neural network (student)—as a mechanism that can sometimes produce a policy with superior test performance compared to the original teacher policy (Lyle et al., 2022). However, the precise reasons for this generalization benefit and the optimal strategies for data utilization during distillation have remained unclear.

Existing explanations for policy distillation's generalization advantages are often indirect. Lyle et al. (2022) suggested that the temporal difference (TD) loss, inherent in many RL training processes, can negatively affect the smoothness of the learned value function, which distillation, by avoiding TD loss, might mitigate. They also partially attributed benefits to the stationarity of distillation targets, contrasting with the non-stationary targets in standard RL training (Igl et al., 2021). Nevertheless, these explanations lacked a comprehensive theoretical justification specifically for how distillation enhances generalization.

Furthermore, recent studies have emphasized the importance of the diversity of the RL training distribution for generalization to unseen contexts (Jiang et al., 2023; Suau et al., 2024; Weltevrede et al., 2025). This raises a specific question: can varying the data distribution used for distillation further increase generalization performance?

This paper directly addresses these lacunae by providing a theoretical analysis of policy distillation in a framework designed to elucidate its generalization capabilities. It specifically investigates the roles of ensemble learning and data diversity in the distillation process, thereby contributing to a more complete understanding of generalization mechanisms in RL and offering actionable strategies for improving agent robustness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to theoretically analyze the process of policy distillation after training in reinforcement learning and to derive practical insights into how and on what data a policy should be distilled to improve generalization, particularly in the zero-shot policy transfer (ZSPT) setting.

The motivation behind this investigation stems from several observations and unresolved questions in current RL research:
1. **Generalization Challenge in RL**: A significant hurdle in developing reliable RL agents is their limited ability to generalize effectively to new, unseen environments. Agents often overfit to their training contexts, leading to poor performance when deployed in similar but novel situations.
2. **Empirical Observation of Distillation's Benefits**: Prior work has demonstrated that policy distillation after training can sometimes yield policies that generalize better than their original teacher policies. This empirical finding suggests that distillation is a valuable tool for generalization, but the underlying mechanisms contributing to this improvement are not fully understood.
3. **Lack of Theoretical Justification**: Existing theoretical explanations for distillation's generalization benefits are often indirect. For instance, arguments related to avoiding non-stationary RL targets or promoting smoother value functions do not directly explain *how* distillation specifically enhances generalizability across diverse contexts. A more direct theoretical framework linking distillation properties to generalization performance is needed.
4. **Role of Data Distribution in Generalization**: The diversity of training data has been shown to influence RL agents' generalization capabilities. This raises a pertinent question regarding policy distillation: does the distribution and diversity of the data used during the distillation phase affect the student policy's ability to generalize? Specifically, the authors question whether increased data diversity during distillation can lead to improved generalization performance.
5. **Leveraging Symmetries for Generalization**: The authors hypothesize that many real-world data distributions exhibit underlying symmetries, and that learning to be invariant to these symmetries is crucial for generalizing to novel inputs. They aim to formalize this idea within a theoretical framework to derive concrete insights.

By addressing these motivations, the paper seeks to answer specific questions:
- What theoretical principles govern the generalization performance of a distilled policy?
- Can these principles inform practical strategies for selecting distillation data?
- Does the use of policy ensembles during distillation further enhance generalization?

The research aims to provide both theoretical proof and empirical evidence to establish practical guidelines for leveraging policy distillation as a powerful tool for improving generalization in RL.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a dual approach, combining theoretical analysis with empirical validation.

**Theoretical Framework: Generalisation Through Invariance ZSPT (GTI-ZSPT)**
The theoretical analysis is grounded in a specialized ZSPT setting, termed Generalisation Through Invariance ZSPT (GTI-ZSPT). This framework is designed to formally analyze how learning invariances to symmetries in the data distribution can improve generalization.
1. **CMDP Setting**: The work considers contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs), where contexts define specific tasks or environments. The ZSPT problem involves training an agent on a set of training contexts (C\_train) and evaluating its performance on a distinct set of testing contexts (C\_test).
2. **Symmetric Structure Assumption**: In GTI-ZSPT, the sets of states visited by the optimal policy in the full CMDP (S\_π\*\_M|C) and the training CMDP (S\_π\*\_M|Ctrain) are assumed to exhibit a symmetric structure. Specifically, these state sets are generated by applying elements from a non-trivial symmetry group G and its finite subgroup B (B ≤ G), respectively, to a proper subset of states (¯S). The optimal policy (π\*) is assumed to be invariant under transformations from the full group G.
3. **Policy Distillation Setup**: For theoretical tractability, a simplified policy distillation setting is considered. The teacher policy (π\_β) and student policy (π\_θ) are deterministic and scalar-valued. The distillation loss is defined as the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the student and teacher outputs over a fixed dataset of states (D). Behavior cloning (BC) is considered a specific instance of policy distillation where the student learns from state-action tuples.
4. **Key Measures**:
 - **Discrepancy Measure (κ)**: A metric defined as `max_{g∈G} min_{b∈B} ||ψ_S(g) - ψ_S(b)||_op`, quantifying how much the finite subgroup B deviates from the full group G in terms of state representations. A smaller κ implies B better covers G.
 - **Ensemble**: An ensemble consists of multiple neural networks, initialized independently and trained on the same dataset. The analysis extends to the "infinite width limit" of neural networks, where an infinite ensemble's behavior can be characterized by Gaussian processes.
5. **Generalization Bound Derivation**: The paper proves a generalization bound for a distilled policy in the GTI-ZSPT setting (Theorem 1). This bound relies on:
 - **Performance Difference Bound**: Theorem 3 from Maran et al. (2023), which bounds the performance difference between an optimal policy and a student policy in Lipschitz-continuous MDPs based on the Wasserstein distance between their action distributions over visited states.
 - **Invariance of Ensembles**: Lemma 6.2 from Gerken and Kessel (2024), which bounds the deviation from invariance of an infinite ensemble of infinitely wide neural networks trained with full data augmentation under a finite subgroup B. This lemma establishes that the deviation is proportional to κ.
 - **Finite Ensemble Approximation**: Lemma 1, which bounds the difference between an infinite ensemble and its finite Monte Carlo estimate, showing an inverse square root dependence on ensemble size (1/√N).
 - By combining these elements, the final bound for the performance difference (J\_π\* - J\_π̂∞) explicitly incorporates κ and N, leading to two theoretical insights.

**Empirical Validation**
The theoretical insights are empirically verified in two distinct environments, extending beyond the strict assumptions of the theory.
1. **Reacher with Rotational Symmetry (Illustrative CMDP)**:
 - **Description**: A continuous control environment where a robot arm must reach a goal. Contexts vary the shoulder location, demonstrating rotational symmetry.
 - **Design**: Experiments investigate the impact of:
 - **Ensemble Size (N)**: Varying N from 1 to 100 (and up to 10,000 in additional experiments) while keeping the distillation data fixed (subgroup C4).
 - **Subgroup Size (B)**: Distilling on data corresponding to different finite subgroups of SO(2) (C2, C4, C8), effectively varying the diversity of symmetries observed during training.
 - **Data Diversity**: Comparing distillation on "Training Contexts" (baseline), "Training Contexts + C4" (augmented with rotated initial poses), and "Training Contexts + Random" (augmented with random initial poses).
 - **Teacher**: A handcrafted optimal policy.
 - **Distillation**: MSE loss for vector-valued actions.
2. **Minigrid Four Rooms (Complex Environment)**:
 - **Description**: A grid world environment where an agent navigates a four-room layout to reach a goal. Contexts vary door topologies, agent starting positions/directions, and goal locations. This environment is chosen because it violates most of the strict assumptions of the theoretical proof (e.g., no explicit invariant symmetry, stochastic policies, non-optimal teacher, non-Lipschitz dynamics).
 - **Teacher**: A PPO+Explore-Go agent (Weltevrede et al., 2025), designed to improve generalization by generating diverse training distributions.
 - **Distillation Datasets**:
 - "Teacher": On-policy trajectories from the teacher in original training contexts.
 - "Explore-Go": Teacher trajectories collected from additional, diverse starting states generated by an exploration policy.
 - "Mixed": A 50/50 blend of "Teacher" trajectories and pure exploration policy trajectories.
 - **Distillation Methods**: Policy Distillation (regressing on action probabilities) and Behavior Cloning (using logarithmic loss for stochastic policies).
 - **Ensemble Size**: Comparing N=1 and N=10 (and N=100 in additional experiments).

**Implementation Details**: Hyperparameters, network architectures, and training procedures for both environments are thoroughly documented in Appendix C. Measures of invariance (total variation over group orbits) and plots comparing empirical results to the `a + b/√N` theoretical relationship are provided in Appendix D.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded both theoretical insights and empirical validations across different experimental settings.

**Theoretical Findings:**
The core theoretical contribution is a generalization bound derived for a distilled policy in the Generalisation Through Invariance ZSPT (GTI-ZSPT) setting. Under assumptions including Lipschitz continuity of the CMDP and policies, an optimal teacher, and using an ensemble of infinitely wide neural networks, the performance difference between the optimal policy (J\_π\*) and the distilled student ensemble (J\_π̂∞) is bounded as:
`J_π* - J_π̂∞ ≤ C * (κ * CΘ + (1/√N) * CΣ∞(ϵ))`
where `C` is a constant, `κ` measures the discrepancy between the full symmetry group and the subgroup observed during distillation, `N` is the ensemble size, and `CΘ` and `CΣ∞` are constants dependent on the network architecture, initialization, dataset, and confidence level `ϵ`.

This bound yields two primary theoretical insights:
1. **Ensemble Size**: A larger ensemble size (`N`) reduces the `1/√N` term, indicating that increasing the number of independently distilled policies decreases the upper bound on the performance gap, thus suggesting improved generalization.
2. **Data Diversity (Subgroup Size)**: A larger subgroup `B` of symmetries observed in the distillation data reduces the discrepancy measure `κ`, which in turn lowers the upper bound on the performance gap, implying better generalization. In practical terms, this translates to distilling on a more diverse set of states.

**Empirical Verification (Reacher with Rotational Symmetry):**
Experiments in the 'Reacher with rotational symmetry' CMDP, which aligns with the GTI-ZSPT assumptions more closely, provided direct evidence for these insights:
- **Impact of Ensemble Size**: Increasing the ensemble size `N` from 1 to 10 and then to 100 (Table 1) consistently improved test performance. A single distilled policy (N=1) achieved a test return of 0.75 ± 0.147, which increased to 0.89 ± 0.107 for N=10 and 1.05 ± 0.117 for N=100. Further increases to N=1000 and N=10,000 showed continued improvement up to 1.17 ± 0.038 (Table 8).
- **Impact of Subgroup Size (Data Diversity)**: Distilling on datasets corresponding to larger subgroups (B=C2, B=C4, B=C8) of rotational symmetries also led to enhanced test performance. Performance improved from 0.39 ± 0.0805 for C2 to 0.75 ± 0.147 for C4, and further to 1.11 ± 0.072 for C8 (Table 1).
- **General Data Diversity**: Experiments with different distillation datasets (Table 2) showed that increasing data diversity, even without strict group symmetries, was beneficial. The "Training Contexts + C4" dataset (augmented by applying C4 rotations to initial poses) yielded a test return of 0.48 ± 0.080, outperforming the baseline "Training Contexts" (0.39 ± 0.051). Notably, the "Training Contexts + Random" dataset, with randomly sampled diverse initial poses, achieved a comparable 0.49 ± 0.077, suggesting that general data diversity beyond strict group augmentations is effective.
- **Invariance Measurement**: As the ensemble size and subgroup size increased, the "total variation over orbits"—a measure of non-invariance—decreased, correlating directly with improved test performance (Figure 5). This supports the theoretical premise that generalization benefits are linked to increased invariance.
- **`1/√N` Fit**: The empirical results for `J_π* - J_π̂∞` versus ensemble size `N` generally exhibited the `a + b/√N` relationship predicted by the theoretical bound, indicating the bound's relevance (Figure 6a).

**Empirical Verification (Minigrid Four Rooms):**
In the more complex 'Minigrid Four Rooms' environment, where theoretical assumptions are largely relaxed, the insights held:
- **Policy Distillation (PD)**:
 - **Ensemble Benefits**: For all dataset types, ensembles (N=10) significantly outperformed single distilled policies (N=1). For the "Explore-Go" dataset, test performance improved from 0.78 ± 0.041 (N=1) to 0.88 ± 0.036 (N=10) (Table 3).
 - **Diverse Data Benefits**: The "Explore-Go" and "Mixed" datasets consistently yielded better generalization than the "Teacher" dataset. The "Explore-Go" dataset, particularly, resulted in an N=10 ensemble test performance of 0.88 ± 0.036, which surpassed the original PPO+Explore-Go teacher's performance of 0.74 ± 0.040. This demonstrates that policy distillation, using diverse data, can enhance generalization beyond the teacher's capabilities with a modest additional computational cost (approximately 12% additional environment steps).
- **Behavior Cloning (BC)**:
 - The insights regarding ensembles and diverse data also applied to behavior cloning. An N=10 BC ensemble trained on "Explore-Go" data achieved a test performance of 0.75 ± 0.045, significantly better than a single BC agent on the "Teacher" dataset (0.26 ± 0.046) (Table 3).
 - BC generally achieved lower absolute performance than PD, especially on the "Mixed" dataset, attributed to the differing learning targets (actions vs. probabilities) and the suboptimality of the exploration policy's actions in the "Mixed" dataset for BC.
- **`1/√N` Fit**: The empirical performance in Four Rooms also reasonably followed the `a + b/√N` relationship with ensemble size (Figure 6b).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research significantly contributes to the understanding and practical application of generalization in reinforcement learning, particularly within the zero-shot policy transfer (ZSPT) setting.

**Theoretical Advancement:**
The paper provides a theoretical foundation for why policy distillation after training can improve generalization. By introducing the Generalisation Through Invariance ZSPT (GTI-ZSPT) setting and proving a generalization bound, the study moves beyond indirect or heuristic explanations. The theoretical result explicitly links generalization performance to two key factors: the size of the distilled ensemble and the diversity of the distillation data (quantified by the discrepancy measure `κ` for group symmetries). This theoretical rigor offers a novel perspective on a previously observed, but not fully understood, phenomenon.

**Practical Implications for RL Practitioners:**
The study translates its theoretical insights into two actionable principles that can be directly applied to improve RL agent generalization:
1. **Ensemble Distillation**: It demonstrates that training an ensemble of distilled policies, rather than a single one, consistently and substantially improves generalization performance. Crucially, this improvement is achieved at a fraction of the computational cost typically associated with training multiple full RL agents from scratch, as only the distillation process needs to be repeated. The inherent parallelizability of ensemble training and inference further enhances its practicality.
2. **Diverse Distillation Data**: The research emphasizes the importance of distilling policies on a diverse set of states, even if these states are sampled from the same training contexts. This extends the notion of "training on a bigger subgroup B" from the theory to a more general concept of data diversity. This finding suggests that practitioners should prioritize collecting rich, varied experience data for distillation, even from limited training environments, to foster better generalization.

**Empirical Validation and Outperformance of Teacher:**
The empirical results are substantial, validating the theoretical insights in both an illustrative environment (Reacher) and a more complex, less constrained environment (Minigrid Four Rooms). A particularly noteworthy finding is that ensembles of policies distilled on diverse datasets can generalize *significantly better* than the original teacher RL agent. For example, in the Four Rooms environment, an ensemble achieved substantially higher test performance than the teacher policy itself, indicating that distillation is not merely a method for knowledge compression but a tool for *enhancing* the teacher's generalization capabilities. This finding strengthens the argument for policy distillation as a powerful technique for developing more robust RL agents.

**Broad Applicability:**
The demonstration that these insights extend to the behavior cloning (BC) setting indicates a broader applicability of the principles across different forms of imitation learning. This suggests that the benefits of ensembles and data diversity in knowledge transfer are not limited to a specific distillation formulation but are more general mechanisms for improving learned policy robustness.

**Future Directions and Limitations:**
The work acknowledges limitations, such as the strict assumptions required for tight theoretical bounds (e.g., infinitely wide networks, Lipschitz continuity). It opens avenues for future research into whether the observed benefits of data augmentation stem purely from induced invariance or from other regularization effects. The paper's analysis of invariance (Appendix D.1) and the fit to the `1/√N` relation (Appendix D.2) provide initial steps in this direction, suggesting that the theoretical bounds, while not perfectly tight, are not vacuous and provide useful guidance.
