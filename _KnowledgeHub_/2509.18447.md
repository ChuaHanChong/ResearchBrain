# PrioriTouch: Adapting to User Contact Preferences for Whole-Arm Physical Human-Robot Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> PrioriTouch is a framework that enables robots to adapt to individual human contact preferences during whole-arm physical human-robot interaction. It integrates online preference learning with hierarchical operational space control, using a simulation-in-the-loop approach for safe and data-efficient policy refinement, leading to improved user comfort and safety in multi-contact caregiving tasks.

## Problem

- Robots lack the ability to personalize physical contact, struggling to adapt to individual preferences regarding touch locations and force, which is essential for safety and user acceptance in pHRI.
- Complex multi-contact scenarios, like whole-arm manipulation, introduce conflicting force requirements across different body parts, making it difficult to satisfy all objectives simultaneously.
- Direct online exploration of user contact preferences through physical trial-and-error is inherently risky and inefficient, potentially causing discomfort or injury and increasing user cognitive load.

## Method

- A ==contextual bandit== algorithm, **LinUCB-Rank**, learns to dynamically rank control objectives (pose-tracking and force-regulation) based on user feedback, enabling the robot to prioritize among potentially conflicting goals.
- ==Hierarchical Operational Space Control (H-OSC)== executes the learned objective rankings by recursively projecting lower-priority objectives into the null space of higher-priority ones, ensuring flexible trade-offs.
- A ==simulation-in-the-loop== strategy allows the robot to safely and efficiently refine its preference-learning policy in a digital twin before deploying validated policies on the physical robot.

## Results

- PrioriTouch significantly reduced force threshold violations in simulated bed-bathing tasks and was preferred by **7 out of 8** human participants over a heuristic baseline in a real-world bed-bathing task, receiving higher ratings for perceived safety and comfort.
- The LinUCB-Rank algorithm demonstrated improved sample efficiency in multi-contact simulations, requiring fewer user feedback signals to adapt to preferences compared to alternative baselines.
- The simulation-in-the-loop learning achieved policy convergence in approximately **52.4 seconds** on average, demonstrating its practical deployability for online adaptation while effectively handling moderate simulation inaccuracies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating contact preference learning as a learning-to-rank problem, integrated with hierarchical control, effectively addresses conflicting force requirements in multi-contact pHRI, enhancing safety and comfort.
> - Using a simulation-in-the-loop approach for policy refinement in pHRI allows for safe and data-efficient exploration of user preferences, mitigating the risks and inefficiencies of direct real-world experimentation.
> - User comfort thresholds for contact can be effectively derived by combining population-level preference data with biomechanical pain limits, providing a robust initial foundation for personalized robot behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is increasingly focused on developing systems capable of safe and effective physical human-robot interaction (pHRI). A significant challenge within pHRI, particularly in assistive and caregiving robotics, is enabling robots to make physical contact with humans in a manner that is both safe and comfortable. This challenge is compounded in scenarios requiring whole-arm manipulation (WAM), where multiple segments of a robot's arm may simultaneously contact various parts of a human body.

Prior research in pHRI has addressed aspects such as distributed tactile sensing for contact localization and regulation, and hierarchical multi-contact control. Some existing WAM systems can manipulate objects or navigate clutter, and others provide social touch. However, explicitly managing multiple, potentially conflicting contact-force objectives in pHRI, especially with dynamic prioritization based on human preferences, remains an area requiring further development. For instance, some hierarchical multi-contact controllers optimize all contact forces with equal weighting, without considering individualized preferences or dynamic ordering.

Within online preference learning in human-robot interaction (HRI), existing work often focuses on high-level action sequences or low-level parameters that can be explored safely without direct physical contact, or assumes human-initiated contact. Robot-initiated physical contact, particularly in multi-contact settings, introduces risks and complexities, as direct trial-and-error exploration for force determination can be unsafe and inefficient. Mitigating discomfort in one body region may inadvertently increase forces in another, making conflict resolution difficult.

This research contributes to bridging these gaps by providing a framework that specifically addresses personalized multi-contact pHRI. It integrates online user contact preference learning directly into low-level robot control, explicitly managing objective prioritization through a learning-to-rank approach. The incorporation of simulation-in-the-loop learning also addresses the critical safety and data-efficiency concerns associated with exploring contact preferences in real-world physical interactions, an aspect not extensively covered in previous online preference learning for HRI. The work aims to advance the capabilities of robots in sensitive caregiving tasks by ensuring personalization, safety, and comfort during physical interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a framework, named PrioriTouch, that enables robots to adapt to individual human contact preferences during whole-arm physical human-robot interaction, thereby enhancing safety and comfort while maintaining task performance. This objective is motivated by several challenges inherent in pHRI, particularly in caregiving contexts:

- **Personalization of Contact:** Individuals exhibit distinct preferences regarding where and with what force a robot can make contact. Current pHRI systems often struggle to personalize their behavior to these individual contact preferences, which is critical for safety and user acceptance.
- **Complexity of Multi-Contact Scenarios:** Whole-arm interaction, common in tasks like bed bathing or dressing, involves multiple simultaneous contacts between the robot and human. This complexity significantly increases the difficulty of identifying and respecting user preferences compared to single-contact scenarios.
- **Conflicting Force Requirements:** In multi-contact situations, different human body parts can impose incompatible force requirements. For example, a robot might need to apply gentle force on a sensitive area while applying more robust force on another area to complete a task. Such conflicts mean that no single control policy can satisfy all objectives simultaneously, necessitating a principled approach to trade-offs and prioritization.
- **Limitations of Static Preferences:** While population-level contact preferences can inform a conservative base policy, these are often insufficient for individual interactions. Stated preferences may diverge from actual comfort under real contact conditions (pressure, shear, approach speed, duration), and preferences can vary with context (e.g., posture, clothing) and over time (e.g., fatigue). Therefore, online adaptation to individual, dynamic preferences is essential.
- **Risks and Inefficiencies of Online Exploration:** Directly experimenting with a user to learn preferences online is inherently risky and inefficient. Each policy update can involve repeated physical contact and multiple feedback exchanges, potentially causing discomfort when forces are suboptimal or excessive, and increasing the user's cognitive workload and interaction time. A safe and data-efficient method for online preference learning is required.

To address these motivations, PrioriTouch aims to:
1. **Formulate contact preference learning as a learning-to-rank problem** over control objectives, allowing the robot to prioritize among potentially conflicting pose-tracking and force-regulation goals.
2. **Integrate this ranking learning with hierarchical operational space control (H-OSC)** to translate learned high-level preferences into low-level force regulation and pose tracking, enabling flexible trade-offs.
3. **Implement a simulation-in-the-loop approach** for preference learning, allowing safe and data-efficient exploration of new control policies in a digital twin before real-world deployment.
4. **Derive personalized comfort thresholds** from user studies to inform realistic models of contact preferences and bootstrap the learning process.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to enable robots to operate more comfortably and safely in diverse multi-contact pHRI scenarios, particularly in the domain of assistive caregiving.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PrioriTouch is a framework designed to adapt to user contact preferences during whole-arm physical human-robot interaction by learning to rank control objectives and executing them using hierarchical control. The methodology comprises three main components: Hierarchical Operational Space Control (H-OSC), a learning-to-rank contextual bandit algorithm (LinUCB-Rank), and a simulation-in-the-loop learning strategy.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Operational Space Control (H-OSC)

PrioriTouch leverages H-OSC to manage multiple, potentially conflicting control objectives. H-OSC facilitates this by enforcing a strict hierarchy among objectives. When a ranking of objectives ($\sigma_t$) is provided, H-OSC computes joint torques by recursively projecting lower-priority objectives into the null space of higher-priority ones. This ensures that higher-priority tasks are executed without interference from lower-priority tasks.

The robot's dynamics are modeled, and torques ($\tau_t$) are generated based on operational-space forces ($F_{j,t}$). These forces are derived from desired task accelerations, which vary depending on the objective type:
- **Pose-tracking objective ($J_p(t)$):** Aims to minimize deviation from a desired end-effector pose. The desired acceleration is calculated using proportional and derivative gains based on the pose error.
- **Force-regulation objective ($J_{f,b}(t)$):** Aims to prevent forces on a specific body part ($b$) from exceeding a comfort threshold ($f_{\max}(b)$). The desired acceleration is computed to reduce any exceedance using a scalar force gain.

#### 3.2. Learning to Rank using Contextual Bandits (LinUCB-Rank)

To determine the priority ranking $\sigma_t$ for H-OSC, PrioriTouch formulates control objective prioritization as a learning-to-rank problem. It employs a contextual bandit (CB) algorithm, LinUCB-Rank, to balance exploration and exploitation in refining the ranking policy from sparse user feedback.

LinUCB-Rank extends the standard LinUCB algorithm to construct a full ranking sequentially at each timestep. Instead of choosing a single best element, it builds the ranking $\sigma_t = (a_{t,1}, \dots, a_{t,|J_t|})$ by iteratively selecting the objective $a_{t,i}$ with the highest Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) from the remaining candidates for each slot $i$. This sequential selection explicitly accounts for interdependencies, where assigning higher priority to one objective affects the null space available for subsequent objectives. While objectives (pose tracking, force regulation on contacted parts) can vary, LinUCB-Rank operates by ranking a fixed surrogate set, specifically the set of all possible body parts ($B$), and then sorts active objectives based on this learned body-part order.

The **context ($z_{t,i}$)** for LinUCB-Rank at each slot $i$ is derived from the current robot-human state ($s_t$) and includes three components:
1. **Max force per body part ($z_{t,f}$):** A vector representing the maximum force experienced on each body part.
2. **Pose error ($z_{t,p}$):** The magnitude of the deviation from the desired end-effector pose.
3. **Ranking state ($z_{t,h}$):** An indicator vector showing which body parts have already been assigned a position in the current ranking.

After H-OSC executes the ranking $\sigma_t$, the robot observes sensor data and receives sparse user feedback ($\phi_t = (b, \delta)$), which indicates a body part of concern ($b$) and a requested change ($\delta$, e.g., decrease force, increase force). This feedback, along with force threshold violations, is used to compute a **per-slot reward ($r_{t,i}$)** for each element $a_{t,i}$ in the executed ranking. The reward consists of two terms:
1. **Feedback alignment ($r_{\phi_{t,i}}$):** Positive if the objective $a_{t,i}$ matches the body part specified in the user feedback, and penalized otherwise based on rank deviation.
2. **Threshold violation ($r_{t,f,i}$):** Penalizes any exceedance of comfort force thresholds on contacted body parts.
These rewards are used to update the parameters ($\hat{\theta}_i$) of the linear reward model for each objective.

#### 3.3. Simulation-in-the-Loop Preference Learning

To ensure safe and data-efficient adaptation, PrioriTouch incorporates a simulation-in-the-loop strategy for high-risk exploration. Direct exploration with a user can lead to undesirable force transients. Therefore, candidate policy re-orderings are first validated in a digital twin of the robot and environment before deployment on the real system. The process involves:
1. **Recording Feedback:** Receiving user feedback ($\phi_t$) and updating the comfort threshold ($f_{\max}(b)$), along with logging the current state ($s_t$).
2. **Safe Retraction:** Moving the physical robot to a predefined safe configuration, away from the human.
3. **Simulated Exploration:** Instantiating the digital twin at the logged state $s_t$ and iteratively refining the ranking policy ($\pi_\sigma(t)$) using LinUCB-Rank within the simulation.
4. **Resuming Operation:** Once the policy converges in the digital twin, the refined policy is deployed, and real-world interaction resumes.

This approach minimizes the need for real-world user interactions for policy refinement, allowing only policies that perform well under simulated reward signals to be deployed, contributing to safer and more data-efficient adaptation.

#### 3.4. User Studies for Contact Preference Elicitation

To ground the robot's initial behavior and user models in realistic preferences, a user study (n=98) was conducted to elicit population-level contact preferences for robot-initiated touch in caregiving contexts (e.g., standing, bathing, dressing). Participants rated comfort for contact across 37 body regions and three touch categories. Findings from this study informed:
- A base ranking of "contact openness" for different body parts.
- Per-part comfort force thresholds ($f_{\max}(b)$), which were derived by combining biomechanical pain limits with a "sensitivity ratio" based on the frequency with which a body part was selected as acceptable for touch. This provides a conservative yet personalized foundation for the robot's force regulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PrioriTouch involved simulation experiments, real-world hardware tests, and user studies, progressively increasing in complexity and realism.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

- **Sphere Environment:** In a simplified simulation with 2-4 spheres interacting with a stationary robot arm (representing body parts), LinUCB-Rank exhibited improved sample efficiency compared to the LinUCB (Sorted) baseline, requiring fewer user feedback signals to learn preferences, particularly as the number of simultaneous contacts increased. This indicates that its explicit accounting for objective interdependencies is beneficial in multi-contact scenarios.
- **Robot-assisted Bed Bathing:** In a simulated bed-bathing scenario involving whole-arm contacts with seven body regions, PrioriTouch (using LinUCB-Rank) significantly reduced force threshold violations compared to Fixed Priority (FP) and Heuristic-based Priority (HP) baselines (paired t-test, p < 0.05). LinUCB-Rank demonstrated an ability to anticipate preferences and reduce excessive forces, in contrast to HP which reacted abruptly to violations. While FP could sometimes achieve faster task completion, PrioriTouch maintained comparable task completion times while enhancing comfort by adhering more closely to varying user comfort thresholds.

#### 4.2. Hardware Testbed

- **3D Goal-Reaching Maze:** PrioriTouch was deployed in a real-world 3D goal-reaching maze where vertical cylinders represented body parts with distinct preferences. When the robot exceeded force thresholds, it retracted, updated its priority policy in a digital twin via LinUCB-Rank, and then resumed the task with the refined policy. This iterative process allowed the robot to adapt to the physical environment and user preferences, demonstrating steady progress toward the goal in a multi-contact setting.

#### 4.3. User Studies

- **Eliciting Population-Level Contact Preferences (n=98):** The study revealed that participants generally preferred robot contact on arms, hands, and the upper/lower back for functional assistance. More sensitive regions like buttocks and genitals were rarely selected. These findings provided a quantitative basis for initializing contact openness rankings and personalized comfort force thresholds ($f_{\max}(b)$) used in the robot's control system, combining survey-derived sensitivity ratios with biomechanical pain limits.
- **PrioriTouch Evaluation with Humans (n=8):** A user study evaluating PrioriTouch against a Heuristic-based Priority (HP) baseline in a realistic robot-assisted bed-bathing task with human subjects showed favorable results for PrioriTouch. Seven out of eight participants preferred PrioriTouch over the HP baseline for tasks involving whole-arm manipulation. The method received higher ratings in perceived safety, comfort, and overall task performance (mean ratings of 3.06 ± 0.97 for task performance, 4.65 ± 0.61 for safety, and 4.82 ± 0.39 for comfort, compared to HP's 2.53 ± 0.94, 4.59 ± 0.71, and 4.65 ± 0.61 respectively, on a 1-5 Likert scale). Qualitative feedback included comments on the method's effectiveness on subsequent attempts (after policy updates) and its ability to achieve "seamless and natural" contacts.
- **Feedback Mechanism Comparison (Appendix A.3):** A separate Wizard-of-Oz study (n=11) indicated that descriptive user feedback (e.g., "uncomfortable on my right arm") resulted in significantly lower cognitive workload for users than magnitude-based feedback (e.g., "reduce force by 5N"). This supported the choice of descriptive feedback for PrioriTouch.

#### 4.4. Additional Experiments (Appendix A.7)

- **Simulation-in-the-Loop Pauses and Fidelity:** The average time for preference learning convergence in simulation was 52.4 ± 21.7 seconds, which progressively reduced to approximately 30 seconds as preference estimates improved, suggesting its suitability for online operation. The method also demonstrated robustness to moderate simulation inaccuracies, with a 12.21% increase in required feedback signals when collision model fidelity was reduced to 75%.
- **Comparison with Alternative Baselines:** PrioriTouch was compared against Residual Reinforcement Learning and an adapted version of Jain et al.'s MPC-based whole-arm manipulation. Residual RL failed to achieve stable convergence within online learning constraints, showing no reduction in force violations. The MPC-based WAM baseline resulted in approximately three times more force-threshold violations than PrioriTouch.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PrioriTouch offers a significant contribution to the field of physical human-robot interaction (pHRI), particularly in the context of whole-arm manipulation and assistive robotics. Its impact stems from several key aspects:

- **Enhanced Safety and Comfort in pHRI:** By systematically adapting to individual user contact preferences, PrioriTouch directly addresses critical concerns regarding safety and comfort during physical interaction between robots and humans. This is particularly relevant for sensitive applications like caregiving (e.g., bed bathing, dressing), where inappropriate contact forces or locations can cause discomfort, anxiety, or even injury. The ability to personalize interaction reduces the risk of adverse events and improves user acceptance of robotic assistance.
- **Bridging High-Level Preferences and Low-Level Control:** The framework effectively translates abstract user feedback (e.g., "uncomfortable on my left thigh") into concrete low-level robot control adjustments (force regulation and pose tracking). This structured integration, achieved through a learning-to-rank policy combined with hierarchical operational space control, provides a principled mechanism for robots to operationalize complex human preferences, a challenge often faced in HRI.
- **Data-Efficient and Safe Online Learning:** The simulation-in-the-loop learning approach is a notable advancement for online preference learning in pHRI. By allowing high-risk exploration and policy refinement to occur in a digital twin, it mitigates the dangers and inefficiencies of direct trial-and-error with human users. This capability is crucial for practical deployment of assistive robots, enabling them to learn and adapt without compromising user safety or causing undue stress.
- **Robustness in Multi-Contact Scenarios:** PrioriTouch provides a method for managing inherent conflicts and trade-offs in multi-contact whole-arm manipulation, where different body parts may have incompatible force requirements. The learning-to-rank approach offers a systematic way to prioritize objectives, ensuring that critical tasks are completed while respecting user comfort across multiple contact points. This expands the practical utility of whole-arm manipulation in complex human environments.
- **Generality and Applicability:** The framework is designed to be controller-agnostic, capable of prioritizing from a general collection of control objectives. This suggests its applicability extends beyond specific caregiving scenarios to broader multi-contact settings in pHRI, enhancing its potential for wider adoption across various robotic applications requiring nuanced physical interaction.
- **Foundation for Future Assistive Robotics:** The insights gained from the user studies on population-level contact preferences and the practical validation of PrioriTouch with human subjects lay a foundation for developing more intelligent and user-centric assistive robots. The method's ability to create personalized comfort thresholds and adapt to evolving preferences pushes the boundaries of personalized robot assistance.

In summary, PrioriTouch represents a step towards creating more empathetic and capable robots for physical assistance, with a particular impact on the safety, comfort, and personalization of human-robot interaction in caregiving and other close-contact applications. Further deployment in real-world settings with individuals with mobility limitations will be key to realizing its full potential.
