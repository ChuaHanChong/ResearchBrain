# G1: Teaching LLMs to Reason on Graphs with Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The G1 project introduces a reinforcement learning framework to enhance Large Language Models' graph reasoning, achieving substantial performance gains and strong zero-shot generalization across diverse graph-theoretic tasks and real-world applications. This approach also improved the LLM's broader general reasoning abilities.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) consistently underperform on tasks requiring reasoning about graph-structured data, despite their advanced natural language processing abilities.
- Prior attempts using natural language encoding schemes, instruction tuning, or specialized graph foundation models face limitations due to modest improvements, data scarcity, and challenges in universal graph representation.
- State-of-the-art LLMs, including models like OpenAI's o1, exhibit low accuracy on fundamental graph problems, indicating a critical gap in their general intelligence.

## Method

- The research proposes Reinforcement Learning (RL) on synthetic ==graph-theoretic tasks== as a scalable method to elicit and enhance LLMs' latent graph reasoning capabilities.
- A large-scale, diverse dataset called Erdős was curated, comprising 50 graph-theoretic tasks with automatically generated ground truths and algorithmic verifiers for rule-based reward attribution.
- The GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) algorithm is employed for RL training, often initialized with Chain-of-Thought Supervised Fine-tuning (CoT-SFT) to teach intermediate reasoning steps.

## Results

- G1-**7B** achieved **66.16%** average accuracy on the Erdős benchmark, outperforming GPT-4o-mini by **18.56%** and showing competitive performance with OpenAI o3-mini.
- G1 models demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization to unseen graph tasks, different graph encoding formats, and real-world node classification/link prediction (e.g., GraphWiz, Cora, PubMed).
- RL training restored and enhanced performance on general reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-pro), with G1-**7B** surpassing its base model across all three.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pre-trained LLMs possess a latent understanding of graph structures and logic, which can be effectively elicited and refined through targeted reinforcement learning.
> - Reinforcement learning with rule-based, verifiable rewards provides a scalable and data-efficient training pathway, bypassing the limitations of scarce human-annotated graph data.
> - Enhancing graph reasoning through RL can synergistically improve an LLM's general reasoning abilities across various domains, rather than coming at their expense.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across various natural language processing tasks, yet their performance on tasks requiring reasoning about graph-structured data remains a significant limitation. This deficiency is a critical barrier to achieving truly general-purpose artificial intelligence, as graph reasoning is fundamental to numerous applications in science, engineering, and social systems (e.g., social network analysis, molecular structure prediction, knowledge graph reasoning).

Prior attempts to address this gap have largely fallen into three categories:
1. **Natural Language Encoding Schemes:** Early efforts focused on representing graphs as text sequences (e.g., adjacency lists, edge lists) for LLM input. However, these methods showed only modest improvements and struggled to find a universally optimal or robust encoding strategy across diverse graph types and tasks. The sequential nature of text fundamentally misaligns with the inherent non-sequential, relational structure of graphs, making it difficult for LLMs to capture complex long-range dependencies.
2. **Instruction Tuning and Preference Tuning:** Researchers have tried fine-tuning LLMs on curated graph problem datasets (e.g., GraphInstruct, GraphWiz). While this can teach specific patterns, such approaches are severely limited by the scarcity and high cost of creating large-scale, diverse, and high-quality human-annotated graph datasets. Furthermore, models trained via distillation or supervised fine-tuning risk memorizing patterns rather than developing genuine, generalizable reasoning skills, potentially leading to overfitting.
3. **Specialized Graph Foundation Models (GFMs):** Another avenue involves pre-training models specifically designed for graph data. However, this is hampered by the lack of large-scale, universally represented graph data suitable for diverse graph types, making it challenging to build truly general-purpose GFMs.

The authors of "G1" challenge the premise that LLMs lack intrinsic graph reasoning capabilities. Instead, they hypothesize that LLMs, having been pre-trained on vast internet-scale data, already possess a latent ability for graph understanding. The core innovation of this work lies in proposing **Reinforcement Learning (RL) on synthetic graph-theoretic tasks** as an efficient and scalable method to *elicit* and enhance these dormant capabilities. This approach is positioned as a paradigm shift, moving away from reliance on scarce human-annotated data or the need for separate graph foundation models, and instead leveraging the LLM's existing knowledge and trial-and-error learning, akin to the success of RL in domains like game playing (AlphaGo-Zero) or complex reasoning in math/coding (DeepSeek R1). By focusing on automatically verifiable graph-theoretic tasks, G1 sidesteps the data annotation bottleneck and offers a path toward building more robust and generalizable graph reasoners.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the G1 project is to overcome the recognized deficiency of Large Language Models in reasoning about graph-structured data. Despite their advanced natural language processing and general reasoning abilities, state-of-the-art LLMs consistently underperform on graph-related tasks, demonstrating significant gaps in their "general intelligence" when confronted with relational data. The authors highlight that even sophisticated models like OpenAI's o1 exhibit low accuracy on fundamental graph problems like connectivity tests.

Against this backdrop, the "G1" research sets forth several key objectives:

1. **To introduce Reinforcement Learning (RL) as a novel and effective framework** for improving LLMs' graph reasoning abilities. The authors specifically aim to demonstrate that RL, applied to graph-theoretic problems with verifiable rewards, can significantly enhance these capabilities.
2. **To curate and introduce Erdős, a large-scale and highly diverse graph-theoretic dataset.** This dataset is explicitly designed to enable rule-based RL training by providing clear, automatically verifiable ground truths for a wide spectrum of graph problems, thus bypassing the limitations of scarce human-annotated data.
3. **To develop G1, an RL-finetuned LLM, that achieves substantial performance improvements** on graph reasoning tasks, ideally outperforming much larger or specially trained baseline models.
4. **To empirically demonstrate strong zero-shot generalization of G1 models** to previously unseen graph tasks, different graph domains, and alternative graph encoding schemes. This objective aims to prove that the learned graph reasoning is robust and not merely an overfitting to the training data.
5. **To show that enhancing graph reasoning does not come at the cost of general reasoning abilities.** The research seeks to confirm that RL training on graph tasks can synergistically improve overall LLM capabilities, or at least maintain performance on non-graph-related reasoning benchmarks (e.g., mathematics, multi-task language understanding).
6. **To establish an efficient, scalable, and data-agnostic pathway** for building powerful graph reasoners within general-purpose LLMs, thereby suggesting that the inherent understanding of graphs might already exist in pre-trained LLMs and can be successfully unlocked through targeted RL.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the "graph reasoning gap" in LLMs through a scalable, principled, and generalizable method, pushing LLMs closer to truly universal intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The G1 methodology introduces a simple yet highly effective approach to enhance LLMs' graph reasoning capabilities: **Reinforcement Learning (RL) on synthetic graph-theoretic tasks**. This approach leverages rule-based rewards for training, addressing the limitations of data scarcity and complex graph representations.

#### 3.1. The Erdős Dataset: A Comprehensive Collection of Graph-theoretic Tasks

To facilitate RL training from verifiable rewards (RLVR), the authors constructed **Erdős**, the largest and most comprehensive graph-theoretic dataset to date:
- **Task Curation:** Erdős comprises 50 diverse graph-theoretic tasks sourced from the NetworkX library. These tasks span a wide range of computational complexity and problem types, from basic graph properties (e.g., node counting, edge existence) to NP-hard problems (e.g., Traveling Salesman Problem, Maximal Independent Set). This diversity ensures broad coverage of graph reasoning challenges. The tasks include those for general, directed, and weighted graphs, with varied answer types (boolean, integer, float, node/edge lists, node mappings).
- **Automatic Answer Generation and Verification:** A key innovation for RLVR is the automatic generation of ground truth answers and verification programs. NetworkX's default solvers are used to produce correct solutions. For problems with multiple valid solutions (e.g., shortest paths), algorithmic verifiers are implemented to check the correctness of LLM-generated outputs, ensuring precise reward attribution without costly and potentially biased human labeling.
- **Graph Sources:** Unlike many prior benchmarks that rely on synthetic random graphs, Erdős utilizes real-world graphs from the Network Repository. These large graphs are downsampled using a random walk with restart strategy to generate subgraphs with 5 to 35 nodes, making them computationally feasible for LLMs while retaining realistic structural properties.
- **Language Encoding:** Graphs are represented in a unified edge list format (e.g., "(1, 2), (2, 3)...") for LLM input, though the model's ability to transfer to other formats is later tested.
- **Scale:** The dataset consists of 100,000 training examples (2,000 per task) and 5,000 test examples, providing ample data for robust training and evaluation.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning of LLMs on Graphs

The core of the G1 training involves an RL phase with rule-based reward models and the GRPO algorithm:
- **Rule-based Outcome Reward Model (ORM):** Rewards are defined based on the correctness of the LLM's generated answer:
 - **Strict Value Matching:** For tasks with a unique ground truth value (e.g., node count), a +1 reward is given for an exact match, 0 otherwise.
 - **Jaccard Index for Set Matching:** For unordered set answers (e.g., common neighbors), the Jaccard Index (|$s \cap \hat{s}$| / |$s \cup \hat{s}$|) between the generated set $\hat{s}$ and ground truth $s$ provides a continuous reward signal, allowing for partial credit.
 - **Algorithmic Verification:** For tasks with multiple correct solutions (e.g., Hamiltonian path), custom algorithmic verifiers check the validity of the generated solution.
- **RL Algorithm (GRPO):** The Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm is employed. This involves:
 - Sampling multiple responses from the policy model for each query.
 - Calculating rewards for each response.
 - Computing group relative advantages based on these rewards.
 - Updating the policy model to maximize an objective function that considers the probability ratios of generated responses and their advantages. A Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty to the reference (base) policy is included to prevent excessive deviation and overfitting. An entropy loss is also added to encourage exploration.
- **Training Setup:** The models (based on Qwen2.5-Instruct 3B/7B) are trained for 300 steps on 8xA800 GPUs.

#### 3.3. Optional Warm-up with Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)

An optional SFT phase is introduced as a warm-up, particularly beneficial for challenging tasks where the base model's initial accuracy is near zero, hindering effective RL signal. Two types of SFT are explored:
- **Direct-SFT:** Fine-tuning on (question, answer) pairs, where answers are directly synthesized programmatically. This teaches target patterns but lacks explicit reasoning steps.
- **CoT-SFT (Chain-of-Thought SFT):** Fine-tuning on (question, CoT, answer) triplets. Reasoning trajectories (CoT) are generated by sampling from a stronger teacher model (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct) and filtering for correct solutions (Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning). This explicitly teaches intermediate reasoning steps, aiming for better generalization. G1 models are typically initialized with CoT-SFT before the main RL phase.

This comprehensive methodology, combining a novel dataset, sophisticated reward mechanisms, and a robust RL algorithm with optional SFT, forms the backbone of the G1 project.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The G1 paper presents compelling results across a spectrum of evaluations, demonstrating significant enhancements in LLMs' graph reasoning abilities and robust generalization.

#### 4.1. Benchmarking G1 on Erdős Graph-theoretic Reasoning Tasks

- **Superior Performance:** G1 models consistently outperform proprietary and open-source baselines across all difficulty levels of the Erdős benchmark.
 - **G1-7B** achieved an average accuracy of 66.16%, surpassing GPT-4o-mini (47.60%) by a substantial margin (18.56%) and demonstrating competitive performance with highly-trained, state-of-the-art reasoning models like OpenAI o3-mini (64.90%). On a sampled subset, G1-7B even exceeded GPT-4o's performance (65.29% vs. 55.13%).
 - **G1-3B**, a much smaller variant, reached 59.76% average accuracy, outperforming models 20 times its size, such as Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (47.16%) and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct (42.28%).
- **Role of SFT:** Direct-SFT was a strong baseline, outperforming larger models. However, CoT-SFT, used for G1's initialization, showed better scaling potential for larger models and generalization, despite sometimes lower direct accuracy.
- **Robustness:** Extensive testing with 32 different random seeds revealed consistently low standard deviations (<1%), affirming the stability of the method. Prompt sensitivity tests, using GPT-4o to generate semantic variants, also showed minimal performance variance (<1.5%), confirming benchmark stability.
- **Scalability to Larger Models:** Extending the methodology, G1-Zero-32B (trained from Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct without SFT warmup) achieved a 27.96% improvement in average accuracy on Erdős (from 47.10% to 75.06%), with particularly notable gains on Hard (+31.87%) and Challenging (+26.43%) problems.
- **Scalability to Larger Graphs:** G1-3B and G1-7B demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization to larger graphs (36-100 nodes), significantly outperforming baselines, with G1-7B achieving an average of 46.46% compared to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct's 23.94%. The paper acknowledges LLM context window limits as a bottleneck for even larger graphs.

#### 4.2. Transferability of G1 to Unseen Tasks and Domains

- **Other Graph Reasoning Benchmarks:** G1 showed strong zero-shot generalization to benchmarks like GraphWiz and GraphArena, which feature different graph distributions, unseen tasks, and unfamiliar graph encoding formats (e.g., human names for nodes). G1-7B achieved the highest overall accuracy on GraphWiz (57.11%), surpassing models specifically trained on that data.
- **Real-world, Non-graph-theoretic Tasks:** G1 models significantly outperformed baselines on node classification and link prediction tasks using real-world citation graphs (Cora and PubMed), demonstrating effectiveness in scenarios requiring graph-text reasoning. G1-7B achieved the highest average score (87.29%) on these tasks.
- **Reasoning Ability Beyond Graphs:**
 - While CoT-SFT initially led to a degradation in general reasoning abilities (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-pro), the subsequent RL phase *restored and even enhanced* these capabilities.
 - G1-7B surpassed its base model, Qwen-7B-Instruct, across all three general reasoning benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K: 87.49% vs. 86.27%; MATH: 71.80% vs. 69.80%; MMLU-pro: 48.56% vs. 45.75%).
 - Notably, G1-7B improved performance even on seemingly unrelated non-STEM MMLU-Pro categories like Economy, suggesting a synergistic improvement in general reasoning. Analysis indicates improvements in numerical calculation and comprehensive utilization of information.

#### 4.3. Training Analysis

- **Data Mixture:** The uniform reward weighting in G1-3B led to suboptimal performance on Hard/Challenging tasks, as the policy optimized for easier, more frequently rewarding tasks. Training G1-Hard-3B exclusively on Hard/Challenging tasks remedied this, achieving higher accuracy on these subsets while still generalizing well to Easy/Medium tasks. This highlights that learning difficult tasks confers transferable reasoning skills.
- **SFT Warmup:** Direct RL from the base model (G1-Zero) was surprisingly strong. However, initializing RL with CoT-SFT (G1) consistently provided clearer and more significant improvements, especially for harder tasks, and resulted in more precise reasoning patterns.

#### 4.4. Understanding the Benefits of RL Training (Case Study: Shortest Path)

- An analysis of solution strategies for the shortest path task showed that RL training reshaped LLMs' reasoning. The base model attempted various algorithms (BFS, Dijkstra, intuitive deduction), with Dijkstra often leading to errors.
- RL-trained models (G1-Zero-3B, G1-3B) largely abandoned Dijkstra, favoring a more robust combination of BFS and intuitive search. G1-3B primarily adopted a precise BFS-style algorithm. This suggests RL guides LLMs toward more "model-aware" and effective reasoning strategies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The G1 paper marks a significant advancement in enhancing Large Language Models' capabilities, particularly in the domain of graph reasoning. Its findings carry profound implications for the development of more general and versatile AI systems.

**Key Significance:**

- **Pioneering RL for Graph Reasoning in LLMs:** G1 is the first work to successfully apply a reinforcement learning framework to significantly improve LLMs' performance on a diverse range of graph reasoning tasks. This opens up a new, promising avenue for tackling a long-standing weakness of LLMs.
- **Scalable and Data-Efficient Training:** By demonstrating that RL on *synthetic, rule-based verifiable* graph-theoretic tasks can effectively train LLMs, G1 provides a scalable and data-efficient pathway. This bypasses the critical bottlenecks of scarce human-annotated data and the challenges of creating universal graph representations for graph foundation models.
- **Eliciting Latent Graph Understanding:** The success of G1 strongly suggests that pre-trained LLMs likely possess a latent understanding of graph structures and logic, which RL can effectively elicit and refine. This insight could fundamentally shift the paradigm for developing graph-aware AI, moving from building specialized graph models to fine-tuning general-purpose LLMs.
- **Strong Generalization Capabilities:** G1's ability to generalize zero-shot to unseen graph tasks, different graph encoding schemes, real-world graph problems (node classification, link prediction), and even improve general reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-Pro) is a testament to the robustness and universality of the learned skills. This indicates that a single, RL-finetuned LLM can become a versatile graph reasoner without compromising its broader intelligence.
- **Understanding Reasoning Strategy Shifts:** The case study on shortest path problems reveals that RL training actively guides LLMs towards more effective and "model-aware" reasoning strategies (e.g., favoring BFS over a flawed Dijkstra implementation), showcasing a deeper impact beyond mere pattern recognition.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Development of Graph-Aware AI:** G1's approach could significantly accelerate the development of AI systems capable of sophisticated reasoning across complex relational data in various fields.
- **Applications Across Domains:** The enhanced graph reasoning could translate into improved performance in diverse practical domains, including drug discovery (molecular graphs), logistics and supply chain optimization, social network analysis, knowledge graph inference, and even complex planning problems in robotics or autonomous systems.
- **Educational Tools:** The ability of LLMs to solve graph theory problems could lead to more intelligent tutoring systems for graph theory and related computational sciences.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens several exciting avenues for future research, such as exploring dynamic difficulty scheduling in RL for better sample efficiency, integrating visual inputs (e.g., graph images) for enhanced real-world applicability, and adapting the methodology to other structured data types beyond graphs (e.g., tabular data, time-series).
- **Shift in LLM Post-training Paradigms:** G1 contributes to the growing evidence that carefully designed RL can unlock complex reasoning abilities in LLMs, possibly more effectively than purely supervised methods, suggesting a powerful direction for LLM post-training.

**Limitations and Social Impact:**

While impactful, the authors acknowledge limitations, primarily the inherent sample inefficiency of GRPO for very challenging (e.g., NP-hard) tasks, which could be addressed with dynamic difficulty scheduling. The current analysis is also limited to graphs of up to 100 nodes due to LLM context window constraints, though the authors are optimistic about scaling with long-context advancements. Generalization to highly domain-specific applications (e.g., molecular property prediction) or other structured data remains untested.

From a social impact perspective, G1's capabilities offer practical benefits for education and scientific research by providing potent tools for solving complex graph problems. The authors emphasize that responsible development, careful dataset design, and human oversight are crucial to mitigate potential risks such as amplifying existing biases present in the training data, ensuring the technology serves as a beneficial aid to human experts.

In conclusion, G1 presents a compelling case for Reinforcement Learning as a transformative approach to imbue LLMs with strong, generalizable graph reasoning abilities, charting an efficient and scalable course toward more sophisticated and truly general-purpose AI.
