# Learning Human-Like Badminton Skills for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Hong Kong and EngineAI developed a progressive reinforcement learning framework that enables humanoid robots to learn and perform human-like badminton skills, demonstrating the first zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of whole-body anthropomorphic movements to a physical humanoid robot with 90% success for forehand lifts.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to achieve agile, whole-body coordination and precise, timing-critical interaction for high-velocity, dynamic tasks like badminton.
- A significant gap exists in blending stylistic human motion naturalness with functional, physics-aware striking capabilities without compromising either aspect.
- Current reinforcement learning approaches face challenges with sparse expert demonstrations and robust sim-to-real transfer for complex athletic behaviors.

## Method

- A four-stage "Imitation-to-Interaction" progressive reinforcement learning framework was designed, incrementally building skills from kinematic motor prior learning to interaction-driven refinement.
- A goal-conditioned state representation with phase-dependent masking and specialized reward designs was introduced to effectively preserve motion priors and guide task-oriented execution.
- Manifold expansion was employed to generalize discrete strike points from sparse human motion capture data into a dense interaction volume, enabling robust learning for varied shuttlecock trajectories.

## Results

- The full framework achieved the first successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of whole-body anthropomorphic badminton skills to a physical humanoid robot (EngineAI PM01).
- In simulation, the proposed method demonstrated superior performance with high success rates (**95.16%** Easy, **91.53%** Hard) and low tracking errors compared to ablations and hierarchical baselines.
- Real-world experiments on the PM01 robot achieved a **90%** success rate for forehand lifts and **70%** for backhand lifts over 10 trials each, exhibiting human-like whole-body coordination and robustness to real-world complexities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The progressive learning framework effectively transforms a robot from a kinematic mimic to a dynamic striker, successfully integrating human-like motion with physics-aware interaction.
> - Robust motor priors, distilled through goal-conditioned learning and stabilized with adversarial motion priors, are crucial for achieving both stylistic realism and functional precision.
> - Manifold expansion is vital for overcoming the sparsity of expert demonstrations, allowing the robot to learn and generalize across a continuous range of interaction targets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly advancing fields: humanoid robotics, reinforcement learning (RL), imitation learning, and dynamic human-robot interaction, particularly in sports. The broader research landscape for humanoids is striving to move beyond basic locomotion and static manipulation towards highly agile, versatile, and human-like performance in complex, dynamic tasks.

**Humanoid Whole-Body Control:** Historically, humanoid control relied on model-based trajectory optimization, which yielded stiff behaviors and struggled with real-world unpredictability. The advent of deep reinforcement learning has enabled robots to learn robust controllers through trial-and-error. Imitation learning, especially techniques like tracking-based rewards and Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), has been instrumental in synthesizing naturalistic, human-like behaviors. However, a significant gap remains: while these methods can mimic motion or perform basic object interaction, they often fail to integrate the explosive, whole-body coordination and precise, timing-critical interaction required for highly dynamic tasks involving small, fast-moving objects. Previous works have explored interaction but often with large, static targets or focused only on upper-limb dexterity, lacking full-body agility. This paper directly addresses this gap by developing a framework that blends stylistic human motion priors with functional, physics-aware striking.

**Dynamic Ball Sports in Legged Robotics:** Ball sports serve as a critical benchmark for integrated locomotion, precision control, and rapid decision-making. While progress has been made in ground-based tasks (e.g., robot soccer), these simplify the interception problem compared to aerial sports. Efforts in aerial sports like badminton and table tennis have often prioritized functional engineering (achieving task success through non-human strategies) over biomechanical realism or have been confined to simulation without real-world validation on physical robots. Some studies have achieved agile, human-like aerial ball sports in simulation, but their transferability to hardware was unproven. This work distinguishes itself by achieving both high-performance striking *and* stylistic realism, validated on a physical humanoid robot, thus pushing the frontier for truly anthropomorphic athletic robots.

**Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL):** Learning agile motor skills for dynamic targets is a GCRL problem, frequently challenged by sparse reward signals in high-dimensional control. Hierarchical approaches attempt to decouple planning from low-level control but often bottleneck agility. Reference State Initialization (RSI) helps exploration but doesn't guarantee plausible behavior post-initialization. This paper contributes to GCRL by proposing a progressive multi-stage framework that effectively structures exploration. By first distilling a robust motor prior and then enabling interaction-driven refinement, the approach allows the agent to retain stylistic quality while autonomously leveraging physics simulation, offering a novel way to balance exploration and expert guidance.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art by tackling the formidable challenge of integrating agile whole-body control, human-like motion, and precise interaction with high-velocity objects on physical humanoid robots. It bridges critical gaps identified in existing literature, particularly concerning the simultaneous achievement of stylistic naturalness and functional precision in demanding sports.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent complexity and demanding nature of high-demand sports like badminton for humanoid robots. Badminton requires an exceptional blend of speed, precision, and motor diversity, encompassing a rich repertoire of strokes that demand sophisticated whole-body coordination—from leg power, through trunk rotation, to precisely timed wrist snaps. Beyond mere functional success, human players exhibit distinctive naturalness and fluency in their movements, with smooth trajectories, rhythmic timing, and biomechanically efficient postures. Furthermore, players must continuously adapt to variable shuttlecock trajectories, making split-second decisions and executing multi-skill motor actions seamlessly. Replicating this integrated sensorimotor intelligence represents a "pinnacle of human achievement" and an ambitious target for robotic systems.

Existing robotic advancements, while significant in areas like locomotion or lifelike motion mimicry, have struggled to bridge the gap between kinematic imitation (how a human moves) and functional, physics-aware striking (how to interact effectively with the world) without compromising the stylistic naturalness of human motion. Many approaches either achieve performance through non-human strategies or are confined to simulation without real-world validation. The sparsity of expert demonstrations for such complex tasks also poses a significant challenge for reinforcement learning.

Given these motivations, the **key objectives** of this research are:

1. **Develop a progressive reinforcement learning framework:** To evolve a robot from a "mimic" (kinematic imitator) to a capable "striker" (dynamic interactant), enabling it to perform agile, high-velocity sports movements that are distinctively human-like in coordination and style.
2. **Bridge the gap between kinematic imitation and dynamic interaction:** To enable the synthesis of functional, physics-aware striking capabilities while preserving the naturalness and stylistic fidelity of human motion.
3. **Introduce a specialized state representation:** To effectively preserve motion priors from human data, ensuring stable transitions from kinematic tracking to physics-aware striking and maintaining natural behavior even during aggressive maneuvers.
4. **Overcome sparse expert demonstrations:** By introducing a manifold expansion strategy that generalizes discrete strike points into a dense interaction volume, allowing the robot to learn from limited human data.
5. **Achieve zero-shot sim-to-real transfer:** To demonstrate the first successful transfer of whole-body anthropomorphic badminton skills to a physical humanoid robot, showcasing robust striking capabilities and stylistic realism in the physical world without any fine-tuning.
6. **Master diverse badminton skills:** To validate the framework's versatility through the successful mastery of various skills, including lifts and drop shots, in simulation and real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed "Imitation-to-Interaction," is a progressive reinforcement learning framework designed to incrementally teach complex, dynamic badminton skills to a humanoid robot. It comprises four distinct stages, each building upon the previous one to refine the robot's capabilities from mere mimicry to skillful, physics-aware interaction.

1. **Stage 1: Kinematic Motor Prior Learning (Teacher)**
 - **Data Collection & Retargeting:** Human expert badminton motions (racket and body 6-DoF poses) are collected using a motion capture (MoCap) system. A parallel optimization-based retargeting scheme adapts these human motions to the robot's morphology. This involves minimizing costs for global and local keypoint alignment, end-effector/racket orientation, collision avoidance, joint limits, and motion smoothness. Crucially, the root pose and joint angles are jointly optimized to compensate for the robot’s spinal flexibility limitations, and overall/local scales are adjusted to reduce morphological discrepancies. Contact sequences are extracted.
 - **Teacher Policy Training:** A "teacher" policy is trained using privileged observations (proprioception, base state, foot contacts) and detailed tracking observations (future root states and joint angles over a horizon H). The reward function heavily emphasizes tracking accuracy (root, joint, end-effector, contact schedule) combined with regularization terms (energy, joint limits) and termination penalties. To enhance robustness, the teacher is trained with extensive domain randomization and external force perturbations, then fine-tuned on its own rollouts to generate precise reference trajectories.

2. **Stage 2: Goal-Conditioned Distillation (Student)**
 - **Knowledge Transfer:** The teacher's robust motor prior is distilled into a "student" policy using DAgger, a supervised imitation learning algorithm. This phase shifts the control strategy from pure motion tracking to task-oriented execution.
 - **Goal-Conditioned State Representation:** The student operates on a more compact, model-based state representation suitable for real-world deployment. Observations include proprioception and goal components:
 - **Time-to-Hit (TTH):** A scalar indicating time until impact (positive for preparation, negative for recovery), clipped to `[-2, 2]` seconds.
 - **Target Hit/Recovery State:** Relative differences between current state and target racket/root pose at impact (TTH=0) or recovery.
 - **Mitigation of OOD Issues:** Phase-dependent masking is employed (recovery targets masked during approach, hit targets masked during recovery) to reduce ambiguity and out-of-distribution issues in the vast state space.
 - **Tailored Reward Design:** Tracking rewards are specialized. During the hitting phase (TTH > 0), the focus is on racket trajectory tracking (not full-body imitation), weighted by an exponential decay on |TTH| to prioritize precision near impact. In the recovery phase (TTH < 0), the reward shifts to root pose tracking without temporal decay.

3. **Stage 3: Motion Stabilization with RL**
 - **Refinement with Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP):** This stage fine-tunes the student policy using reinforcement learning augmented with AMP. The goal is to improve physical stability and racket tracking precision, which can be suboptimal after distillation due to the global pose imitation trade-off.
 - **Discriminator Network:** A discriminator network `D` is trained to distinguish between the robot’s generated motion transitions and ground-truth human motion, using a history of `H_AMP=5` frames of kinematic states. This provides a "style reward."
 - **Optimization Objective:** The agent's reward becomes a weighted combination of the task reward (from Stage 2) and the style reward derived from `D`'s output, formulated using a Least-Squares GAN (LSGAN) objective with a gradient penalty. This stabilizes motion and enforces stylistic plausibility.

4. **Stage 4: Interaction-Driven Refinement**
 - **Physics Integration:** The policy interacts within a physics-simulated environment that includes a realistic shuttlecock model (aerodynamic forces, distinct head/skirt material properties for contact).
 - **Manifold Expansion:** To address the sparsity of discrete strike points in the original MoCap data, a dense, near-continuous manifold of reachable strike targets is created. This involves pre-generating numerous shuttlecock trajectories and selecting strike points around original dataset samples, enabling the policy to generalize across a volumetric interaction space.
 - **Refined Reward Design:** The hitting tracking reward is modified to be sparse, active only within a small time window `epsilon` around impact (1(|TTH|<epsilon)), focusing the policy on accurate motion precisely at the strike instant. A new physics-informed reward, `r_hit`, encourages the ball to land within the opponent’s court (`r_dir`) and penalizes weak returns (`r_speed`).
 - **Temporal Rhythm Randomization:** The time interval between a hit and the subsequent serve is randomized ( seconds). This trains the robot to handle varied temporal states, maintaining stability during idle periods and robustness during rapid, unrecovered rallies.

**Implementation Details:** The training environment uses NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab for high-throughput parallel RL (200 Hz physics, 50 Hz policy). Real-world evaluations are conducted on the EngineAI PM01 humanoid robot. An FZMotion optical motion capture system tracks the robot's base pose and real-time 3D ball position. An Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) estimates ball state and predicts future trajectories for proactive interception planning. Extensive domain randomization (robot dynamics, payload, CoM offsets, PD gains, control latency, ground friction, restitution, base velocity perturbations, terrain height noise) is applied during training to facilitate robust zero-shot Sim-to-Real transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating the efficacy of the "Imitation-to-Interaction" framework.

**A. Simulation Experiments:**
The Lift task was used to evaluate the robot's interception capabilities in two difficulty modes (Easy: 2.0x0.4x0.3 m³, Hard: 4.0x1.0x0.3 m³ target volume). Performance was quantified by Success Rate (SR), Tracking Error (MSE), and In-Bounds Reward (IBR).

- **Overall Performance of "Ours":** The full proposed method achieved the best overall performance, with high success rates (SR: 0.9516 Easy, 0.9153 Hard) and low tracking errors (MSE: 0.0062 Easy, 0.0108 Hard). This indicates superior reliability and precision across diverse interception scenarios.
- **Importance of Progressive Stages:**
 - **w/o Stab. (Stage 3 removed):** Performed comparably to "Ours" in final metrics but the authors noted its critical role in *development efficiency* by providing robust policy initialization, thus reducing convergence time for tuning.
 - **E2E-AMP (Stages 1-3 removed):** Showed a significant performance drop (SR: 0.8586 Easy, 0.7391 Hard; MSE: 0.2109 Easy, 0.6004 Hard). This highlights that the initial imitation and distillation stages (1 & 2) are crucial for establishing a valid motor prior; without them, the RL agent struggles to explore effective striking poses. The slightly higher IBR in E2E-AMP for the easy setting was attributed to survivorship bias, as it only successfully intercepted the easiest, most returnable balls.
 - **w/o Interact. (Stage 4 removed):** Exhibited very poor performance (SR: 0.3787 Easy, 0.3583 Hard; negative IBR). This critically underscores that kinematic fidelity alone is insufficient; the interaction-driven refinement (Stage 4) is essential for functional success, allowing the policy to learn physically valid shuttlecock trajectories and generalize from sparse MoCap data.
- **Comparison with Hierarchical Baselines:** Both ASE-Based and VQ-Based hierarchical methods performed extremely poorly (SR < 0.005, very high MSE, negative IBR). This suggests that these approaches, possibly due to mode collapse or insufficient granularity in compressed latent spaces, struggle with the strict hardware constraints and dynamic precision demands of this task.
- **Qualitative Analysis of Learned Behaviors (Simulation):** The policy demonstrated sophisticated, non-conservative, and biomechanically plausible movement patterns:
 - **Whole-Body Torque Generation:** For backhand lifts, the robot learned to utilize torso rotation to generate "whipping" power, compensating for limited wrist motion.
 - **Dynamic Lunge and Recovery:** For distant forehand targets, the robot executed aggressive lunges with significant CoM shifts, followed by rapid recovery using ground reaction forces, indicating foresight in stability management.
 - **Inertial Follow-Through:** During drop shots, the robot displayed a natural follow-through, dissipating kinetic energy smoothly, mirroring human motor control strategies.
 - **Racket Slicing Motions:** The robot learned to intentionally strike the shuttlecock with non-orthogonal racket face velocities, allowing for precise trajectory control beyond simple elastic collisions.

**B. Real-World Experiments (Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer):**
The learned lift policies were deployed directly onto the EngineAI PM01 humanoid robot without any real-world fine-tuning.

- **Qualitative Analysis: Whole-Body Coordination:**
 - The robot faithfully reproduced the temporal structure of professional strokes for both forehand and backhand lifts (Fig. 1).
 - Specific human-like actions observed included pronounced trunk rotation in backhand lifts to generate torque and deep lunges in forehand lifts for extended reach.
 - Despite high-velocity swings, the robot maintained stability throughout the follow-through, actively adjusting foot placement for recovery.
 - Crucially, the policy successfully exploited whole-body coordination (torso yaw, base orientation) to compensate for the PM01 robot's hardware limitation of only 5-DoF arms, enabling correct racket-shuttlecock alignment.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap Analysis:**
 - While successful, the physical robot showed "distinct behavioral shifts" compared to simulation. It exhibited high-frequency footwork adjustments (rapid shuffling) during approach and recovery, attributed to system latencies and unmodeled joint friction causing slight tracking delays.
 - Despite these "oscillations" and a less "stable visual appearance" than in simulation, the robot remained balanced and successfully intercepted the ball. This highlights the policy's robustness and adaptability to real-world uncertainties, prioritizing balance recovery over kinematic smoothness.
- **Quantitative Results:** The real-world tests achieved a 90% success rate for forehand lifts and 70% for backhand lifts over 10 trials each, confirming the framework's effectiveness and robustness for physical deployment despite hardware limitations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in humanoid robotics, particularly in the domain of dynamic, interactive sports. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

**Significance:**
- **Pioneering Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The most prominent contribution is the demonstration of the *first zero-shot sim-to-real transfer* of anthropomorphic, whole-body badminton skills to a physical humanoid robot. This is a monumental step, as achieving robust transfer from simulation to complex physical hardware without real-world fine-tuning remains a grand challenge in robotics.
- **Novel Progressive RL Framework:** The "Imitation-to-Interaction" framework offers a robust, multi-stage approach to blend kinematic imitation with dynamic, physics-aware interaction. This progressive learning paradigm effectively tackles the complexities of sparse rewards and high-dimensional control by systematically building capabilities.
- **Human-Like Agility and Precision:** The work successfully synthesizes policies that achieve not only functional precision (hitting the shuttlecock accurately and effectively) but also stylistic naturalness and whole-body agility, mirroring human biomechanics. This moves beyond merely solving a task to solving it *like a human*, which is critical for natural human-robot collaboration and understanding.
- **Effective Handling of Sparse Data:** The manifold expansion strategy for strike targets effectively addresses the challenge of sparse expert demonstrations, generalizing discrete human movements into a dense, continuous interaction space for the robot.
- **Robustness to Reality Gap:** The observed real-world footwork adjustments, while differing from simulation, underscore the policy's inherent robustness. It adapts to unmodeled dynamics and latencies by prioritizing task success and balance, rather than rigidly adhering to a simulated kinematic ideal.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancement in Humanoid Dexterity and Versatility:** This research pushes the boundaries of what humanoid robots can achieve, moving them closer to being truly versatile, multi-functional agents capable of complex, dynamic interactions in unstructured environments.
- **New Benchmarks for Athletic Robotics:** Badminton, with its high demands, serves as an excellent benchmark. The methods developed here can inspire and inform future research in other challenging sports or dynamic manipulation tasks.
- **Applications in Rehabilitation and Training:** Robots capable of human-like sports movements could revolutionize physical rehabilitation, offering personalized and engaging exercise regimes. They could also serve as advanced sparring partners or trainers in sports, providing consistent, adaptable opponents or demonstrating ideal techniques.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration:** Robots that move and interact more naturally can foster better human-robot collaboration, making them more intuitive and safer to work with in various settings, from manufacturing to service industries.
- **Foundation for Future AI and Robotics Research:** The insights into bridging the sim-to-real gap, handling sparse rewards in complex tasks, and integrating imitation with interaction will be invaluable for future research in general-purpose AI and embodied intelligence.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges key limitations, primarily the delicate trade-off between motion naturalness, dynamic stability, and task precision, which often compete. Balancing these conflicting objectives currently requires meticulous tuning. Furthermore, the current system focuses on intercepting single shots within a localized area. Future work aims to address the stability-agility conflict through more adaptive control frameworks and extend the robot's capabilities to enable sustained, full-court gameplay against human opponents, ultimately working towards truly autonomous and adaptive athletic robots.
