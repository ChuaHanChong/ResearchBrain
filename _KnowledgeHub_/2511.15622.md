# The SA-FARI Dataset: Segment Anything in Footage of Animals for Recognition and Identification

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed SA-FARI, the largest open-source dataset for multi-animal tracking (MAT) in wild environments, comprising 46 hours of video with over 940,000 manually verified segmentation masks across 99 species. Fine-tuning vision-language models like SAM 3 on SA-FARI significantly improved MAT performance, achieving a +32.9 cgF1 gain over the base model.

## Problem

- Existing multi-animal tracking (MAT) datasets for wild animals lacked sufficient scale, species diversity, geographical coverage, and high-quality, manually verified segmentation masks, hindering the development of generalizable MAT models.
- The increasing volume of data from camera traps and other in-situ sensors overwhelms human processing capabilities, creating a bottleneck for wildlife conservation efforts.
- Current datasets often focus on a limited number of species, short temporal windows, or rely on bounding box annotations rather than precise segmentation masks, leading to inaccuracies.

## Method

- The **SA-FARI** dataset was compiled from 11,609 camera trap videos (46 hours) collected over approximately ten years from 741 diverse sampling locations across four continents.
- The dataset provides 99 animal species categories, 16,224 individual masklet identities, and 942,702 exhaustive spatio-temporal segmentation masks and bounding boxes, meticulously annotated through a human-in-the-loop verification process.
- A rigorous train/test split strategy was implemented to maximize species diversity in the test set while ensuring no geographical overlap between training and testing camera trap locations.

## Results

- Fine-tuning SAM 3 on SA-FARI achieved the highest performance in species-specific tracking, with 46.9 cgF1, 68.1 pHOTA, and 84.6 TETA, representing a +32.9 cgF1 gain over the base SAM 3 model.
- Using a species-agnostic "animal" prompt, SAM 3 (SA-FARI) outperformed vision-only tracking methods, achieving 71.1 IDF1 and 83.7 HOTA, surpassing the closest competitor (MD+BoostSort++) by +23.9 IDF1.
- Performance analysis on test subsets showed that tracking large masklets was +29.2 pHOTA higher than small masklets (81.4 vs. 52.2), and challenging subsets (occlusions/motion) experienced reduced detection (pHOTA-Det 4.1 points lower) and association (pHOTA-Ass 9.3 points lower) accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The scale, diversity, and high-quality, manually verified segmentation masks of SA-FARI are crucial for training and evaluating generalizable multi-animal tracking models that can adapt to various wild animal populations and ecological contexts.
> - Incorporating domain-specific data from SA-FARI during training leads to substantial performance improvements for state-of-the-art vision-language models in MAT tasks.
> - Challenges persist in tracking small, heavily occluded, or rapidly moving animals, highlighting specific areas for future model development and targeted annotation efforts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Automated video analysis plays a role in wildlife conservation, supporting tasks such as species identification, individual re-identification, and behavior recognition. A foundational component of these applications is multi-animal tracking (MAT), which involves localizing individual animals in space and time within video footage. The increasing volume of data from in-situ sensors, particularly camera traps (CTs), has surpassed the processing capabilities of human experts, necessitating automated solutions.

Despite progress in related computer vision domains, MAT for wild animals remains comparatively underdeveloped. This is largely attributed to limitations in existing datasets for model training and evaluation. Current MAT-specific datasets, such as AnimalTrack and GMOT, typically lack scale, often comprising less than an hour of annotated footage and not including data from standard ecological sensors like camera traps. While larger datasets exist, they frequently focus on a limited number of species (e.g., fewer than five) and rarely exceed ten hours of annotated video.

Furthermore, many existing in-the-wild tracking datasets, often based on Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) footage (e.g., BuckTales, BaboonLand, KABR, WildLive), tend to be collected from single geographical locations or protected areas over short temporal windows. This restricts their ecological diversity by capturing a narrow range of habitats and behaviors. Datasets that include MAT annotations as a secondary feature for tasks like pose estimation or re-identification (e.g., LoTE, MammAlps, PanAf500, CCR) also often lack the species coverage, spatio-temporal breadth, or annotation precision required for developing generalizable MAT models. A common issue is the reliance on bounding box annotations, with segmentation masks being less common, and when present, often generated automatically without extensive manual verification, leading to potential inaccuracies.

The SA-FARI dataset addresses these limitations by providing a large-scale, diverse, and meticulously annotated resource. It aims to bridge the gap in data availability, offering a benchmark suitable for training and evaluating general-purpose MAT models that can be applied across a wide range of wild animal populations and ecological contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed decline in biodiversity at an accelerating rate. Effective conservation efforts require scalable tools, and automated video analysis is identified as a critical method for achieving this. Outputs from such analyses, including species, individual identity, and behavioral data, are essential for downstream ecological and conservation tasks like abundance estimation, occupancy modeling, and health monitoring. The rapid growth in data collected by in-situ sensors, particularly camera traps, has created a bottleneck, as the volume of footage now significantly exceeds the capacity for human processing within relevant timeframes for conservation action.

The key objective is to establish a robust foundation for automated wildlife monitoring by improving the ability to localize individual animals in space and time, a task referred to as multi-animal tracking (MAT). MAT is crucial because it underpins several computer vision applications, including individual, species, and behavior recognition, by providing spatio-temporal representations of animals. These representations are important for robust categorization and for preventing models from learning spurious background cues that can hinder generalization to new environments (out-of-distribution generalization). Both spatial information (e.g., coat patterns, body shape, pose) and temporal dynamics (e.g., gait, locomotion, social interactions) are critical for distinguishing species, individuals, and behaviors.

The authors identified that progress in MAT has been hampered by the absence of suitable datasets. Existing datasets are characterized by limitations in scale, species diversity, geographical and temporal coverage, and annotation quality, particularly regarding segmentation masks. These limitations prevent the development and evaluation of general MAT models capable of operating effectively across diverse wild animal populations.

Therefore, the specific objectives of this work were to:
1. Compile a large-scale, open-source MAT dataset for wild animals, addressing the lack of temporal, geographical, and species diversity in existing resources.
2. Provide exhaustive, high-quality spatio-temporal annotations, including bounding boxes, segmentation masks, species labels, and individual identities, with a focus on manually verified segmentation masks.
3. Establish a benchmark for state-of-the-art vision-language models and vision-only methods on this dataset, evaluating their performance in both species-specific and generic animal tracking scenarios.
4. Contribute a resource that facilitates the development of generalizable MAT models, ultimately supporting more effective and scalable biodiversity monitoring globally.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SA-FARI dataset was constructed through a multi-stage process involving data collection, multi-level annotation, and rigorous dataset partitioning.

**Data Collection:**
The dataset comprises 11,609 camera trap videos collected over approximately ten years (2014-2024) from 741 independent sampling locations across four continents (Central Africa, South America, Mesoamerica, Southern Europe). Data was contributed by seven partner organizations. Various brands of commercially available camera traps were used, set up following standardized procedures, typically 0.3m to 2m from the ground, with some arboreal cameras positioned between 8m and 24m. Cameras were motion-triggered and utilized IR flash for nighttime recording. Videos were captured at resolutions ranging from 320x194 to 2688x1234, frame rates from 10 to 60 FPS (mostly 30 FPS), and durations from 0.5s to 90s (mostly 15s). All videos contain at least one animal. Date and time stamps, along with anonymized sampling site strings, are provided for each video. Audio streams are also available but were not utilized in this work.

**Species Annotation:**
Each video was initially annotated with common names of observed animals by local experts. A subsequent step involved 2-4 independent annotators confirming these common names. For videos containing multiple species, disambiguation occurred during the temporal segmentation and tracklet annotation process, assigning independent common names to each tracklet. The dataset includes 99 species categories. Latin names, taxonomic ranks, and hierarchy (down to at least family, usually species level) are also provided.

**Segmentation Mask Annotation:**
Spatio-temporal segmentation annotations were generated using a human-in-the-loop process as part of the SAM 3 data engine, annotating videos at 6 FPS.
1. **Automatic Stage:** SAM 3 was used to pseudo-annotate all video-species category pairs, generating initial masks. Redundant annotations were removed via Intersection over Union (IoU)-based deduplication.
2. **Human Review and Correction:** Human annotators first verified that animals were visible and segmentable, excluding cases of excessive blur or indistinctness (e.g., within a flock). A second annotator then corrected masklets, removing incorrect ones, and utilizing online SAM 2 in the loop to refine or add masklets as necessary.
3. **Exhaustivity Checking:** A final round ensured that all possible masklets were included. Bounding boxes were not manually annotated but were derived from the spatial limits of the segmentation masks.

**Train & Test Splits:**
The dataset was divided into training and test subsets, with a maximum of 1,000 videos allocated to the test set. The objective was to maximize species diversity in the test set while ensuring that all videos from a single camera trap location were assigned to the same split, preventing geographical overlap between training and testing. Camera traps were sorted by their ratio of species categories to videos. Selection for the test set was performed greedily, prioritizing camera traps that introduced the most new species categories. The ordering was dynamically updated to deprioritize already represented species categories.

**Category Augmentation:**
To facilitate evaluation and training of detection models, the dataset was augmented with negative species category labels (species not present in a given video). These were generated in two difficulty levels:
1. **Hard Negatives:** For each video-species pair, a random species category from the same taxonomic group (family, order, or class, grouped into 29 overall groups) not present in the video was selected.
2. **Easy Negatives:** A random species category from a different taxonomic group, also not present in the video, was selected. This augmentation is possible due to the exhaustive nature of the original positive species annotations.

**Test Set Partitioning:**
The SA-FARI test set was further partitioned into five subsets for detailed analysis of model performance under specific conditions:
1. **Challenging (106 videos):** Defined by masklets with average inter-frame IoU < 0.7 (indicating significant movement) or ≥ 2 occlusion events.
2. **Night (598 videos):** Videos recorded during nighttime.
3. **Multi-masklet (136 videos):** Videos containing two or more individual animals.
4. **Large-masklet (211 videos):** Videos where the average masklet size (normalized by image size) was above the 75th percentile.
5. **Small-masklet (212 videos):** Videos where the average masklet size was below the 25th percentile.

**Dataset Statistics:**
SA-FARI contains 11,609 videos, summing to approximately 46 hours of densely annotated footage. It encompasses 99 animal species categories, distributed across 4 classes, 23 orders, 53 families, and 82 genera. Mammalia (67.7%) and Aves (27%) are the most represented classes. The dataset includes 16,224 individual masklet identities and 942,702 bounding box and segmentation mask annotations. On average, each video contains 1.4 masklets, with 22.7% of videos having two or more masklets (up to 14). The most common species categories are spider monkeys, collared peccaries, and agoutis. The dataset sources from 741 independent sampling sites across 4 continents, with 22.3% of locations contributing 90% of the data and 19.8% of locations providing 90% of the species category variety. The test set comprises 833 videos, 83 species, and 91 independent locations, ensuring no overlap with the training set's camera trap locations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented SA-FARI as the largest open-source Multi-Animal Tracking (MAT) dataset for wild animals to date, providing approximately 46 hours of exhaustively annotated footage, encompassing 99 species categories from 741 locations across 4 continents. The dataset includes 16,224 masklet identities and 942,702 individual bounding boxes, segmentation masks, and species labels, with high-quality, manually verified segmentation masks. This scale and diversity exceed prior MAT datasets, offering a suitable benchmark for generalizable MAT models.

**Species-Specific Prompt Evaluation:**
Three vision-language models were benchmarked: GLEE, LLMDet (paired with SAM 3's tracker for tracking), and SAM 3. The evaluation used video promptable concept segmentation (PCS) protocol with metrics cgF1, pHOTA, and TETA.
- The base SAM 3 model demonstrated a notable performance advantage over GLEE and LLMDet + SAM 3 TR, achieving 14.0 cgF1, 48.5 pHOTA, and 83.4 TETA.
- Incorporating SA-FARI data during SAM 3 training (SAM 3 (SA-FARI)) led to substantial performance improvements, reaching 39.0 cgF1, 63.1 pHOTA, and 83.9 TETA.
- Fine-tuning SAM 3 specifically on SA-FARI (SAM 3 FT (SA-FARI)) resulted in the highest performance across all models, achieving 46.9 cgF1, 68.1 pHOTA, and 84.6 TETA. This indicates a significant gain of +32.9 cgF1, +19.6 pHOTA, and +19.1 TETA compared to the base SAM 3 model, underscoring the value of domain-specific data from SA-FARI for state-of-the-art models.

**Species-Agnostic Prompt Evaluation:**
This evaluation used the generic query "animal" to mimic common practice in camera trap detection literature, employing standard F1 and HOTA metrics (IDF1, HOTA, DetA, AssA).
- Compared to vision-only tracking methods using MegaDetector (MD) as the detector (MD+ByteTrack, MD+OCSort, MD+BoostSort++), SAM 3 (SA-FARI) (used with the "animal" prompt) exhibited a substantial lead.
- SAM 3 (SA-FARI) achieved 71.1 IDF1 and 83.7 HOTA, surpassing the closest competing vision-only model (MD+BoostSort++) by +23.9 IDF1 and outperforming MD+OCSort by +18.9 HOTA. This suggests SAM 3's robust generalization even when the generic "animal" query was not explicitly part of its SA-FARI training.

**SA-FARI Subset Evaluation:**
Performance of SAM 3 (SA-FARI) using species-specific prompting was analyzed across specific test subsets:
- **Masklet Size:** Samples with smaller masklets were observed to be notably more challenging for detection and tracking compared to larger masklets. Performance on large masks was +29.2 pHOTA higher than on small masks (81.4 vs. 52.2).
- **Challenging Subset (Occlusions and Motion):** Masklets with occlusions or significant motion demonstrated reduced detection and tracking accuracy. The pHOTA-Det metric was 4.1 points lower and pHOTA-Ass was 9.3 points lower than the overall test set.
- **Multiple Animals:** Videos containing multiple animals showed performance comparable to the average difficulty across the entire test set (67.8 pHOTA for multi-animal vs. 68.1 pHOTA overall). This outcome is attributed to a positive correlation between the presence of multiple animals and larger mask sizes, which partially mitigates the inherent tracking complexity of multiple individuals.
- **Nighttime Conditions:** Nighttime videos presented more challenging detection scenarios, leading to slightly lower scores (44.1 cgF1, 66.0 pHOTA) compared to the overall test set (46.9 cgF1, 68.1 pHOTA), although performance remained broadly comparable. The night subset constitutes a majority of the test videos.

These findings indicate that SA-FARI is a valuable resource that significantly improves the performance of advanced vision-language models in MAT. The subset analysis also highlights specific areas of challenge, such as tracking small or heavily occluded/moving animals, which can guide future model development and annotation strategies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SA-FARI dataset represents a contribution to the field of computer vision for wildlife conservation. Its significance lies in addressing several limitations present in previous multi-animal tracking (MAT) datasets. By consolidating over 11,000 camera trap videos from more than 700 sampling sites across four continents, SA-FARI offers a scale and diversity in terms of species (99 categories), geographical coverage, and temporal span (a decade) that surpasses existing resources. The provision of exhaustive, manually verified spatio-temporal segmentation masks, totaling over 16,000 high-quality masklets, fills a critical gap, as prior datasets often lacked this level of annotation precision or relied on less reliable automated methods.

This dataset provides a robust foundation for the development and evaluation of generalizable and robust MAT models. The benchmarking results demonstrate that incorporating SA-FARI into the training process of state-of-the-art vision-language models, such as SAM 3, leads to substantial performance gains, with fine-tuned models achieving a notable improvement over baselines. This indicates that SA-FARI directly contributes to advancing the capabilities of automated systems in a domain where real-world data presents significant challenges.

The potential impact of SA-FARI extends to broader ecological and conservation applications. Accurate and automated localization of individual animals in space and time is a prerequisite for a range of downstream tasks, including abundance estimation, occupancy modeling, behavior recognition, and health monitoring. By enabling the development of more effective and scalable MAT systems, SA-FARI can facilitate more efficient biodiversity monitoring and protection efforts globally. This can aid conservationists in obtaining timely and comprehensive data, which is crucial for informed decision-making and responsive management strategies in the face of ongoing biodiversity decline.

The detailed analysis of performance across various challenging subsets (e.g., small masklets, occlusions, nighttime) also provides insights for future research directions. These insights can guide targeted data collection efforts, refine annotation protocols for difficult scenarios, and inform the design of more robust computer vision models capable of handling the complexities of wild environments. Future extensions, such as integrating new modalities like animal body pose, depth information, natural language descriptions, and particularly leveraging the integrated audio streams for multi-modal model development, offer further avenues for enhancing detection, species classification, and tracking robustness. Prioritizing data integration from additional ecoregions is also identified as a way to broaden species representation and mitigate potential geographic biases, further enhancing the dataset's utility for global conservation challenges.
