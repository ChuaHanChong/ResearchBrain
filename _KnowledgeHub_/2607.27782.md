# RedFlow: Redirect Failure into Action-Level Corrections for Flow-matching VLA Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> The RedFlow framework converts robotic manipulation failures into precise action-level corrections for flow-matching Vision-Language-Action policies, enabling robust recovery and enhanced deployment reliability. It improved average success rates on the LIBERO benchmark by 12.0 percentage points and achieved comparable performance to on-policy reinforcement learning with an order of magnitude fewer training samples.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, especially those trained via imitation learning, are prone to compounding errors from distribution shifts in real-world deployment.
- Current offline reinforcement learning methods for VLA policies often neglect valuable diagnostic information from failures or provide only coarse, trajectory-level guidance.
- Extracting precise, action-level correction signals from pre-collected failure data without human intervention remains a challenge, limiting scalability.

## Method

- A fine-grained ==offline reinforcement learning== framework, **RedFlow**, is introduced to transform failure experiences into structured action-level corrections.
- ==Context-Aware Corrective Matching== estimates action-level advantages and derives precise corrective targets by clustering execution contexts and identifying high-quality actions in similar states.
- An ==Adaptive Redirection Objective== integrates these signals through quality-weighted attraction for successful actions, suppression for negative actions, and targeted correction towards desired alternatives.

## Results

- RedFlow boosted the average success rate on the LIBERO benchmark by **12.0 percentage points**, reaching **68.2%** and outperforming state-of-the-art offline RL baselines.
- The framework showcased high sample efficiency, achieving performance comparable to on-policy methods while requiring significantly fewer training trajectories (an order of magnitude reduction).
- Real-robot experiments demonstrated a **74.7%** average success rate across three complex manipulation tasks and enabled emergent recovery behaviors not seen in the base policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Failures in robotic manipulation contain rich, specific diagnostic information that can be leveraged for policy improvement if extracted at an action-level.
> - Decomposing coarse trajectory failures into action-level advantages and using context clustering can generate precise, empirical corrective targets.
> - An adaptive training objective that selectively attracts, suppresses, and corrects based on action quality is necessary for effective learning from mixed successful and failed experiences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, particularly those built upon flow matching, have emerged as a paradigm for generalist robotic manipulation. These policies are capable of modeling multimodal action distributions and integrating strategies derived from real-world data, often benefiting from large-scale vision-language backbones. However, a fundamental limitation of such policies, especially when trained via imitation learning (IL), is their susceptibility to compounding errors under distribution shifts during real-world deployment. Small prediction inaccuracies can accumulate, leading to unrecoverable failures as the robot encounters states outside its training distribution.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) offers a method to mitigate this issue by learning from interactions with the environment. Online RL approaches continuously collect and learn from new on-policy rollouts, enabling continuous improvement. However, their high interaction costs on physical robots restrict their scalability for real-world applications. Offline RL presents a more sample-efficient alternative, learning from pre-collected deployment rollouts without requiring further environment interaction.

Existing offline RL methods exhibit limitations in addressing deployment failures effectively. Classical offline RL techniques often focus on reweighting or imitating successful behaviors, thereby neglecting failure data and the diagnostic information it contains. Preference-based methods attempt to learn from failures by making coarse trajectory-level comparisons, signaling what behaviors to avoid but lacking specific action-level guidance on how to improve. Human-in-the-loop interventions can provide both failure awareness and action-level guidance, but their reliance on expert effort limits scalability. Consequently, a gap exists in the development of scalable mechanisms that can extract high-fidelity, action-level correction signals from pre-collected failure experiences without human intervention. This work positions itself to address this specific gap by proposing a method for fine-grained, action-level redirection of failure experiences for flow-matching VLA policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable and sample-efficient offline reinforcement learning framework that can effectively learn from both successful and failed robotic manipulation trajectories, specifically targeting flow-matching VLA policies. The overarching motivation is to enhance the robustness and recovery capabilities of these policies, enabling them to bridge the gap between training and real-world deployment where distribution shifts frequently lead to compounding errors.

The authors identify a central question: How can failure experiences be precisely redirected into action-level corrections that offer explicit supervisory targets for policy improvement, moving beyond mere repulsion from undesirable actions?

To address this, the research is motivated by two fundamental challenges inherent in leveraging failure data for policy improvement:

1. **Granularity Mismatch between Failure Labels and Policy Updates:** Trajectory-level failure labels provide coarse information, obscuring the specific, sparse action-level errors that precipitate a task collapse. Within a failed rollout, many intermediate actions may be reasonable, making it difficult to isolate the exact failure-inducing steps and derive precise corrective targets without extensive human supervision. The objective is to overcome this by pinpointing specific erroneous actions and identifying appropriate alternatives.

2. **Rigorous and Precise Integration of Corrective Signals into Learning:** Conventional flow-matching policies, typically trained via imitation learning, treat all provided actions as uniformly valid samples. This "uniform imitation" approach becomes problematic when dealing with mixed-quality data, as it can lead to the policy inadvertently learning suboptimal behaviors or suffering from over-correction. The motivation is to integrate corrective signals in a way that avoids these pitfalls, modulates training signals based on data quality, and aligns with the flow-matching velocity-field parameterization.

RedFlow is proposed as a solution to these challenges, aiming to transform failures into dense, structured supervision signals. This is intended to enable VLA policies to learn robust recovery behaviors from mixed-quality data, thereby improving deployment reliability and efficiency without requiring additional human demonstrations or costly online interactions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RedFlow is structured as a fine-grained offline RL framework designed to convert failure experiences into action-level correction signals for Flow-matching VLA policies. It comprises two main components: Context-Aware Corrective Matching and an Adaptive Redirection Objective.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

The problem is formulated as a language-conditioned Markov Decision Process (MDP). At each action-chunk step `t`, the agent receives an observation `o_t` (visual input `I_t` and proprioceptive state `q_t`) and a task instruction `l`. The policy `π_θ` predicts an action chunk `a_t` (K consecutive D-DoF commands). An episode yields a trajectory `τ` and a binary outcome label `y_τ ∈ {0, 1}`. The goal is to maximize the task return `J(θ)`, with local policy improvement governed by the advantage `A_π(o_t, a_t, l) = Q_π(o_t, a_t, l) - V_π(o_t, l)`. The VLA policy `π_θ` is instantiated as a conditional flow-matching model, learning a velocity field `v_θ(x_n, n, o_t, l)` that maps noise `x_1` to the action chunk `a_t = x_0`, pretrained on expert demonstrations `D_exp` using the standard flow-matching objective `L_FM`.

#### 3.2. Context-Aware Corrective Matching

This component addresses the granularity mismatch by estimating a proxy advantage for each action chunk and clustering execution contexts to derive corrective targets.

- **Action-level advantage estimation:**
 - A pretrained General Reward Model (GRM) `R(o_t, l) ∈` provides task-progress estimates.
 - Raw GRM scores are smoothed into `¯p_t` using a box filter of half-window size `W`.
 - A signed action-level score `ˆA_t` is computed as `¯p_{t+W} - ¯p_{t-W} + b · (2 · 1[y_τ=1] - 1)`, combining local progress change with a trajectory-level outcome bias `b`.
 - Chunks with `ˆA_t > 0` are labeled positive, and those with `ˆA_t < 0` are labeled negative.

- **Context clustering for corrective targets:**
 - An execution context for a chunk is defined using the task instruction, normalized proprioceptive state `˜q_t`, and smoothed progress `¯p_t`.
 - A `progress-state feature f_t = [˜q_t; β¯p_t]` is constructed, where `β` balances the scales of progress and state.
 - All action chunks in the offline buffer `D` are clustered using HDBSCAN over their `{f_t}` features, yielding clusters `{C_c}`.
 - For a negative chunk `a_t` belonging to a cluster `C_c` that contains a non-empty subset of positive chunks `C_c^+`, a corrective target `a_⋆_t` is computed. This target is a quality-weighted action centroid of the positive chunks in `C_c^+`, with weights `α_i = exp(ˆA_i/κ) / Σ_{j∈C_c^+} exp(ˆA_j/κ)`, where `κ` controls concentration towards high-advantage chunks.
 - `a_⋆_t` serves as an empirical positive barycenter, defining a local transport direction for probability mass away from failure modes.
 - A negative chunk is designated "uncorrectable" if it's an outlier or if its cluster `C_c` has no positive chunks, meaning it only undergoes suppression during training.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Redirection Objective

This objective precisely integrates the corrective signals, modulating the training based on the quality of matched guidance. It avoids uniform imitation by adapting the training signal at three complementary levels:

- **Quality-weighted attraction (L_att):** The estimated action-level score `ˆA_t` is converted into a soft weight `w_t = σ(ˆA_t / T_w) ∈ (0, 1)`, where `T_w` is a temperature. This weight modulates the standard flow-matching loss: `L_att = w_t · ||v_θ(x_n, n, o_t, l) - u_n||^2`. This term attracts the policy toward high-quality chunks while providing a weaker signal for low-quality ones.

- **Failure suppression (L_sup):** For low-quality chunks, a repulsive hinge loss is introduced: `L_sup = λ_sup (1 - w_t) max(0, m - e_t)`. Here, `λ_sup` controls suppression strength, `e_t = ||ˆx_0 - a_t||^2` is the reconstruction error, and `m` is an adaptive margin (a stop-gradient running average of reconstruction error). This term increases the distance between the predicted action and the negative chunk if they are closer than `m`, becoming inactive when sufficiently far.

- **Target-guided correction (L_cor):** For correctable failure chunks (`c_t=1`), an attractive correction term is added toward the cluster-derived target `a_⋆_t`: `L_cor = c_t · λ_cor (1 - w_t) ||ˆx_0 - a_⋆_t||^2`. Here, `λ_cor` controls correction strength. This provides an endpoint-level bias to move probability mass towards locally supported positive regions.

#### 3.4. Total Objective and Pipeline

The final training objective is `L = E[(L_att + L_sup + L_cor)]`. The overall RedFlow pipeline involves: (1) collecting a fixed rollout buffer with a pretrained flow-matching VLA policy, (2) deriving chunk-level advantages and corrective targets using the Context-Aware Corrective Matching, and (3) optimizing the policy on the frozen buffer using the Adaptive Redirection Objective. This process is a single-iteration offline procedure.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of RedFlow was evaluated through simulation experiments on the LIBERO benchmark and real-robot manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments (LIBERO Benchmark)

RedFlow was compared against state-of-the-art offline RL baselines, AWR and DPO, across four LIBERO suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long.
- RedFlow achieved the highest average success rate across all four suites, improving the average from 56.2% (Base Policy) to 68.2%. This represents a 12.0 percentage point increase over the base policy.
- RedFlow outperformed AWR by 5.9 points and DPO by 8.5 points on average.
- The most substantial gain was observed on LIBERO-Goal, where RedFlow reached 71.2% success, compared to 57.8% for AWR and 51.8% for DPO, indicating a benefit from explicit localization of failure-inducing chunks and redirection towards corrective targets.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted on three LIBERO suites (Spatial, Object, Goal) to assess the contribution of RedFlow's components:
- **Rollout Data Composition:** Removing failure rollouts (relying only on successes) reduced the average success rate to 68.0%. Conversely, removing successful rollouts (relying only on failures) further decreased it to 62.4%. These results indicate that both successful and failed rollouts provide complementary signals critical for optimal performance.
- **Context-Aware Corrective Matching:** Removing the "uncorrectable-failure separation" (indiscriminately assigning targets to all failure chunks) led to the largest performance drop, from 72.5% to 61.0% on average, with a 20.4-point loss on LIBERO-Goal. This highlights the importance of judiciously identifying and assigning corrective targets only to recoverable failures.
- **Adaptive Redirection Objective:** Removing the suppression term (`L_sup`) resulted in an average success rate of 68.8%. Removing the correction term (`L_cor`) yielded 69.1%. Removing both reduced the average to 65.7%. This demonstrates that `L_sup` and `L_cor` are complementary, with suppression preventing undesirable actions and correction guiding the policy towards alternatives.

#### 4.3. Sample Efficiency Analysis

A comparison with on-policy RL baselines (PPO, GRPO, DDPO) on LIBERO-Spatial demonstrated RedFlow's sample efficiency:
- RedFlow achieved 75.8% success using 1,536 offline trajectories and no additional environment interaction.
- To reach a comparable success level, PPO, GRPO, and DDPO required approximately 13,000, 16,000, and 24,000 rollout trajectories, respectively. This suggests that RedFlow's structured failure reuse can significantly reduce the need for expensive on-policy environment interactions, by approximately an order of magnitude.

#### 4.4. Real-Robot Results

Experiments on a dual-arm Agilex Cobot Magic robot for three manipulation tasks (clothes folding, object sweeping, table cleaning) further validated RedFlow:
- RedFlow achieved the highest average success rate of 74.7% across these tasks, outperforming all baselines on each individual task.
- A notable improvement was observed in clothes folding, where RedFlow improved the base policy's success rate from 36.0% to 67.0%, a 31.0 percentage point absolute increase. This result confirms the framework's transferability to complex bimanual manipulation scenarios.
- **Qualitative Recovery Analysis:** RedFlow-trained policies exhibited emergent recovery behaviors not present in the base policy. For instance, in clothes folding, when a T-shirt fell out of reach of one arm, the policy learned to use the opposite arm to retrieve it before resuming the folding task. This behavior emerged from offline buffer reuse, specifically from Context-Aware Corrective Matching retrieving relevant left-arm actions from similar progress–state contexts and the Adaptive Redirection Objective redirecting failed attempts toward these.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RedFlow framework presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, specifically concerning the robustness and learning efficiency of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies.

- **Addressing Compounding Errors:** The work provides a systematic method to mitigate compounding errors, a prevalent limitation in VLA policies trained with imitation learning. By effectively utilizing deployment failures, RedFlow enables policies to become more resilient to distribution shifts encountered in real-world scenarios.
- **Scalable and Sample-Efficient Offline Learning:** RedFlow introduces a scalable mechanism to extract high-fidelity action-level corrections from pre-collected failure experiences, circumventing the need for extensive human intervention or costly online interactions. Its ability to match the performance of strong on-policy RL baselines with approximately an order of magnitude fewer training samples highlights its significant advantage in sample efficiency, which is critical for real-robot learning.
- **Enhanced Policy Robustness and Recovery:** The framework's ability to turn both successes and failures into dense, structured supervision enables VLA policies to learn robust recovery behaviors. The demonstration of emergent corrective actions, such as using an alternative arm to retrieve an object, illustrates a key impact on policy intelligence and adaptability.
- **Structured Failure Reuse:** RedFlow establishes structured failure reuse as a viable and effective direction for post-training generalist VLA policies. This paradigm shifts the focus from merely avoiding failures to actively learning from them, transforming undesirable outcomes into valuable learning signals.
- **Real-World Applicability:** The consistent performance improvements observed across diverse real-robot manipulation tasks, including complex bimanual operations, affirm the practical applicability and transferability of RedFlow. This enhances the feasibility of deploying VLA policies in more dynamic and less constrained environments.

Future directions for this research include exploring uncertainty-aware target assignment for out-of-distribution states, extending the framework to longer-horizon tasks, and investigating iterative offline–online improvement schemes. These potential avenues could further expand the capabilities and impact of RedFlow in advancing generalist robotic manipulation.
