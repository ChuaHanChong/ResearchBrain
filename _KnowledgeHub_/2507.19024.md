# A Survey of Multimodal Hallucination Evaluation and Detection

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of hallucination evaluation and detection in multi-modal large language models, covering both image-to-text and text-to-image generation. It proposes a faithfulness-versus-factuality taxonomy, catalogues 38 evaluation benchmarks by discriminative, generative, and comprehensive task type, and reviews 29 detection methods split into black-box and white-box families, then maps open challenges and future directions.

## Problem

- Prior hallucination surveys concentrate almost exclusively on image-to-text models, leaving text-to-image hallucination and cross-modal comparison largely outside their scope.
- Existing categorizations miss benchmarks that mix discriminative and generative tasks, so inconsistent hallucination behavior across task formats stays unaddressed.
- Detection research is fragmented across modalities with no unified view, and benchmarks emphasize faithfulness while factuality and world-knowledge errors remain underexplored.

## Method

- A ==two-axis hallucination taxonomy==: ==faithfulness== split into ==object==, ==attribute==, and ==scene== levels, ==factuality== split into ==commonsense-based== and ==domain-specific==, applied uniformly to both generation directions.
- A ==benchmark classification== adding a third ==comprehensive== category beyond ==discriminative== and ==generative==, comparing data sources, size, hallucination type, and metric in one unified table.
- A ==detection taxonomy== separating ==black-box methods== by mechanism from ==white-box methods== using attention, feature, or logit access, plus a two-source cause analysis over data limitations and model mechanisms.

## Results

- Of **38** catalogued benchmarks, **27** target image-to-text and **11** text-to-image; discriminative benchmarks outnumber generative ones, and only **10** benchmarks test factuality at all.
- All **9** text-to-image and both unified detectors are black-box; white-box detection appears in only **5 of 29** methods, all image-to-text.
- Domain evidence shows grounding failures capping driving accuracy below **70 percent**, generic models under **54 percent** on remote-sensing questions, and truncated-SVD filtering lifting traffic captioning to **87 percent**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Benchmarks assembled from public datasets overlap model training data, so reported hallucination scores are systematically optimistic and poor predictors of deployment behavior.
> - Detection pipelines lean on external detectors, retrievers, and judge models, buying accuracy at the cost of deployment complexity, latency, and reproducibility.
> - White-box detection needs internal generation signals, which is why diffusion-based generation lags; static exam-style accuracy misses long-tail, interactive, safety-critical failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) represent an advanced paradigm for integrating and processing both visual and textual information. Models such as GPT-4o, Gemini, Qwen-VL (for Image-to-Text, I2T tasks), and Stable Diffusion, DALL-E (for Text-to-Image, T2I tasks) have demonstrated capabilities across a range of multimodal understanding and generation tasks. Despite these advancements, MLLMs continue to exhibit limitations, including vulnerability to distribution shifts, adversarial attacks, and the phenomenon of hallucination.

Hallucination in MLLMs refers to the generation of content that appears coherent and plausible but contradicts the provided input or established world knowledge. This issue poses a significant challenge to the reliability and trustworthiness of MLLMs in practical applications. Earlier research predominantly focused on hallucination within I2T models, specifically addressing inconsistencies between generated textual outputs and visual inputs. More recent efforts have extended this focus to T2I models, where hallucinations manifest as misalignments between generated visual outputs and textual prompts.

Existing survey papers on MLLM hallucinations have primarily concentrated on I2T tasks. This current survey distinguishes itself by broadening the scope to include hallucinations in T2I tasks and by providing a unified overview that encompasses both faithfulness and factuality aspects of hallucination across both modalities. It aims to offer a systematic review of existing evaluation benchmarks and detection methods, introducing a comprehensive categorization framework. By analyzing the complementary relationship between evaluation and detection, and highlighting efforts toward unified approaches, this work contributes to a more integrated understanding of hallucination phenomena in MLLMs within the evolving landscape of artificial intelligence research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this survey stems from the pervasive and challenging issue of hallucination in MLLMs. Despite their advanced capabilities in integrating visual and textual data for various understanding and generation tasks, MLLMs frequently produce outputs that, while plausible, are inconsistent with their inputs or factual world knowledge. This unreliability significantly impedes their safe and trustworthy deployment, particularly in critical applications.

The authors identify several specific motivations for conducting this comprehensive review:
- **Addressing a Gap in Existing Surveys:** Previous surveys on MLLM hallucinations predominantly focused on Image-to-Text (I2T) tasks, leaving a fragmented understanding of hallucinations in Text-to-Image (T2I) models and a lack of unified perspectives across both modalities. This survey aims to bridge this gap by offering an integrated analysis.
- **Establishing a Unified Definition and Taxonomy:** The varied manifestations of hallucination across different MLLM tasks necessitate a clear, consistent definitional framework. The authors are motivated to propose a taxonomy that categorizes hallucinations based on their nature (faithfulness vs. factuality) and granularity (object-level, attribute-level, scene-level), which can be universally applied to both I2T and T2I contexts.
- **Systematizing Evaluation Benchmarks:** The proliferation of hallucination evaluation benchmarks for both I2T and T2I tasks requires a systematic review to highlight their construction methodologies, evaluation objectives, and employed metrics. The motivation is to provide a structured comparison that identifies emerging trends and commonalities, as well as differences.
- **Summarizing Detection Methods:** Beyond benchmark-based evaluation, instance-level hallucination detection methods are crucial for practical application. The survey is motivated to summarize recent advancements in these detection methods, categorizing them (e.g., black-box vs. white-box) and discussing their feasibility for I2T and T2I models.
- **Identifying Limitations and Future Directions:** A critical objective is to analyze the shortcomings of current benchmarks and detection methods, such as issues related to data leakage, coverage of hallucination types, and the granularity of evaluation. This analysis is intended to inform and guide future research efforts toward more robust, scalable, and reliable MLLM development.

In summary, the key objective is to advance the understanding and mitigation of hallucination in MLLMs by providing an in-depth, systematic, and unified review of evaluation benchmarks and detection methods across both I2T and T2I tasks, thereby offering valuable guidance for future research and development in this critical area.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a structured and comprehensive methodology to analyze the landscape of multimodal hallucination. Their approach encompassed defining core concepts, developing a unified taxonomy, and systematically reviewing existing literature on evaluation benchmarks and detection methods.

**Definition of Models and Hallucination:**
The survey begins by formally defining the two primary categories of multimodal generative systems under consideration: I2T models and T2I models. For I2T models (e.g., LLaVA, Qwen-VL, GPT-4V), the architecture is described as typically comprising a visual encoder, an alignment adapter, and an LLM decoder. The discussion details how architectural constraints (e.g., resolution limits), adapter design (e.g., MLP-based vs. Q-former), the LLM decoder's linguistic capabilities (e.g., language priors, sycophancy, parametric knowledge interference), and training strategies (e.g., reliance on positive image-text pairs, RLHF/DPO) contribute to hallucinations. Similarly, for T2I models (e.g., Stable Diffusion 3, DALL·E 3), the components—text encoder (e.g., CLIP, T5), latent encoder (VAE), backbone (U-Net, Diffusion Transformers), and training paradigms (e.g., Rectified Flow Transformers, DPO, LoRA)—are analyzed in relation to their roles in hallucination generation.

Central to the methodology is a unified definition of hallucination, applicable to both I2T and T2I tasks: "any inconsistency between the generated content and either the input conditions or established world knowledge." This definition aligns with the psychological concept of perception without external stimulus that contradicts reality. Based on this, a novel taxonomy of hallucination is proposed:
- **Faithfulness Hallucination:** Inconsistencies between model outputs and user inputs or prior output. This is further categorized by visual content granularity:
 - *Object-level:* Errors in basic object information (type, number, localization).
 - *Attribute-level:* Errors in fine-grained intra-object content (color, shape).
 - *Scene-level:* Inconsistencies in global visual inputs or inter-object information (relationships, events, scene context).
- **Factuality Hallucination:** Contradictions between model outputs and established world knowledge. This is categorized by knowledge source:
 - *Commonsense-based:* Violations of general, universally accepted knowledge (landmarks, celebrities).
 - *Domain-specific:* Inconsistencies with factual information in specialized domains (medical, physical laws).

**Review of Hallucination Evaluation Benchmarks:**
The survey systematically reviews existing benchmarks for I2T and T2I models.
- **I2T Benchmarks:** These are categorized into discriminative (Yes/No questions, Multiple-Choice questions), generative (Image Captioning, Visual Question Answering), and comprehensive benchmarks. The review analyzes their data sources (e.g., MSCOCO, Visual Genome), construction processes (manual, LLM-assisted, synthetic), evaluation objectives (object, attribute, relationship, factuality), and employed metrics (e.g., Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 Score, CHAIR, GPT Rate, FaithScore). Trends such as the shift to automated construction, fine-grained diagnosis, and mitigation of "Yes-bias" are highlighted.
- **T2I Benchmarks:** These are predominantly generative, assessing image generation correctness. The review covers benchmarks focusing on spatial hallucination (SR^2D), color hallucination (ABC-6K & CC-500), compositional fidelity (T2I-CompBench), and factuality (T2I-FactualBench, WISE). Evaluation methods range from human evaluation to automated approaches using object detection, VQA models, and LLM-based scoring.

**Review of Hallucination Detection Methods:**
The authors classify detection methods based on their access to model internals:
- **I2T Detection Methods:**
 - *Black-box:* Rely on external tools without access to VLM internals. Subcategories include external model-based (LLM, VQA, caption models), word matching, uncertainty-based, and similarity-based methods.
 - *White-box:* Utilize internal signals for detection. Subcategories include attention-based, feature-based, and logits-based methods, offering insights into the model's generation process.
- **T2I Detection Methods:** Primarily black-box, leveraging external tools like object detectors, VQA models, and caption models to verify image-text consistency.
- **Unified Detection Methods:** Explores emerging approaches that aim to provide cross-modal hallucination detection for both I2T and T2I tasks, often leveraging question decomposition and graph-based reasoning (e.g., UNIHD, CSN).

**Comparative Analysis and Identification of Limitations:**
Throughout the review, the authors conduct comparative analyses, summarizing key details of benchmarks and detection methods in tables to highlight their strengths, limitations, and emerging trends. This includes discussing data leakage, computational costs, and the granularity of detection. The paper also delves into the underlying causes of hallucinations in both I2T and T2I models, linking them to data limitations (quantity, noise, bias) and model-specific mechanisms (architecture, attention, cross-modal misalignment).

Finally, the methodology concludes with a dedicated section on challenges and future directions, synthesizing the identified limitations and proposing pathways for future research to address them.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey presents a structured analysis of hallucination in MLLMs, yielding several main findings and highlighting key trends and limitations in current research.

**Unified Hallucination Taxonomy:**
A central finding is the development and application of a unified taxonomy that categorizes hallucinations across both I2T and T2I tasks. Hallucinations are divided into:
- **Faithfulness Hallucinations:** Inconsistencies with input data. This is further granularized into object-level (e.g., misidentified objects, incorrect numbers), attribute-level (e.g., wrong colors, shapes), and scene-level (e.g., incorrect relationships, events, scene context).
- **Factuality Hallucinations:** Contradictions with established world knowledge. This includes commonsense-based (e.g., incorrect landmarks, celebrity features) and domain-specific hallucinations (e.g., medical plausibility, physical laws). This taxonomy provides a consistent framework for understanding diverse hallucination types.

**I2T Hallucination Benchmarks:**
- **Evolution:** Benchmarks have progressed from basic object existence evaluations (e.g., POPE) to include attribute, relationship, and some factuality hallucinations (e.g., MME, HallusionBench).
- **Construction Trends:** There is a notable shift towards automated benchmark construction, increasingly leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate questions (e.g., CIEM, RAH-Bench) and synthetic images (e.g., AutoHallusion, PhD, VHtest, ODE using T2I models). This addresses the scalability and data leakage concerns.
- **Evaluation Paradigms:** Benchmarks are categorized into discriminative (YNQs, MCQs, more common due to simplified evaluation), generative (open-ended captions, VQA), and comprehensive (combining both).
- **Limitations:** Factuality hallucinations, particularly those requiring specialized domain knowledge, remain underexplored. Most benchmarks focus on single-turn tasks, limiting assessment of complex interactions.
- **Root Causes:** I2T hallucinations are attributed to data limitations (insufficient, noisy, or biased data, including a "Yes-bias" and lack of negative samples) and model-specific mechanisms (architectural constraints like low-resolution visual encoders, attention misallocation, cross-modal misalignment leading to over-reliance on language priors or internal parametric knowledge).

**T2I Hallucination Benchmarks:**
- **Scope Expansion:** Benchmarks have expanded from evaluating image-text alignment for basic properties like color and spatial relationships (e.g., SR^2D, ABC-6K) to include compositional fidelity (T2I-CompBench) and adherence to world knowledge (e.g., T2I-FactualBench, WISE, I-HallA v1.0).
- **Automated Evaluation:** The use of VLMs for fine-grained evaluation (e.g., VQA models in TIFA v1.0, VQAScore in GenAI-Bench) is becoming common, moving away from sole reliance on human annotation or coarse image quality metrics.
- **Limitations:** Similar to I2T, domain-specific factuality hallucinations are underrepresented. Many evaluations still rely on coarse-grained VLM scoring, lacking detailed insights into factual correctness or physical plausibility.
- **Root Causes:** T2I hallucinations are linked to data limitations (e.g., underrepresentation of negative prompts, gaps in compositional data, object co-occurrence bias) and model architecture/attention issues (e.g., text encoder semantic bottlenecks leading to "bag-of-words" comprehension, U-Net backbones causing structural inconsistencies, diffusion dynamics failing to separate concepts, and cross-attention failures like attribute bleeding).

**Relationship Between I2T and T2I Benchmarks:**
There is a growing synergy, with I2T benchmarks utilizing T2I models for synthetic data generation and T2I benchmarks employing VLMs for evaluation. Both fields exhibit similar trends in broadening hallucination categories, increasing automation, and moving towards fine-grained analysis. However, a fully unified, comprehensive evaluation capturing the complexity of real-world scenarios and factual content across both modalities remains a challenge.

**Hallucinations in Key Domains:**
MLLMs demonstrate significant reliability issues in safety-critical domains (e.g., embodied AI, medical imaging, remote sensing, agriculture). Hallucinations in these contexts can lead to severe consequences, such as unsafe autonomous actions or misdiagnoses. Generic mitigation strategies are often insufficient, necessitating domain-specific interventions, tailored datasets (e.g., MedHallTune), uncertainty quantification, and iterative reasoning frameworks.

**Hallucination Detection Methods:**
- **I2T Detection:**
 - *Black-box methods:* Predominantly rely on external LLMs, VQA, or caption models (e.g., FaithScore, GAVIE, M-HalDetect) or use word matching, uncertainty, and similarity metrics. These are generally deployable but limited by auxiliary model accuracy.
 - *White-box methods:* Leverage internal model signals like attention patterns (e.g., DHCP, OPERA), feature representations (e.g., HallE-Control), or logits (e.g., PROJECTAWAY, ContextualLens). These offer interpretability and trace origins but are often limited to coarse granularity and single-step analysis.
- **T2I Detection:** Primarily black-box, utilizing object detectors (e.g., SR^2D, DALL-Eval), VQA models (e.g., TIFA, T2I-CompBench), or caption models (e.g., LLMScore) to verify image-text consistency.
- **Unified Detection:** Emerging approaches (e.g., UNIHD, CSN) aim for cross-modal detection by assessing image-text alignment through question decomposition and graph-based reasoning, often tool-assisted.
- **Limitations:** Most detection efforts focus on faithfulness, with factuality detection being underexplored. Black-box methods are constrained by external tool accuracy, while white-box methods require further development for fine-grained, dynamic reasoning trajectory monitoring. For T2I, detection methods are primarily semantic, often missing physical or cultural inconsistencies and internal generation issues.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey significantly contributes to the field of multimodal large language models by providing a detailed and structured synthesis of research on hallucination. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Comprehensive Framework for Understanding Hallucination:** The survey’s key contribution is its unified taxonomy of hallucination, categorizing it into faithfulness and factuality, further broken down by granularity. This consistent framework applies across both I2T and T2I tasks, offering a clearer conceptualization of the problem than previously available. This foundational understanding can help researchers and practitioners to precisely identify, characterize, and discuss different types of MLLM errors.
- **Identification of Research Gaps and Limitations:** By systematically reviewing existing benchmarks and detection methods, the survey rigorously identifies critical shortcomings. These include the underrepresentation of domain-specific factuality hallucinations, the reliance on single-turn evaluation formats, challenges with data leakage in benchmarks, and the limitations of current detection methods (e.g., dependency on external models for black-box approaches, and insufficient granularity for white-box methods). Pinpointing these gaps provides a clear roadmap for future research efforts.
- **Guidance for Future Research and Development:** The explicit outlining of challenges and proposed future directions serves as a valuable guide for researchers. Suggestions such as developing scalable and comprehensive evaluation, enhancing explainability, prioritizing domain-specific factuality evaluation, advancing unified detection frameworks, and shifting towards real-world scenario evaluation offer concrete avenues for advancing the field. This guidance can accelerate progress towards more robust and reliable MLLMs.
- **Enhancing MLLM Reliability and Trustworthiness:** Addressing hallucination is paramount for the safe and ethical deployment of MLLMs, particularly in high-stakes domains like embodied AI, medical diagnostics, and autonomous systems. By clarifying the nature of hallucinations, evaluating current mitigation strategies, and suggesting improvements, the survey directly supports the development of more trustworthy MLLMs whose outputs can be relied upon in critical applications.
- **Fostering Cross-Modal Synergy:** The unified treatment of I2T and T2I hallucination evaluation and detection encourages researchers to identify common challenges and leverage insights and techniques from one modality to improve the other. For instance, using T2I models to generate synthetic hallucination data for I2T benchmarks, or employing VLMs for T2I evaluation, highlights areas for mutual enhancement and integrated research.

In conclusion, this survey provides a foundational reference for the MLLM community. By offering a comprehensive analysis of the current state, identifying critical limitations, and proposing clear directions, it is poised to significantly impact the development of more robust, transparent, and reliable multimodal AI systems.
