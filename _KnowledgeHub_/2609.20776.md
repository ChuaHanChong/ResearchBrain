# GeoAAC: Geometry-Based Adaptive Action Chunking from Denoising Trajectories in VLA Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> GeoAAC is a training-free adaptive action chunking method for flow-matching VLA policies. It reads the geometry of the denoising trajectory, measuring how intermediate velocity predictions change for each action prefix, builds a temporally weighted horizon-wise reliability profile, and cuts execution at the median of its cumulative positive growth. It beats fixed horizons by up to 8.7 points in simulation and lifts real-world success from 53.3% to 74.4%.

## Problem

- Most VLA policies execute a fixed action horizon, yet task stages differ in needed continuity, precision and feedback, so any single horizon fails at some stage.
- Learned adaptive-horizon methods train extra horizon predictors or horizon-specific policies, demanding additional data, supervision, or optimization beyond the base policy.
- Training-free multi-sample uncertainty proxies conflate multimodality with unreliability: disagreement among sampled action chunks may reflect distinct valid action modes rather than an unreliable prediction.

## Method

- ==Prefix-wise denoising geometry==: from a single Flow Matching generation, the step-normalized change between consecutive velocity predictions restricted to each action prefix, normalized by velocity magnitude, gives a per-prefix ==variation score==.
- ==Temporal correction==: a mean-normalized weight s(1-s)^2 over denoising progress emphasizes intermediate transitions and suppresses unreliable late-stage variation, yielding a horizon-wise ==geometric profile== across all candidate horizons.
- ==Cumulative-growth boundary selection==: positive relative growth between adjacent prefixes forms a normalized cumulative distribution; execution stops at its interpolated median, floored by a ==motion-aware minimum horizon== for small predicted motions.

## Results

- LIBERO: GR00T N1.5 reaches **95.5%** average versus **94.7%** best fixed horizon, **94.6%** multi-sampling, **93.8%** self-attention; π0.5 reaches **98.0%** versus **97.1%**, with Long suite **93.2%** to **96.4%**.
- RoboCasa365 (18 tasks, GR00T N1.5): **75.1%** versus **66.4%** best fixed, **71.1%** multi-sampling, **68.9%** self-attention; LIBERO-Pro position shifts with π0.5: **36.2%** versus **30.9%** fixed, **35.2%** multi-sampling.
- Real dual-arm robot, same checkpoint, 30 trials per task: average success rises **53.3%** to **74.4%** over fixed-50; horizons adapt per primitive, **7.77** for placement versus **10.52** for turning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The generative process itself carries uncertainty: intermediate velocity changes during flow-matching denoising give a free, single-pass reliability signal, avoiding multi-sample ensembles that confuse multimodality with doubt.
> - Not all denoising steps are equally informative: late-stage variation is largest but least correlated with uncertainty, so weighting intermediate stages matters when using denoising trajectories as uncertainty signals.
> - Execution horizon is a free deployment knob, but real-world gains are measured only against full 50-step open-loop execution, not a tuned fixed horizon or adaptive baseline.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies combine visual perception, language conditioning, and robot control. Recent VLA systems increasingly generate action chunks: instead of predicting one action at a time, the policy produces a sequence of future motor commands and executes several of them before observing the environment again. This approach can produce smoother and more temporally coherent behavior, reduce the frequency of policy inference, and support longer-horizon manipulation.

The main design variable in action chunking is the action horizon, namely the number of predicted actions executed before replanning. Existing systems commonly use a fixed horizon. A short horizon gives the robot frequent visual feedback and enables rapid corrections, but increases inference frequency and can interrupt otherwise continuous motions. A long horizon improves motion continuity and may reduce computational overhead, but it increases open-loop execution and can allow errors to persist.

Recent research has therefore investigated adaptive action chunking. Some methods train a separate horizon predictor or multiple horizon-specific policies. These approaches can learn task-dependent execution lengths, but require additional data, supervision, or optimization. Training-free alternatives estimate uncertainty from inference-time signals. For example, multi-sampling methods compare several stochastic action predictions, while other methods use internal attention or policy features to identify when a prediction may be unreliable.

GeoAAC occupies this training-free adaptive-action-chunking category, but uses a different signal. Rather than comparing multiple final action predictions, it analyzes the geometry of the denoising trajectory generated inside a Flow Matching action head. The authors argue that this process-level information can distinguish uncertainty from ordinary multimodality in the action distribution. The method is designed specifically for flow-based VLA policies and does not require policy retraining.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to select an execution horizon dynamically from a single action generation. The authors seek to address three related problems.

First, the appropriate action horizon changes throughout a task. For example, a robot may benefit from long chunks while transporting an object through free space, but require short chunks when aligning a gripper, grasping a handle, or placing an object. A fixed horizon cannot respond to these changes within the same rollout.

Second, prediction reliability can vary across the predicted action sequence. Early actions in a chunk may be reliable, while later actions become increasingly uncertain because they depend on future states that have not yet been observed. Executing the entire chunk open loop can therefore amplify errors.

Third, the authors question whether output-level disagreement is always an appropriate uncertainty measure. VLA policies may produce multiple plausible action modes for the same observation. Differences between sampled action sequences can reflect valid behavioral alternatives rather than a failure of the current prediction. GeoAAC instead examines how the policy’s internal velocity field changes during generation. Large changes in the denoising dynamics are treated as evidence that the prediction is being substantially revised.

The paper’s specific research hypothesis is that geometric variation in Flow Matching denoising trajectories remains correlated with predictive uncertainty when calculated separately for different action prefixes. If this relationship holds, the geometry can be used to infer which portion of a generated action chunk is sufficiently reliable to execute.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Flow Matching Preliminaries

In a Flow Matching action head, an initial noise sample is progressively transported toward a conditional robot action sequence. The action state \(x_\tau\) evolves over flow time according to a learned velocity field:

\[
\frac{dx_\tau}{d\tau}=v_\theta(x_\tau,\tau\mid o),
\]

where \(o\) is the current observation. Numerical integration produces an action chunk \(A=(a_1,\ldots,a_H)\), with \(H\) possible action steps. During this process, the model evaluates intermediate velocity predictions at several denoising steps.

For every prefix length \(k\), the method considers only the first \(k\) actions and the corresponding prefix of each intermediate velocity vector. Each prefix therefore represents a candidate execution horizon.

#### 3.2. Prefix-Wise Geometric Variation

GeoAAC measures local changes between consecutive velocity predictions. For prefix \(k\), the change between denoising steps is normalized by the interval between flow-time samples. The resulting quantity captures how much the predicted velocity field changes as generation proceeds.

The local variations are aggregated over denoising steps and normalized by the overall velocity magnitude of the prefix. This normalization is intended to reduce sensitivity to the absolute scale of robot motion and focus on relative geometric variation.

The authors first test an uncorrected geometric measure against a reference uncertainty measure. They collect 200 observations from the LIBERO suites using GR00T N1.5, generate 32 stochastic action chunks per observation, and use leave-one-out prefix variance as the reference predictive uncertainty. Spearman correlations remain positive across action-prefix lengths, with the reported correlations generally exceeding 0.5. This supports the proposed relationship between trajectory geometry and prefix-level uncertainty.

#### 3.3. Temporal Correction

The reliability of geometric variation is not uniform across the denoising process. The paper reports that local geometric variation tends to increase toward later denoising stages, while its correlation with reference uncertainty declines after intermediate stages. Consequently, simply summing all geometric changes would overemphasize late-stage variations that may be less informative.

GeoAAC introduces a temporal weighting function that emphasizes intermediate denoising stages and downweights the beginning and end of the process. The late-stage contribution is suppressed more strongly through a quadratic decay term. The weights are mean-normalized so that temporal correction changes the relative contribution of stages without changing the overall scale substantially.

The temporally corrected values are calculated for all prefixes, producing a horizon-wise geometric profile:

\[
\widetilde{U}=(\widetilde{U}_1,\widetilde{U}_2,\ldots,\widetilde{U}_H).
\]

This profile describes how the estimated reliability-related geometric variation changes as the candidate action horizon expands.

#### 3.4. Adaptive Boundary Selection

Because the absolute scale of the geometric profile can vary across observations and tasks, GeoAAC does not use a fixed threshold. Instead, it computes relative growth between adjacent prefixes:

\[
r_k=\frac{\widetilde{U}_k-\widetilde{U}_{k-1}}
{|\widetilde{U}_{k-1}|+\epsilon}.
\]

Only positive growth is retained. A positive value indicates that extending the action prefix increases geometric variation, whereas negative growth is treated as providing no additional evidence for truncation.

The positive growth values are accumulated across the horizon and normalized into a cumulative distribution. The execution boundary is selected at the median, where 50% of the total positive growth has been accumulated. This procedure is intended to avoid decisions being dominated by one isolated peak. If there is no positive growth, the method executes the full predicted horizon.

GeoAAC also applies a motion-aware lower bound. When the predicted action chunk has small overall motion, executing only a very short segment may produce little physical progress while still requiring another inference. The method therefore increases the minimum allowed horizon for low-motion chunks and permits shorter horizons when the predicted motion magnitude is larger. The final horizon is the maximum of the geometry-based boundary and the motion-based lower bound.

The complete procedure operates on the intermediate outputs of one Flow Matching generation. It does not require additional training, multiple sampled action chunks, or a separate horizon-prediction network.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. LIBERO

The authors evaluate GeoAAC with GR00T N1.5 using a prediction horizon of 16 and with \(\pi^{0.5}\) using a prediction horizon of 10.

On LIBERO, GR00T N1.5 with GeoAAC achieves an average success rate of 95.5%. The strongest fixed-horizon result is 94.7% with horizon 8, while multi-sampling and self-attention adaptive methods reach 94.6% and 93.8%, respectively. GeoAAC obtains suite-level results of 96.4% on Spatial, 89.2% on Long, 99.0% on Object, and 97.2% on Goal.

With \(\pi^{0.5}\), GeoAAC reaches an average of 98.0%, compared with 97.1% for the fixed horizon of 5 and 97.2% for multi-sampling. The largest suite-level difference appears on the Long suite, where GeoAAC achieves 96.4% versus 93.2% for the fixed-horizon baseline.

The selected horizons also vary by action primitive. Alignment and placement/release receive relatively short average horizons of 7.94 and 7.77 actions. Transport, push/pull, and turning receive longer averages of 9.56, 9.19, and 10.52 actions, respectively. This pattern is consistent with the proposed motivation: precision-sensitive stages receive more frequent replanning, while continuous motions are executed in longer segments.

#### 4.2. LIBERO-Pro

LIBERO-Pro tests robustness to object-position shifts. Across Shift-0.2, Shift-0.3, and Shift-0.4 settings, GeoAAC obtains an average success rate of 36.2%. The fixed-horizon baseline reaches 30.9%, while self-attention and multi-sampling achieve 33.8% and 35.2%.

GeoAAC performs at 58.5% under Shift-0.2 and 37.6% under Shift-0.3. Under the strongest Shift-0.4 condition, its 12.4% result is slightly below multi-sampling’s 12.8%, indicating that the method does not dominate every individual condition. Overall, the results suggest that adaptive horizon selection can provide some robustness under spatial distribution changes.

#### 4.3. RoboCasa365

On 18 household manipulation tasks in RoboCasa365, GeoAAC achieves an overall success rate of 75.1%. The strongest fixed-horizon comparison is 66.4%, corresponding to an absolute improvement of 8.7 percentage points. Multi-sampling and self-attention reach 71.1% and 68.9%.

GeoAAC matches or exceeds the best compared method on 9 of the 18 tasks. Its gains are particularly visible on tasks such as OpenDrawer, where it achieves 94.0% compared with 76.0% for fixed-12 and 82.0% for fixed-16. Performance remains task-dependent; on some tasks, including CloseBlenderLid and CloseFridge, another method obtains a higher individual success rate.

#### 4.4. Real-World Manipulation

The real-world experiments use a dual-arm system with two PiperX arms and three Intel RealSense D435i cameras. The policy predicts 50-step chunks at a control frequency of 50 Hz. The tasks are alcohol-lamp extinguishing, rubber-tube disconnection, and test-tube uncapping. Each task uses 50 teleoperated demonstrations for training, and each method is evaluated over 30 trials per task.

Using the same policy checkpoint, Fixed-50 achieves an average success rate of 53.3%, while GeoAAC reaches 74.4%, an absolute increase of 21.1 percentage points. Task-level results are 36.7% versus 16.7% for extinguishing, 100.0% versus 76.7% for tube disconnection, and 86.7% versus 66.7% for uncapping. The qualitative analysis attributes improvements to more frequent observation and correction during grasping, alignment, and uncapping stages.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GeoAAC contributes a mechanism for adapting action execution without modifying the underlying VLA policy. Its main conceptual contribution is to treat the denoising process itself as a source of reliability information. This differs from methods based solely on final-action disagreement and may be useful when action distributions are multimodal.

The practical value of the method is that it can improve the balance between motion continuity and feedback responsiveness within a single task. The reported simulation and real-world results indicate that this can affect both benchmark success and performance under spatial shifts.

Several limitations define the scope of the findings. GeoAAC depends on access to intermediate Flow Matching velocity predictions and therefore may not transfer directly to autoregressive, discrete-action, or non-flow policies. The real-world evaluation covers three tasks and one policy architecture, so broader physical validation is needed. The method also includes fixed parameters for the motion lower bound and a hand-designed temporal weighting function. Additional studies could examine the sensitivity of these choices, computational overhead, and performance across different control frequencies and embodiments.

Overall, the paper presents adaptive horizon selection as an inference-time control problem and shows that geometric information already available within flow-based VLA generation can be used for that purpose. Its potential impact lies in improving closed-loop execution while preserving the efficiency benefits of action chunking, particularly in tasks that alternate between continuous motion and precision-sensitive interaction.
