# IMPASTO: Integrating Model-Based Planning with Learned Dynamics Models for Robotic Oil Painting Reproduction

> [!summary] Summary
> IMPASTO is a robotic system that reproduces oil paintings by integrating a learned neural pixel dynamics model with model-based planning, trained solely on robot self-play. The system accurately replicates individual human brushstrokes and complex multi-stroke paintings from static images, outperforming baseline methods.

## Problem

- Robotic oil painting reproduction involves complex low-level control of deformable tools (soft brushes) and fluid pigments, requiring precise force-sensitive interaction.
- Challenges include real-time visual perception of the evolving artwork and high-level planning of multi-step stroke sequences to match a target painting.
- The domain typically lacks extensive human action demonstration data or highly accurate physical simulators, making learning brushstroke dynamics difficult.

## Method

- A differentiable forward pixel dynamics model, **IMPASTO**-UNet, was developed using a U-Net-style architecture to predict the visual consequence of brush actions given the current canvas state.
- This dynamics model was trained through robot self-supervision by exploring the brushstroke parameter space and collecting a dataset of (current state, action, next state) tuples.
- A receding-horizon ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) strategy, using MPPI, integrates the learned dynamics model to plan optimal sequences of brushstroke actions (trajectory, orientation, force, color) for multi-step painting reproduction.

## Results

- The learned pixel dynamics model achieved an Lℓ1 error of **0.0285** with 900 self-play samples, demonstrating its fidelity in predicting canvas state changes.
- IMPASTO-UNet reduced LPIPS by an average of **15.68%** to **35.36%** and execution Lℓ1 loss by **5.33%** to **21.21%** compared to baselines when reproducing individual human brushstrokes.
- In closed-loop, multi-step painting tasks, IMPASTO-UNet reduced planning Lℓ1 loss by **24.01%** and execution Lℓ1 loss by **10.55%** against baselines, successfully approximating complex human oil paintings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A robust pixel dynamics model for complex brush-canvas interactions can be effectively learned through robot self-play, circumventing the need for human demonstrations or detailed simulators.
> - Integrating such a learned, data-driven dynamics model into a model-based planning framework (MPC) enables a robot to infer and execute nuanced artistic control actions for complex reproduction tasks.
> - Precise parameterization of brushstrokes (including force and bend) combined with real-time visual feedback and force-sensitive control is critical for achieving high-fidelity oil painting reproduction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot painting encompasses automating artistic creation and reproduction, a pursuit with historical roots and evolving technical challenges. Early efforts, such as Harold Cohen's AARON in 1986, largely relied on open-loop, programmed approaches. More recently, systems like e-David and the FRIDA series have introduced closed-loop control, integrating visual feedback and differentiable simulations for stroke planning. While FRIDA aimed for collaborative painting and generating human-like results, its primary objective was not the exact reproduction of existing artworks.

Modern robotic painting increasingly leverages machine learning techniques. Researchers have explored reinforcement learning for optimizing paint coverage and stroke planning, genetic algorithms for stroke sequence generation, imitation learning from human demonstrations, and deep generative models for synthesizing and stylizing brushstrokes. These methods contribute to moving robotic art beyond predefined programming towards more autonomous capabilities.

Concurrent to developments in robot painting, the broader field of robotics has seen significant progress in planning with learned neural dynamics models. Deep neural networks trained on interaction data have become a common approach for modeling manipulation tasks, with models operating either directly in pixel space or in compact latent representations. Structured scene encodings like keypoints, particles, and meshes have further enhanced modeling fidelity and generalization. Planning over these learned dynamics, however, remains challenging due to their nonlinearity and nonconvexity, often addressed through online sampling-based optimization methods such such as cross-entropy methods (CEM) and Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), or gradient-based trajectory optimization.

This work situates itself within this landscape by addressing the specific and challenging problem of robotic oil painting reproduction. Unlike many prior works, it focuses on accurately replicating human expert brushstrokes using soft brushes and wet pigments. A key distinction from existing machine learning approaches is its stated independence from relying on painting simulators (as in some RL settings) or human action demonstrations (as in IL settings). Instead, it proposes to integrate learned neural dynamics models with model-based planning, a combination that the authors indicate has not been previously applied to robot painting. This approach aims to provide a robot with the ability to infer and execute nuanced stroke actions solely from target painting images, approximating the capabilities of an art student attempting to replicate a piece.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether a robot can infer and execute the necessary control actions—including trajectory, orientation, and applied force—to reproduce an oil painting, given only a sequence of static images of an expert artist's work. This problem is framed as replicating the level of a beginning art student who can copy an existing painting.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from the inherent complexities and unique technical challenges associated with robotic oil painting reproduction. These challenges include:

- **Low-level Control of Deformable Tools and Fluids:** Oil painting requires precise manipulation of soft brushes and fluid pigments, demanding fine-grained control that differs from manipulating rigid objects.
- **Force-Sensitive Interaction:** The robot must accurately control the force applied by the brush against the canvas surface to achieve specific stroke characteristics, such as thickness and paint deposition.
- **Visual Perception of Evolving Artwork:** The robot needs to continuously perceive and assess the state of the canvas as it changes with each stroke, using this feedback to guide subsequent actions.
- **High-level Planning of Stroke Sequences:** Beyond individual stroke execution, the robot must develop and execute multi-step plans to compose multiple strokes into a coherent and accurate reproduction of the target painting.
- **Absence of Action Demonstration Data or Faithful Simulators:** A significant challenge in this domain is the typical lack of human step-by-step demonstrations (e.g., through teleoperation or brush tracking) or highly accurate simulators that can reliably predict the physical effects of brushstrokes. This necessitates a learning approach that can operate with limited prior knowledge of the painting dynamics.

To address these challenges, the research proposes a specific methodological approach:
1. **Learning a 2D Dynamics Model:** The robot learns a forward dynamics model that predicts the visual consequence (i.e., the resulting state of the canvas) of its brush actions, given the current visual state. This model is intended to be trained through robot self-play.
2. **Integrating with Model Predictive Control (MPC):** Once trained, this dynamics model is integrated into an MPC framework. This allows the robot to infer optimal actions within local stroke windows and plan sequences of strokes to approximate a complex painting.

The work aims to make several contributions:
- Developing a neural network-based pixel dynamics model for brushstrokes, trained exclusively on robot self-play data, capable of predicting stroke shape and appearance from action parameters.
- Integrating this learned model into a model-based planning framework (MPC) for optimizing trajectories and forces for single or multiple consecutive strokes, given a desired visual outcome.
- Implementing a comprehensive robotic painting system on a 7-DoF robotic arm, equipped with a force-torque sensor, that automates processes such as brush dipping, cleaning, and stroke execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The IMPASTO system integrates a learned neural pixel dynamics model with model-based planning and force-sensitive robot control to approximate human oil paintings. The methodology encompasses brushstroke parameterization, color estimation, hardware setup, the dynamics model architecture and training, and a multi-step planning algorithm.

**Brushstroke Model:**
Each brushstroke is parameterized by six values: a starting location `p0 = (x0, y0)`, a length `l`, a scalar bend `b` for curvature, an in-plane orientation `α`, a normal force `F` to modulate thickness and width, and a paint color `C = (R, G, B, A)`. These geometric parameters (p0, l, b, α, F) define a quadratic Bézier curve, which is then rasterized with a width function `w(F)` calibrated to force and composited onto the canvas with the specified color. This parameterization allows for representing curved strokes with precise force control.

**Color Prediction:**
Color estimation is separated from shape prediction. A nearest-neighbor search is performed over a database of pigment patches. Within a stroke-difference mask (identifying areas where the stroke changed the canvas), a representative feature is extracted and compared to color prototypes using linear-RGB with Mahalanobis distance to identify the closest color. Transparency is determined by a ratio test comparing observed change magnitude to the selected color's contrast.

**Hardware System and Robot Control:**
The system utilizes a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda robotic arm equipped with a 6-axis force/torque sensor mounted at its end-effector. A round synthetic-filament brush, trimmed to 5 cm, is rigidly attached and held perpendicular to the canvas. Robot control is managed via the Franka Control Interface in impedance mode. A force loop along the surface normal implements force-sensitive control. At the start of a stroke, an outer normal-force admittance loop accumulates a smoothed feedforward force based on the desired force and measured normal force. Once the target contact force is established, the controller transitions to impedance mode, maintaining the vertical position for the remainder of the stroke. The canvas is an 18 × 21 inch board. Premixed pigments are provided in palette trays, and a motorized water spinner cleans the brush between colors. A top-down Intel RealSense D435 camera captures RGB observations of the canvas, which are then undistorted, homography-warped, and center-cropped to 100x100 pixels for use by the learning system.

**Pixel Dynamics Model and Training:**
The core component is a differentiable forward dynamics model, `fθ(It, ut) → I(t+1)̂`, which predicts the next canvas observation `I(t+1)̂` given the current observation `It` and a stroke action `ut`. The model, termed IMPASTO-UNet, employs a U-Net-style encoder-decoder architecture. An image encoder processes the concatenated current canvas state `It` and a pre-rendered image `Rt` of the candidate stroke. An action encoder processes the 6D vector of stroke parameters `ut`. The embeddings from both encoders are fused by a decoder to output the predicted next canvas image. The pre-rendered stroke image `Rt` bridges the gap between low-dimensional vector controls and high-dimensional raster states, providing the network with visual cues about the stroke's footprint, orientation, and thickness.

The model is trained through robot self-supervision. The robot collects a dataset of `(It, ut, I(t+1))` tuples by uniformly exploring the parameter space of brushstroke actions and cycling through a fixed set of colors. The training objective is a weighted `ℓ1` loss, which emphasizes pixels within the region of change between `It` and `I(t+1)`. Standard data augmentation techniques (cropping, rotation, flipping) are applied. The network is trained end-to-end using the Adam optimizer.

**Multi-Step Stroke Planning:**
For multi-step painting reproduction, IMPASTO employs a receding-horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC) strategy. Starting from the current canvas observation `I0` and a goal image `Ig`, the system optimizes a sequence of `H=5` actions. After optimizing, the first two actions in the sequence are executed, and the canvas state is re-observed. The planning process then replans for the remaining horizon. The optimization objective is a weighted `ℓ1` image-space difference between the predicted final state `I(H-1)̂` and the goal image `Ig`, with weights up-emphasizing goal-relevant pixels. The optimization uses MPPI (Model Predictive Path Integral) with adaptive sampling and elite-set updates, initialized with heuristic stroke candidates generated by a PCA–RANSAC multi-way splitter that segments the difference map between the base and target images. This closed-loop, sample-and-refine procedure is designed to be robust to model errors and inherently incorporates action and force limits.

**Baseline Methods:**
For comparative evaluation, several baselines are introduced:
- **IMPASTO-LR:** A linear regression variant that maps normal force to stroke thickness, then directly renders a stroke based on geometric parameters for compositing.
- **Heuristics-only:** A non-learning method that extracts stroke geometry (start, length, bend, angle) from a binarized difference mask using skeletonization, with a fixed force.
- **FRIDA-CNN:** A variant based on FRIDA's `param2stroke` model, a convolutional neural network that predicts a stroke occupancy field from `(l, b, F)`, which is then transformed and blended with `(p0, α, C)`.
Notably, baselines predict only the incremental stroke, while IMPASTO-UNet predicts the full next image state, which can present a disadvantage in `ℓ1` loss due to requiring prediction of the entire image.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate the dynamics model's fidelity, the system's ability to reproduce individual human strokes, and its performance in closed-loop, multi-step painting tasks.

**Q1: Fidelity of the Pixel Dynamics Model:**
The dynamics model's ability to predict the next canvas state was evaluated against increasing numbers of training samples. Results indicated that as the training dataset size grew, the prediction error (unweighted `Lℓ1` loss) decreased. With 900 self-play samples, the model achieved an `Lℓ1` error of 0.0285, suggesting that the dynamics model can be trained to a reasonable level of fidelity with a moderate amount of robot-generated data.

**Q2: Reproduction of Human Individual Strokes:**
IMPASTO-UNet was compared against baseline methods (IMPASTO-LR, Heuristics-only, FRIDA-CNN) on two datasets: "Single Strokes" (12 strokes from each of five human artists) and "Overlaid Strokes" (50 robot-painted layered strokes). Evaluation metrics included the weighted `Lℓ1` loss and LPIPS (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity), which aligns with human visual judgments. Experiments were conducted for both a planning stage (model prediction) and an execution stage (robot painting), with brush and pigment conditions controlled for consistency across trials.

- **Performance on Single Strokes:** IMPASTO-UNet consistently demonstrated lower LPIPS scores across all human artists compared to the baselines, indicating higher perceptual similarity in reproduced strokes. In terms of weighted `Lℓ1` loss, IMPASTO-UNet generally performed comparably or superiorly, although IMPASTO-LR occasionally achieved marginally lower `Lℓ1` values for specific artists. Quantitatively, IMPASTO-UNet achieved average reductions in planning `Lℓ1` loss by 16.45% (vs. FRIDA-CNN) and 5.28% (vs. IMPASTO-LR). For execution, it reduced `Lℓ1` loss by 19.48% (vs. FRIDA-CNN), 5.33% (vs. IMPASTO-LR), and 21.21% (vs. Heuristics-only). LPIPS was reduced by 35.36% (vs. FRIDA-CNN), 18.21% (vs. IMPASTO-LR), and 15.68% (vs. Heuristics-only). These results suggest that the self-supervised dynamics model generalized effectively to various human stroke styles.
- **Performance on Overlaid Strokes:** On the "Overlaid" dataset, IMPASTO-UNet also outperformed all baselines. It reduced planning `Lℓ1` loss by 10.42% (vs. FRIDA-CNN) and 1.90% (vs. IMPASTO-LR). Execution `Lℓ1` loss was reduced by 12.28% (vs. FRIDA-CNN), 2.37% (vs. IMPASTO-LR), and 3.51% (vs. Heuristics-only). LPIPS was reduced by 16.48% (vs. FRIDA-CNN), 10.68% (vs. IMPASTO-LR), and 13.51% (vs. Heuristics-only). This indicates that IMPASTO-UNet is more robust in making predictions when the canvas contains existing, potentially noisy, background strokes.

**Q3: Closed-Loop, Multi-Step Planning for Complex Oil Paintings:**
The system's capability for long-horizon, multi-step planning was evaluated on two complex oil paintings ("Flower" with 17 strokes and "Fish" with 18 strokes) painted by a human artist. A planning horizon of `H=5` was used with receding-horizon MPC. IMPASTO-UNet was compared with FRIDA-CNN.

- **Multi-Step Painting Performance:** IMPASTO-UNet consistently outperformed FRIDA-CNN in multi-step planning and execution. On average, it reduced planning `Lℓ1` loss by 24.01% and execution `Lℓ1` loss by 10.55% compared to FRIDA-CNN. LPIPS was reduced by 14.63%. Qualitative results demonstrated IMPASTO's ability to approximate these paintings with enhanced detail in terms of force and shape. Furthermore, IMPASTO was able to approximate a more complex painting consisting of 204 strokes with notable visual similarity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a robotic system, IMPASTO, that integrates learned neural dynamics models with model-based planning for oil painting reproduction. This work addresses several technical challenges inherent in robotic artistic creation, particularly the precise control of deformable tools and fluids, force-sensitive manipulation, real-time visual perception of the evolving artwork, and complex multi-step stroke planning, often in the absence of human demonstrations or accurate simulators.

The development of a pixel dynamics model trained solely on robot self-play data demonstrates a method for a robot to acquire an understanding of complex brush dynamics without external supervision. The integration of this learned model into a receding-horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework enables the system to infer and execute stroke trajectories, forces, and colors effectively, outperforming several baselines in reproduction accuracy. This capability for closed-loop planning and execution, coupled with low-level force control, represents an advancement in robotic manipulation for artistic applications.

The findings indicate that IMPASTO can reproduce individual human artists' brushstrokes with high visual similarity and generalize across different stroke styles. Its performance in multi-step painting tasks suggests a pathway towards robots that can undertake more complex artistic reproductions.

The potential impact of this work extends to several areas:
- **Robotics Research:** It contributes to the field of learning-based control for complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks involving deformable objects and fluids, particularly where traditional model-based approaches or direct imitation learning are difficult. The methodology of combining self-supervised learned dynamics with MPC could be applicable to other domains beyond art.
- **Art and Heritage Preservation:** The ability to reproduce oil paintings with high fidelity could have implications for art restoration, archival, or the creation of high-quality replicas, allowing for detailed study or public display while protecting originals.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration in Creative Fields:** As robots gain greater autonomous artistic capabilities, this work contributes to the foundation for future human-robot collaborative artistic endeavors, where robots might assist artists with detailed replication or explore new artistic styles.

In summary, IMPASTO represents a step toward developing robotic systems capable of manipulating real brushes and paints with a level of finesse that approximates human artistic capability, bridging the gap between perception, learning, and control in complex art domains.
