# MatchMaker: Automated Asset Generation for Robotic Assembly

> [!summary] Summary
> MatchMaker is an automated pipeline that generates diverse, simulation-compatible assembly asset pairs by combining a Vision-Language Model for semantic understanding, a 3D generative diffusion model for shape completion, and a clearance specification module. This approach yielded a higher asset diversity score of 0.43 compared to existing datasets and enabled policies trained on these assets to achieve an 82% success rate in real-world robotic assembly tasks.

## Problem

- Developing generalist robotic assembly agents is hindered by the scarcity of diverse, high-quality, simulation-compatible assembly asset pairs.
- Existing datasets often contain geometrically incompatible assets with interpenetrations, making them unsuitable for high-fidelity physics simulations and real-world manufacturing.
- Manual curation of assembly assets is labor-intensive and unscalable, severely limiting dataset size and diversity for modern robot learning paradigms.

## Method

- An automated three-stage pipeline, **MatchMaker**, generates simulation-compatible assembly asset pairs by formulating the problem as shape completion.
- A ==Vision-Language Model (VLM)== identifies semantic properties and assembly axes for robust contact surface extraction from an input asset.
- A 3D generative latent diffusion model (BrepGen) then generates the mating counterpart, using the extracted contact surfaces as fixed geometric constraints, followed by a clearance specification module to ensure penetration-free and user-defined clearances.

## Results

- MatchMaker generated assets with a diversity score of **0.43**, significantly surpassing existing datasets like AutoMate (**0.19**) and Assemble Them All (**0.24**).
- Automated repair of 100 incompatible assets for policy learning took less than 1 minute per asset, demonstrating substantial efficiency gains over manual methods which required approximately 40 hours for 100 assets.
- Policies trained on MatchMaker-generated assets successfully transferred to a physical robot, achieving an average success rate of **82%** across 5 distinct real-world assembly tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Framing paired assembly asset generation as a constrained shape completion problem, guided by extracted contact surfaces, enables automated and diverse asset creation.
> - Vision-Language Models can provide high-level semantic reasoning and assembly axis identification to effectively guide lower-level geometric processing for contact surface extraction.
> - The integration of a B-rep based 3D generative model with a precise, physics-informed clearance specification step is essential for producing simulation-compatible and physically realizable assembly assets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic assembly represents a persistent challenge within robotics due to the intricate requirements of visual perception, precise functional grasping, contact-rich manipulation, and the need for high-precision task execution. Recent advancements in simulation-based learning and sim-to-real transfer methods have shown promise in addressing these complexities, enabling robots to perform assembly tasks despite object pose variations, perception noise, and control errors. These developments have led to specialized policies capable of mating specific asset pairs.

However, a significant obstacle to developing "generalist" (multi-task) robotic agents that can robustly handle a wide array of assembly tasks—including those with novel or previously unseen geometries—has been the manual and labor-intensive process of curating diverse, simulation-compatible assembly assets. Existing datasets, while sometimes large, often suffer from critical limitations:
- **Geometric Incompatibility**: Many assets found in public datasets (e.g.,) contain interpenetrations or insufficient clearance between components in their assembled state. This renders them incompatible with high-accuracy physics simulators that enforce strict non-penetration constraints and also makes them physically unfeasible for real-world manufacturing and assembly (e.g., 3D printing).
- **Manual Effort**: Previous efforts to create compatible datasets, such as AutoMate, involved substantial manual human intervention to adjust and refine assets, requiring approximately 40 hours of effort to process only 100 assets. This manual approach severely limits the scalability of dataset generation.
- **Lack of Paired Assets**: While large datasets of single 3D assets exist (e.g., ABC dataset), they do not provide the geometrically mating pairs necessary for assembly tasks.

The broader robotics research landscape increasingly emphasizes the importance of large-scale, diverse data for enhancing the generalization capabilities of robot learning agents, as demonstrated in various works. The ability to automatically collect large-scale demonstration data in simulated environments, combined with effective sim-to-real transfer, presents a pathway toward developing agents capable of solving a wide range of manipulation tasks.

MatchMaker directly addresses this data bottleneck by proposing an automated pipeline for generating diverse, simulation-compatible assembly asset pairs. It leverages recent successes in generative AI, particularly 3D shape generation using diffusion models, to automate the asset creation process. By providing a scalable method for generating high-quality assembly data, this work aims to unlock opportunities for training more generalizable and robust robotic assembly policies, thus fitting into the paradigm of data-driven robot learning. It bridges the gap between sophisticated generative models for 3D shapes and the specific requirements for robotic assembly simulation and real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to overcome the data scarcity and quality issues that currently impede the development of generalist robotic assembly agents. The authors aim to automate the generation of diverse, simulation-compatible assembly asset pairs, thereby reducing reliance on manual curation and enabling scalable data production for policy learning.

The key motivations and specific objectives driving the MatchMaker pipeline are:

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity for Generalization**: The development of generalist policies capable of assembling a wide range of objects, including novel geometries, is critically limited by the availability of sufficiently large and diverse datasets of assembly asset pairs. Current datasets are either too small, lack diversity, or are geometrically unsuitable for high-fidelity simulation and real-world physical assembly. MatchMaker seeks to generate diverse assets to facilitate the training of policies that can generalize beyond a small set of seen objects.

2. **Ensuring Simulation and Real-World Compatibility**: A major challenge with existing assembly datasets is the presence of interpenetration or insufficient clearance between mating parts when assembled. This leads to unstable or incorrect behavior in physics simulators and makes physical assembly impossible. The research is motivated by the need to generate assets that are explicitly "simulation-compatible" (i.e., penetration-free) and "realizable" (i.e., manufacturable and physically assemblable).

3. **Automating Asset Creation**: Manual asset curation is time-consuming and costly, making it unfeasible for generating the vast quantities of data required for modern robot learning paradigms. The paper aims to develop an automated pipeline that minimizes human effort in creating assembly assets, transitioning from a labor-intensive process to a scalable, generative one.

4. **Enabling Controllable Clearance**: Real-world assembly tasks involve varying levels of clearance and manufacturing tolerances. The ability to control the clearance between mating parts is important for simulating realistic contact interactions, preventing instability, and for curriculum learning approaches that might gradually increase task difficulty. A specific objective is to allow user-specified clearance parameters during asset generation.

To achieve these motivations, MatchMaker sets out to perform the following specific functions:
- **Convert Incompatible Pairs**: Automatically convert existing simulation-incompatible, interpenetrating asset pairs into compatible, interpenetration-free ones.
- **Generate Mating Counterparts**: Take an arbitrary single 3D asset as input and automatically generate a geometrically mating second asset to complete an assembly pair.
- **Specify Realistic Clearances**: Automatically apply a post-processing step to erode contact surfaces, ensuring user-specified clearances and preventing interpenetration in the generated pairs.

By achieving these objectives, MatchMaker aims to significantly advance the field of robotic assembly by providing a foundation for developing robust and broadly applicable assembly skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MatchMaker is structured as a three-stage pipeline designed to automatically generate diverse, simulation-compatible assembly asset pairs, primarily focusing on single-axis insertion tasks. The core idea is to formulate the paired-asset generation as a shape completion problem. The pipeline can accept no input (generating a base asset), a single asset (generating a mating counterpart), or an existing assembly pair (applying only clearance specification).

**1. Contact-Surface Extraction:**
This initial stage aims to identify the crucial geometric features on an input asset that define how a mating part would assemble with it. It involves two sub-stages:
- **a) VLM Identification**: An initially sampled or generated single asset (Asset A) is rendered from a top-front view and provided as input to a Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically GPT-4o. The VLM is prompted using a "chain-of-thought" approach to first describe the asset, then identify whether it functions as a "plug" or a "receptacle," and finally determine a plausible "assembly axis" and "direction" (e.g., top-down, left-right). This high-level reasoning guides the subsequent analytical process.
- **b) Analytic Method**: Utilizing the assembly direction identified by the VLM, an analytical procedure, akin to 3D flood-filling, identifies the specific faces of Asset A that would be in contact with a mating part. The asset is rotated to align the identified assembly direction with the z-axis. A 512³ voxel grid is initialized above the asset and moved downwards. Cells of the grid that contact Asset A are removed. For receptacles, cells outside the convex hull are also removed. The system then identifies which faces of Asset A's B-rep representation are fully in contact with the remaining voxel grid. These are designated as "contact surfaces" and will serve as the geometric constraints for generating the second asset. When generating a plug, the top surfaces of the receptacle are removed, retaining only the faces of the assembly orifices to allow for more varied generated plug structures. Finally, these identified contact surfaces are translated to the bottom of a unit box to align with the training data format of the subsequent generative model.

**2. Shape Completion:**
Following the extraction of contact surfaces, this stage generates the geometry of the second, mating asset (Asset B).
- **Generative Model**: The process utilizes a 3D generative model, specifically BrepGen, which is a latent diffusion model designed for generating 3D CAD models in B-rep (Boundary Representation) format. B-rep is chosen for its mathematical precision, smooth surface representation, and widespread use in CAD.
- **Constraint Application**: The contact surfaces identified in Stage 1 are used as fixed geometric constraints for the diffusion model. Leveraging techniques similar to Repaint, the model generates the external geometry of Asset B, ensuring it seamlessly incorporates and mates with these predefined contact surfaces. The pre-alignment of contact surfaces to the unit box aids in consistency with the diffusion model's training framework.

**3. Clearance Specification:**
The final stage refines the generated asset pair to ensure simulation compatibility and realism, addressing potential interpenetrations and allowing for user-defined clearances.
- **Interpenetration Removal**: The generated CAD model of Asset B is converted into an occupancy grid representation. Starting from an assembled state, this occupancy grid is "moved" along the assembly axis until the two parts are completely separated. During this conceptual "disassembly," any grid cells of Asset B that come into contact with, or fall within a specified "clearance distance" (c) of, Asset A are removed from Asset B. This process effectively "erodes" the contact regions of Asset B.
- **Mesh Reconstruction**: After refining the occupancy grid, the marching cubes algorithm is employed to convert the grid back into a triangular mesh, producing the final version of Asset B. This ensures that the generated asset pairs are free of intersections and maintain the desired, parameterizable clearance in their assembled state, making them suitable for both high-fidelity physics simulations and real-world manufacturing.

If the input is an already-existing assembly pair, only the Clearance Specification stage is applied to refine the geometry and introduce the desired clearance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted to validate MatchMaker focused on generation efficiency, the quality and diversity of generated assets, their utility for policy learning, and their realizability in the real world.

**A. Generation Efficiency:**
- **Contact Surface Extraction (VLM)**: The use of chain-of-thought prompting significantly improved the recognition accuracy of GPT-4o for identifying asset semantics (plug/receptacle), assembly axis, and orientation. Accuracy improved from 56% to 70% for semantic, 56% to 66% for axis, and 46% to 60% for orientation compared to an ablated version without chain-of-thought prompting.
- **Shape Completion**: This was identified as the most time-consuming stage. Sample efficiency (ratio of valid generated pairs to total samples) varied from 0.01 to 0.10, and time costs ranged from 114 to 1070 seconds per asset pair, depending on the number of shared faces and geometric complexity. Higher complexity generally led to lower efficiency and longer generation times. Common issues included failures to produce valid B-rep formats and the generation of mirrored instead of complementary assets.
- **Clearance Specification**: This post-processing step was efficient, taking approximately 120 seconds per asset pair at a 512³ resolution using 64 CPU cores. This represents a substantial speedup compared to manual methods, which previously required about 40 hours to process 100 asset pairs.

**B. Analysis of Generated Results:**
- **Qualitative Diversity**: Figure 3 illustrates that MatchMaker is capable of generating visually diverse and geometrically intricate paired assets.
- **Quantitative Diversity and Complexity**: Using metrics for shape complexity, grasp difficulty, and diversity from, MatchMaker's generated assets were compared against AutoMate and Assemble Them All (ATA).
 - MatchMaker exhibited similar ranges of shape complexity (0.65-5.27) and grasp difficulty (0.0-1.0) compared to existing datasets.
 - Crucially, MatchMaker achieved a significantly higher diversity score of 0.43, compared to 0.19 for AutoMate and 0.24 for ATA (when selecting 100 assets to maximize diversity). This increased diversity is attributed to generating novel geometries and focusing on single-axis insertion, contrasting with datasets like ATA which often contain many cylindrical parts or limited single-axis pairs. MatchMaker has generated 1000 asset pairs in this study, with continuous generation ongoing.

**C. Policy Learning:**
- **Asset Repair**: MatchMaker's clearance-specification module was used to automatically repair 100 assets from (to achieve AutoMate-level compatibility) in less than 1 minute per asset. Policies learned on these automatically repaired assets achieved success rates comparable to those reported for manually processed assets in, indicating that the automated repair process yields high-quality assets suitable for policy learning.
- **Asset Generation**: Assembly policies were successfully learned for newly generated asset pairs using the AutoMate policy-learning framework. The generated assets offered a range of task difficulties, including some (e.g., asset 10168, 11159) that posed significant challenges for the AutoMate method due to complex geometries or limited grasping opportunities in the assembled state. This demonstrates the pipeline's ability to generate novel benchmarks for future assembly algorithms.

**D. Real-World Demonstrations:**
- Five generated asset pairs were 3D-printed and used for real-world policy deployment. Policies trained in simulation for these assets were transferred to a physical robot.
- The robot achieved an average success rate of 82% over 10 trials per asset in real-world assembly tasks. This outcome validates that the assets generated by MatchMaker are realizable in the physical world and that policies trained using these assets in simulation can effectively transfer to real robotic systems. The authors note that compliant fixturing in the real world might contribute to slightly higher success rates by allowing some self-alignment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MatchMaker presents a notable advancement in the field of robotic assembly by introducing an automated, scalable pipeline for generating diverse, simulation-compatible, and physically realizable assembly asset pairs.

**Primary Significance:**
- **Addressing the Data Bottleneck**: The most significant impact is alleviating the critical data scarcity problem in robotic assembly. By automating the creation of high-quality assembly datasets, MatchMaker removes a major bottleneck that previously hindered the training of generalist robot policies. This shift from manual to automated asset generation is crucial for scaling up robot learning.
- **Enabling Generalization**: The ability to generate diverse and challenging assembly tasks contributes directly to the development of more robust and generalizable robotic assembly skills. Instead of learning policies for a limited set of manually curated objects, robots can now be trained on a vastly expanded and varied dataset, potentially leading to agents that can handle novel geometries and scenarios with higher success rates.
- **Seamless Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The explicit focus on generating assets that are both simulation-compatible (penetration-free with controllable clearances) and physically realizable (demonstrated through 3D printing and real-world deployment) facilitates more effective sim-to-real transfer. This ensures that learned policies in simulation are directly applicable and perform well in physical environments.

**Broader Impact and Future Directions:**
- **Advancing Generative CAD and Robotics**: The approach of formulating paired-asset generation as a shape completion task, leveraging Vision-Language Models for semantic understanding and assembly axis identification, and employing diffusion models for B-rep generation, represents a novel integration of generative AI with robotic application needs. This could inspire further research in constrained 3D content generation for robotics.
- **Facilitating Research and Benchmarking**: The planned release of the MatchMaker code and its integration with platforms like NVIDIA Isaac Lab will provide the research community with a powerful tool. This will enable researchers to automatically generate custom assembly benchmarks, experiment with varying clearances, and create diverse training data for policy learning, potentially fostering rapid progress in the field.
- **Data Augmentation Opportunities**: Beyond generating new datasets, MatchMaker offers several data augmentation possibilities. It can create new mating parts for existing assets, generate asset pairs with varying clearance levels to enhance policy robustness against manufacturing tolerances, and produce similar but diverse assets to aid curriculum learning frameworks (e.g., starting with easier versions before progressing to more complex ones).

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that indicate avenues for future research:
- **Geometric Complexity**: The current generation capabilities are limited to shapes with approximately 50 surfaces due to GPU memory constraints during training. Future work could explore methods to scale to more complex geometries.
- **Generative Model Efficiency**: The diffusion model sometimes requires multiple samples to produce a valid B-rep output. Improvements in model architectures could enhance the rate of valid generations.
- **Scope of Assembly Tasks**: The current approach primarily focuses on single-axis, two-part assembly tasks. Future research will explore generating assemblies requiring more complex motions (e.g., rotational, helical) or multi-step assembly sequences, broadening the applicability of the pipeline.

Overall, MatchMaker contributes a critical tool for scaling up data-driven approaches in robotic assembly, laying a foundation for more generalizable and robust robotic manipulation capabilities.
