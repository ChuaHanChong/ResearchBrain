# SHRIMP: Iterative Refinement of Robot Task Plans

> [!summary] Summary
> SHRIMP is an end-user robot-programming interface that turns a natural-language task prompt into a hierarchical plan of high- and low-level robot primitives, grounded by YOLO object tracking and validated in a physics-based Isaac Sim digital twin. Users re-prompt or edit primitive parameters, replay steps in simulation, then execute on bimanual Franka arms; a 35-participant ablation showed higher perceived control and transparency.

## Problem

- Natural language task specification is inherently ambiguous: an instruction like 'move the object left' leaves distance, reference frame, and target object unspecified for precise pose goals.
- LLM and vision-language-action planners act as black boxes, so end users cannot inspect or verify a generated plan before the robot executes it physically.
- Prior end-user interfaces rely on text- or code-centric editing with simplified perception and trajectory-only previews, giving no physics-grounded check of actual robot-object interaction.

## Method

- ==Hierarchical primitive plan==: an ==LLM== prompted with robot configuration, available primitives, and tracked object poses emits high-level primitives (pick, pour, pick_and_place) expandable into low-level ones (move_to_pose, grasp, tilt_in_hand).
- ==Three-stage plan verification==: grasp-target check that substitutes tracked object poses when the gripper is out of range, arm-to-arm collision retracts, and trajectory height raising against robot-object collisions.
- ==Isaac Sim digital twin== shares a ==PD torque controller== with ==Coriolis compensation== with two physical Franka Panda arms, streamed by ==WebRTC== for step-by-step playback, parameter editing, and revertible task history.

## Results

- Full Plan+Edit beat the no-plan baseline on perceived autonomy (**3.50** versus **3.15**, p=**0.007**) and transparency on TOROS (**4.77** versus **4.34**, p=**0.026**) with **35** participants.
- The plan-only condition differed significantly from neither the baseline nor the full system, so viewing high-level plans alone was insufficient; usability (SUS) and task success showed no condition differences.
- Editing raised workload: NASA-TLX **37.86** versus **29.92** for plan-only (p=**0.002**); users revised plans **6.88** times and simulated **7.61** times, versus **3.68** and **4.65** at baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transparency needs inspection plus intervention: exposing a high-level plan without editable low-level parameters produced no measurable gain in perceived control or system understanding.
> - Control trades against workload: fine-grained primitive editing helps complex tasks but adds needless cognitive load on easy ones, favoring gradual disclosure of low-level controls.
> - Users adopt stepwise, cumulative, and oneshot prompting strategies, so interfaces assuming one complete prompt mismatch practice; LLM nondeterminism frustrates users lacking failure localization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits Into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses challenges within the growing field of human-robot interaction, specifically concerning end-user robot programming (EURP) for collaborative robots (cobots). As cobots are increasingly deployed in various domains such as manufacturing, agriculture, and healthcare, there is a recognized need for programming interfaces that do not require extensive robotics expertise. Natural language has emerged as a promising modality to lower this barrier, leveraging recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to translate high-level human instructions into robot task plans.

However, existing language-based robot planning systems face several limitations. Semantic ambiguity inherent in natural language can lead to imprecise robot actions, and the "black-box" nature of generative AI models limits transparency, making it difficult for users to understand and validate generated plans before physical execution. While previous EURP research has explored various modalities like visual programming, augmented reality, and programming-by-demonstration, many existing natural language interfaces are rigid or lack support for instruction revision.

Current LLM-based robot planning systems, such as SayCan, VoxPoser, RT-2, and ProgPrompt, have demonstrated capabilities in grounding reasoning and generating executable code or actions. Nevertheless, these systems often lack user interfaces for reviewing or modifying generated plans, constrain perception to simplified settings, or are predominantly text- or code-centric. This necessitates manual inspection by users, with limited interactive editing or pre-deployment simulation. Furthermore, prior work in robot plan visualization and simulation primarily focuses on previewing robot motion or inspecting plans, without always integrating automatic plan generation or providing comprehensive, physics-based interaction.

SHRIMP aims to bridge these gaps by introducing a system that integrates automatic LLM-based plan generation with an iterative, simulation-driven refinement process. Unlike systems that simplify perception or rely heavily on text-based verification, SHRIMP provides a hierarchical primitive plan representation and a physics-grounded simulation environment. This approach is positioned to enhance transparency and user control, addressing the inherent ambiguity of language and the opacity of generative models, while maintaining the accessibility of natural language for non-expert users. The work extends previous research by combining automated planning with interactive, physics-based simulation to enable iterative plan improvement and validation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing SHRIMP stems from the recognized difficulties non-expert users face in programming and adapting the behavior of collaborative robots. Despite the increasing deployment of cobots in diverse industrial and service sectors, existing programming tools often demand specialized robotic expertise, operating at low levels of abstraction that require users to specify precise joint positions or construct plans step-by-step. This represents a significant barrier to broader adoption and on-the-fly adaptation by end-users.

Natural language is identified as a highly accessible modality for non-expert users to convey their intent for robot tasks. However, its inherent imprecision and semantic ambiguity pose significant challenges when translating high-level instructions into the precise pose goals and trajectories required for robot execution. For instance, a simple instruction like "move the object left" lacks specificity regarding distance, reference frame, or target object.

A further critical motivation is the lack of transparency in recent advancements in LLMs and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models used for robot task planning. These generative models are often perceived as "black-boxes," making it difficult for users to understand how their language instructions are translated into robot actions. This opacity hinders users' ability to validate task plans effectively before they are executed on a physical robot, raising concerns about safety, reliability, and alignment with user intent. Even when LLMs provide textual plans or explanations, interpreting and verifying complex robot sequences can remain challenging for non-experts.

To address these motivations, SHRIMP was designed with several key objectives:
1. **Enable Automatic Plan Generation:** To allow users to generate initial, hierarchical robot primitive plans directly from natural language input, leveraging LLMs.
2. **Facilitate Iterative Plan Refinement:** To provide mechanisms for users to iteratively revise generated plans. This includes re-prompting with natural language and explicit correction through direct manipulation of primitive parameters (e.g., end-effector poses) or the sequence of primitives.
3. **Provide Simulation-Driven Validation:** To offer a safe, physics-grounded simulation environment where users can test and validate their revised plans. This simulation supports full execution and step-by-step playback, allowing users to identify and correct issues without the risks associated with physical robot operation.
4. **Enhance Transparency:** To improve user understanding of robot behavior by presenting plans as a hierarchy of high- and low-level robot primitives. This structured representation, combined with realistic object manipulation in simulation, aims to make the expected execution transparent.
5. **Improve Perceived Control:** To empower users with a greater sense of control over the robot's actions by providing explicit means to inspect, correct, and verify plans at multiple levels of abstraction.

Ultimately, the research aims to validate that such a system can improve users' perceived control and enhance robot transparency, thereby making collaborative robot programming more accessible and reliable for non-expert end-users.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SHRIMP (Simulation-driven Human-in-the-loop Refinement Interface for Manipulation Planning) is a system designed to enable non-expert users to generate and iteratively refine robot task plans through natural language and simulation.

**User Workflow:**
The user workflow in SHRIMP proceeds through several stages:
1. **Scene Tracking and Initialization:** Users first track objects in the physical environment using a camera, replicating them within the simulation environment.
2. **Task Prompt Input:** Users input their desired task using natural language (e.g., "Clean up the table by putting everything in the box").
3. **Plan Generation and Initial Simulation:** A hierarchical primitive plan is automatically generated by an LLM. Users can then execute this plan in the physics-based simulation, either fully or step-by-step, to observe its expected behavior. The plan includes both high-level (e.g., `pick`) and low-level (e.g., `move_to_pose`) robot primitives, which can be expanded and inspected.
4. **Iterative Plan Refinement:** Users can modify the plan through three main methods:
 - **Re-prompting:** Entering new natural language instructions.
 - **Primitive Editing:** Re-ordering or adding new primitives.
 - **Parameter Modification:** Directly adjusting primitive parameters, such as robot end-effector poses, visualized by a red arrow in the simulation.
5. **Task History:** All generated and edited plans are stored, allowing users to revert to previous versions and iterate from any point.
6. **Physical Execution:** Once satisfied with the simulated plan, users can execute it on the physical robot.

**Plan Generation:**
- **Primitives:** The system utilizes a hierarchical structure of robot primitives. **Low-level primitives** represent fundamental robot actions (e.g., `home`, `move_to_pose`, `grasp`, `release`, `tilt_in_hand`, `wait`). **High-level primitives** are sequences of these low-level actions, designed for common tasks (e.g., `pick`, `pour`, `pick_and_place`). Each primitive is associated with specific parameters.
- **LLM Integration:** GPT 5.1 is used as the LLM. It receives the robot configuration, a list of available primitives, the positions of all detected objects in the scene, and the user's natural language task description. The LLM is then prompted to generate the required sequence of primitives, prioritizing high-level primitives where applicable.
- **Plan Verification:** After LLM generation, the plan undergoes three automated checks to enhance robustness:
 1. **Grasp Primitive Validation:** Confirms that grasp primitives target the intended object and that the gripper is within grasping range. If not, pose parameters are adjusted based on object tracking.
 2. **Arm-to-Arm Collision Prevention:** Detects potential collisions between the two robot arms and inserts `retract` motions into the plan to prevent them.
 3. **Robot-Object Collision Prevention:** Identifies collisions between the robot arm (or an object it holds) and other scene objects during transit, and increases the trajectory height to avoid these.

**Hardware and Software Architecture:**
The system comprises a dual-robot setup and a distributed software architecture:
- **Hardware:** Two 7-degree-of-freedom Franka Emika Panda Robot arms, each equipped with a 12-DOF Tesollo 3 Finger gripper. A single RGB-Depth d435 Realsense Camera localizes objects in the scene using YOLOv11.
- **Simulation:** NVIDIA Isaac Sim serves as the physics-based simulation engine, acting as a digital twin to the physical robots.
- **Control:** A shared motion control layer ensures consistency between physical and simulated robots. Inverse kinematics (IK) is handled by RangedIK. A PD Torque controller with Coriolis compensation is used for both Franka robots. Gravity compensation is added for the simulated robots, as the physical Franka Control Interface (FCI) handles it automatically. The simulation runs at approximately 200 Hz, while physical robots run at higher rates (Franka 1000 Hz, Tesollo 500 Hz), with gains tuned to match motion response.
- **Backend:** A REST API implemented with FastAPI manages user plans, storing them as JSON files.
- **Frontend:** Built using Javascript, HTML, and Tailwind CSS, featuring a lightweight state storage system and streaming the simulation to the user interface via WebRTC.
- **Communication:** ROS2 Jazzy facilitates communication between two dedicated computers: one with a Real-Time Kernel Patch for physical robot control, and another with an NVIDIA GPU for simulation, UI, and task planning.

**Evaluation:**
An ablation within-participants experiment (N=35) was conducted to evaluate SHRIMP. Participants were assigned one of three tabletop kitchen tasks (Set the Table, Prepare a Snack, Clean the Table) and performed each task across three interface conditions, with the order counterbalanced to mitigate learning effects:
1. **Plan+Edit (Full System):** All SHRIMP features enabled, including hierarchical plan visibility, step-through, and primitive parameter editing.
2. **Plan-Only:** High-level primitives visible along with arm and object parameters, but no parameter editing, no low-level expansion, and no step-through.
3. **No-Plan (Baseline):** No primitive plan visible; users could only revise robot behavior through natural language re-prompting.

Data collected included logged user input and feature usage, post-trial questionnaires (Transparency Of Robots Scale (TOROS), Perceived Autonomy Scale, System Usability Scale (SUS), NASA-TLX for task load), trial completion time, and binary task success scores (0-3). Open-ended questions and demographic information were gathered at the end. Hypotheses centered on Plan+Edit leading to higher perceived control (H1), transparency (H2), and usability (H3) compared to other conditions. Data analysis involved linear mixed effects models (LMMs) for quantitative measures and thematic analysis for qualitative data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SHRIMP through a within-participants ablation study (N=35) yielded several findings regarding user experience, system transparency, control, and interaction strategies.

**Support for Hypotheses:**
- **Perceived Control (H1):** The hypothesis that Plan+Edit would lead to greater perceived control was supported. Participants reported significantly higher perceived autonomy (p = 0.007) in the Plan+Edit condition (Mean, M = 3.50, Standard Deviation, SD = 0.84) compared to the No-Plan baseline (M = 3.15, SD = 0.99). However, no significant difference was observed between Plan-Only and either No-Plan or Plan+Edit, indicating that merely viewing a high-level plan is insufficient; explicit interaction features are necessary.
- **Transparency (H2):** The hypothesis that Plan+Edit would enhance robot transparency was supported. Participants reported significantly higher transparency (TOROS scores) (p = 0.026) in the Plan+Edit condition (M = 4.77, SD = 1.27) compared to the No-Plan baseline (M = 4.34, SD = 1.34). Similar to perceived control, Plan-Only did not show significant differences, suggesting the combined value of both viewing and interacting with the hierarchical plan.
- **Usability (H3):** The hypothesis that Plan+Edit would lead to higher usability was not supported. No significant differences in System Usability Scale (SUS) scores were found across any conditions. All conditions received below-average usability scores, which the authors interpreted as reflecting participants' general lack of familiarity with robot interfaces and tasks.

**Additional Quantitative Findings:**
- **Task Load (NASA-TLX):** Participants reported a significantly higher perceived task load (p = 0.002) for Plan+Edit (M = 37.86, SD = 16.30) compared to Plan-Only (M = 29.92, SD = 20.24). This suggests that the added features in Plan+Edit, while providing control and transparency, increased cognitive demand.
- **Task Load Nuance by Task:** The increased task load in Plan+Edit was only significant for the "Clean the Table" task, which was identified as the easiest. For the two more complex tasks, there were no significant differences in task load between conditions, suggesting that the additional capabilities of Plan+Edit might be more appropriate for challenging scenarios.
- **Task Success and Time:** No significant differences were found across conditions for task completion time or overall task success.

**Qualitative Findings (Four Thematic Groupings):**

1. **Explicit Engagement with Hierarchical Primitives Increases Perceived Control:**
 - Twenty-two participants explicitly stated that Plan+Edit's features provided more control.
 - Reasons cited included: (1) precise, non-ambiguous control through parameter editing, which was easier than describing changes verbally; and (2) the ability to adjust the plan manually rather than relying solely on language.
 - Perceived autonomy was positively correlated with usability (r = 0.635, p < 0.001).
 - The higher task load in Plan+Edit was attributed to the increased complexity and information provided by the added plan details and editing features, particularly for simpler tasks where these details felt "unnecessary."

2. **Viewing the Primitive Plan Improves Overall System Understanding:**
 - Nineteen participants noted that seeing the primitives in Plan+Edit improved their understanding of the plan.
 - The primitive sequence helped users discern how the system's generated plan differed from their intended natural language prompt, aiding in the revision process.
 - It also assisted users in reviewing and refining their prompts by providing a concrete representation of the robot's actions.

3. **Users Interact with the SHRIMP Iteratively:**
 - Participants consistently revised their plans multiple times across all conditions, with Plan+Edit showing the highest average number of revisions (M = 6.88) and simulations (M = 7.61).
 - The simulation environment was reported to facilitate efficient iteration, saving time compared to physical execution.
 - Three distinct prompt iteration strategies were identified:
 - **Stepwise:** Testing subtasks individually before combining them (21 participants).
 - **Cumulative:** Gradually adding subtasks to the plan (10 participants).
 - **Oneshot:** Specifying the entire plan initially, then iterating to add or change specific details (28 participants), with 8 participants even using this for spatial parameter updates despite direct parameter editing being available.

4. **Inconsistent Outcomes Lead to Frustration:**
 - Six participants reported that similar natural language prompts sometimes produced different robot plans, leading to frustration due to the non-deterministic nature of LLMs.
 - This inconsistency, particularly when a previously successful prompt failed, undermined usability.
 - Users adapted to this by relying on the transparency provided by the primitive plan and employing iterative strategies to refine their prompts.
 - A learning effect was observed, with task success moderately correlated with trial progression (r = 0.337, p < 0.001), as participants became more proficient and comfortable with the system over time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of human-robot interaction by proposing and evaluating SHRIMP, a system designed to improve end-user programming of collaborative robots. The work presents three main contributions:

**1. System Contribution:** SHRIMP offers a novel system architecture that integrates automatic LLM-based plan generation with a physics-grounded, iterative refinement interface. By enabling users to refine generated robot plans through multi-level language and primitive editing, the system provides both transparency into the robot's actions and granular control over its execution. The use of a physics-based simulation allows for safe, realistic validation of object interactions, a capability not always present in prior visualization-focused approaches.

**2. Empirical Contribution:** The ablation study conducted with 35 participants provides empirical evidence for the effectiveness of SHRIMP's key features. The findings confirm that the ability to both view and interact with a hierarchical primitive plan significantly enhances users' perceived control and transparency over LLM-generated robot plans, a crucial outcome for building trust and reliability in AI-driven robot systems. This empirical validation differentiates the work from theoretical proposals or systems evaluated without rigorous user studies.

**3. Design & Research Implications:** Based on the evaluation, the paper offers practical design implications and outlines future research directions for language-based robot planning systems:
 - **Diverse Prompting and Editing Strategies:** The observation that users employ Stepwise, Cumulative, and Oneshot prompting strategies, alongside the need for both high-level language and low-level parameter editing, suggests that future interfaces should support this diversity for efficient iteration. The utility of these options, however, depends on task complexity, implying adaptive interface design.
 - **Managing Cognitive Load:** While multi-level control is beneficial, it can increase cognitive load. This implies that low-level plan details and editing controls should be introduced judiciously, perhaps through gradual feature disclosure, to accommodate users with varying experience levels.
 - **Active Guidance Across Abstraction Layers:** The research highlights a challenge where users may not know which abstraction layer (language prompt vs. primitive parameters) is most effective for a given correction. Future research should focus on creating natural mappings between semantic language and physical parameters, and developing interaction techniques that actively guide users in developing mental models for effective multi-layer engagement.
 - **Localizing Failures in the Pipeline:** The frustration caused by inconsistent LLM outputs underscores the need for systems that can help users not just fix errors, but also understand *why* failures occurred. Future work should investigate mechanisms for identifying and localizing the causes of robot program failures across iterations, which is critical for improving predictability, perceived autonomy, and control in generative AI-based systems.

**Potential Impact:**
SHRIMP has the potential to significantly impact the accessibility and usability of collaborative robots, particularly for end-users without specialized robotics training. By making robot programming more intuitive, transparent, and controllable, it could accelerate the adoption of cobots in diverse non-industrial settings. The insights into user interaction patterns and cognitive load also inform the design of more human-centered AI systems, moving beyond black-box models towards collaborative interfaces where humans can confidently inspect, understand, and refine AI-generated plans. This work lays a foundation for future robot planning systems that are not only capable but also genuinely usable and trustworthy.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations. Technologically, plan generation can be slow (up to 1.5 minutes for long prompts), the vision localization system is sensitive to lighting, and grasp points were manually defined. Future work could focus on faster generation, more robust vision, and automated grasp point determination. From a study design perspective, participants felt time pressure in initial trials due to system unfamiliarity, suggesting the benefit of a tutorial period. The study also focused on relatively simple tasks, indicating that future work should stress-test SHRIMP with more complex or long-horizon tasks requiring extensive iterations to fully evaluate its scalability and robustness.
