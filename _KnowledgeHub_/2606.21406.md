# Robot Self-Improvement via Human-Video Dynamics Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for robot self-improvement utilizes dynamics and value models pretrained on human videos, enabling autonomous evaluation and correction of robot failures through a novel Dynamics-Guided Action Correction (DGAC) mechanism. The method achieved an 85.3% success rate on real-world manipulation tasks, outperforming previous approaches by 9.3 percentage points, and demonstrated cross-embodiment generalization.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to autonomously self-improve after initial deployment, encountering embodiment-specific errors and novel situations without robust mechanisms for correction.
- Existing robot learning methods primarily leverage human videos for initial policy deployment, but not for continuous refinement from a robot's own experiences and encountered failures.
- Real-world robot learning is bottlenecked by the extensive human supervision required for monitoring executions, diagnosing failures, and providing corrective actions during the improvement phase.

## Method

- Jointly train ==embodiment-agnostic policy==, dynamics, and value models on human videos to extract reusable structures that enable cross-embodiment transfer for prediction and evaluation.
- Autonomously adapt these human-pretrained dynamics and value models to a target robot's specific embodiment using robot-collected interaction data, without requiring teleoperation.
- Introduce Dynamics-Guided Action Correction (DGAC), a training-free module that converts failed robot rollouts into corrective supervision by retrieving progress-aligned successful references and synthesizing optimal, context-adapted actions using the learned dynamics and value models.

## Results

- The framework achieved an **85.3%** average success rate across five real-world manipulation tasks on a Hello Robot Stretch 3 platform, outperforming the strongest prior method, RISE, by **9.3** percentage points.
- Dynamics-Guided Action Correction (DGAC) was critical for performance, with ablation studies showing its removal led to a significantly lower success rate of **62.7%**, and it boosted a strong policy backbone by **20.0** percentage points over an existing method (RECAP).
- The learned human-video priors and improvement pipeline successfully transferred to a different embodiment (Franka Panda robot), improving average task success from an initial **36.7%** to **70.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human videos can serve as a rich, scalable prior for learning transferable predictive models (dynamics and value models) that facilitate robot self-improvement, extending beyond initial policy deployment.
> - Explicitly converting robot failures into actionable corrective supervision, rather than simply discarding them, is crucial for efficient and autonomous policy refinement without constant human intervention.
> - Combining semantic visual tokens with 3D point flows provides a robust world-state representation that is essential for both accurate dynamics prediction and fine-grained action correction across different robot embodiments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The acquisition of physical skills by robots is an active area of research, often drawing parallels to human learning paradigms involving observation, practice, and the experience of failure. Prior work in robot learning has explored leveraging human videos as a data source. These efforts have primarily focused on using human videos to initialize useful robot policies, for instance, by pretraining visual-motor representations or by extracting actionable structures such as affordances, point flows, or wrist motions. The application of human videos in this context has largely aimed at enabling initial deployment, essentially allowing a new robot embodiment to imitate observed human behaviors.

However, a recognized gap in this landscape concerns whether human-video priors can extend beyond initial deployment to support robot self-improvement. After initial deployment, robots frequently encounter embodiment-specific errors and novel situations that necessitate identification and correction through their own experiences. The challenge lies in developing representations that can transfer across different robot embodiments while simultaneously supporting predictive capabilities, outcome evaluation, and corrective actions.

Within the domain of robot learning, dynamics models (or world models) have been employed to improve sample efficiency and aid policy optimization, evaluation, and steering. These models typically require substantial robot-specific interaction data for accurate learning. Concurrently, efforts to improve deployed policies in the real world involve strategies such as online human-in-the-loop corrections or post-training objectives from reinforcement learning. The present work distinguishes itself by aiming to reduce the reliance on extensive robot interaction data for learning dynamics models by first pretraining these models on human videos. It further proposes an offline failure correction mechanism, thereby reducing the need for costly online human supervision during the self-improvement phase. This research therefore seeks to bridge the gap between leveraging abundant human observational data and enabling autonomous, experience-driven robot policy refinement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether priors extracted from human videos can support not only the initial deployment of robot policies but also the robot's capacity for self-improvement from its own real-world failures. The motivation for this inquiry stems from several observations:

Firstly, human skill acquisition rarely relies on a single successful demonstration. Instead, humans learn iteratively through observation, repeated practice, and crucially, by recognizing and correcting mistakes. The authors argue that robots should ideally emulate this learning process, continually improving their deployed policies by leveraging their own rollouts and encountered failures as supervision.

Secondly, real-world robot learning is frequently constrained by the demands of human supervision. Prior to deployment, robots often require extensive, task-specific human demonstrations. During the improvement phase, this supervisory burden intensifies, necessitating human experts to monitor executions, diagnose failures, intervene appropriately, and provide corrective behaviors for recovery. This requirement represents a significant bottleneck for scaling robot capabilities.

Thirdly, human videos offer a potentially abundant and diverse source of supervision. They contain rich information about object interactions, world dynamics, and task progression. While prior work has established the utility of human videos for initial policy deployment, robots invariably face embodiment-specific errors and novel scenarios after deployment. The ability to identify and rectify these errors through experience is crucial for robust and generalizable robotic systems.

To address these motivations, the research establishes three specific objectives:
1. **Develop Transferable Representations**: To learn embodiment-agnostic action, predictive world-state, and task-progress value representations from human videos. These representations are designed to transfer across different robot embodiments, providing the necessary foundation for prediction, evaluation, and correction.
2. **Enable Autonomous Improvement**: To demonstrate that these human-video-derived priors can ground a robot's own attempts sufficiently to enable autonomous evaluation, correction, and subsequent improvement from its real-world rollouts and failures.
3. **Introduce a Failure-to-Supervision Mechanism**: To propose Dynamics-Guided Action Correction (DGAC), a training-free approach that leverages the learned and adapted dynamics and value models. DGAC's purpose is to repair failed states by proposing and ranking corrective actions, thereby converting robot failures into actionable supervision for subsequent policy updates.

In achieving these objectives, the research aims to establish a framework that allows robots to learn more robustly and autonomously, moving beyond mere imitation to genuine self-improvement based on their own experiences.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates human-video pretraining with an iterative robot self-improvement loop. The methodology is structured into two main phases: learning representations from human videos, and then adapting these representations for real-world robot self-improvement.

#### 3.1. Learning Representations from Human Videos

The initial phase focuses on extracting reusable structures from human videos to enable cross-embodiment transfer and facilitate downstream policy improvement.

- **Embodiment-Agnostic Representations**:
 - **Action Representation**: Actions are defined as a 6-DoF wrist motion (ξ_t ∈ SE(3)) and a scalar hand-closure variable (c_t ∈). This representation abstracts away specific robot articulation details while retaining core motion cues.
 - **World-State Representation (o_t)**: The world state combines current-frame DINO-v3 visual tokens (z_t) with short-horizon 3D point flows (P_t). This design aims for compactness, capturing semantic context via visual tokens and fine-grained geometry/contact dynamics via point flows.
 - **Value Representation (V_t)**: The value function is trained using a discounted return derived from a sparse terminal reward, where r_T ∈ {1, -1} indicates success or failure at the terminal timestep.

- **Policy, Dynamics, and Value Modeling**: Three models are jointly learned from human videos:
 - **Policy Model (π_θ)**: Predicts an action chunk (a_t:t+H-1) based on a history of wrist states and visual tokens, optionally conditioned on auxiliary inputs like language embeddings.
 - **Dynamics Model (f_ϕ)**: Predicts a future world state (ô_t+H-1) given a history of world states and a candidate action chunk, also with auxiliary conditioning inputs.
 - **Value Model (V_ψ)**: Predicts a scalar value (V̂_t) for the current world state, representing task progress.

- **Optimization Objective**: The action and dynamics models are trained under a flow-matching paradigm, specifically using the x-prediction parameterization, where the networks directly regress clean targets from noisy samples. The value model is optimized using a standard L2 regression objective.

#### 3.2. From Human-Video Pre-training to Robot Self-Improvement

This phase involves adapting the human-pretrained models to a target robot and then iteratively improving the robot policy.

- **Autonomous Robot Adaptation**: To bridge the embodiment gap and account for robot-specific dynamics, the human-pretrained dynamics and value models are adapted using real robot interaction data. This data is collected autonomously: a Visual-Language Model (VLM) proposes atomic interaction instructions, which the frozen human-pretrained policy then executes on the robot. This process exposes the models to robot-specific states and natural failure modes without requiring teleoperation.
- **Policy Model Initialization**: For each new task, a small amount of task-specific teleoperation data (D_tele) is collected. The human-pretrained policy is initialized with the same architecture and fine-tuned on D_tele. The robot policy also incorporates gripper-view observations and an "improvement indicator" for advantage-conditioned policy extraction.
- **Iterative Self-Improvement Loop**:
 1. **Data Collection**: The current policy (π_θ) is executed in the real environment to collect rollouts, categorized into successful (D_succ) and failed (D_fail) trajectories.
 2. **Model Fine-tuning**: The dynamics (f_ϕ) and value (V_ψ) models are fine-tuned on all collected data to adapt to the policy-induced state distribution.
 3. **Dynamics-Guided Action Correction (DGAC)**: This module converts failed rollout states from D_fail into corrective supervision.
 - **Reference Retrieval**: For a failed state o_t, DGAC first identifies a set of progress-aligned successful states in D_succ based on value similarity (using V_ψ). From this set, the most compatible reference state (˜o_t) is selected using state similarity.
 - **Recoverability Check**: A correction is generated only if the failed state is sufficiently similar to the retrieved successful reference in terms of value and state, defined by thresholds δ_v and δ_s. This prevents correction when the robot has drifted too far.
 - **Candidate Action Generation**: Using the policy's conditional flow matching framework, DGAC composes the velocity fields associated with the current failed state and the retrieved successful reference. This composition, weighted by a randomly sampled factor, generates N candidate action chunks (a^(n)_t:t+H-1).
 - **Candidate Selection**: Each candidate action chunk is evaluated by the dynamics model (f_ϕ) to predict a future state (ô^(n)_t+H-1), which is then scored by the value model (V_ψ). The action chunk that predicts the highest future value is selected as the optimal correction (a*_t:t+H-1). This corrected transition is added to a repaired dataset (D_repair).
 4. **Advantage-Conditioned Policy Update**: Advantages (Â_t) are estimated over the combined successful and repaired trajectories (D_succ ∪ D_repair). The policy (π_θ) is then updated using an advantage-conditioned policy extraction objective, similar to CFGRL, to emphasize higher-advantage behaviors.
- **Optimization Objective Refinement**: For robot deployment, the value model loss (L_V) is augmented with a temporal-difference (TD) term to further refine value estimates and improve discrimination between successes and failures.

The methodology emphasizes an iterative learning process where human-video priors are grounded through autonomous robot experience, and failures are explicitly converted into beneficial training signals via DGAC, thereby enabling continuous policy improvement without direct human intervention in the loop.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the efficacy of the proposed framework in enabling robot self-improvement:

- **Overall Performance Improvement**: The framework achieved an average success rate of 85.3% across five real-world manipulation tasks on a Hello Robot Stretch 3 platform. This performance represents a substantial improvement over the initial Expert BC baseline (41.3%) and outperformed the strongest prior method, RISE (76.0%), by 9.3 percentage points. This indicates that the combination of human-video priors and failure-to-supervision learning is effective for complex manipulation tasks.
- **Effectiveness of Dynamics-Guided Action Correction (DGAC)**:
 - Ablation studies demonstrated that removing DGAC entirely resulted in a lower success rate of 62.7%, underscoring the importance of explicitly repairing failures.
 - Directly retrieving and copying successful trajectories (instead of adapting them) also yielded lower performance (58.7%), suggesting that actions need to be adapted to the specific context of the failed scene rather than merely copied.
 - The ranking mechanism within DGAC, which uses the learned value model (V_ψ) to select the most promising corrective action among candidates, was found to be critical. Random selection or lower-ranked selections significantly underperformed, and even a strong VLM-based selector was outperformed by DGAC by 21.3 percentage points, highlighting the necessity of physically grounded dynamics and value prediction for fine-grained action repair.
 - Qualitative analysis indicated that DGAC successfully repairs failure-inducing actions by steering predicted futures towards successful references, leading to interpretable corrections such as rectifying incorrect approach directions or preventing collisions.
- **Policy-Agnostic Improvement**: DGAC demonstrated its ability to act as a plug-and-play module by improving a strong policy backbone, π^0.5, which was pretrained on large-scale robot data. After supervised fine-tuning, π^0.5 achieved 62.7% success. Applying RECAP to this policy led to a modest improvement to 68.0%, while applying DGAC boosted performance to 88.0%, a 20.0 percentage point increase over RECAP. This result suggests that DGAC can provide substantial gains irrespective of the underlying policy's initial strength or source. The full framework, starting from a weaker behavior-cloned policy, achieved 85.3%, suggesting that human-video priors combined with DGAC can close the performance gap to policies pretrained on extensive robot data.
- **Cross-Embodiment Generalization**: The improvement pipeline was successfully transferred to a different robot embodiment, a Franka Panda robot. On two tasks (Box and Sweep), the method improved average success from 36.7% (Expert BC) to 70.0%. This indicates that the learned human-video priors and embodiment-agnostic representations are generalizable across different robot morphologies.
- **Impact of Human-Video Scale and Robot Adaptation**:
 - Scaling the human-video pretraining corpus monotonically increased final task success rates, demonstrating that human videos provide scalable priors for downstream policy improvement.
 - Removing the robot adaptation phase consistently reduced performance, confirming that robot rollouts are necessary to ground the human-video priors in embodiment-specific states and dynamics for reliable DGAC corrections. This adaptation is autonomous and efficient compared to teleoperation.
- **World-State Representation Choices (Appendix A.5.1)**: The combination of DINO-v3 visual tokens and 3D point trajectories as the world-state representation outperformed single-modality variants. Using only point trajectories (68.0%) or only visual tokens (72.0%) led to degraded performance compared to the full model (85.3%), emphasizing the complementary nature of semantic and geometric information.

These findings collectively indicate that passive human observation, combined with the DGAC mechanism, can establish a predictive foundation for robots to autonomously learn from their own experiences and convert failures into a scalable source of supervision for continuous policy improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a framework that offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robot learning:

- **Expanded Role for Human Videos**: This work redefines the role of human videos in robot learning. Beyond merely serving as initial demonstrations for imitation, human videos are shown to provide crucial predictive models (dynamics and value models) that enable robots to reason about the outcomes of their actions, evaluate task progress, and learn from their own experiences. This paradigm shift could unlock more comprehensive utilization of the vast amounts of publicly available human video data.
- **Scalable Autonomous Policy Improvement**: The introduction of Dynamics-Guided Action Correction (DGAC) provides a mechanism for robots to autonomously improve their policies by converting failed executions into corrective supervision. This ability to learn from failures without requiring online human intervention or repetitive, task-specific human demonstrations addresses a significant bottleneck in scaling real-world robot learning. It suggests a pathway towards continuous robot improvement post-deployment, allowing robots to adapt to novel situations and refine their skills over time.
- **Bridging Human Observation and Embodied Experience**: The framework effectively combines internet-scale human observation with embodiment-specific robot experience. By learning embodiment-agnostic representations from human videos and then grounding these priors through autonomous robot rollouts, the approach offers a complementary paradigm to existing methods. This integration could lead to more robust and generalizable robotic systems that benefit from both broad human interaction knowledge and precise robot-specific adaptation.
- **Policy-Agnostic Utility**: The demonstration that DGAC can be plugged into different policy backbones, including those pretrained on large-scale robot data (e.g., π^0.5), highlights its versatility and potential as a generic policy improvement module. This suggests that the framework's benefits are not limited to a specific policy architecture, making it broadly applicable across various robot learning contexts.
- **Reduced Human Supervision Burden**: By enabling autonomous self-improvement through offline failure correction, the research contributes to reducing the demanding human supervision often required for iterative policy refinement. This can accelerate the development and deployment of robotic systems in diverse real-world environments.

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge several limitations that also point towards future research directions. These include the reliance on human annotations for rollout outcomes (suggesting a need for automatic feedback from foundation models), the local nature of DGAC's action corrections (prompting exploration of longer-horizon dynamics prediction and hierarchical planning for complex failures), and the ongoing challenge of scaling human-video pretraining to even larger and more diverse interaction datasets to learn richer physical priors. Overcoming these limitations could further enhance the autonomy and capabilities of self-improving robotic systems.
