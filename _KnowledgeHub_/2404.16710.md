# LayerSkip: Enabling Early Exit Inference and Self-Speculative Decoding

> [!summary] Summary
> Meta's LayerSkip accelerates Large Language Model (LLM) inference by enabling accurate early exit and self-speculative decoding within a single model. It introduces a training recipe with non-uniform layer dropout and early exit loss, allowing the model to produce accurate predictions from intermediate layers and to efficiently verify tokens drafted by its own early layers. This approach achieves 1.34x to 2.16x inference speedups across various LLM sizes and tasks with no degradation in final output accuracy.

## Problem

- High computational and memory costs of Large Language Model (LLM) inference limit widespread deployment and increase operational expenses.
- Existing LLM acceleration techniques often involve accuracy trade-offs or require complex multi-model setups (e.g., separate draft models for speculative decoding), increasing memory footprint and implementation complexity.
- Standard LLM training does not incentivize earlier layers to produce accurate predictions, leading to redundant computation as models process all layers even for 'easy' tokens, and a lack of robust early exit capability.

## Method

- A novel training recipe combines ==non-uniform layer dropout==, which progressively increases dropout rates for later layers, with an ==early exit loss== that directly supervises intermediate layer outputs.
- Utilizes a single, ==shared Language Model (LM) head== across all transformer layers during training and inference, avoiding auxiliary modules and reducing memory footprint.
- Introduces ==self-speculative decoding== where the model uses its early layers to generate draft tokens and then verifies them using its remaining layers, seamlessly reusing KV and 'exit query' caches for efficiency.

## Results

- Achieves inference speedups ranging from **1.**34x** to 2.16x** across various LLM sizes (**1.5B to 13B**) and tasks (summarization, code generation, semantic parsing) with lossless accuracy compared to autoregressive baselines.
- Significantly improves the accuracy of predictions from earlier layers (e.g., Llama2 7B on NaturalQuestions baseline **0.0554%** to LayerSkip **4.07%**), enabling effective early exit inference for speed-accuracy trade-offs.
- Demonstrates competitive performance against other self-speculative decoding methods, and shows a near **2x speedup** on CPU inference, highlighting its efficiency and broad applicability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models can be made robust to early exits and support self-speculative decoding through a novel training recipe that combines non-uniform layer dropout and early exit loss.
> - A single LLM can efficiently perform self-speculative decoding by using its early layers for drafting and later layers for verification, significantly benefiting from shared KV and exit query cache reuse.
> - Standard LLM pretraining can cause intermediate layers to diverge in perplexity, making them less suitable for early exit; LayerSkip's training counteracts this, improving the accuracy of earlier layers while maintaining final layer performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has seen explosive growth, but their immense computational and memory requirements pose significant challenges for deployment and widespread adoption. "LayerSkip" directly addresses this critical issue by proposing a novel, end-to-end solution for accelerating LLM inference. This work situates itself at the intersection of several key research areas: LLM acceleration techniques, early exit inference, and speculative decoding.

**LLM Acceleration Techniques:** The paper acknowledges the existing landscape of LLM acceleration, which primarily focuses on reducing the model's footprint or computational load. These include:
- **Sparsity:** Reducing the number of non-zero weights (e.g., Xia et al., 2023).
- **Quantization:** Decreasing the number of bits per weight (e.g., Xiao et al., 2023).
- **Head Pruning:** Reducing the number of attention heads per layer (e.g., Shim et al., 2021).
- **Reducing Number of Layers:** This category, which LayerSkip falls into, involves using fewer layers during inference (e.g., Fan et al., 2020; Elbayad et al., 2020). The authors specifically highlight that reducing layers, unlike quantization or sparsity, does not necessitate specialized hardware or software kernels, making it broadly applicable.

**Early Exit Inference:** The concept of early exiting, where a model outputs a prediction before completing all its layers, has been explored in deep learning since the mid-2010s, primarily in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) (Panda et al., 2016; Teerapittayanon et al., 2017). Its application to language models has gained traction with works like BERxiT (Xin et al., 2021) and BE3R (Mangrulkar et al., 2022) for encoder-only models, Elbayad et al. (2020) and CALM (Schuster et al., 2022) for encoder-decoder models, and SkipDecode (Geva et al., 2022; Corro et al., 2023) for decoder-only models.

A distinguishing feature of LayerSkip within this sub-field is its departure from common practices. Many prior early exit approaches introduce auxiliary layers, dedicated LM heads for each exit point, or trainable routers (e.g., Zhang et al., 2019; Elbayad et al., 2020; Schuster et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2023; Raposo et al., 2024). LayerSkip, in contrast, maintains a *single, shared LM head* for all transformer layers and avoids any auxiliary modules. This design choice simplifies training, reduces memory footprint, and eases deployment, making it highly practical for real-world systems.

**Speculative Decoding:** This is a prominent lossless acceleration technique (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) that uses a smaller, faster "draft" model to propose several tokens, which are then verified in parallel by a larger "main" model. While effective, traditional speculative decoding demands maintaining and training two distinct models, leading to higher memory consumption and increased implementation complexity.

LayerSkip introduces a *novel self-speculative decoding* approach. Unlike typical speculative decoding, it *does not require a separate draft model*. Instead, it leverages its early-exit capability to generate draft tokens from the *same* LLM (using a subset of its layers) and then verifies them using the remaining layers. This "self-speculation" paradigm has been explored before (e.g., Zhang et al., 2023, "Draft & Verify"), but LayerSkip's method offers a key advantage: it reuses the KV (Key-Value) cache and activations from the draft stage in the verification stage. This is possible because LayerSkip's draft stage uses the *initial* layers, and verification proceeds sequentially from where the draft left off. Other self-speculative methods (like Zhang et al., 2023) that skip *intermediate* layers cannot benefit from this seamless reuse. This shared compute and activation reusability significantly enhances efficiency.

Furthermore, LayerSkip's training recipe incorporates **Layer Dropout** (also known as stochastic depth), first explored in ResNets (Huang et al., 2016) and later in transformers (Fan et al., 2020; Zhang and He, 2020). LayerSkip's unique application involves *non-uniform dropout rates* that are lower for earlier layers and higher for later layers. This design choice explicitly trains the model to rely more on its initial layers, making them more robust for early exiting. This specific application of layer dropout to decoder-only models and its combination with early exit loss to improve inference efficiency for self-speculative decoding is a novel contribution to the field.

In summary, LayerSkip distinguishes itself by an innovative training recipe that makes LLMs inherently amenable to early exit, combined with a highly efficient self-speculative decoding mechanism that leverages computational reuse, all within a single model architecture. This holistic approach offers significant practical advantages for deploying LLMs at scale.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of LayerSkip is to **significantly speed up the inference of large language models (LLMs)** while maintaining high accuracy and minimizing the computational and memory overhead associated with current acceleration techniques.

The motivation for this research stems from several critical challenges in the widespread deployment of LLMs:

1. **High Computational and Memory Costs:** LLMs, despite their remarkable capabilities, are notoriously resource-intensive. Their high compute and memory requirements translate directly into substantial financial and energy costs when deployed on GPU servers (Samsi et al., 2023). This limits their accessibility and scalability for many applications.
2. **Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-off in Existing Solutions:** While various acceleration techniques like sparsity and quantization exist, they often come with a trade-off in accuracy. Deploying LLMs to commodity hardware like laptops with existing acceleration solutions can suffer from significant accuracy drops (Zhu et al., 2023). The goal is to achieve acceleration with minimal or no accuracy compromise.
3. **Limitations of Current Speculative Decoding:** Speculative decoding is a popular technique that offers lossless acceleration. However, it typically necessitates maintaining two distinct models (a faster, less accurate "draft" model and a larger, more accurate "main" model). This dual-model setup increases memory footprint and complicates implementation, hindering its efficiency in certain scenarios.
4. **Inefficiency in Standard LLM Layer Utilization:** The authors observe that in standard LLMs, not all transformer layers are equally crucial for every token prediction. As illustrated in Figure 2, earlier layers often produce irrelevant predictions or "hesitate" before the final layers converge on the correct token. Even for "easy" tokens, models tend to process all layers, leading to redundant computation. The default training paradigm does not incentivize models to produce correct predictions early, spreading computation across all layers (Voita et al., 2019, 2023).
5. **Lack of Robust Early Exit Capability:** Standard LLM language model (LM) heads are typically trained to process embeddings only from the *last* transformer layer. They are not designed to accurately unembed (convert embedding to token index) from earlier layers. This means that simply "exiting early" from an untrained model leads to a drastic drop in accuracy.
6. **Desire for Simplified Deployment:** Many existing early exit solutions introduce additional auxiliary layers or dedicated LM heads for each early exit point, increasing model complexity and memory during both training and inference. The authors aim for a simpler, more integrated solution.

Based on these motivations, LayerSkip sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

- **Develop a novel training recipe:** This recipe combines layer dropout and an early exit loss function to make LLMs more robust to early exits. Specifically, it aims to encourage earlier layers to learn more effectively and predict tokens accurately, reducing reliance on later layers.
- **Enable accurate early exit inference:** By modifying the training process, the paper aims to increase the accuracy of predictions made at earlier layers during inference, without adding any auxiliary modules or layers. This enables direct speedup by simply skipping later layers.
- **Propose a novel self-speculative decoding solution:** Building upon the enhanced early exit capability, the paper seeks to create a self-speculative decoding method where the model can "draft" tokens using its early layers and "verify" and "correct" them using its remaining layers. This approach aims to reduce memory footprint and benefit from shared compute and activations between the draft and verification stages, overcoming the limitations of traditional speculative decoding.
- **Demonstrate substantial and consistent speedups:** The ultimate goal is to achieve significant inference speedups across various LLM sizes, training paradigms (pretraining from scratch, continual pretraining, finetuning), and tasks, with minimal or no degradation in final output accuracy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LayerSkip presents an end-to-end solution for LLM inference acceleration, structured into three interconnected stages: a novel training recipe, early exit inference, and self-speculative decoding.

#### 3.1. Training using Layer Dropout & Early Exit Loss

The core of LayerSkip's methodology lies in its unique training recipe, designed to make LLMs inherently more robust to early exits and to enable efficient self-speculative decoding.

- **Layer Dropout:** The first modification to standard LLM training is the application of layer dropout, where transformer layers are stochastically skipped during the forward pass. For a given layer `l` at training iteration `t`, its operation `f_l` is applied with a probability `1 - p_l,t`, where `p_l,t` is the dropout rate. If a layer is "dropped," its input is passed directly to the next layer, effectively creating an ensemble of models with varying depths during training.
 - **Non-Uniform Dropout Rates:** Crucially, `p_l,t` is not uniform. It is defined as `p_l,t = S(t)D(l)p_max`.
 - `p_max` is a hyperparameter for the maximum dropout rate.
 - `D(l)` is a per-layer scaling function, designed to increase dropout rate *exponentially* from 0.0 in the first layer (`l=0`) to 1.0 in the last layer (`L-1`). This configuration actively discourages later layers from becoming indispensable, forcing earlier layers to learn more robust and informative representations.
 - `S(t)` is a per-time step scaling function (curriculum). For finetuning or continual pretraining, `S(t)` is set to 1 (no curriculum). For pretraining from scratch, an exponential curriculum `S_exp(t)` is applied, where the dropout rate gradually increases over training steps.
 - **Implementation Detail:** To maintain speed during training, the authors ensure that the same samples are dropped across all GPUs for a given layer at each iteration, enabling efficient batch processing.

- **Early Exit Loss:** To ensure that the model's single, shared Language Model (LM) head (`g`) can accurately "unembed" embeddings from *any* transformer layer, an early exit loss is incorporated during training. This loss directly supervises the outputs of intermediate layers.
 - The total loss is a sum of cross-entropy losses from each layer's output, weighted by `˜e(t, l)`.
 - `˜e(t, l)` is a normalized per-layer loss scale, which emphasizes later layers more. Specifically, the scale `e(l)` increases across layers such that the penalty for later layers is quadratically higher (i.e., `e(l)` is proportional to the sum of scales of all previous layers, plus an `e_scale` hyperparameter). The intuition is that predicting correctly at later layers is easier, so penalizing them more helps ensure earlier layers also learn effectively.
 - **Shared LM Head:** A critical design choice is the use of a *single LM head* for all layers, unlike other early exit approaches that train dedicated heads for each exit point. This simplifies the model, reduces memory consumption for both training and inference, and streamlines deployment.
 - **Early Exit Loss Curriculum:** To prevent early exit losses from slowing down training or reducing the final layer's accuracy, two curricula for `C(t, l)` are explored:
 - **Rotational (`C_rot,R`):** Early exit is enabled for every `R` layers, and this set rotates across iterations. This reduces the number of unembedding operations per iteration.
 - **Gradual (`C_grad`):** Early exit loss is gradually enabled from the last layer (`L-1`) down to the first (`0`) over training.

#### 3.2. Inference using Early Exit

After training with the LayerSkip recipe, the model gains the ability to predict accurately even if inference is stopped prematurely. During autoregressive decoding, LayerSkip can simply run the first `E` transformer layers and then skip directly to the model's shared LM head (`g(x_E)`), bypassing the remaining `L-E` layers. This directly translates to speedup, with `E` being a configurable parameter representing the chosen exit layer.

#### 3.3. Inference using Self-Speculative Decoding

To recover any accuracy loss from pure early exit inference (without increasing model size or complexity), LayerSkip introduces a novel self-speculative decoding algorithm, leveraging the model's newfound robustness to early exits.

- **Self-Drafting:** Instead of using a separate draft model, LayerSkip uses its own early exit capability. It computes the first `d` candidate draft tokens by repeatedly applying the first `E` layers of the model auto-regressively for each token, and then immediately exiting to the LM head. `d` is the "number of speculations."
- **Self-Verification:** Once `d` draft tokens are generated, the crucial verification step occurs. The *entire sequence* (prompt + `d` draft tokens) is fed back into the model. However, instead of recomputing all layers, the verification stage only computes the *remaining* layers (`L-E`) for each token in parallel. This is efficient because speculative decoding's strength lies in parallel verification.
- **Reusing the Cache (KVQ Cache):** A key innovation for efficiency is the seamless reuse of computational states. Since both the drafting and verification stages operate on the *same model* and execute layers in the *same sequential order* (first `E` then `L-E`), the KV (Key-Value) cache generated by the first `E` layers during drafting can be directly passed and reused by the subsequent `L-E` layers during verification. The authors introduce an "exit query cache" alongside the KV cache to store the query vector of the exit layer `E-1`, allowing direct continuation from layer `E` to the final layer `L`. This "KVQ cache" significantly reduces redundant computation and memory footprint compared to traditional speculative decoding setups or other self-speculative methods that might skip intermediate layers.

This integrated approach means LayerSkip trains a single model that, during inference, can flexibly perform early exit for speed (with some accuracy trade-off) or self-speculative decoding for lossless acceleration, all while minimizing memory and computational overhead through clever cache reuse.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

LayerSkip's effectiveness is rigorously evaluated across various LLM sizes (Llama 1.5B, 7B, 13B, Llama3 8B, Llama3.2 1B) and different training regimes, including pretraining from scratch, continual pretraining, and finetuning on domain-specific or task-specific datasets.

#### 4.1. Early Exit Inference Results

The evaluation of early exit inference focuses on the accuracy achieved when models are forced to output predictions from an intermediate layer `E`, effectively skipping later layers.

- **Continual Pretraining (Llama2 7B/13B, Llama3 8B/1B):**
 - **Improved Earlier Layer Accuracy:** LayerSkip consistently and significantly enhances the accuracy of predictions from earlier layers compared to baseline models across a diverse set of evaluation tasks (e.g., BoolQ, PIQA, HellaSwag, ARC, MMLU, NaturalQuestions, HumanEval). This improvement is particularly pronounced for "generation" tasks (like summarization, QA, code generation) where baseline models often yield near-zero accuracy when exiting early (e.g., NaturalQuestions drops from 25.1% to 0.0554% for Llama2 7B baseline but only to 4.07% with LayerSkip).
 - **Minimal Last Layer Accuracy Drop (Llama2):** For Llama2 models, LayerSkip maintains minimal drop in accuracy at the final layer compared to their respective baselines. This confirms that the training recipe does not significantly harm the full model's performance.
 - **Notable Last Layer Drop (Llama3):** For Llama3 models, a larger drop in last layer accuracy is observed. The authors attribute this to the inherently higher perplexity of earlier layers in baseline Llama3 models (due to more pretraining tokens), making the task of improving early layer accuracy while maintaining final layer performance more challenging. This suggests LayerSkip could be more beneficial if applied during initial pretraining of such large models.

- **Pretraining from Scratch (Llama2 1.5B/7B on 26B tokens):**
 - LayerSkip's training components (layer dropout (LD), early exit loss (EE), or their combination (LD+EE)) lead to consistently higher accuracy on earlier layers compared to models pretrained from scratch without LayerSkip.
 - For the last layer, LayerSkip shows a slight drop or sometimes an increase in accuracy, depending on the downstream task. This demonstrates that LayerSkip effectively trains the model to be robust across different depths from the very beginning.

- **Finetuning on Code Data (Llama1 7B):**
 - LayerSkip, especially with both layer dropout and early exit loss, shows a significant improvement in early layer accuracy for coding tasks (HumanEval, MBPP) while maintaining nearly identical last-layer accuracy compared to the baseline finetuned model. The ability to use a higher `e_scale` (1.0) for domain-specific finetuning likely contributed to this strong performance.

- **Finetuning on Task-Specific Dataset (Llama 1.5B on TOPv2):**
 - For semantic parsing on TOPv2, baseline models entirely fail (0% Exact Match) when layers are removed. In stark contrast, LayerSkip enables robust early exit inference, achieving up to 77% EM at layer 12, demonstrating the approach's capability to make intermediate layers highly functional for complex, task-specific generation.

#### 4.2. Self-Speculative Decoding Results

The self-speculative decoding algorithm is evaluated for its ability to provide lossless acceleration.

- **Significant Speedups with Lossless Accuracy:** LayerSkip achieves substantial inference speedups, ranging from **1.34x to 2.16x** across various tasks (CNN/DM summarization, XSUM summarization, HumanEval coding, TOPv2 semantic parsing) and model sizes. Crucially, these speedups are achieved with *no drop in final output accuracy* (e.g., ROUGE-2, EM), matching or slightly exceeding the autoregressive baseline.
- **Competitive with External Speculative Decoding:** When compared to "Draft & Verify" (Zhang et al., 2023), a similar self-speculative approach, LayerSkip demonstrates competitive performance. It achieves a higher speedup on CNN/DM (1.86x for Llama2 7B vs. 1.56x for Draft & Verify on Llama2 13B) and slightly lower on XSUM (1.34x vs. 1.48x). This highlights the efficiency gained from LayerSkip's unified model and cache reuse.
- **High Token Acceptance Rates:** The self-speculative decoding process exhibits high token acceptance rates (e.g., 68.9% for Llama2 7B on CNN/DM, up to 98.9% on TOPv2), indicating that the early-exit drafts are frequently correct, leading to efficient parallel verification.
- **CPU Inference Performance:** The benefits of LayerSkip extend beyond GPU, showing a near 2x speedup on CPU inference as well (Table 11), underscoring its broad applicability to resource-constrained environments.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Scaling with Pretraining Tokens:** A critical finding is that, by default, the perplexity of *middle layers* in LLMs drastically *increases* as more pretraining tokens are used. This implies that earlier layers tend to "diverge" in their predictions as training progresses. LayerSkip's early exit loss and layer dropout effectively mitigate this divergence, maintaining or improving the perplexity of intermediate layers while still improving the final layer's performance. This provides strong motivation for incorporating LayerSkip into future large-scale LLM pretraining.
- **KV Cache in Self-Speculation:** The unique KVQ cache reuse mechanism significantly contributes to efficiency, consistently saving 9-20ms per token depending on the task. This validates the design choice of sequential layer execution for draft and verification.
- **Layer Dropout Configurations:** An exponentially increasing layer dropout rate across layers (as used in LayerSkip) leads to lower training loss compared to a constant average dropout rate, suggesting better training dynamics and model optimization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

LayerSkip presents a significant advancement in the efficiency of Large Language Models, with profound implications for their deployment and broader impact across various applications.

1. **Enabling Widespread LLM Deployment:** By offering substantial inference speedups (up to 2.16x) without compromising accuracy, LayerSkip directly tackles the high computational and memory costs that bottleneck LLM deployment. This makes LLMs more financially viable and energy-efficient for large-scale production environments, such as those at Meta, and could accelerate their adoption in resource-constrained settings like mobile or edge devices.
2. **Lossless Acceleration with Simplified Architecture:** Unlike many other acceleration techniques that involve accuracy trade-offs or complex multi-model setups, LayerSkip's self-speculative decoding is *lossless* in terms of final output quality. Crucially, it achieves this by training a *single model*, eliminating the memory and implementation overhead associated with maintaining separate draft and main models. The clever reuse of KV and exit query caches between the draft and verification stages is a notable innovation that optimizes compute and memory.
3. **Enhanced Model Robustness and Flexibility:** The LayerSkip training recipe—combining non-uniform layer dropout and early exit loss with a shared LM head—trains LLMs to be inherently more robust to early exits. This effectively transforms a single LLM into an "ensemble of models of various depths," allowing for dynamic trade-offs between speed and accuracy at inference time. This built-in flexibility is valuable for adapting LLMs to diverse latency requirements.
4. **Influence on LLM Training Paradigms:** The finding that standard LLM pretraining causes earlier layers to "diverge" and become less accurate as training progresses is a significant insight. LayerSkip's ability to counteract this by making earlier layers more robust and functionally independent could reshape how future LLMs are pretrained. It suggests that architectural or training modifications might be necessary from the outset to build models that are efficient and performant across all depths. This could lead to a new standard for foundation model training, where models are designed for efficient multi-level inference from scratch.
5. **Broad Applicability:** The method's effectiveness across different LLM sizes (from 1.5B to 13B) and various training scenarios (pretraining, continual pretraining, finetuning on diverse domains like general language, code, and semantic parsing) demonstrates its versatility. This suggests that LayerSkip is not a niche solution but a generalizable technique applicable to a wide range of LLM use cases.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** LayerSkip opens several avenues for future research. Improving the accuracy of very early layers could unlock even greater speedups. Exploring dynamic conditions to determine the optimal exit layer for each token (as opposed to a fixed `E`) could further enhance efficiency and acceptance rates in self-speculative decoding. Furthermore, integrating LayerSkip with other parameter-efficient finetuning techniques like LoRA could lead to even more efficient and adaptable LLM solutions.

In conclusion, LayerSkip represents a significant step towards democratizing LLM access and deployment by making inference more efficient and sustainable. Its innovative training and inference strategies offer a practical and impactful solution for the ongoing challenge of LLM scalability.
