# RRLS : Robust Reinforcement Learning Suite

> [!summary] Summary
> RRLS (Robust Reinforcement Learning Suite) is a Gymnasium-compatible benchmark for robust RL built on MuJoCo. It supplies six continuous-control tasks, each with 1D/2D/3D parameter uncertainty sets plus RARL-style force perturbations, wrappers for domain randomization and adversarial two-player training, and a standardized mesh-based evaluation protocol. Benchmarking TD3, DR, RARL, NR-MDP and M2TD3 shows M2TD3 leads worst-case performance while DR leads average-case.

## Problem

- Robust RL optimizes worst-case performance across adversarial dynamics, yet the field has no standardized benchmark, so results are hard to reproduce or compare across papers.
- Existing suites like Gym, DeepMind Control, ProcGen, D4RL and SafetyGym target generalization, offline, or safety settings, none expose uncertainty sets over transition dynamics.
- Prior robust methods each define their own environments, perturbation ranges and evaluation protocols, letting seeds, initial states and kernel choices dominate reported outcome statistics.

## Method

- ==ModifiedParamsEnv== interface adds `set_params`/`get_params` to Gymnasium, letting an adversary read and write MuJoCo physics parameters (masses, friction) inside the interaction loop.
- Six MuJoCo tasks (Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, HumanoidStandup, InvertedPendulum, Walker) ship with two non-rectangular ==uncertainty-set== families, parameter ranges in 1D/2D/3D and ==RARL==-style applied-force sets.
- Wrappers ==DomainRandomization==, ==Adversarial== (zero-sum Markov game action-space extension) and ==ProbabilisticActionRobust== (convex mix a_pr = alpha*a + (1-alpha)*a_bar), plus `generate_evaluation_set` building a uniform parameter mesh.

## Results

- On normalized static worst-case scores over 10 seeds, **M2TD3** averages **1.0** versus DR **0.52**, RARL **0.33**, TD3 **0.0** and NR-MDP **-0.58** across five environments.
- On average-case scores, DR leads at **1.68** versus M2TD3 **1.0**, while RARL drops to **-0.3**, performing worse than plain TD3 in most environments despite better worst-case scores.
- Variance often blocks conclusions: HumanoidStandup shows **3.32** std for M2TD3 and **4.1** for DR; DR beats M2TD3 on HalfCheetah worst-case (**1.07** vs **1.0**) non-significantly.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversarial robustness and average performance trade off: a method winning worst-case evaluation can degrade below a non-robust baseline on the average distribution, limiting practical use.
> - Domain randomization remains a strong worst-case baseline despite never optimizing for it, so robust algorithms must be measured against it, not only against vanilla agents.
> - High seed variance in robust control evaluation means single-run comparisons are unreliable; mesh-based evaluation sets and many seeds are needed for defensible claims.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities across various domains; however, its application in real-world scenarios is often hindered by its susceptibility to environmental uncertainties and dynamic conditions. This limitation necessitates the development of control policies that can maintain performance even when confronted with adversarial or unforeseen changes in the environment.

The field of Robust Reinforcement Learning (RRL) addresses this challenge by focusing on methods to learn policies that achieve optimal worst-case performance across a defined range of adversarial conditions. For example, in safety-critical applications such as aircraft control, a policy must reliably perform under various atmospheric conditions without requiring re-training. Robustness, in this context, prioritizes consistent performance without adaptation, distinguishing it from concepts like resilience. The framework for RRL is typically formalized through robust Markov Decision Processes (RMDPs), where transition dynamics are subject to uncertainty.

The broader RL community has benefited from standardized benchmarks such as the Atari Arcade Learning Environment (ALE), OpenAI Gym, Gymnasium, and the DeepMind Control Suite (DMC). These benchmarks have been instrumental in fostering reproducibility, comparability, and accelerated development of RL algorithms across discrete and continuous action spaces. Specialized benchmarks, including DeepMind Lab for 3D navigation, ProcGen for generalization, D4RL for offline RL, Meta-World for multi-task learning, and SafetyGym for constrained control, further segment the evaluation landscape.

Despite these advancements in general RL benchmarking, the specific sub-field of robust RL has largely lacked a unified and standardized set of benchmarks. This absence impedes the rigorous evaluation, comparison, and replication of results for robust RL algorithms, making it difficult to assess their true capabilities and limitations. The presented work, Robust Reinforcement Learning Suite (RRLS), aims to fill this gap by providing a standardized benchmark suite. This initiative seeks to facilitate consistent testing of various robust RL algorithms, including nominal-based approaches, methods framed as two-player games (where an adversary minimizes reward), and Domain Randomization techniques, thereby promoting progress and enabling more reliable assessments of algorithm performance within the robust RL research domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a standardized benchmark for robust reinforcement learning algorithms. This objective is driven by several motivations aimed at addressing critical gaps and challenges within the field:

1. **Address the Lack of Standardized Benchmarks:** The research community in robust RL has lacked a consistent set of environments and evaluation procedures. This absence has historically hindered the reproducibility and comparability of experimental results, making it difficult for researchers to accurately assess the advancements and limitations of new algorithms. RRLS aims to rectify this by providing a unified platform.
2. **Facilitate Reproducible and Comparable Experiments:** By offering a fixed set of tasks and uncertainty configurations, RRLS intends to enable researchers to conduct experiments that are easily replicated by others. This standardization is expected to promote a more rigorous scientific process, allowing for direct comparisons between different robust RL methodologies.
3. **Support Evaluation of State-of-the-Art Robust RL Algorithms:** The benchmark is designed to be compatible with, and suitable for evaluating, current and future robust RL algorithms. This includes methods that learn robust policies against adversarial environments, ensuring that new contributions can be tested against a common baseline.
4. **Promote Robustness for Real-World Deployment:** A fundamental motivation for robust RL is to develop agents that can operate reliably in environments characterized by uncertainties, dynamic changes, or adversarial conditions. The limited applicability of conventional RL algorithms in such real-world scenarios underscores the necessity for robust policies that do not require continuous re-training. RRLS aims to foster research that enhances this real-world applicability, particularly in safety-critical domains where performance degradation is unacceptable.
5. **Enable Analysis of Algorithm Strengths and Weaknesses:** By providing a diverse set of environments and uncertainty models, RRLS seeks to allow researchers to identify the specific conditions under which different robust RL algorithms perform well or poorly. This comparative analysis can reveal the inherent trade-offs (e.g., between worst-case performance and average performance) of various methods, contributing to a deeper understanding of their underlying mechanisms.
6. **Ensure Expandability and Flexibility:** While providing initial environments and uncertainty sets, RRLS is also motivated by the need for future adaptability. The design allows for easy integration of new environments, tasks, and uncertainty models, ensuring its long-term relevance to the evolving robust RL research landscape.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Robust Reinforcement Learning Suite (RRLS) is introduced as a benchmark suite built upon Mujoco environments, extending the Gymnasium API. This extension includes two primary methods, `set_params` and `get_params`, within a `ModifiedParamsEnv` interface, which are crucial for dynamically altering environment parameters.

**RRLS Architecture:**
The RRLS architecture operates as an interaction loop involving an adversary, agent, and the environment. The adversary first retrieves and sets specific parameters from a defined uncertainty set into the environment using the `ModifiedParamsEnv` interface. The agent then interacts with the environment based on its current state. The Mujoco Physics Engine subsequently updates the environment state, which is then observed by the agent, completing a single step in the interaction.

**Environments:**
RRLS includes six continuous control tasks derived from Mujoco, each presenting distinct action and observation spaces. These environments are:
- **Ant:** A 3D robot tasked with moving forward, featuring an 8-dimensional action space and a 27-dimensional observation space.
- **HalfCheetah:** A 2D robot focused on running forward quickly, with a 6-dimensional action space and a 17-dimensional observation space.
- **Hopper:** A 2D one-legged figure aiming to hop forward, with a 3-dimensional action space and an 11-dimensional observation space.
- **Humanoid Stand Up:** A 3D bipedal robot that starts lying down and must stand up and maintain balance, possessing a 17-dimensional action space and a 376-dimensional observation space.
- **Inverted Pendulum:** A cart-pole system where the goal is to balance a pole, with a 1-dimensional action space and a 4-dimensional observation space.
- **Walker:** A 2D two-legged figure designed to walk forward, with a 6-dimensional action space and a 17-dimensional observation space.
In all environments, observation spaces consist of positional values followed by velocities of various body parts.

**Uncertainty Sets:**
RRLS provides non-rectangular uncertainty sets to model adversarial conditions, inspired by existing work on M2TD3 and RARL. These sets involve modifications to physical parameters within the Mujoco engine.
- **M2TD3-inspired Uncertainty Sets:** These sets involve modifications to environment parameters such as torso mass, leg mass, world friction, and thigh mass. For each environment, three variants (1D, 2D, and 3D) are provided, representing increasing complexity in the parameter space (e.g., Ant 1 involves only torso mass, while Ant 3 includes torso mass, front left leg mass, and front right leg mass). The ranges for these parameters are specifically defined (e.g., torso mass in HumanoidStandUp can range from 0.1 to 16.0).
- **RARL-inspired Uncertainty Sets:** These sets introduce destabilizing forces at specific points of the robot (e.g., torso, feet, legs). The adversary applies forces within specified ranges (e.g., [−3.0, 3.0] for x and y forces on various body parts) to perturb the system dynamics.

**Wrappers:**
To facilitate the implementation and comparison of robust RL baselines, RRLS provides several environment wrappers:
- **`ModifiedParamsEnv`:** The core interface allowing dynamic parameter adjustment via `set_params` and `get_params`.
- **`DomainRandomization` wrapper:** Enables domain randomization by sampling environment parameters from the uncertainty set at the beginning of each episode. By default, parameters are sampled uniformly if no specific function is provided.
- **`Adversarial` wrapper:** Transforms an environment into a zero-sum Markov game, extending the action space to include adversarial actions that modify transition kernel parameters within a specified uncertainty set. This is suitable for methods like RARL.
- **`ProbabilisticActionRobust` wrapper:** Defines the adversary's action space as sharing the agent's action space. The final action applied to the environment is a convex sum of the agent's action and the adversary's action (`a_pr = αa + (1 − α)¯a`). This wrapper is designed for algorithms such as NR-MDP or RAP.

**Evaluation Procedure:**
RRLS proposes a systematic evaluation approach using a `generate_evaluation_set` function. This function creates a list of evaluation environments by spanning a uniform mesh over the defined parameter set for static conditions. The `nb_mesh_dim` parameter controls the granularity of this mesh. A default evaluation set is provided for each uncertainty configuration to enable assessment of both worst-case and average-case performance. Agent policies are run for multiple trajectories in each environment within the evaluation set.

**Benchmarking Robust RL Algorithms:**
The paper evaluates five baseline algorithms: TD3 (Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic policy gradient), Domain Randomization (DR), Noise Robust MDP (NR-MDP), Robust Adversarial Reinforcement Learning (RARL), and Max-Min TD3 (M2TD3). These baselines were tested in the Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, HumanoidStandup, and Walker environments using the 3D uncertainty sets normalized between^3. All baselines utilized a consistent TD3 architecture with two 256-unit linear layers for both the actor and critic networks. Experiments ran for five million steps, with performance metrics gathered after convergence. Results were averaged over ten distinct random seeds. Scores were normalized relative to TD3's performance (as the minimum baseline) and M2TD3's performance (as the target), using the formula `(v - v_TD3) / (|v_M2TD3 - v_TD3|)`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RRLS involved benchmarking five robust RL algorithms: TD3, Domain Randomization (DR), NR-MDP, RARL, and M2TD3, across six Mujoco environments using challenging 3D uncertainty sets.

**Static Worst-Case Performance:**
The results, normalized relative to TD3 and M2TD3, indicated the following:
- **General Performance:** M2TD3, RARL, and DR generally exhibited better worst-case performance compared to vanilla TD3, which served as the baseline.
- **DR vs. RARL:** Domain Randomization often outperformed RARL in worst-case scenarios, with exceptions in HalfCheetah, Hopper, and Walker environments.
- **M2TD3's Performance:** M2TD3 generally achieved the highest worst-case performance among the tested baselines. A notable exception was the HalfCheetah environment, where DR showed a slightly, though not statistically significant, better score. This finding for HalfCheetah aligns with previous observations that some environments are inherently balanced, rendering conservative value function training less critical for robust performance.
- **NR-MDP's Performance:** NR-MDP generally showed negative normalized scores, indicating poorer worst-case performance than TD3 in most environments.
- **Variance:** High variance was observed in the evaluations for certain environments, particularly HumanoidStandup (e.g., M2TD3 with a variance of 3.32, DR with 4.1), which complicated the drawing of definitive statistical conclusions regarding performance differences.

**Static Average Performance:**
When evaluating the average performance across a uniform distribution of environments in the uncertainty set:
- **DR's Superiority:** Domain Randomization consistently outperformed other algorithms on average. This outcome was anticipated, as DR is designed to optimize policy performance across a distribution of environments rather than specifically for worst-case scenarios.
- **RARL's Drawback:** RARL performed worse on average than standard TD3 in most environments (except HumanoidStandup), despite demonstrating better worst-case scores. This indicates a potential trade-off where algorithms explicitly optimizing for worst-case scenarios might yield policies with reduced average applicability.
- **M2TD3's Balanced Approach:** M2TD3 maintained better average performance than TD3, confirming its capability to optimize for worst-case conditions while preserving overall average scores.
- **NR-MDP's Average Performance:** Similar to its worst-case performance, NR-MDP generally exhibited lower average scores than other methods.

**Dynamic Adversaries:**
RRLS allows for the evaluation of policies against dynamic adversaries, which is relevant in cases where the transition kernel uncertainty is non-rectangular. The paper demonstrated this by reporting raw scores for RARL and NR-MDP when evaluated against their respective adversaries over ten episodes. For instance, in HumanoidStandup, RARL scored 9.84 ± 3.36, while NR-MDP scored 9.37 ± 0.14. It was noted that such comparisons are illustrative of RRLS's capability to assess dynamic adversarial interactions but should not be interpreted as a direct performance dominance of one algorithm over another due to differing uncertainty sets used during their training.

**Training Curves:**
Analysis of training curves (e.g., for the Walker environment, with others in the appendix) revealed:
- **Initial Learning Speed:** TD3 and DR exhibited steep learning curves and rapid initial progress.
- **Robust Algorithm Learning:** Robust counterparts like M2TD3 and RARL demonstrated a more significant delay in learning and stabilization, with M2TD3 eventually achieving the highest performance at five million steps. RARL also showed delayed stabilization.
- **Variance in Training:** Significant variance across different random seeds was observed in the training curves (e.g., for M2TD3 and RARL), highlighting the challenges in consistently comparing robust RL methods throughout their learning process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of the Robust Reinforcement Learning Suite (RRLS) carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of reinforcement learning:

1. **Standardization of Robust RL Benchmarking:** RRLS directly addresses a recognized gap in the robust RL community: the absence of a standardized and comprehensive benchmark. By providing a consistent framework, it enables reproducible and comparable experiments, which is fundamental for scientific progress. This standardization is poised to become a reference point, similar to how OpenAI Gym or the DeepMind Control Suite shaped general RL research.

2. **Facilitation of Research and Development:** The benchmark's provision of six continuous control tasks, coupled with predefined uncertainty sets and adaptable wrappers, offers a robust platform for evaluating new robust RL algorithms. This will enable researchers to more efficiently test novel methodologies, identify their strengths and weaknesses across diverse conditions, and accelerate the development cycle for robust control policies.

3. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Comparability:** By standardizing evaluation procedures, environments, and uncertainty models, RRLS significantly improves the reproducibility of experimental results. This allows for direct, fair comparisons between different algorithms, fostering a clearer understanding of their relative performance and underlying mechanisms. The public availability of the source code further supports this objective.

4. **Practical Insights into Algorithm Performance:** The systematic comparison of various robust RL algorithms (TD3, DR, NR-MDP, RARL, M2TD3) within RRLS provides practical insights into their performance characteristics, including worst-case and average-case returns. The findings, such as the trade-offs between worst-case optimization and average performance (e.g., RARL vs. DR) and the identification of high variance in some environments, inform researchers about the practical applicability and limitations of existing methods. This can guide the design of future algorithms that better balance robustness with overall performance.

5. **Addressing Real-World Applicability:** The core motivation behind robust RL is to bridge the gap between simulated environments and real-world deployment, where uncertainties are prevalent. By offering environments with controlled adversarial conditions, RRLS helps develop and validate policies that are more resilient to unpredictable external factors, thereby increasing the potential for deploying RL in safety-critical applications such as robotics, autonomous vehicles, and aerospace control.

6. **Expandability for Future Research:** The modular design of RRLS, with its `ModifiedParamsEnv` interface and wrappers, ensures its expandability. Researchers can easily integrate new environments, custom uncertainty sets, and additional robust RL algorithms, allowing the benchmark to evolve with the field and remain relevant to emerging research questions.

In summary, RRLS is positioned to become a valuable resource for the RL community, fostering a more rigorous, comparable, and practically relevant approach to developing and evaluating robust reinforcement learning algorithms.
