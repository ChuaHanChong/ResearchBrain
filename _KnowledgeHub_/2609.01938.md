# One Demonstration, Many Objects: Generalizing Manipulation via Local Contact Geometry

> [!summary] Summary
> DemoMimic learns dexterous manipulation from a single human demonstration by retargeting it into a robot reference, refining that reference with residual RL under contact-centric rewards for surface-normal alignment and sustained contact, then distilling into an RGB high-level wrist-trajectory policy plus a wrist-depth low-level policy. One real-world policy reaches 71% success across 16 objects, four tasks, and two robot hands.

## Problem

- Prior sim-to-real dexterous RL rarely rewards contact explicitly, or gates it coarsely by contact-point counts, so simulated policies form fragile contacts that fail on hardware.
- Kinematic retargeting of human demonstrations is open-loop: small geometry or physical-property mismatches prevent intended contacts, and nothing recovers when contact deviates during execution.
- Existing demonstration-conditioned policies assume object or hand pose estimation at inference and stay tied to the single training instance, not generalizing across scale, mass, friction.

## Method

- ==Residual RL== in simulation over ==retargeted reference joint trajectories==, with ==anisotropic asset scaling== augmenting the references so one policy covers objects of differing size.
- Contact-centric rewards: an **Alignment Reward** matching hand-link and object surface normals at current and future contact timesteps, plus a **Sustained Contact Reward** growing quadratically with force-thresholded contact streaks.
- Distillation into a ==diffusion== low-level policy over wrist stereo depth and proprioception, guided by an RGB high-level policy that predicts open-loop wrist trajectories from ==Cosmos==-augmented images.

## Results

- **71%** average real-world success over **16 objects** and **four tasks** at **20 rollouts** each, with **76%** on the Sharpa hand versus **65%** on the Tesollo hand.
- Adapted DexMachina and HERMES baselines score higher in simulation but transfer far worse, showing misplaced contacts or insufficient force; DemoMimic has the smallest sim-to-real drop.
- Removing the sustained-contact reward pushes drop rate near **100%** on open the box; removing alignment broadens force histograms and increases drops, despite near-ceiling simulation scores.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulation success can hide contact-modeling slack; reward terms that look inert in simulation may be exactly what decides real-hardware transfer.
> - Conditioning low-level control on local contact geometry rather than global object pose lets a single policy span object scale, mass, and friction.
> - Generalization tracks local contact structure, not object category: the one failing box had a curved lid lip, dropping success to 39%.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation aims to enable robots to perform intricate tasks requiring human-level dexterity. While parallel-jaw grippers have achieved success in structured routines, many real-world interactions, such as tool use, assembly, or part inspection, necessitate multi-fingered dexterous robot hands. A significant challenge in this area is the difficulty of collecting large-scale training data for dexterous hands. Traditional teleoperation methods face limitations due to finger-object occlusions, limited tactile feedback, and the need for diverse, high-quality demonstrations across varied objects and interaction styles.

Learning from human demonstrations has emerged as a strategy to provide scalable priors on object interaction and contact strategies. Prior work in this domain can be broadly categorized. One approach involves heuristic retargeting of human demonstrations to generate robot-executable, open-loop hand-arm trajectories. However, this method often proves insufficient for dexterous manipulation, as minor mismatches in geometry or physical properties can prevent intended contacts, and it lacks closed-loop recovery mechanisms for deviations.

A second, more recent approach treats human demonstrations as reference motions, refining them with reinforcement learning (RL), typically through a residual policy trained in simulation. While promising, this approach frequently encounters limitations in real-world deployment. These limitations include making impractical assumptions, such as executing open-loop trajectories or relying on precise object pose estimation, and experiencing poor transfer of learned motions from simulation to hardware. Furthermore, the performance of these methods is often restricted to the specific object instance used during training, exhibiting poor generalization to unseen objects.

This paper addresses these identified gaps by focusing on improving real-world sim-to-real transfer and enhancing generalization across object instances. It specifically highlights the absence of explicit rewards incentivizing precise contact in many existing sim-to-real RL methods, which contributes to weak real-world performance, and the general deficiency in policies to generalize to novel object instances. By proposing a contact-centric approach, the work aims to bridge these gaps, offering a method for robust and generalizable dexterous manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a dexterous manipulation policy that can learn from a single human demonstration and effectively generalize to different, previously unseen objects. This policy is intended to operate in real-world scenarios, demonstrating robustness to variations in object shape, scale, mass, and friction, provided that the local contact structure relevant to the task remains consistent.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in current dexterous robotic manipulation systems:

1. **Data Scarcity for Dexterous Hands:** Collecting extensive, high-quality training data for multi-fingered robot hands is inherently difficult. Traditional methods like teleoperation are labor-intensive and prone to issues such as finger-object occlusions and limited tactile feedback, making them difficult to scale for diverse manipulation tasks and objects. Human demonstrations offer a more scalable alternative by providing rich priors on how to interact with objects.

2. **Limitations of Existing Demonstration-Based Methods:**
 - **Heuristic Retargeting:** Simply replaying human motions on a robot often fails due to embodiment differences, minor geometric mismatches, or variations in physical properties between simulation and the real world. These methods typically lack closed-loop control for error recovery.
 - **RL Refinement:** While RL can refine retargeted trajectories, existing sim-to-real RL methods often suffer from two key shortcomings:
 - **Lack of Precise Contact Incentives:** Many methods do not explicitly reward precise contact formation and maintenance. This can lead to policies that exploit simulator-specific artifacts, resulting in fragile contacts in simulation that fail catastrophically when deployed on real hardware, thus contributing to a significant sim-to-real gap.
 - **Poor Generalization:** Policies trained on specific object instances often perform poorly when presented with new instances, even within the same category, if their physical properties (e.g., scale, mass, friction) vary. This limits the practical applicability of such policies.

3. **Need for Robust Real-World Transfer:** To move beyond simulation-only results, robotic manipulation policies require mechanisms that explicitly account for the discrepancies between simulated and real-world physics and perception. Ensuring robust contact is a critical aspect of this.

To address these motivations, the authors propose DemoMimic, a framework designed to learn policies that emphasize the geometry local to contact points. This contact-centric approach aims to:
- Encourage precise and sustained contact through specialized reward functions, thereby improving sim-to-real consistency.
- Enable generalization to diverse objects by focusing on local contact geometry, which is assumed to be consistent across objects relevant to a specific manipulation task.
- Reduce reliance on privileged information at inference, such as object pose estimation, by utilizing egocentric visual inputs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DemoMimic (Dexterous Motion Mimic) is a sim-to-real reinforcement learning (RL) framework designed to learn dexterous manipulation policies from a single human demonstration. Its methodology involves three main phases: Residual RL training in simulation, distillation into imitation learning policies, and a two-level inference mechanism.

#### 3.1. Training: Residual RL in Simulation

The initial phase involves training an RL policy in simulation to reproduce a demonstrated object trajectory while the robot hand mimics the demonstrated human hand motion.

- **Reference Creation:**
 - Human demonstrations provide an object state sequence (position, rotation, articulation) and human hand keypoints.
 - These human keypoints are kinematically retargeted to robot joint trajectories ({q_ref}), accounting for embodiment differences.
 - The retargeted trajectories undergo trajectory optimization and post-processing, including smoothing and the addition of reaching and leaving motions.
 - For tasks not covered by existing datasets, manual clip creation using Meta Quest hand tracking and FoundationPose for object tracking is performed.
 - To promote robustness, anisotropic scaling of object assets is applied, along with geometric post-processing for certain objects.
- **Residual RL Policy Training (π_RL):**
 - An RL policy (π_RL) learns residual actions (∆q_t) on top of the reference joint trajectories (q_ref_t,i), such that the command executed is q_cmd_t = q_ref_t,i + ∆q_t.
 - Training incorporates substantial domain randomization, including variations in initial object pose, mass, friction coefficients (object and hand), articulation joint friction loss, external perturbations, and correlated observation noise. This aims to improve robustness and sim-to-real transfer.
 - The policy is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) augmented with a behavioral cloning loss.
 - **Observation Space:** Includes robot proprioception (joint errors, positions, velocities, keypoint positions, wrist pose, reference joint targets), object state (part poses, angular/linear velocities, articulation joint positions, state difference to target), fingertip keypoint-to-object-surface distance, contact forces, and episode progress.
 - **Action Space:** 54-dimensional residual actions for arm and finger joints, clipped and scaled before being added to reference joint positions.
- **Reward Design:** The environment reward function is a weighted sum of task completion and contact-centric terms, with a smoothness penalty: r(t) = w_task * r_task(t) + w_alignment * r_alignment(t) + w_sustained * r_sustained(t) - w_p * p_smooth(t).
 - **Task Reward (r_task):** Measures the agreement between the achieved object state in simulation and the reference object state from the demonstration. It exponentially penalizes position, rotation, and articulation errors.
 - **Alignment Reward (AR):** Encourages contacting hand links to align their surface normals with the corresponding object surface normals. It computes current cosine similarity between normals and includes a future alignment term based on the next demonstrated contact timestep, promoting correct hand orientation before contact.
 - **Sustained Contact Reward (SCR):** Derived from demonstration-extracted contact windows, this reward encourages continuous contact. It tracks the number of consecutive in-contact timesteps, increasing quadratically with the streak length, provided contact force exceeds a minimum threshold. This aims to prevent intermittent contact behavior that may appear stable in simulation but fail in reality.
 - **Smoothness Penalty (p_smooth):** Penalizes high arm and finger velocities and large residual actions to encourage fluid motion.

#### 3.2. Imitation Learning Policy Training (Distillation)

The high-performance residual RL policy (π_RL), which relies on privileged state information, is then distilled into student policies for real-world deployment under partial observability.

- **Dataset Generation (D_RL):** The trained π_RL is rolled out in diverse simulation environments to generate a dataset containing executed actions (q_cmd), robot observations (wrist-mounted depth images I_t, proprioceptive state q_t), human wrist reference trajectories (τ_H), and initial ego-view RGB observations (I_rgb).
- **Depth-conditioned Low-Level IL Policy (π_L):** A Diffusion Policy is trained on D_RL. This policy takes processed wrist-mounted depth images, robot proprioceptive states, and the human wrist reference trajectory as inputs. It outputs absolute joint positions (q_cmd), rather than residuals, for direct execution. It also incorporates a history of recent and sampled robot states for temporal context.
- **High-Level Guidance Policy (π_H):** For each task, a separate high-level guidance policy (π_H) is trained to generate the wrist reference trajectory (τ_H) from an initial ego-view RGB image. To bridge the sim-to-real visual gap, the initial RGB images from the simulation dataset are augmented using Cosmos-Transfer-2.5, a model that generates realistic RGB images conditioned on simulation images and text prompts describing scene textures and backgrounds.

#### 3.3. Inference

At inference time, the system operates as follows:

1. An initial ego-view RGB image of the scene is captured.
2. The high-level policy (π_H) predicts an open-loop wrist reference trajectory (τ_H) from this initial RGB image.
3. Throughout the rollout, τ_H conditions the low-level policy (π_L).
4. At each timestep, wrist-mounted stereo RGB pairs are processed by FoundationStereo to obtain higher-quality depth images, which are then used as input to π_L.
5. The low-level policy (π_L) takes these depth images and robot proprioception to predict and execute actions (absolute joint positions) until the task horizon is reached. The depth images are clipped for values beyond 0.4m, focusing on local geometry.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of DemoMimic was evaluated through extensive real-world experiments across diverse objects, tasks, and robot embodiments.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

The experimental setup utilized two Franka arms, each equipped with either a Tesollo DF-5F or Sharpa five-fingered robotic hand, along with wrist-mounted stereo cameras and a single egocentric camera. Four manipulation tasks were investigated: "open the box," "lift the lid," "push the box," and "move the bottle," each requiring precise contact placement and force control. Human demonstrations were sourced from an existing motion-capture dataset (ARCTIC) or collected manually.

Performance metrics included a task-specific success score (quantified as one minus the relative deviation from the human reference for articulation/distance tasks, and a four-stage score for "move the bottle") and a "drop rate" (fraction of rollouts where the object was lost after task-relevant motion began).

For comparison, two baselines were used: HERMES* and DEXMACHINA*. These baselines were adapted by incorporating a residual action space and the task reward used in DemoMimic to enhance their simulation performance. Ablation studies were also conducted on DemoMimic's reward components: OURS_−AR (without alignment reward), OURS_−SCR (without sustained contact reward), and OURS_−AR,−SCR (only task reward).

#### 4.2. Generalization to Diverse Object Instances

DemoMimic was tested on 16 unique objects, encompassing variations in material, weight, and size, across the four tasks.
- DemoMimic achieved an average real-world success rate of 71% across all tasks and objects.
- Consistent performance was observed for "lift the lid," "push the box," and "move the bottle" tasks across different object instances.
- A notable performance drop (39% success) occurred for the "robot-hand box" in the "open the box" task. This was attributed to a curved lid lip, which significantly altered the local contact geometry required for manipulation, differentiating it from the training instances.
- Performance was comparable across the two robot hand embodiments: 76% success with the Sharpa hand and 65% with the Tesollo hand.
These results indicate that DemoMimic exhibits robustness to object-level physical variations (material, weight, size) as long as the task-relevant local contact geometry remains consistent with the training data.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Prior Sim-to-Real RL Methods

When compared against the adapted baselines, HERMES* and DEXMACHINA*, trained and evaluated on a single object scale, DemoMimic demonstrated improved real-world transfer.
- While both baselines performed effectively in simulation for tasks like "open the box," they experienced a substantial degradation in performance when transferred to hardware, exhibiting lower success rates and higher drop rates.
- Analysis of contact quality revealed that HERMES* often resulted in poor contact placement or misaligned contact. DEXMACHINA* achieved better alignment but frequently applied insufficient force in real-world execution.
- In contrast, DemoMimic consistently produced more precisely placed and forceful contacts, contributing to more stable real-world task execution.
- For "lift the lid," baselines transferred better than for "open the box" but still showed substantial degradation compared to their simulation performance. DemoMimic, while also experiencing some degradation, maintained a smaller sim-to-real gap.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study of Reward Terms

The ablation studies highlighted the importance of DemoMimic's contact-centric reward terms for real-world performance.
- **Sustained Contact Reward (SCR):** Removing SCR (OURS_−SCR and OURS_−AR,−SCR) led to nearly 100% drop rates for "open the box." This suggests that without SCR, policies might exploit simulation contact modeling errors, allowing intermittent contact that appears stable in simulation but causes irreversible task failure on hardware. SCR was found to be critical for maintaining continuous contact.
- **Alignment Reward (AR):** Removing AR (OURS_−AR) resulted in lower contact alignment and broader force histograms, indicating larger fluctuations in applied force. These fluctuations had limited impact in simulation but led to more object drops on hardware. AR was found to improve contact alignment and stabilize applied forces.
- The combination of both SCR and AR in DemoMimic (OURS) yielded the strongest real-world performance, despite all variants achieving near-ceiling simulation results, underscoring that both rewards address complementary failure modes crucial for real-world reliability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, through the DemoMimic framework, contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing key challenges in sim-to-real transfer and generalization from human demonstrations.

**Significance:**

- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** DemoMimic's primary significance lies in its contact-centric reward shaping, specifically the Alignment Reward (AR) and Sustained Contact Reward (SCR). These rewards explicitly incentivize precise surface alignment and persistent contact, which are critical for robust real-world interaction but often overlooked in traditional RL reward formulations. The empirical results demonstrate that this design strategy leads to a significantly smaller sim-to-real performance drop compared to baseline methods, despite comparable simulation performance. This indicates a more effective transfer of learned policies from simulated environments to physical hardware.
- **Enhanced Generalization Across Object Instances:** The framework's ability to generalize a learned policy from a single human demonstration to diverse, unseen object instances (varying in shape, scale, mass, and friction) is a notable advance. This generalization is achieved by focusing on local contact geometry, which remains consistent across task-relevant objects. This reduces the need for collecting extensive, object-specific training data, thereby increasing the practical utility of the robot system.
- **Minimal Inference Assumptions:** The policy relies solely on egocentric RGB-D observations at inference time, avoiding the need for precise object pose estimation, which is often difficult and prone to errors in real-world settings. This pragmatic approach enhances the deployability of the system.
- **Decoupled Policy Architecture:** The hierarchical design, separating global guidance (high-level RGB policy for wrist trajectory) from local control (low-level wrist-depth policy for precise actions), provides a structured way to manage the complexity of dexterous manipulation. This allows the low-level policy to focus on fine-grained contact interactions using local depth cues.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Broader Applicability of Dexterous Robots:** By making dexterous manipulation more generalizable and robust in real-world scenarios, DemoMimic has the potential to expand the range of tasks multi-fingered robot hands can perform autonomously. This could include complex assembly, inspection, and tool-use applications in unstructured environments.
- **Reduced Data Collection Burden:** The ability to learn from a single human demonstration and generalize to multiple objects significantly reduces the laborious and time-consuming process of data collection and policy retraining for each new object or variant. This can accelerate the development and deployment of robotic manipulation skills.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified importance of contact-centric rewards provides a clear direction for future sim-to-real RL research, emphasizing the need for explicit incentives for physical interaction quality. The limitations identified by the authors also highlight avenues for further development, such as incorporating closed-loop, language-conditioned high-level policies for adaptive task guidance and exploring broader workspace randomization.
- **Advancements in Perception for Manipulation:** The use of advanced stereo reconstruction (FoundationStereo) and image augmentation (Cosmos-Transfer-2.5) to bridge the visual sim-to-real gap underscores the importance of robust perception pipelines for real-world robot learning, potentially spurring further innovation in these areas.

**Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**
The paper also candidly discusses limitations, including the open-loop nature of the high-level policy (preventing online adaptation to global scene changes), limited initial object-pose randomization during low-level policy training, evaluation on a restricted set of four tasks, and the assumption of access to reference object pose trajectories from human demonstrations. These limitations indicate areas for future work aimed at enhancing the framework's adaptability and generality.
