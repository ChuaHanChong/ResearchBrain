# GraspGraphNet: Graph-Structured Multi-Embodiment Dexterous Grasp Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KAIST developed GraspGraphNet, a framework for dexterous grasp generation that directly outputs executable palm poses and joint commands for multiple robot hands. It leverages topology-aware graph representations and conditional flow matching, achieving an 83.48% average grasp success rate and 40 ms inference time in simulation, along with 91% success on real-world objects.

## Problem

- Scaling dexterous grasp generation across diverse robot hands is challenging due to their distinct kinematic topologies and actuation dimensions.
- Many learning-based methods rely on intermediate, non-executable representations, necessitating computationally expensive and hand-specific post-processing steps like inverse kinematics or optimization.
- Existing models often struggle to adequately capture and dynamically update robot-object interaction neighborhoods as the hand configuration evolves during grasp formation.

## Method

- **GraspGraphNet** employs ==topology-aware graph representations== for robot hands, derived from ==URDF==, and objects, enabling a single model to process hands with varying kinematic structures.
- It utilizes a ==conditional flow-matching== approach to directly generate executable palm poses and normalized joint configurations, bypassing the need for post-processing.
- ==Dynamic world-edge message passing== is introduced, rebuilding local robot-object interaction graphs at each flow step to adapt to evolving hand configurations during grasp generation.

## Results

- Achieved an average grasp success rate of **83.48%** across Barrett, Allegro, and Shadow hands in simulation, outperforming prior interaction-centric methods like DRO-Grasp (**77.60%**) and TRO-Grasp (**81.00%**).
- Demonstrated significantly faster inference, generating grasps in an average of **40 ms**, a substantial improvement over GenDexGrasp (**15550 ms**), DRO-Grasp (**569 ms**), and TRO-Grasp (**265 ms**).
- Showed strong generalization to topology-modified hands (e.g., finger ablations) with a **72.70%** overall success rate without model retraining, and achieved a **91%** success rate on **10** diverse real-world objects using a physical robot arm and hand.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representing robot hands as kinematic graphs allows for a unified model that accommodates diverse hand embodiments and naturally handles topological modifications without retraining.
> - Conditional flow matching provides an effective mechanism for directly generating complex, multi-modal dexterous grasp distributions through iterative refinement of a velocity field.
> - Continuously updating robot-object interaction neighborhoods via dynamic world edges is critical for robust grasp generation, as it captures the evolving local contact geometry throughout the grasping process.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous grasping, which involves multi-fingered robot hands coordinating high-dimensional joint motions to establish contact with objects, is a foundational capability for robotic manipulation. Recent advancements in learning-based methods have improved the generation of stable and diverse grasps for complex object geometries. However, a persistent challenge in this field is the ability to scale grasp generation across a variety of robot hands. This difficulty arises because different robot hands possess distinct kinematic topologies, actuation dimensions, joint limits, and native command spaces, preventing the direct application of models designed for one embodiment to another.

Existing learning-based grasp generation methods can be broadly categorized into robot-centric and object-centric approaches. Robot-centric methods predict executable grasp states (e.g., wrist poses, joint values) directly, offering efficient execution but typically limiting the model's applicability to a specific hand embodiment. In contrast, object-centric methods employ transferable representations, such as contact points, contact maps, or affordance cues, which facilitate transfer across objects and robot hands. Examples of such methods include UniGrasp, GenDexGrasp, CEDex, and RobotFingerPrint, which improve transferability by avoiding direct prediction in embodiment-specific joint spaces.

More recent work has shifted towards interaction-centric representations, explicitly modeling the relationships between the robot and the object. DRO-Grasp uses robot-object distance relationships, while TRO-Grasp introduces graph-based transformation representations between object patches and hand links. While these contact and interaction-level representations enhance transfer across different hands, they commonly share a significant limitation: the generated representations (e.g., contact points, distance maps, transformation graphs) are not directly executable on a robot hand. Converting these representations into executable palm poses and joint commands necessitates a subsequent stage of post-processing, such as optimization, inverse kinematics, or retargeting. This additional conversion step contributes to increased inference costs and can make the overall process susceptible to variations in hand kinematics, joint limits, and initialization procedures.

GraspGraphNet is positioned within this landscape as an advancement over interaction-centric approaches. It aims to overcome the limitations of post-processing by directly generating executable robot commands. By employing a topology-aware, graph-based representation of robot hands, it seeks to create a unified model that supports multiple dexterous hands and can be directly applied to hands with varied kinematic structures, including those with topological modifications, without requiring retraining. This approach endeavors to integrate the benefits of transferable representations with the efficiency and direct executability typically associated with robot-centric methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified and efficient framework for dexterous grasp generation that can operate across a diverse range of robot hands, despite their inherent differences in kinematic structure and actuation spaces. The core motivations for GraspGraphNet stem from the identified limitations in current approaches to multi-embodiment grasping:

1. **Addressing Hand-Specific Kinematics and Actuation:** Dexterous robot hands exhibit substantial variations in their physical characteristics, including the number of fingers, link geometry, joint arrangements, and kinematic trees. These differences result in distinct configuration spaces and joint command outputs for each hand, making it challenging to design a single grasping model applicable to multiple embodiments. The research is motivated by the need for a framework that can naturally accommodate these variations.

2. **Eliminating Post-Processing for Executable Commands:** Prior learning-based methods, particularly those employing transferable grasp cues (e.g., object contacts, contact maps, interaction relationships), often predict intermediate representations that are not directly executable by a robot. The subsequent conversion of these representations into actual palm poses and joint commands typically requires computationally intensive and embodiment-specific post-processing steps, such as optimization, inverse kinematics, or retargeting. This process increases inference latency and can introduce sensitivity to specific hand kinematics, joint limits, and initial conditions. A key motivation is to bypass this post-processing, thereby reducing inference cost and improving robustness.

3. **Leveraging Structural Information of Robot Hands:** The underlying structure of robot hands, often described by URDF (Unified Robot Description Format), forms an articulated kinematic tree. This structural information, which defines how joints connect physical links, is a rich source of topological data. The authors are motivated to exploit this inherent graph structure to represent hands, enabling a single model to process embodiments with varying graph sizes, connectivities, and joint dimensions.

4. **Modeling Dynamic Robot-Object Interactions:** Grasp formation involves continuous changes in the proximity and interaction between the robot hand and the object surface. Existing models may not adequately capture how relevant interaction neighborhoods evolve during the grasping process. The research aims to develop a mechanism that dynamically updates these interaction features in real-time as the hand configuration changes.

5. **Improving Efficiency and Robustness:** Beyond achieving grasp success, the research seeks to significantly improve the inference efficiency of grasp generation. By directly generating executable commands through a flow-matching formulation, the aim is to reduce the computational time required per grasp. Furthermore, the framework intends to demonstrate robustness to structural variations in robot hands, such as the removal of fingers, without requiring model retraining.

In summary, the key objective is to create a topology-aware, efficient, and robust dexterous grasp generation framework that directly outputs executable commands across diverse robot hands, overcoming the complexities introduced by hand-specific kinematics and the computational overhead of post-processing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GraspGraphNet is a topology-aware framework designed for dexterous grasp generation across multiple robot hand embodiments. It integrates graph-structured representations for both objects and robot hands, combined with a conditional flow-matching approach to directly generate executable palm poses and joint configurations.

#### 3.1. Grasp-State Formulation

The grasp state for a robot hand with `J` actuated joints is represented as a vector `x = [t_palm, R_6D_palm, q_norm] ∈ R^(9+J)`.
- `t_palm ∈ R^3` denotes the palm's translation in an object-centered frame.
- `R_6D_palm ∈ R^6` represents the continuous 6D palm orientation in a robot-independent canonical palm frame.
- `q_norm ∈ R^J` contains the normalized actuated joint angles, scaled using the limits specified in the robot's URDF (Unified Robot Description Format).
The dimension of the grasp state, `9+J`, varies depending on the number of actuated joints in the input robot hand.

#### 3.2. Object and Robot Graph Representations

GraspGraphNet employs distinct but complementary graph representations for the target object and the robot hand.
- **Object Representation:** For a given object mesh or point cloud, 1,024 surface points are sampled, each characterized by its position, surface normal, and local curvature. A four-level hierarchical structure (1,024, 256, 64, and 32 points) is created using farthest-point sampling, with local features aggregated across levels. A k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) graph is constructed within each level for message passing. The 64-point embeddings are utilized as `fine object features (O_f)` for local, contact-relevant geometry, while the 32-point embeddings serve as `coarse object features (O_c)` for global shape context. A global object descriptor is derived by mean pooling the `O_c` features.
- **Robot Representation:** A robot kinematic graph `G_R = (V_R, E_R)` is directly constructed from the input robot's URDF. Each node `i ∈ V_R` corresponds to a physical robot link, and each directed edge `(i, j) ∈ E_R` represents a joint connecting a parent link `i` to a child link `j`. Virtual or unused links are excluded. This graph naturally accommodates variations in size and connectivity across different robot embodiments. Each kinematic edge stores attributes such as joint type, axis, limits, and fixed parent-to-child transformation. The geometry of each link is captured by sampling points from its visual mesh and encoding them using a shared PointNet, generating a per-link geometry embedding.

#### 3.3. State-Conditioned Robot Encoding

At each flow step `t`, the current grasp state `x_t` is used to articulate the hand. Joint values are denormalized, and differentiable forward kinematics (FK) is applied to `G_R` to compute the world-space transformation `T_t_i` for each robot link. The link's position `p_t_i` and 6D orientation `R_t_i` are extracted. A state-conditioned node feature for each robot link is formed by concatenating its link-geometry embedding, current FK-derived position and 6D orientation, kinematic-tree depth embedding, a fingertip indicator, and the normalized state of its associated joint (zero if not actuated). These features are then projected into a robot-node embedding space via an MLP. A three-layer edge-conditioned Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) processes these node features over the kinematic edges, with messages conditioned on joint attributes, capturing the articulated hand state and preserving its topology.

#### 3.4. Object-Robot Fusion

The encoded robot state is fused with the object representation through two mechanisms:
- **Coarse Cross-Attention:** Robot node embeddings act as queries, while coarse object features (`O_c`) serve as keys and values. This operation integrates global object shape context into the articulated robot state embeddings.
- **Dynamic World-Edge Message Passing:** For each robot link, `K_W` nearest fine object points (`O_f`) are identified, and dynamic edges `E_t_W` are constructed from these object points to the robot node. These edges scale linearly with the number of robot links. Each message propagated along these dynamic edges combines the fine object feature, current robot embedding, relative displacement, and distance. Messages from neighboring object points are aggregated at each robot node through residual update layers. Crucially, `E_t_W` is rebuilt at every flow step, allowing the local robot-object interaction graph to adapt as the hand configuration evolves during grasp generation.

#### 3.5. Velocity Field and Flow-Matching Objective

- **Velocity Prediction:** The object-aware robot embeddings are mapped to palm and joint velocities. A dedicated palm velocity head processes the embedding of the palm-hub node (the link node with the highest out-degree, typically the physical palm). A shared joint velocity head is applied to the child-link embedding associated with each actuated joint. Both heads are conditioned on the global object descriptor and a sinusoidal time embedding. Scalar predictions are assembled into `v_q ∈ R^J`. The final grasp-state velocity is `v_θ(x_t, t) = [v_palm; v_q] ∈ R^(9+J)`. The shared per-joint head allows the network to handle hands with varying actuation dimensions.
- **Initial and Target States:** For training, a target state `x_1` is derived from a ground-truth grasp annotation. An initial state `x_0` is an open-hand pose sampled around the object, with joints set to an open-hand configuration. During training, the initial palm approach direction is sampled within a cone, and in-plane palm rotation is sampled uniformly. The target grasp `x_1` is selected as the one closest to the sampled `x_0`.
- **Flow-Matching Objective:** Grasp synthesis is formulated as conditional flow matching. A time `t` is sampled uniformly from ``, and a linearly interpolated state `x_t = (1 - t)x_0 + tx_1` is generated. The target conditional velocity `v* = x_1 - x_0`. The model is trained to predict `v_θ(x_t, t)` by minimizing an MSE loss: `L = MSE(v_palm, v*_palm) + λ_q MSE(v_q, v*_q)`, where `λ_q` balances palm and joint supervision.

#### 3.6. Grasp Generation (Inference)

During inference, an initial open-hand state `x^(0)` is sampled. The learned velocity field `v_θ` is then integrated over `K` Euler steps to refine the grasp state: `x^(k+1) = x^(k) + Δt v_θ(x^(k), t_k)`, where `t_k = k/K` and `Δt = 1/K`. For most experiments, `K=3` steps are used. At each step, the updated grasp state leads to new articulated hand geometry, prompting the recomputation of the dynamic object-robot world edges. The final state `x^(K)` provides the palm pose and normalized actuated joint configuration, which are then converted to an executable wrist pose and physical joint commands for the robot by translating the canonical palm orientation to the robot-native frame, applying a fixed palm-to-wrist offset, and denormalizing joint values using URDF limits.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

GraspGraphNet was evaluated on its ability to generate stable and executable grasps across multiple robot hands with different kinematic structures, its robustness to hand-topology variations, and its performance in real-world scenarios.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

The framework was evaluated against prior multi-hand grasp generation baselines (GenDexGrasp, DRO-Grasp, TRO-Grasp) using Barrett Hand, Allegro Hand, and Shadow Hand on a benchmark of 40 unseen objects (10 from CMapDataset and 30 from Google Scanned Objects).
- **Grasp Success Rate:** GraspGraphNet achieved an average success rate of 83.48% across the three hands, which was higher than DRO-Grasp (77.60%) and TRO-Grasp (81.00%). It demonstrated competitive or superior performance on each individual hand, achieving the highest success rate for Barrett Hand, Allegro Hand, and Shadow Hand. GenDexGrasp exhibited lower performance in this evaluation, suggesting challenges in converting hand-agnostic contact maps to executable grasps for diverse hands.
- **Inference Time:** GraspGraphNet demonstrated substantial efficiency improvements, requiring an average of 40 ms per grasp. This was significantly faster compared to GenDexGrasp (15550 ms), DRO-Grasp (569 ms), and TRO-Grasp (265 ms). This efficiency is attributed to GraspGraphNet's direct generation of executable commands, avoiding the intermediate representations and subsequent optimization steps of other methods.

#### 4.2. Generalization to Topology-Modified Hands

To assess robustness to topological changes, the model was evaluated on finger-ablated variants of Allegro Hand (three variants) and Shadow Hand (four variants). These experiments were conducted without any retraining or adaptation of the model.
- GraspGraphNet achieved the highest overall success rate of 72.70% on these modified hands, outperforming DRO-Grasp (56.78%), TRO-Grasp (15.29%), and GenDexGrasp (12.99%). The performance drop for GenDexGrasp and TRO-Grasp suggested their reliance on learned interaction structures or contact maps was more sensitive to changes in hand topology. GraspGraphNet's graph-based representation allowed direct incorporation of changes in the kinematic tree, number of links, and joints, facilitating better accommodation of topology variations.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

GraspGraphNet was deployed on a UFactory xArm6 robot arm equipped with a Leap Hand and an Azure Kinect RGB-D camera. After fine-tuning on Leap Hand data, the system generated grasps directly from partial point clouds acquired by the RGB-D camera.
- On 10 diverse real-world objects, GraspGraphNet achieved a success rate of 91% over 100 grasp attempts. This demonstrated its capability for robust grasp generation from real-world sensor observations and direct execution on a physical robot system.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Flow Matching vs. Direct Regression:** Comparing the conditional flow matching approach with a direct-regression variant (single-step displacement prediction) revealed that flow matching (83.48% success with K=3) significantly outperformed direct regression (61.98%). This suggested that iterative refinement through a velocity field is crucial for handling the multimodality inherent in dexterous grasp distributions.
- **Object-Robot Fusion Modules:** Both coarse cross-attention and dynamic world-edge message passing were found to be important. Removing cross-attention (global object context) reduced average success to 54.80%, while removing dynamic world-edge message passing (local interaction) further decreased it to 51.27%. This indicated the complementary roles of global shape understanding and local contact-relevant interaction modeling.
- **Effect of Dynamic World Edges:** A comparison between dynamic world edges (recomputed at each flow step) and static world edges (constructed once from the initial state) showed that dynamic updates were critical. Static world edges led to a substantial drop in average success rate from 83.48% to 50.23%. This highlighted the necessity of updating interaction neighborhoods as the hand configuration evolves during grasp formation.
- **Integration Steps K:** Increasing the number of Euler integration steps `K` from 1 to 3 improved average success from 78.94% to 83.48%. However, further increases in `K` (e.g., to 20) provided only marginal gains in success while significantly increasing inference time. `K=3` was chosen as a practical balance between grasp quality and inference efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GraspGraphNet represents a contribution to the field of robotic dexterous grasping by addressing several challenges in developing generalizable and efficient manipulation capabilities.

**Unified and Topology-Aware Framework:** The framework's ability to represent diverse robot hands as URDF-derived kinematic graphs and operate with a single, shared model across different embodiments is a significant step towards creating more universal robotic manipulation systems. This approach eliminates the need for hand-specific model retraining, simplifying the deployment of dexterous grasping across a broader range of robotic hardware.

**Direct Executable Grasp Generation:** By directly generating executable palm poses and joint commands through conditional flow matching, GraspGraphNet bypasses the computationally expensive and often kinematically sensitive post-processing steps (e.g., inverse kinematics, optimization) that characterize many prior methods. This direct approach contributes to significantly faster inference times, making real-time or near real-time grasp generation more feasible for dynamic robotic tasks.

**Robustness to Hand Topology Variations:** The demonstrated robustness to structural modifications, such as finger removal, without requiring model retraining, indicates a higher level of generalization capability. This is particularly impactful for scenarios involving custom hand designs, damage, or reconfigurable robot hands, where adapting existing models would typically require extensive retraining or specialized engineering.

**Enhanced Robot-Object Interaction Modeling:** The introduction of dynamic world-edge message passing, which continuously updates robot-object interaction neighborhoods based on the evolving hand configuration, allows the model to capture local contact geometry throughout the grasp generation process. This dynamic interaction modeling contributes to improved grasp success rates, as evidenced by ablation studies.

**Practical Applicability:** The successful deployment and high success rate in real-world experiments with a physical robot arm and hand, using sensor-captured object observations, underscore the practical potential of GraspGraphNet. This suggests that the framework is capable of translating simulation-trained policies to real-world scenarios, a crucial step for wider adoption in robotic applications.

**Potential Future Impact:** This research lays groundwork for more generalized and adaptive dexterous manipulation. By enabling a single model to understand and control various hand morphologies, it could accelerate the development of robotic systems that are less constrained by specific hardware designs. Future work could investigate zero-shot generalization to robot hands with substantially different morphologies (beyond topology variants) and expand the framework to tackle broader dexterous manipulation tasks beyond just grasping, such as in-hand manipulation or tool use. This would further reduce the engineering effort required to integrate new robot hands or to adapt existing systems to novel manipulation challenges.
