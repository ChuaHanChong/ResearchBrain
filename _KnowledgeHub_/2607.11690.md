# Requirement-Driven Design of Whole-Body Social Tactile Sensing via Virtual Human-Robot Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign introduced a requirement-driven framework for designing whole-body social tactile sensing systems, leveraging virtual human-robot interaction to derive optimal sensor placement and spatial resolution. This methodology quantifies how touch patterns vary by social context and identifies that approximately 1.3 cm² per taxel is sufficient for robust social gesture classification on a robot arm.

## Problem

- Traditional tactile sensing design is hardware-driven, resulting in costly and customized systems that are often constrained by engineering feasibility rather than interaction needs.
- Existing social human-robot interaction (HRI) studies using fixed sensor configurations couple data collection and recognition to specific hardware, complicating generalization to different robot morphologies.
- A lack of quantitative analysis exists regarding the specific sensing fidelity, such as spatial resolution, required to reliably detect and distinguish various social touch gestures.

## Method

- Developed a ==virtual reality (VR)== platform with ==haptic feedback== to collect high-resolution, hardware-independent human-robot contact data from user interactions with a virtual robot.
- Conducted two user studies: one to observe natural social touch behaviors for gesture identification, and a second controlled study to collect balanced datasets of specific gestures.
- Simulated tactile sensor readings offline from the collected VR contact data, then analyzed the impact of varying ==spatial resolutions== on social gesture classification performance using ==Random Forest== and ==CNN-GRU== models.

## Results

- Established a quantitative baseline for the Reachy robot arm, recommending a spatial resolution of approximately **1.3 cm² per taxel** (equivalent to **18 taxels per 24 cm**) for effective binary contact social gesture recognition.
- Found statistically significant differences (**p < 0.05**) in touch distributions across all pairwise comparisons of five interaction scenarios, confirming the context-specificity of social touch.
- Created an open-source VR gesture dataset comprising **5,520 trials** from **18 participants** performing **8** common social touch gestures on a virtual humanoid robot's arm and torso.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Social touch patterns on a robot are context-dependent and convey distinct social messages, as evidenced by statistically significant differences in contact distributions across various interaction scenarios.
> - The upper torso, shoulders, and grippers are identified as critical body regions for tactile sensing coverage to effectively capture a diverse vocabulary of social touch gestures.
> - There is a clear trade-off between tactile sensor density and gesture recognition accuracy, with performance rapidly improving at lower resolutions and stabilizing beyond a certain taxel density.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The increasing deployment of robots in public and domestic environments necessitates enhanced human-robot interaction (HRI) capabilities, particularly in natural and intuitive communication modalities. Social touch, a fundamental aspect of human-human communication for conveying affect, intention, reassurance, and social bonding, remains an underexplored area in HRI. Enabling robots to perceive and interpret social touch without predefined location constraints presents a significant technical challenge.

Historically, the design of tactile sensing systems for robots has largely followed a hardware-driven paradigm. In this approach, tactile sensors are developed first, often with large-area tactile skins, and then gesture recognition algorithms are built upon the fixed configurations of these sensors. This method is constrained by factors such as cost, extensive customization cycles, and the inherent coupling of data collection and recognition methods to specific sensing hardware. Consequently, tactile sensor design in this paradigm is often dictated by engineering feasibility rather than the authentic requirements of natural human-robot interaction.

Prior research has addressed social-physical HRI through various means, including instrumented robots (e.g., PARO, Huggable) and studies on structured interactions like hugging or social games. Some studies have collected social touch interaction data using third-person video recordings, which can suffer from occlusion and limited precision in contact localization. Others have developed custom tactile sensing systems utilizing modalities such as pressure-sensitive resistive sensors, capacitive sensors, or electrical impedance tomography, deploying them on specific robot morphologies like robotic dogs or NAO robots. While these efforts enable gesture recognition and social intent inference, the data generated is typically tightly coupled to the specific hardware and robot embodiment used, which complicates the derivation of generalized tactile sensing requirements.

This work introduces a requirement-driven alternative. Instead of beginning with existing hardware, the research posits that tactile signals required for natural social-physical HRI should be independently reconstructed from human-robot contact, allowing sensing requirements to be explored before hardware design. While human studies have identified common interpersonal touch gestures, a quantitative analysis of the sensing fidelity needed to recognize these gestures, particularly derived directly from hardware-independent human-robot contact data, has been largely absent. The paper leverages virtual reality (VR) as a controlled platform for studying HRI, noting prior findings that human responses in virtual environments can translate to interactions with physical robots, supporting the use of VR for this purpose. By collecting high-resolution contact data in a VR environment, the research aims to decouple data collection from hardware constraints, thereby informing the design of tactile skins and sensor layouts based on observed interaction patterns rather than predefined sensor capabilities.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate a requirement-driven framework for designing whole-body social tactile sensing systems for humanoid robots. This framework aims to derive specific sensing requirements, particularly concerning spatial resolution and placement (coverage), directly from human-robot interaction data, prior to the fabrication of physical hardware.

The motivation for this approach stems from several limitations observed in the traditional hardware-driven design of tactile sensing systems:
1. **Hardware Constraints and Cost**: Current tactile sensor development often involves expensive, large-area tactile skins that require lengthy customization cycles. This leads to designs constrained by engineering feasibility and budget rather than the actual needs of social interaction.
2. **Coupling of Data and Hardware**: When gesture datasets are collected using fixed sensor configurations, recognition algorithms become inherently coupled to the specific sensors used. This makes it challenging to generalize findings or adapt designs for different robot morphologies or interaction contexts without recollecting extensive data.
3. **Lack of Quantitative Requirements**: While prior studies have identified social touch gestures, there is a noted absence of quantitative analysis regarding the specific sensing fidelity (e.g., spatial resolution) required to reliably detect and distinguish these gestures. Without such baselines, sensor design relies on iterative, trial-and-error physical prototyping.
4. **Enabling Natural HRI**: To allow robots to engage in natural and intuitive social-physical interactions, they must be capable of perceiving and interpreting a diverse range of social touches. Designing effective tactile sensing systems is a prerequisite for improving the efficiency, safety, and quality of HRI in increasingly prevalent robot deployments.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of this work include:
- Developing a VR-based data collection platform equipped with customized haptic feedback to enable participants to interact with a virtual robot, thereby capturing high-resolution whole-body contact distributions without the constraints of physical tactile sensors.
- Using this platform to create two datasets: an initial dataset from natural interaction scenarios to allow gesture classes to emerge, and a controlled dataset of balanced demonstrations for quantitative analysis.
- Identifying recurring social touch gestures from the collected interaction data to define a vocabulary for tactile recognition.
- Analyzing contact distributions to determine the robot body regions most frequently engaged during interaction, thereby providing morphology-specific recommendations for sensor placement and coverage.
- Quantitatively evaluating the trade-off between sensing fidelity and gesture recognition accuracy by progressively degrading simulated spatial resolution and assessing classification performance. This aims to establish a baseline for tactile sensor density.
- Demonstrating the transferability of this methodology to derive morphology-specific sensing requirements for other robot platforms prior to physical hardware fabrication.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a systematic, data-driven methodology that informs tactile sensor design from interaction requirements, thereby reducing hardware development costs and accelerating the deployment of socially capable robots.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The research adopted a requirement-driven framework for tactile sensing design, which fundamentally reverses the traditional hardware-first approach. This framework comprises three stages: defining the interaction context, collecting unconstrained touch data, and translating observed behaviors into tactile sensing requirements.

**Virtual Platform Development:**
To collect hardware-independent interaction data, a virtual environment was developed in Unity. This environment enabled users to perform social touch with a virtual robot while receiving vibrotactile haptic feedback.
- **Hand Tracking and Haptic Feedback**: Users wore a Meta Quest 3 headset for embedded vision-based hand tracking (OpenXR, ~30 Hz). Custom haptic gloves, each containing six linear resonant actuators (one on each finger, one on the palm), provided vibrotactile feedback upon virtual contact. The control circuitry for the gloves was mounted on the user's shoulder to minimize hand-mounted weight.
- **Contact Detection**: A custom Unity module detected collisions between the user's virtual hands and the robot surface. Hands were approximated using sphere primitives attached to tracked joint markers (multiple spheres per finger segment and palm). The robot body was represented by convex triangle meshes. Binary contact events were registered when the distance between a hand sphere's center and the robot surface was less than the sphere's radius.

**Virtual Robot:**
The chosen robot platform was Pollen Robotics' 2021 Reachy, implemented in Unity with articulation bodies for physically constrained joint motion. The robot's movable limbs (arms, head) responded passively to participant-applied forces, while its base was fixed except in scripted scenarios. The torso was modeled as a rigid body to prevent penetration.

**Virtual Tactile Sensor Simulation:**
The hand-robot collision data collected in the VR environment was used to simulate tactile sensor readings offline.
- **Geometric Mapping**: For the robot's left arm, its geometry was approximated as a cylinder. Triangle vertices were projected onto this cylindrical surface, and surface points were parameterized as (h, a) (normalized axial and angular positions).
- **Contact Rasterization**: For each frame, triangle-local circle centers (representing hand contact) were converted to barycentric coordinates and mapped into (h, a) space. The circle radius was transformed into an ellipse, which was then rasterized onto a discrete grid G_t. Each cell in G_t encoded binary contact information, without capturing force or velocity. This simulation pipeline was implemented specifically for the left arm, limiting the resolution analysis to arm-contact gestures.

**Experimental Design (User Studies):**
Two user studies were conducted to gather social touch data.
1. **Stage 1: spHRI Dataset (Natural Scenarios)**:
 - **Participants**: Twelve users (6 male, 5 female, 1 non-binary; age 19–67).
 - **Scenarios**: Five predefined social scenarios in a virtual living room environment, each repeated three times:
 - Attention Seeking (robot turned away).
 - Path Redirection (robot moving towards puzzle).
 - Move Aside (robot blocking path).
 - Behavior Correction (robot incorrectly sorting items).
 - Appreciation (robot "baked a cake").
 - **Robot Response**: Minimal acknowledgment (head turn/gaze) upon first contact if not already facing the participant; robot halted movement in correction scenarios.
 - **Data Collection**: 6-DoF poses of hand joints and robot meshes were recorded. Trials concluded after the first coded gesture, defined as a continuous contact episode lasting at least two seconds.
 - **Objective**: To allow natural touch behaviors to emerge and identify recurring social touch gestures for defining a classification vocabulary.
2. **Stage 2: VR Gesture Dataset (Controlled Collection)**:
 - **Participants**: Eighteen participants (10 male; mean age 24.1).
 - **Gesture Selection**: Eight specific social touch gestures (identified from Stage 1) were selected.
 - **Controlled Trials**: Participants performed each gesture 20 times on the robot's arm and 20 times on its torso, totaling 40 repetitions per gesture per participant. Gesture order was randomized within blocks. The final dataset comprised 5,520 trials.
 - **Procedure**: Participants viewed a demonstration video for each gesture, positioned their hand away from the robot, performed the gesture, and returned to a neutral pose.
 - **Data Collection**: Full 6-DoF poses of hand joints and robot mesh were recorded. Contact events were computed offline.

**Data Analysis:**
1. **Contact Region Analysis for Social Gestures (Coverage)**:
 - Contact frequency maps were generated for each scenario by mapping contact regions onto the robot's mesh UV space, aggregated, and smoothed using kernel density estimation (KDE).
 - The Sliced Wasserstein Distance (SWD) was computed between normalized surface density maps to quantify differences in touch distributions across scenarios. Statistical significance was assessed using a paired sign-flip permutation test with Holm–Bonferroni corrections.
 - Body regions contacted for individual gestures were visualized, and gesture distributions across scenarios were histogrammed.
2. **Effect of Sensor Resolution on Gesture Classification**:
 - The controlled VR Gesture Dataset (arm contacts) was used. Tactile signals were simulated at 50 different spatial resolutions. Salt-and-pepper noise (2%) was added to simulate sensor noise.
 - Two classification models were employed:
 - **Random Forest (RF)**: Trained on 32 hand-crafted features (activation, motion, spatial extent, spatial spread) derived from temporal summary statistics of the simulated sensor readings.
 - **CNN-GRU**: A deep learning model comprising a three-layer CNN for spatial feature extraction, followed by a single-layer GRU for temporal dynamics, and a linear layer for gesture classification.
 - **Evaluation**: Model performance was assessed using Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation across all 50 resolutions, reporting mean accuracy and macro F1-score. This method ensured models were tested on data from participants not seen during training.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research yielded quantitative baselines for tactile skin sensing requirements across two key variables: sensing coverage and spatial resolution.

**Contact Region Analysis for Social Gestures (Sensing Coverage)**
- **Scenario-Specific Contact Patterns**: The areas of the robot touched by participants varied depending on the social message being conveyed.
 - In "Attention Seeking" and "Move Aside" scenarios, the shoulder was the most frequently contacted area, indicating its importance for initiating interaction or redirecting the robot.
 - In the "Appreciation" scenario, contact was distributed across multiple robot regions, including the head, shoulders, hands, and back. This suggests that affectionate touch often involves broader contact areas, with hands and back being particularly significant.
- **Sensor Placement Recommendations**: From a design perspective, whole-body tactile coverage would capture all social touch signals. However, if full coverage is not feasible, the study recommends prioritizing coverage of the upper torso, grippers, and shoulders. The specific context of robot deployment may further influence sensor placement priority.
- **Quantified Differences in Touch Distributions**: Sliced Wasserstein Distance (SWD) analysis between normalized surface density maps showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05 after Holm–Bonferroni correction) across all pairwise comparisons of interaction scenarios. This confirms that touch patterns are distinct for different social contexts.
- **Identified Gesture Vocabulary**: Nine recurring social touch gestures were observed in the natural interaction study. Eight of these (tap, grab, stroke, push, pat, poke, rub, hit) were selected for controlled data collection. The distribution of these gestures varied by scenario, with some, like "push" and "tap," appearing in four out of five scenarios, indicating their versatility across different communicative intents.

**Effect of Sensor Resolution on Gesture Classification (Spatial Resolution)**
- **Performance vs. Resolution**: Both the Random Forest (RF) and CNN-GRU classifiers demonstrated a sharp improvement in gesture classification performance (accuracy and F1-score) as taxel density increased from very low resolutions. This indicates that a coarse tactile representation discards crucial spatial information needed for gesture discrimination.
- **Stabilization Point**: Classification performance rapidly increased before approximately 8 taxels per 24 cm and then stabilized after reaching approximately 18 taxels per 24 cm. This suggests that beyond a certain density, the returns from adding more taxels diminish, as sufficient spatial detail for gesture discrimination is already captured.
- **Quantitative Baseline**: For binary contact encoding on the Reachy robot arm, the results suggest that a spatial resolution of approximately 1.3 cm² per taxel (equivalent to 18 taxels per 24 cm) provides a reasonable baseline for maintaining gesture discrimination performance. Resolutions coarser than 4.8 cm² per taxel were found to substantially degrade classification accuracy.
- **Dataset Contribution**: The research produced an open-source VR gesture dataset consisting of 5,520 trials from 18 participants performing 8 social touch gestures on the arm and torso of a virtual humanoid robot.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This work presents a notable contribution to the field of human-robot interaction and robotic sensing by proposing and demonstrating a requirement-driven framework for tactile sensor design. This approach fundamentally shifts the design paradigm from a hardware-constrained model to one informed by empirical interaction data.

The primary significance lies in its ability to decouple the collection of human-robot interaction data from specific tactile sensor configurations. By leveraging a VR-based platform, the researchers were able to reconstruct high-resolution contact geometry independently of any physical sensor hardware. This enables the systematic derivation of sensing requirements, such as optimal sensor placement and spatial resolution, *before* costly hardware fabrication begins. This can significantly reduce development time and expenses associated with iterative physical prototyping and customization cycles.

The quantitative baselines established for the Reachy robot platform, specifically the recommended spatial resolution of approximately 1.3 cm² per taxel for binary contact on the arm, provide concrete guidance for future tactile skin development. These findings can inform engineers and designers on how to balance sensing fidelity with cost and complexity, ensuring that implemented tactile systems are sufficiently sensitive to recognize social gestures without unnecessary over-engineering.

Furthermore, the methodology presented is designed to be transferable. While demonstrated on the Reachy robot, the framework can, in principle, be applied to other robot morphologies and interaction contexts. This transferability allows for the derivation of morphology-specific sensing requirements tailored to diverse robot embodiments and their intended social roles, promoting more effective and context-aware HRI across a wider range of robotic applications.

The creation of an open-source VR gesture dataset is a valuable contribution to the research community. This dataset, comprising 5,520 trials of various social touch gestures, provides a rich resource for further studies in social touch recognition, machine learning for tactile perception, and the development of new tactile sensing algorithms.

The potential impact of this work extends to enhancing the naturalness, intuition, and safety of human-robot interactions. By enabling robots to perceive and interpret social touch more effectively, this research lays a foundation for robots that can better understand human intent, convey empathy or reassurance, and respond appropriately in social-physical settings. This capability is particularly relevant for robots deployed in assistive roles, customer service, education, and domestic environments, where natural communication can foster greater user comfort, trust, and engagement.

Future work outlined, such as incorporating richer tactile modalities (force, pressure, shear, velocity) and exploring non-uniform taxel layouts, suggests a roadmap for even more sophisticated and bio-inspired tactile sensing systems. The ultimate validation of this framework will involve fabricating hardware based on the derived requirements and evaluating its performance in real-world human-robot interactions, which would further solidify its practical significance.
