# CCDP: Composition of Conditional Diffusion Policies with Guided Sampling

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, Composition of Conditional Diffusion Policies (CCDP), enhances robot recovery from failed actions by dynamically guiding action sampling away from failure regions. The approach, which utilizes only successful demonstration data, showed improved task success rates and maintained adherence to implicit objectives across diverse robotic manipulation scenarios.

## Problem

- Robots using imitation learning policies inefficiently re-sample actions after a failure, often repeating unsuccessful attempts.
- Existing imitation learning methods lack inherent mechanisms for failure recovery, typically requiring additional failure data, complex high-level planners, or risky exploration.
- Incorporating long-term failure history into policies to enable effective recovery often leads to increased dimensionality and complexity.

## Method

- **CCDP** integrates a ==guided sampling== mechanism into ==Diffusion Policies== by decomposing the denoising process into components conditioned on current state, recent history, and multiple distinct failure features.
- It synthesizes a ==recovery dataset== from successful demonstrations to train specific ==failure models== that actively steer actions away from regions associated with observed failures.
- The framework uses a ==product of experts== inspired composition to dynamically combine these guidance cues, adapting the policy during runtime without re-training.

## Results

- CCDP demonstrated significantly higher task success rates, achieving **99%** in Door Opening and **96%** in Button Pressing, compared to **70%** and **10%** for standard Diffusion Policies, respectively.
- The framework showed improved adherence to implicit task objectives from demonstrations, such as optimal manipulation strategies (**66%** adherence in Object Manipulation) and prioritizing nearest available resources (**73%** in Object Packing, **97%** in Bartender), often outperforming an enhanced baseline.
- CCDP achieved comparable or superior performance to baselines **without requiring explicit labeling** of solution space partitions or prior classification of failure types.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging past failures as "negative guidance" can effectively steer policy sampling away from undesirable action spaces.
> - Decomposing the policy into a composition of simpler, conditionally-guided diffusion models allows for flexible handling of a variable number of failure conditions.
> - It is possible to infer effective recovery strategies solely from successful demonstration data by carefully synthesizing an auxiliary dataset that defines appropriate recovery actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of Imitation Learning (IL), specifically addressing challenges associated with policy execution during inference in real-world robotic applications. Recent advancements in IL, such as Implicit Behavior Cloning and diffusion/flow-matching policies, have enabled robots to learn complex action distributions from successful demonstrations. However, a persistent challenge in these methods is the robot's response when a sampled action fails. Traditional approaches often involve simply re-sampling from the same distribution, which can be inefficient and lead to repeated unsuccessful attempts.

The broader landscape of failure recovery in robotics includes several categories:
- **Supplementary Resources:** Some methods assume access to additional resources like simulated environments, advanced reasoning systems (e.g., foundational models), or direct expert guidance. These resources are not always available in practical deployment scenarios.
- **Two-Level Planning:** This approach typically involves a high-level planner that selects action primitives or task parameters, often based on reinforcement learning or behavior cloning, while a low-level controller executes these primitives. While capable, multi-layer planners can introduce complexity, suboptimality due to separation between planning levels, and can suffer from combinatorial explosion as the number of options increases.
- **Low-Level Controller Approaches:**
 - **Robust Policies:** Methods in robust reinforcement learning or model predictive control aim to learn policies that are inherently resilient to failures caused by environmental shifts. However, these often require prior task knowledge, models, or simulators for training and may not generalize to a wide range of failure types where a single robust policy is insufficient.
 - **History-Dependent Policies:** Policies trained to consider a history of observations and actions can adapt decisions based on past outcomes. A primary difficulty with this approach is the need for failure examples during demonstrations or extensive, potentially risky, autonomous exploration in real robots to learn recovery behaviors. Furthermore, capturing long-horizon failure events necessitates maintaining a long memory of past actions and observations, which adds complexity.

The proposed CCDP method fits into the low-level controller approach, focusing on enhancing the sampling strategy directly within the policy. It distinguishes itself by aiming to infer recovery actions solely from successful demonstration data, without requiring additional exploratory behavior, explicit failure data, or a high-level controller. It also addresses the long-history challenge by leveraging the concept of "composition of multiple models" or "product of experts," which decomposes complex problems into simpler sub-problems, a technique previously applied in areas such as sensory fusion, combining coordinate systems, and integrating optimality with feasibility in control. This work extends the application of model composition to guide low-level policy inference by steering away from regions associated with observed failures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an enhanced sampling strategy for imitation learning policies that enables robots to recover from failed actions efficiently and without relying on extensive additional resources or data.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges inherent in existing imitation learning paradigms:

- **Inefficiency of Repeated Sampling:** When a robot operating with a learned policy encounters a failure, simply resampling an action from the original learned distribution can lead to repeatedly attempting actions that have already proven unsuccessful. This process is inefficient and does not leverage information gained from the failure itself.
- **Lack of Explicit Failure Recovery:** Standard imitation learning methods, particularly those based on generative models like diffusion policies, are primarily designed to replicate successful expert behaviors. They typically lack inherent mechanisms to adapt or recover when an action deviates from successful outcomes or fails.
- **Data Requirements for Recovery:** Learning robust failure recovery often requires exposing the robot to failure scenarios during demonstrations or through autonomous exploration. This complicates data collection, necessitates modeling the task environment, or involves potentially hazardous real-robot training. The authors aim to overcome this by inferring recovery actions using only successful demonstration data.
- **Limitations of High-Level Planning:** While two-level planning approaches can address failure recovery, they introduce issues such as increased complexity, potential for suboptimal results due to abstraction, and combinatorial explosion when many options are considered. The authors are motivated to find a solution directly within the low-level controller to avoid these drawbacks.
- **Managing Long-Horizon Failure History:** Effective recovery may require remembering multiple successive failures and adjusting future actions accordingly. Incorporating a long history of past actions and observations into a policy increases its dimensionality and complexity, making learning and inference more difficult.
- **Need for Adaptability and Modularity:** A robust recovery system should adapt to a variable number of failures and different types of failure conditions without requiring re-training or complex classification. The authors are motivated to create a modular system that can compose different recovery strategies.
- **"Negative Guidance" for Action Selection:** Conventional conditional policies often guide actions towards desirable behaviors. This work is motivated by the idea of using failure cases as "negative guidance," actively steering the policy away from action spaces that have led to unsuccessful outcomes, analogous to obstacle avoidance.

In summary, the core motivation is to equip robots with a low-level, self-correcting capability that efficiently adapts its action sampling space following failures, without external aids, additional failure data, or complex hierarchical control structures, thereby enhancing the robustness and applicability of imitation learning in dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Composition of Conditional Diffusion Policies (CCDP), extends the standard Diffusion Policy framework by incorporating a mechanism for guided sampling that leverages information from past failures.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The foundation is a Diffusion Policy that models the conditional distribution $p^D_\pi(a_t | x_t, h^H_t)$ to generate actions $a_t$ given the current state $x_t$ and a history of past $H$ actions and states $h^H_t$. The paper extends this by conditioning the policy on a set of `N` failure features, $z^f_{1:N}$, extracted from previous failure events: $a_t \sim p_\pi(a_t | x_t, h^H_t, z^f_{1:N})$. This formulation aims to reduce the need for a very long history `H` by summarizing past failures through these features.

**B. Composition of Diffusion Policies:**
To address the high dimensionality and complexity arising from a variable number of failure features, CCDP decomposes the conditional distribution into multiple simpler sub-problems, inspired by the Product of Experts concept. This decomposition is applied to the denoising term $\hat{\varepsilon}(a^k_t, k)$ in the inverse diffusion process, which is responsible for iteratively generating actions from noisy samples.

The decomposed denoising term is formulated as:
$\hat{\varepsilon}(a^k_t, k) = \varepsilon_a(a_t, k) + w_s [\varepsilon_s(a_t, x_t, k) - \varepsilon_a(a_t, k)] + w_h [\varepsilon_h(a_t, h^H_t, k) - \varepsilon_a(a_t, k)] + \sum_{i=1}^N w^z_i [\varepsilon_z(a_t, z^f_i, k) - \varepsilon_a(a_t, k)]$.

Each term contributes to guiding the action sampling:
- $\varepsilon_a(a_t, k)$: A base term that encourages sampling actions similar to those in the general demonstration dataset, without specific state or history conditioning.
- $w_s [\varepsilon_s(a_t, x_t, k) - \varepsilon_a(a_t, k)]$: This component biases actions toward those appropriate for the current state $x_t$. It explicitly separates current state information from history to avoid bias from past failures.
- $w_h [\varepsilon_h(a_t, h^H_t, k) - \varepsilon_a(a_t, k)]$: This term promotes temporal continuity by aligning actions with the recent history $h^H_t$. Its weight $w_h$ can be adjusted; a reduction after failure prevents adherence to a detrimental history, while a higher value ensures smooth motion in success.
- $\sum_{i=1}^N w^z_i [\varepsilon_z(a_t, z^f_i, k) - \varepsilon_a(a_t, k)]$: This is the core recovery component. For each observed failure $i$, a separate diffusion model $\varepsilon_z(a_t, z^f_i, k)$ is learned to steer actions away from regions associated with the specific failure feature $z^f_i$. This modular approach allows handling a variable number of distinct failure cases without needing to train a single model for all possible failure combinations.

The weights $w_s, w_h, w^z_i$ are positive coefficients that determine the influence of each component.

**C. Recovery Model:**
Since the provided demonstration dataset is assumed to contain only successful examples, an auxiliary dataset `R` is synthesized to train the failure recovery models ($\varepsilon_z$ terms).

1. **Defining Recovery Actions:** A recovery action $a$ for a failed action $a_f$ at state $x_f$ is defined as one where the extracted key features $z(a,x)$ are sufficiently dissimilar from $z(a_f, x_f)$ (i.e., $\|z(a, x) - z(a_f, x_f)\|_2 > \delta_z$), while the state $x$ is similar to $x_f$ (i.e., $\|x - x_f\|_2 < \delta_x$). This assumes the cause of failure is static.
2. **Data Synthesis:** To create a dense recovery dataset:
 - States $x_s$ are randomly selected from the successful demonstrations.
 - For each $x_s$, multiple actions are sampled using a noise estimator $\bar{\varepsilon}(a, x, k) = \varepsilon_a(a, k) + w_s [\varepsilon_s(a, x, k) - \varepsilon_a(a, k)]$. This estimator excludes history and, with $w_s \le 1$, encourages exploration of a broader range of actions to find diverse recovery solutions.
 - Pairs $(a, x_s)$ from this synthetic dataset that satisfy the recovery action criteria (Eq. 5) are extracted to form the recovery dataset.
 - The $\varepsilon_z$ models are then trained on this recovery dataset using the DDPM method.
3. **Failure Key Features `z(·)`:** The function $z(\cdot)$ extracts key features from failed actions and states. The paper discusses three practical choices, acknowledging that autonomous learning of these features is a future direction:
 - Directly using the failed action itself, $z(a_f, x_f) = a_f$.
 - Using the final state reached if the failed action were executed, $z(a_f, x_f) = x^T_f$.
 - Using a discrete label representing the action primitive, $z(a_f, x_f) = m$.

**D. Online Inference Phase:**
During robot execution, a failure detection system identifies when an action is unsuccessful. Upon detection, the key features of the failed action and state ($z^f_i$) are extracted and added to a set of active failure conditions. The composed denoising term (Eq. 4) is then used to generate the next action. This process dynamically guides the sampling away from the observed failure regions, adapting the policy to overcome challenges encountered during runtime.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated CCDP against a standard Diffusion Policy (DP) and an enhanced baseline (DP\*), which partitions the solution space and uses rejection sampling. Experiments were conducted across various robotic tasks, assessing both success rates and adherence to implicit objectives.

**A. Overall Performance Trend:**
CCDP consistently demonstrated higher task success rates compared to the standard DP across all evaluated tasks. Furthermore, CCDP generally outperformed or achieved comparable success to DP\* while notably preserving or better adhering to implicit objectives from the demonstration data, which DP\* sometimes struggled with due to its rejection sampling strategy.

**B. Task-Specific Results:**

- **Door Opening (DO):**
 - Task: Opening a door with an unknown opening direction.
 - Results: CCDP achieved a 99% success rate, significantly higher than DP (70%). It performed comparably to DP\* (94%) but without requiring explicit labeling of door opening directions.
- **Button Pressing (BP):**
 - Task: Pressing a button at an unknown location within a predefined area.
 - Results: CCDP achieved a 96% success rate, substantially outperforming DP (10%) and DP\* (73%). DP\*'s approach of discretizing the area into bins and removing failed ones was less efficient than CCDP's continuous guidance.
- **Object Manipulation (OM):**
 - Task: Moving an object of unknown mass, where the optimal manipulation strategy (single-arm, bimanual, or pushing) depends on the weight (implicit objective).
 - Results: CCDP achieved a 76% success rate with 66% adherence to the implicit objective. DP\* achieved 100% success but only 38% adherence to the implicit objective (often selecting sub-optimal but successful primitives). DP had a 27% success rate and 88% implicit objective adherence (being biased towards the dominant single-hand primitive, but when it worked, it was often the intended one). This highlights CCDP's ability to balance success with adherence to the demonstrated strategy.
- **Objects Packing (OP):**
 - Task: Placing objects into baskets, prioritizing the nearest available one as baskets fill up (implicit objective).
 - Results: Both CCDP and DP\* achieved high success rates (100% and 97% respectively), while DP performed poorly (40%) due to repeatedly attempting to use filled baskets. Critically, CCDP demonstrated 73% adherence to the implicit objective (selecting the nearest available basket), significantly outperforming DP\* (48%). This shows CCDP's effectiveness in managing dynamic environmental constraints beyond simple failure recovery.
- **Bartender (BT):**
 - Task: Filling multiple cups, prioritizing the nearest unfilled cup (implicit objective).
 - Results: Both CCDP and DP\* achieved 100% success in filling all cups. However, CCDP exhibited 97% adherence to the implicit objective of selecting the closest available cup, whereas DP\* only achieved 12%. DP achieved 72% success with 100% adherence to the implicit objective when it did succeed. This again demonstrates CCDP's capability to integrate success with subtle task preferences encoded in demonstrations.

**C. Comparison with DP\*:**
The paper notes that DP\* requires prior classification or partitioning of the solution space, which CCDP avoids. CCDP's "negative forcing" strategy (steering away from failures) allows for broader exploration and better integration of contextual cues compared to DP\*'s "positive forcing" (constraining to a specific region), leading to better implicit objective fulfillment in several tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CCDP framework offers several advancements and potential impacts on the field of robot imitation learning and autonomous systems:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Autonomy:** CCDP provides a mechanism for robots to intrinsically adapt and recover from failures during task execution. This reduces the need for constant human intervention or the reliance on external high-level planning systems, thereby increasing robot autonomy in unstructured or partially known environments.
- **Data Efficiency in Learning Recovery:** A significant impact is the ability to infer recovery actions solely from successful demonstration data. This eliminates the need for collecting explicit failure demonstrations, which can be complex, time-consuming, or even hazardous in real-world settings. It makes imitation learning more practical by leveraging existing, readily available successful datasets more effectively.
- **Improved Sampling Efficiency:** By dynamically guiding the policy away from regions of the action space associated with past failures, CCDP enhances the efficiency of the sampling process. This reduces the number of unsuccessful attempts and leads to faster task completion, compared to methods that repeatedly sample from the original, unchanged distribution.
- **Modularity and Flexibility:** The decomposition of the diffusion policy into sub-problems (general action, state-conditioned, history-conditioned, and failure-conditioned) introduces a high degree of modularity. This allows for flexible integration of different guidance cues and makes the system adaptable to a variable number of failure instances without requiring extensive re-training or complex combinatorial models.
- **Addressing Combinatorial Complexity:** By handling individual failure events through separate, composable models, CCDP mitigates the combinatorial explosion issue often encountered in multi-layer planning approaches that need to consider all possible failure combinations or long action histories.
- **Beyond Simple Failure Recovery:** The experimental results on tasks like Object Packing and Bartender demonstrate that the method's guided sampling approach is applicable beyond basic failure recovery, enabling robots to dynamically adapt to evolving environmental states (e.g., filled baskets, filled cups) and adhere to implicit task objectives or preferences (e.g., selecting the nearest available option).
- **Reduced Labeling Burden:** CCDP does not require manual labeling of demonstration data into different failure categories or action primitives, simplifying the data preparation phase for developers.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the manual definition of failure key features and unoptimized combination weights, point to clear avenues for future research. Automating feature extraction (e.g., using latent space methods) and developing adaptive weight optimization strategies could further enhance the method's capabilities and robustness. The suggestion of incorporating offline exploratory mechanisms also offers a path to refine recovery policies without real-time risks.

Overall, CCDP represents a step towards more robust and adaptive robotic systems that can operate effectively in dynamic environments by learning to recover from errors efficiently and intelligently from observed successes.
