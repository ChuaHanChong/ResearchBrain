# ASAP: Aligning Simulation and Real-World Physics for Learning Agile Humanoid Whole-Body Skills

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and NVIDIA developed ASAP, a two-stage framework that aligns simulation and real-world physics for learning agile humanoid whole-body skills. The approach utilizes a learned "delta action model" to compensate for dynamics discrepancies, enabling a Unitree G1 humanoid robot to perform complex, human-like motions and reducing tracking errors by up to 52.7% in real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to perform agile, whole-body human-like skills due to the inherent "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in idealized simulations fail on physical robots.
- Existing sim-to-real transfer methods like System Identification (SysID) are labor-intensive, limited by predefined parameter spaces, and cannot account for all unmodeled real-world phenomena.
- Domain Randomization (DR), while robust, often leads to overly conservative policies that sacrifice the agility and fine coordination required for dynamic whole-body skills in humanoids.

## Method

- A two-stage framework that first pre-trains motion tracking policies in a high-fidelity simulator (IsaacGym) using retargeted human video data.
- Introduced a novel "delta action model" trained on real-world robot data to learn a residual correction to the nominal simulated actions, effectively aligning the simulator's dynamics with real-world physics.
- Fine-tuned the pre-trained motion tracking policy within the 'aligned' simulator (where the delta action model perturbs the dynamics) to adapt the policy to the more accurate, real-world-representative dynamics.

## Results

- ASAP achieved superior dynamics matching in open-loop replay experiments, consistently outperforming SysID and Delta Dynamics baselines by accurately reproducing real-world trajectories in simulation.
- Closed-loop policy evaluations demonstrated that ASAP significantly reduced motion tracking errors (e.g., E_g-mpjpe, E_mpjpe) and maintained a **100%** success rate across all difficulty levels in sim-to-sim transfer.
- Successfully deployed on a real Unitree G1 humanoid robot, enabling agile motions and reducing tracking errors for challenging movements, with E_g-mpjpe for a kick motion decreasing from 61.2mm to 50.2mm and for a "Silencer" motion from 159mm to 112mm.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly learning a residual (delta) action model from real-world data effectively compensates for the simulator-to-real dynamics mismatch in complex humanoid robots.
> - The dynamics mismatch between simulation and reality is often structured and non-uniform across robot joints (e.g., larger discrepancies in lower-body motors), which a learned delta model can specifically correct.
> - Fine-tuning motion tracking policies within a simulator that has been dynamically aligned with real-world physics enables superior agility and motion performance on physical humanoids compared to traditional robustness methods or direct policy transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of agile, whole-body human-like skills has long been a grand challenge in robotics. While traditional approaches have focused heavily on stable locomotion (e.g., walking, running) or isolated upper-body manipulation, achieving truly dynamic and coordinated full-body movements akin to human athletes (e.g., jumping, intricate celebrations) remains elusive. The primary bottleneck is the "sim-to-real gap"—the inherent discrepancy between simulated physics, where control policies are typically trained, and the complexities of real-world dynamics, sensor noise, and actuator limitations.

The existing landscape of sim-to-real transfer methods primarily includes:
- **System Identification (SysID):** These methods aim to precisely model real-world robot parameters (e.g., mass, friction, motor characteristics) and transfer them to the simulator. While effective for calibrating known parameters, SysID is often labor-intensive, requires predefined parameter spaces, and frequently relies on ground truth torque measurements that are not available on all hardware. Crucially, it struggles to capture unmodeled dynamics or parameters outside its defined space.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** This approach trains control policies in simulation by randomizing various physical parameters (e.g., friction, mass, latency) within a wide range, hoping that the resulting policy will be robust enough to generalize to the real world. While effective for robustness, DR often leads to overly conservative policies that sacrifice agility, as the policy must perform well across a broad, uncertain distribution of dynamics, limiting its ability to exploit specific real-world characteristics for high-performance maneuvers.
- **Learned Dynamics Models:** This nascent field involves learning a predictive model of real-world physics directly from real-world data. While showing promise in simpler systems like drones or ground vehicles, its application to high-dimensional, complex systems like humanoid robots for agile whole-body control has been largely unexplored.

ASAP positions itself as a significant advancement by addressing the limitations of SysID and DR head-on. It acknowledges the complexity of humanoids, where a complete and accurate physics model is intractable, and direct parameter tuning (SysID) is insufficient. Instead of merely robustifying against uncertainty (DR), ASAP aims to *align* the simulation with real-world physics by learning to compensate for the *specific discrepancies*. By introducing a "delta action model," ASAP leverages the strengths of learned dynamics in a novel way: it learns a *residual correction* to the nominal simulated actions to mimic real-world effects. This allows the primary control policy to be fine-tuned in an effectively "real-world-aligned" simulator, thereby enabling highly agile and expressive whole-body skills that were previously unattainable with conservative DR or incomplete SysID methods.

Furthermore, the paper builds on recent progress in using human motion data for robot control. The pipeline of capturing human videos, reconstructing SMPL motions, and then cleaning and retargeting them to robots for policy training is a growing trend, enabling robots to learn more natural and diverse movements. ASAP integrates this, pushing the boundaries from mere motion tracking to agile, dynamic execution in the physical world. The open-sourcing of a multi-simulator codebase also contributes to the broader research community by facilitating further studies on sim-to-sim and sim-to-real transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the ASAP research is to enable humanoid robots to execute **agile and coordinated whole-body skills** that are comparable to human movements. This ambition is directly motivated by the perceived potential of humanoid robots for unparalleled versatility, yet currently hindered by fundamental technical challenges.

The core motivation stems from the persistent **dynamics mismatch between simulation and the real world**. Policies trained in perfectly modeled, idealized simulation environments often degrade significantly or fail outright when deployed on physical robots. This gap prevents the transfer of complex, high-agility skills learned in simulation to real-world applications.

Specifically, the paper identifies the shortcomings of existing sim-to-real transfer approaches, which serve as key motivators for ASAP:
- **System Identification (SysID):** While useful, SysID demands labor-intensive parameter tuning and is limited by a predefined parameter space. It cannot account for all unmodeled real-world phenomena, especially when real dynamics fall outside the assumed distribution. Furthermore, its reliance on ground truth torque measurements often renders it impractical for many widely used robotic platforms.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** Although effective in inducing robustness, DR often leads to "overly conservative policies." These policies are designed to work across a broad range of perturbed dynamics, but this robustness comes at the cost of sacrificing the agility and fine coordination needed for dynamic, high-performance whole-body skills.

Against this backdrop, ASAP sets forth the following key objectives:
1. **Develop a two-stage framework:** Create a robust and practical framework that explicitly addresses and compensates for the dynamics mismatch between simulation and reality.
2. **Enable agile whole-body skills:** Move beyond conservative locomotion or upper-body movements to achieve truly dynamic and expressive full-body actions in humanoid robots.
3. **Bridge the sim-to-real gap effectively:** Demonstrate that the proposed approach significantly reduces motion tracking error and improves agility compared to state-of-the-art baselines like SysID, DR, and learned delta dynamics.
4. **Validate on diverse transfer scenarios:** Prove the generalizability of the framework across different simulation environments (sim-to-sim transfer, e.g., IsaacGym to IsaacSim, IsaacGym to Genesis) and crucially, to a real-world humanoid robot (IsaacGym to Unitree G1).
5. **Successful real-world deployment:** Achieve previously difficult-to-achieve humanoid motions (e.g., Cristiano Ronaldo's celebration, Kobe Bryant's fadeaway jump shot, large jumps) directly on a physical robot.
6. **Provide open-source tools:** Release a multi-simulator training and evaluation codebase to foster further research and accelerate progress in the field.

In essence, ASAP is motivated by the desire to unlock the full potential of humanoid robots by overcoming the most significant hurdle in modern robotics: the intractable nature of real-world physics in simulation, allowing for the natural, dynamic, and agile movements envisioned for these complex machines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ASAP (Aligning Simulation and Real Physics) is a meticulously designed **two-stage framework** that combines pre-training of policies in simulation with a novel post-training phase that learns to align simulation dynamics with real-world physics using a "delta action model."

**Stage 1: Motion Tracking Pre-training (in Simulation)**

The first stage focuses on training base motion tracking policies in a high-fidelity simulator (IsaacGym).
- **Data Generation: Retargeting Human Video Data:**
 - **Human Video to SMPL Motions:** Videos of humans performing expressive and agile motions (e.g., sports celebrations) are recorded. The TRAM system is used to reconstruct 3D human motions from these videos, representing them in the SMPL (Skinned Multi-Person Linear) parameter format, which captures global trajectory, orientation, body poses, and shape.
 - **Simulation-based Data Cleaning:** To ensure physical feasibility and filter out noise from the reconstruction, a "sim-to-data" cleaning procedure is employed. MaskedMimic, a physics-based motion tracker, attempts to imitate the SMPL motions in the IsaacGym simulator. Only motions successfully tracked in simulation are retained, forming a cleaned dataset.
 - **Retargeting SMPL Motions to Robot Motions:** The cleaned SMPL motions, designed for various human body shapes, are then retargeted to the specific kinematics and morphology of the target humanoid robot (Unitree G1). This involves a two-stage process: first optimizing shape parameters to approximate a humanoid, then minimizing joint distances to ensure accurate motion transfer, resulting in physically plausible robot trajectories.
- **Phase-based Motion Tracking Policy Training:**
 - The problem is framed as a **goal-conditioned Reinforcement Learning (RL) task**. The policy (π) learns to track the retargeted robot trajectories from the cleaned dataset.
 - **State Representation:** The robot's state (`s_t`) includes proprioceptive information (joint positions, velocities, root angular velocity, projected gravity, past actions) and a crucial `time phase variable` (`φ`). This `φ` (ranging from 0 to 1) acts as a goal state for single-motion tracking, effectively indicating progression through the reference motion.
 - **Action Space:** Actions (`a_t`) are target joint positions, which are fed to a low-level PD controller to actuate the robot's degrees of freedom.
 - **Optimization:** The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used to maximize cumulative discounted rewards.
 - **Key Design Choices for Stable Training:**
 - **Asymmetric Actor-Critic Training:** The critic network has access to "privileged information" (e.g., global reference motion positions, root linear velocity) that is unavailable to the real robot. This helps stabilize and accelerate training in simulation. The actor, however, only uses proprioceptive and time-phase inputs, ensuring it can be deployed in the real world without requiring external localization systems (like odometry).
 - **Termination Curriculum of Tracking Tolerance:** To enable learning of difficult agile motions (e.g., jumps), a curriculum is implemented. Initially, a generous termination threshold (1.5m deviation from reference) prevents early failures. As training progresses, this threshold is progressively tightened (to 0.3m), forcing the policy to achieve higher tracking precision.
 - **Reference State Initialization (RSI):** Instead of always starting at the beginning of a motion, episodes are initialized by randomly sampling a time-phase variable and setting the robot's state to the corresponding reference motion state. This allows the policy to learn different phases of a motion in parallel, preventing it from getting stuck on early, difficult parts (e.g., requiring a perfect landing before a jump).
 - **Reward Terms:** A comprehensive reward function (Table I) combining penalties (DoF limits, torque limits, termination), regularization (torques, action rate, feet orientation/heading, slippage), and task-specific rewards (body/joint position, velocity, rotation tracking) guides the learning process.
 - **Domain Randomizations:** Basic domain randomization techniques (Table VI) are applied during pre-training to improve policy robustness and generalization.

**Stage 2: Post-training: Training Delta Action Model and Fine-tuning Motion Tracking Policy**

This critical stage addresses the sim-to-real gap by leveraging real-world data to refine the dynamics model within the simulator and consequently fine-tune the policy.
- **Data Collection:** The pre-trained policy (from Stage 1) is deployed on the real Unitree G1 robot. Real-world trajectories, including robot states (base position/velocity/orientation, joint positions/velocities) and executed actions, are recorded using motion capture and onboard sensors.
- **Training Delta Action Model:**
 - The core idea: When real-world collected trajectories (state-action pairs) are replayed in simulation, the simulated trajectory deviates from the real one due to the dynamics mismatch. This deviation provides a learning signal.
 - A **delta (residual) action model** (`πΔ(s_t, a_t)`) is trained using RL. This model takes the current state `s_t` and the intended action `a_t` as input and outputs a corrective action `∆a_t`.
 - The simulated dynamics are effectively "perturbed" by this delta action: `s_t+1 = f_sim(s_t, a_r_t + ∆a_t)`, where `f_sim` is the nominal simulator dynamics and `a_r_t` is the action recorded from the real world.
 - The RL training for `πΔ` minimizes the discrepancy between the simulated state (`s_t+1`) and the actually recorded real-world state (`s_r_t+1`), along with an action magnitude regularization (Table II). This allows `πΔ` to learn compensations for unmodeled real-world phenomena (e.g., under-performing motors).
 - *Practical constraint:* Due to severe hardware constraints (robot breakage, overheating) limiting real-world data collection, a simplified 4-DoF ankle delta action model was trained for the real-world experiments, focusing on areas with significant sim-to-real gaps.
- **Fine-tuning Motion Tracking Policy under New Dynamics:**
 - Once the `πΔ` model is trained and *frozen*, it's integrated into the simulator. The effective simulator dynamics become `s_t+1 = f_ASAP(s_t, a_t) = f_sim(s_t, a_t + πΔ(s_t, a_t))`.
 - The pre-trained motion tracking policy from Stage 1 is then fine-tuned within this "aligned" simulator using the original reward function (Table I). This process adapts the policy to the dynamics that more accurately reflect the real world.
- **Policy Deployment:** Finally, the fine-tuned policy is deployed directly onto the real Unitree G1 robot. Crucially, the delta action model (`πΔ`) is *not* deployed on the robot; it was only used during fine-tuning in the simulator to align its dynamics. The fine-tuned policy is expected to implicitly handle the real-world dynamics. For real-world deployment, a robust locomotion policy is also trained to facilitate transitions between different motion-tracking tasks without manual resets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ASAP's performance was rigorously evaluated across various transfer scenarios, including sim-to-sim (IsaacGym to IsaacSim, IsaacGym to Genesis) and sim-to-real (IsaacGym to Unitree G1 robot) setups, using metrics such as success rate, global body position error (E_g-mpjpe), root-relative mean per-joint position error (E_mpjpe), acceleration error (E_acc), and root velocity error (E_vel).

**Q1: Can ASAP outperform other baseline methods to compensate for the dynamics mismatch (Open-Loop Performance)?**
- **Superior Dynamics Matching:** In open-loop replay experiments (replaying real-world state-action trajectories in simulation), ASAP consistently outperformed the "Vanilla" (no alignment), "SysID," and "Delta Dynamics" baselines across all motion lengths and in both IsaacSim and Genesis simulators.
- **Addressing Long-Horizon Issues:** While SysID offered minor improvements for short horizons, it struggled with cumulative error over longer periods. Delta Dynamics showed better long-horizon performance but suffered from overfitting. ASAP, however, demonstrated superior generalization by effectively learning residual policies that truly bridge the dynamics gap, achieving the lowest E_g-mpjpe and E_mpjpe. This highlights ASAP's efficacy in accurately reproducing testing-environment trajectories in the training environment, thus improving the underlying physics alignment.

**Q2: Can ASAP finetune policy to outperform SysID and Delta Dynamics methods (Closed-Loop Performance)?**
- **Significant Performance Gains:** When evaluating closed-loop motion imitation (where the fine-tuned policy directly controls the robot), ASAP consistently outshone all baselines across Easy, Medium, and Hard difficulty levels in both IsaacSim and Genesis.
- **Reduced Tracking Errors:** ASAP achieved the lowest E_g-mpjpe, E_mpjpe, E_acc, and E_vel values. For instance, in Hard-level tasks, ASAP dramatically reduced motion-tracking errors compared to SysID and Delta Dynamics.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Success Rate:** Unlike Delta Dynamics, which showed reduced success rates in harder environments, ASAP consistently maintained a 100% success rate across all difficulty levels and simulators, demonstrating superior robustness and adaptability to new dynamics. Visualizations further reinforced that ASAP maintains stable tracking performance where baselines accumulate errors.

**Q3: Does ASAP work for sim-to-real transfer?**
- **Successful Real-World Deployment:** ASAP was successfully deployed on the real Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Despite the significant and more pronounced sim-to-real gap compared to sim-to-sim transfers (due to sensor noise, modeling inaccuracies, and actuator differences), ASAP enabled the robot to perform highly agile motions.
- **Quantifiable Real-World Improvement:** For both in-distribution (kick) and out-of-distribution (LeBron "Silencer") motions, ASAP fine-tuning led to a considerable reduction in tracking errors (E_g-mpjpe, E_mpjpe, E_acc, E_vel) compared to the "Vanilla" baseline. For the kick motion, ASAP reduced E_g-mpjpe from 61.2 to 50.2mm, and for the challenging "Silencer" motion, it reduced it from 159 to 112mm. This validated ASAP's effectiveness in improving real-world performance for agile humanoid motion tracking.
- **Practical Data Constraints:** While the full 23-DoF delta action model was data-hungry, focusing on a 4-DoF ankle delta action model for real-world experiments proved sufficient and feasible with limited data (100 motion clips), demonstrating a pragmatic approach to real-world challenges.

**Q4: How to best train the delta action model of ASAP?**
- **Dataset Size:** Increasing dataset size improves the delta action model's generalization (reduced out-of-distribution errors), but closed-loop policy performance gains saturated beyond a certain point, suggesting diminishing returns for very large datasets in fine-tuning.
- **Training Horizon:** Longer training horizons generally improved open-loop performance. However, for closed-loop policy fine-tuning, an optimal horizon of 1.0s was found; excessively long horizons did not provide further benefits.
- **Action Norm Weight:** This hyperparameter is crucial. An optimal action norm weight of 0.1 achieved the lowest errors. Too high a weight caused performance degradation, highlighting a trade-off between minimizing corrective actions and effectively compensating for dynamics.

**Q5: How to best use the delta action model of ASAP?**
- **RL Fine-Tuning is Superior:** Comparing various strategies (fixed-point iteration, gradient-based optimization, and RL fine-tuning) for using the learned delta action model to refine the policy, RL fine-tuning consistently achieved the lowest tracking error during deployment.
- **Limitations of Training-Free Methods:** The RL-free approaches (fixed-point and gradient-based) performed worse than the baseline, indicating that their myopic, one-step matching approximations and susceptibility to out-of-distribution issues make them unsuitable for robust multi-step control.

**Q6: Why and how does ASAP work?**
- **Beyond Random Noise:** ASAP fine-tuning significantly outperformed fine-tuning with random action noise. While random noise provided some robustness, it could not match the precision of ASAP.
- **Structured Discrepancies:** Visualization of the learned delta action model's output magnitude revealed that the dynamics mismatch is not uniform across all robot joints. Specifically, lower-body motors (especially ankle and knee joints) exhibited larger discrepancies than upper-body joints, and even showed left-right asymmetries. This detailed, structured compensation is precisely what a learned delta action model can provide, unlike simple, uniform random noise, thus explaining its superior performance.

In summary, ASAP's comprehensive evaluation demonstrates its ability to effectively align simulation and real-world physics through a learned delta action model, leading to substantial improvements in humanoid robot agility and motion tracking performance across various complex tasks and environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ASAP framework represents a significant step forward in the field of humanoid robotics and sim-to-real transfer, offering solutions to long-standing challenges that have hindered the development of truly agile and versatile humanoid robots.

**Key Significance:**

- **Enabling Agile Whole-Body Skills:** Unlike much prior work focused on robust locomotion or upper-body manipulation, ASAP successfully enables humanoid robots to perform highly dynamic, whole-body, human-like skills such as jumping, complex athletic celebrations, and kicks in the real world. This capability is crucial for humanoid robots to integrate into human environments and perform complex tasks that require full-body coordination.
- **Novel Approach to Sim-to-Real:** ASAP introduces a unique and effective method for bridging the sim-to-real gap. By learning a "universal delta action model" from real-world data, it directly compensates for the complex, unmodeled dynamics mismatch between simulation and reality. This approach moves beyond the limitations of traditional System Identification (SysID) (which struggles with unmodeled parameters) and Domain Randomization (DR) (which often leads to conservative policies sacrificing agility). The delta action model's ability to identify and correct *structured* discrepancies, rather than just adding random noise, is a key innovation.
- **Quantitative and Qualitative Improvements:** The extensive evaluation, across both sim-to-sim and crucial sim-to-real scenarios, demonstrates significant quantitative reductions in motion tracking errors (up to 52.7% in sim-to-real tasks). Qualitatively, the visual results (Fig. 1, 6, 8) clearly show the Unitree G1 robot executing movements with a level of agility and coordination previously difficult to achieve.
- **Reduced Reliance on Perfect Models:** ASAP's methodology effectively "corrects" the simulator, making it more representative of the real world without requiring exhaustive, manual system identification or perfect physics modeling. This significantly lowers the barrier to deploying complex policies on real hardware.
- **Robust Policy Fine-Tuning:** The approach demonstrates that fine-tuning pre-trained policies within an aligned simulation environment is more effective than direct transfer or less sophisticated alignment methods. The findings on optimal delta action model training parameters and the superiority of RL fine-tuning provide valuable insights for future research.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerating Humanoid Robot Development:** By making it easier and more effective to transfer complex skills from simulation to reality, ASAP could significantly accelerate the pace of research and development in humanoid robotics. This allows researchers to rapidly prototype and test advanced behaviors in simulation with a higher likelihood of real-world success.
- **Broader Applications for Humanoids:** Humanoid robots with enhanced agility and whole-body control can find applications in a wider range of domains, from disaster response and elder care to logistics, manufacturing, and even entertainment. Enabling them to perform human-like actions opens up new possibilities for human-robot collaboration and interaction.
- **Advancements in AI and Robotics:** The principles of learning residual models to compensate for real-world dynamics can be generalized to other robotic platforms and control problems, contributing broadly to the fields of reinforcement learning, control, and sim-to-real transfer.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The open-sourcing of the multi-simulator training and evaluation codebase is a substantial contribution to the research community. It lowers the barrier for other researchers to build upon ASAP's findings, fostering collaboration and accelerating progress.

**Acknowledged Limitations and Future Directions:**

The paper is commendably transparent about its limitations, which also highlight promising avenues for future research:
- **Hardware Constraints:** Agile motions cause significant stress and lead to hardware failures, limiting real-world data collection. Future work could explore "damage-aware policies" or more robust hardware.
- **Reliance on MoCap:** The current pipeline requires a motion capture (MoCap) system for real-world data collection, which is a practical barrier in unstructured environments. "MoCap-free alignment" is a crucial future direction.
- **Data-Hungry Delta Action Training:** Training a full 23-DoF delta action model remains impractical due to the large data demand. Developing "sample-efficient few-shot alignment" techniques for delta action models is critical for scalability.

Despite these limitations, ASAP's contributions lay a strong foundation for future advancements, paving the way for more versatile, expressive, and truly agile humanoid robots that can seamlessly interact with and operate in complex real-world environments.
