# ZETA: A Controlled Study of Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment VLA Transfer for Tabletop Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ZETA is a controlled study of zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer for vision-language-action policies in stationary tabletop manipulation with two-finger grippers. It separates strict from pretrain-exposed zero-shot protocols, builds a benchmark of 14 held-out target embodiments across four shift categories, and varies state-action representation, source-embodiment diversity, auxiliary co-training, and target exposure, finding gains of roughly 15, 18, 7, and 13.4 percentage points respectively.

## Problem

- Zero-shot transfer is defined inconsistently: some studies exclude the target robot from all training, others only from post-training, making reported numbers incomparable across papers.
- Existing evaluations entangle embodiment shift with differing tasks, scenes, cameras, data budgets and protocols, so failures cannot be attributed to embodiment mismatch itself.
- Prior multi-embodiment work varies data scale, task distribution, embodiment coverage and architecture simultaneously, leaving representation and supervision choices unmeasured under matched conditions.

## Method

- ==Protocol taxonomy== separating ==strict zero-shot==, where the target is absent from all training, from ==pretrain-exposed zero-shot==, paired with four shift categories: appearance-only, gripper-only, arm-only, full-embodiment.
- Controlled benchmark using 512 procedurally generated Franka-style source embodiments, a fixed 640K-trajectory pretraining budget, ==domain randomization==, and seven held-out commercial targets mirrored by matched real hardware.
- Factorized comparison on a generalist VLA backbone of ==EEF-Delta== versus world-frame and absolute state-action encodings, plus three auxiliary co-training objectives: ==language-action==, ==subgoal prediction==, ==task-conditioned bounding boxes==.

## Results

- EEF-Delta state with EEF-Delta actions reached **75.7%** average simulation progress versus **60.3%** for absolute-state world-delta, and **89.9%** versus **56.0%** in real-world trials.
- Under the fixed **640K** budget, **512** source embodiments beat a single source by about **18 points**, though real-world transfer dipped at eight sources before recovering.
- Bounding-box co-training raised average progress from **75.7%** to **82.3%**; **5%** target-embodiment pretraining data lifted UR5eUMI from **69.3%** to **78.6%** and GoogleRobot from **51.4%** to **68.9%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Express both state and action in the current end-effector frame: local motion semantics survive arm and gripper swaps where base-frame coordinates do not.
> - Embodiment diversity trades off against per-embodiment data density under a fixed budget, so more source robots is not monotonically better without more data.
> - Even small target exposure during pretraining compresses gaps between representations, so zero-shot claims are uninterpretable unless the exposure protocol is reported explicitly.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalizable vision-language-action (VLA) models capable of operating across diverse robotic hardware is a central objective in robotics research. A key aspect of this generalization is zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer, where policies trained on one or more "source" robots are expected to perform tasks on new, "target" robots without requiring additional task-specific demonstrations on the target platform. This capability is motivated by the rapid evolution of robot hardware and the high cost associated with collecting task-specific data for every new robot.

Despite the recognized importance of zero-shot transfer, a systematic understanding of the factors influencing its success remains limited in the existing literature. The authors identify two primary challenges that this work aims to address:
1. **Inconsistent Definition of "Zero-Shot Transfer"**: Previous studies have used the term "zero-shot transfer" to describe different evaluation protocols. Some consider it a scenario where the target embodiment is entirely absent from all training data (pretraining and post-training), while others include the target embodiment during pretraining but exclude it from task-specific post-training. This ambiguity hinders direct comparison and interpretation of reported transfer results.
2. **Uncontrolled Evaluation Settings**: Many existing evaluations often confound embodiment shifts with other variables such as changes in tasks, environments, camera configurations, data quantities, or training protocols. This entanglement makes it challenging to isolate the specific impact of embodiment mismatch on policy performance.

The paper situates itself within a growing body of work on multi-embodiment pretraining and cross-embodiment policy design. Prior research has explored pooling heterogeneous robot data for foundation models, scaling generalist VLA policies across platforms, and designing policies with shared skill spaces or embodiment-specific interfaces. Empirical studies have also investigated data diversity and embodiment scaling. However, these works frequently vary data scale, task distribution, embodiment coverage, and architectural choices concurrently.

This research distinguishes itself by introducing a controlled experimental framework specifically designed to isolate and quantify the effects of key factors on cross-embodiment VLA transfer. By clearly defining zero-shot protocols and systematically varying factors under matched conditions, this study aims to provide a more rigorous empirical foundation for understanding and improving robot policy generalization. The focus on stationary tabletop manipulation with two-finger grippers provides a tractable setting for these controlled comparisons, while acknowledging the broader scope of future research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to systematically enhance the understanding of zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer for vision-language-action (VLA) models. This understanding is crucial for developing generalizable robotic policies that can adapt to new hardware without extensive, costly data collection.

The primary motivations for undertaking this study stem from two critical gaps in the existing literature:

1. **Defining Zero-Shot Transfer Protocols**: The term "zero-shot transfer" has been applied inconsistently across different studies, leading to difficulties in comparing results and understanding true generalization capabilities. Some studies define it as a scenario where the target robot is entirely unseen during *any* stage of training (pretraining or post-training), while others consider it "zero-shot" if the target robot is only seen during *pretraining* but not during task-specific *post-training*. This paper explicitly aims to clarify this distinction and evaluate its impact. The motivation is to establish a more precise nomenclature and demonstrate whether these different exposure levels indeed lead to distinct transfer difficulties, thereby advocating for separate reporting protocols.

2. **Controlled Evaluation of Embodiment Shifts**: Existing evaluations often conflate embodiment changes with other experimental variables, such as variations in tasks, environments, camera setups, data quantities, or training procedures. This lack of control makes it challenging to attribute observed performance differences directly to the embodiment mismatch itself. The motivation here is to design a controlled benchmark where only the robot embodiment varies, while other non-embodiment factors are kept consistent. This controlled setting allows for a more granular analysis of how specific types of embodiment changes affect transfer performance. The study specifically focuses on stationary tabletop manipulation with two-finger grippers to maintain a consistent manipulation regime, enabling the isolation of embodiment-specific factors.

To address these motivations, the study articulates four specific research questions (RQ1-RQ4), each designed to probe a critical factor influencing cross-embodiment transfer:

- **RQ1: Which state-action representation best supports strict zero-shot transfer to unseen embodiments?** This objective investigates how different ways of representing robot state and actions (e.g., in world coordinates versus end-effector (EEF) local frames, absolute poses versus delta changes) influence a policy's ability to generalize to a robot it has never encountered. The motivation is to identify representations that encode motion semantics in a more embodiment-agnostic manner.

- **RQ2: Under different pretraining budget controls, how does varying the number of source embodiments within a procedurally generated pool affect strict transfer?** This question examines the impact of diversity in pretraining data on generalization. It explores whether training on a larger number of distinct source embodiments, even if it means less data per embodiment (under a fixed total budget), improves transfer. Alternatively, it also investigates the effect when the per-embodiment data is fixed, leading to a larger total budget. The motivation is to understand the interplay between source embodiment diversity and data allocation strategies.

- **RQ3: Do co-training objectives improve transfer beyond imitation learning alone?** This objective assesses whether auxiliary supervision signals, such as structured language-action descriptions, subgoal prediction, or task-conditioned bounding box predictions, can imbue VLA models with more transferable semantic, spatial, or motion-level understanding, thereby improving zero-shot performance. The motivation is to explore complementary learning signals that might enhance generalization beyond pure imitation learning.

- **RQ4: How much does seeing the target embodiment during pretraining change the difficulty of zero-shot transfer?** This question directly addresses the protocol ambiguity. It quantifies the performance difference between "strict zero-shot transfer" (target embodiment never seen) and "pretrain-exposed zero-shot transfer" (target embodiment seen only during pretraining). The motivation is to provide empirical evidence for the necessity of distinguishing and separately reporting these two scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a controlled study design to systematically analyze zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer in VLA models for stationary tabletop manipulation with two-finger grippers.

#### 3.1. Protocol Clarification and Taxonomy

The study formally distinguishes two zero-shot transfer protocols:
- **Strict zero-shot transfer**: The target embodiment is entirely absent from all training data (pretraining and post-training). Formally, $e_t \notin E_{pre}$.
- **Pretrain-exposed zero-shot transfer**: The target embodiment appears only during pretraining ($e_t \in E_{pre}$) but is excluded from task-specific post-training.

Both protocols evaluate on the target embodiment ($e_t$) for downstream tasks ($T_{post}$), and in both cases, pretraining tasks ($T_{pre}$) are disjoint from downstream tasks ($T_{post}$). The post-training data ($D_{post}$) always comes from a source embodiment ($e_s \neq e_t$).

#### 3.2. Controlled Benchmark Construction

To address the challenge of uncontrolled evaluation settings, a benchmark was constructed with the following characteristics:
- **Simulation Environment**: A simulator (MuJoCo for physics, IsaacSim for rendering) was used to create a large-scale pretraining dataset and evaluate policies. Domain randomization (visual and physical properties) was extensively applied to reduce sim-to-real gaps.
- **Target Embodiments**: 14 held-out target embodiments were utilized across simulation and real-world validation, deliberately excluded from the source pretraining pool.
- **Embodiment Shift Taxonomy**: Embodiment changes were categorized into four types for fine-grained analysis:
 - **Appearance-only (APP)**: Changes only visual texture (e.g., Franka with logo, Franka with green fingers).
 - **Gripper-only (GRP)**: Alters end-effector geometry while keeping the arm fixed (e.g., Franka with UMI gripper).
 - **Arm-only (ARM)**: Changes arm morphology/kinematics while preserving the end effector (e.g., UR5e with Franka hand, Google Robot with Franka hand).
 - **Full-embodiment (FULL)**: Changes both arm/platform and end-effector factors (e.g., UR5e+UMI, GoogleRobot, Humanoid robot).
- **Controlled Non-Embodiment Factors**: For evaluation, non-embodiment factors (backbone, downstream source data, evaluation setting, budget controls, camera poses, lighting, background, table layout, task descriptions, objects, and initial pose sampling regions) were kept consistent across experiments to isolate embodiment changes.

#### 3.3. Model Backbone and Training Protocol

- **Architecture**: A Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) backbone, following $\pi_{0.5}$, was used. The vision-language stream was initialized from pretrained VLM weights and coupled with an action expert via layer-wise attention.
- **Training Stages**: All models followed a two-stage training protocol:
 1. **Pretraining**: On $D_{pre}$, with its composition varying based on the research question.
 2. **Post-training**: On $D_{post}$, using a single source embodiment ($e_s$) for a set of downstream tasks ($T_{post}$).
- **Inputs and Outputs**: Visual observations included wrist-mounted and third-person views (resized to 224x224). Proprioceptive inputs used a 4-step state history. The policy predicted a 12-step action chunk. Action outputs were supervised with a flow-matching imitation loss.
- **Data Budgets**: A fixed pretraining budget of 640K trajectories was primarily used. Post-training involved 40K trajectories per task in simulation and 50 demonstrations per task in the real world.

#### 3.4. Research Question Specific Methodologies

- **RQ1: State-Action Representations**: Four combinations of state and action representations were compared (Table 2):
 - **Action Representations**: World-Delta (motion in robot base frame) and EEF-Delta (motion in current gripper frame).
 - **State Representations**: Absolute EEF state (history of absolute gripper poses) and EEF-Delta state (history of gripper poses relative to current gripper pose).
 - The evaluation was under strict zero-shot transfer, using data from 512 source embodiments.

- **RQ2: Source-Embodiment Diversity**: Procedurally generated 512 Franka-style source embodiments were created with geometric and visual variations (Appendix D, Figure 9). Two budget control settings were examined:
 - **Fixed Total Pretraining Budget**: 640K trajectories total, varying source embodiment count (1, 8, 512). This implies decreasing per-embodiment data with increasing source count.
 - **Fixed Per-Embodiment Budget**: Each source contributed the same amount of data, so total budget scaled with source count (32, 128, 512).
 - All RQ2 models used the best state-action representation from RQ1.

- **RQ3: Auxiliary Co-training Objectives**: Three types of auxiliary objectives were evaluated in simulation, in addition to standard imitation learning, using the best state-action representation from RQ1 and the 512-source pretraining pool:
 - **Language-Action Prediction (LAP)**: Predicts structured language descriptions of actions (EEF-frame or mixed-frame).
 - **Subgoal Prediction**: Supervises task progress with a next-step goal representation.
 - **Task-conditioned Bounding-Box Prediction**: Grounds instructions in object-centric image regions.
 - Auxiliary objectives were applied during both pretraining and post-training, using cross-entropy loss with a weight of 0.25 relative to imitation loss.

- **RQ4: Target-Embodiment Exposure**: This compared strict zero-shot transfer with pretrain-exposed zero-shot transfer on two representative targets (UR5eUMI and GoogleRobot). Target-embodiment data was introduced into the pretraining pool at varying percentages (0%, 5%, 30%, 100%), replacing an equal amount of source data to keep the total pretraining budget fixed. A non-zero-shot oracle (post-trained on target-embodiment downstream data) served as an upper bound reference.

#### 3.5. Metrics and Evaluation Scale

- **Metric**: Task progress scored on a% scale, with partial credit for subgoals (e.g., 0.5 for contacting an object).
- **Simulation Evaluation**: 3 downstream tasks, 7 test embodiments, 100 rollouts per task-embodiment pair, over 3 independent training runs (total 6,300 rollouts per model). Mean task progress ± standard deviation reported.
- **Real-World Validation**: 2 downstream tasks, 7 test embodiments, 10 trials per task-embodiment pair (total 140 rollouts per model).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The controlled study yielded several findings regarding zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer in VLA models:

#### 4.1. RQ1: State-Action Representations Affect Transfer

- **Impact of EEF-Centered Representations**: EEF-centered representations demonstrated improved strict zero-shot transfer. Specifically, switching the action representation from World-Delta to EEF-Delta, while keeping absolute EEF state, raised the average progress score from 60.3% to 64.6% in simulation.
- **EEF-Delta State Benefit**: Replacing absolute EEF state with EEF-Delta state significantly improved transfer, particularly for larger embodiment shifts. For arm-only shifts, progress increased from 38.5–39.9% to 69.8–74.3%, and for full-embodiment shifts, it rose from 33.9–38.6% to 58.4–60.4%. This suggests that local state representations preserve more transferable motion structure.
- **Overall Best Performance**: The combination of EEF-Delta state with EEF-Delta actions achieved the highest average score of 75.7% in simulation and 89.9% in real-world validation, outperforming other combinations (Tables 3, 4).

#### 4.2. RQ2: Source-Embodiment Diversity Affects Transfer Under Different Budget Controls

- **Fixed Total Pretraining Budget**: When the total pretraining budget was fixed at 640K trajectories, increasing the number of source embodiments from 1 to 512 led to an improvement in performance. The 512-source model outperformed the single-source model by approximately 18 percentage points, with the largest gains observed on gripper-only, arm-only, and full-embodiment shifts (Figure 5a). Attention visualizations indicated that the 512-source model focused more on task-relevant objects and the gripper. UMAP analysis showed greater feature alignment between unseen target embodiments for the 512-source model (Figure 4).
- **Real-World Non-Monotonic Trend**: In real-world validation under a fixed total budget, increasing source diversity from 1 to 8 initially led to a decrease in performance for arm-only and full-embodiment shifts, before recovering and surpassing the single-source model at 512 sources (Figure 5b). This suggests a trade-off where increasing source coverage can dilute per-embodiment data density, negatively impacting performance until sufficient diversity is achieved.
- **Fixed Per-Embodiment Pretraining Budget**: When the per-embodiment trajectory budget was fixed, increasing the source pool from 32 to 128 to 512 embodiments consistently improved average progress in both simulation and real-world evaluations (Figure 6). This indicates that, without the constraint of a fixed total budget, broader source coverage is beneficial.

#### 4.3. RQ3: Co-training Tasks Affect Transfer

- **General Improvement**: All evaluated auxiliary co-training objectives improved strict zero-shot transfer compared to the no-co-training baseline (average progress increased from 75.7% to 77.7–82.3%) (Table 5).
- **Gains on Larger Shifts**: The improvements from co-training were concentrated on gripper-only, arm-only, and full-embodiment shifts, indicating their utility in bridging larger embodiment gaps.
- **EEF-Frame LAP Superiority**: Among language-action variants, the EEF-only objective outperformed the mixed-frame LAP, suggesting that auxiliary supervision is more effective when its action semantics align with the local EEF representation.
- **Top Performer**: Task-conditioned bounding box prediction achieved the highest average progress of 82.3%.

#### 4.4. RQ4: Target-Embodiment Exposure Affects Transfer

- **Significant Transfer Gap Reduction**: Even a limited exposure of the target embodiment during pretraining (e.g., 5% of data) substantially reduced the transfer gap (Figure 7). For UR5eUMI, progress improved from 69.3% (strict zero-shot) to 78.6% (5% exposure), and for GoogleRobot, it increased from 51.4% to 68.9%.
- **Below Oracle Performance**: Despite the substantial gains, pretrain-exposed performance remained below that of an oracle model that was post-trained on target-embodiment downstream data, indicating that pretraining exposure mitigates but does not eliminate the need for target-task adaptation.
- **Interaction with Representation Choice**: Target exposure reduced the performance gaps between different state-action representations. This suggests that while representation choice is critical under strict zero-shot conditions, even limited target-embodiment exposure can partially compensate for less transferable representations (Table 6).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study provides a structured and empirically supported framework for understanding and improving zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer in VLA models for robotic manipulation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

#### 5.1. Protocol Clarification and Standardization

By formally distinguishing between "strict zero-shot transfer" (target embodiment absent from all training data) and "pretrain-exposed zero-shot transfer" (target embodiment present only during pretraining), the paper addresses a critical ambiguity in the field. The quantitative results demonstrate that these two scenarios yield distinct transfer difficulties, validating the need for separate reporting. This clarification offers a standardized taxonomy that can improve the comparability and interpretability of future research on zero-shot generalization, fostering more precise scientific communication.

#### 5.2. Development of a Controlled Benchmark

The introduction of a controlled benchmark that isolates embodiment mismatch from other confounding factors (such as task, scene, or camera changes) is a methodological contribution. This benchmark, spanning 14 held-out target embodiments across simulation and real-world settings and categorized into appearance-only, gripper-only, arm-only, and full-embodiment shifts, provides a robust platform for rigorous empirical analysis. Its design allows researchers to attribute policy performance directly to embodiment-specific challenges, facilitating targeted model development and evaluation.

#### 5.3. Factorized Understanding of Transfer Mechanisms

The study systematically quantifies the effects of four key factors: state-action representations, source-embodiment diversity, auxiliary co-training objectives, and target-embodiment exposure. This factorized analysis moves beyond anecdotal observations to provide concrete evidence for design choices. For instance, demonstrating the superiority of local EEF-centered representations or the benefits of diverse pretraining data under specific budget controls offers actionable insights into how to engineer VLA policies for better cross-embodiment generalization.

#### 5.4. Practical Guidance for VLA Model Development

The findings translate into practical recommendations for developing and evaluating VLA models in stationary tabletop manipulation with two-finger grippers:
- **Explicit Reporting of Target Exposure**: Future zero-shot evaluations should clearly state whether target embodiments were seen during pretraining.
- **Local EEF-Centered Representations**: Utilizing EEF-Delta state and EEF-Delta actions is recommended, particularly for transfers with significant embodiment gaps.
- **Strategic Source-Embodiment Diversity**: Source diversity should be considered in conjunction with data-budget allocation, as its benefits can be non-monotonic under fixed total budgets.
- **Auxiliary Co-training**: Incorporating objectives like task-conditioned bounding box prediction or EEF-frame language-action prediction can consistently improve transfer performance.

#### 5.5. Motivation for Future Research Directions

While the study provides a controlled analysis within a specific domain (stationary tabletop manipulation with two-finger grippers and short-horizon tasks), it explicitly acknowledges its limitations. The work motivates future investigations into broader and more complex settings, including:
- **Mobile Manipulation**: Incorporating base motion and navigation capabilities.
- **Dexterous Hands**: Addressing the increased complexity of contact topology, action dimensionality, and skill structures associated with multi-fingered grippers.
- **Long-Horizon Tasks**: Examining whether the observed transfer patterns hold for tasks requiring extended sequences of actions and complex reasoning.
- **Optimization for Deployment**: The study primarily characterized policy behavior rather than optimizing every system-level component for real-world deployment, highlighting an area for future engineering efforts.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the systematic understanding of zero-shot cross-embodiment VLA transfer. By providing methodological clarity, a controlled benchmark, and quantified insights into critical design factors, it lays a foundation for more effective development of generalizable robotic policies.
