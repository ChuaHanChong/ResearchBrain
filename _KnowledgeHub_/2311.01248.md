# Multimodal and Force-Matched Imitation Learning with a See-Through Visuotactile Sensor

> [!summary] Summary
> This work uses a see-through-your-skin (STS) visuotactile sensor for imitation learning on contact-rich door opening and closing. Two mechanisms are introduced: tactile force matching, which rewrites kinesthetic demonstration poses so an impedance controller reproduces recorded contact wrenches, and learned mode switching, which makes the visual/tactile sensor mode a policy output. Combined, they raise average policy success by 64.2% across four real-robot tasks.

## Problem

- Kinesthetic teaching records only robot motion, so demonstration replays cannot reproduce the contact forces the human applied during manipulation.
- Wrist force-torque and joint-torque sensing mix human pushing wrenches with robot-environment contact wrenches, making measured forces unusable for reproducing demonstration force profiles.
- Prior see-through sensors switched between visual and tactile modes using hand-crafted rules and known object information, requiring separate policies for motion and sensing.

## Method

- ==Tactile force matching== inverts the ==impedance controller=='s spring relation to compute new replay setpoint poses that add an open-loop wrench term reproducing demonstrated forces.
- Unscaled four-dimensional wrenches are read from marker tracking on the gel: ==Voronoi-based depth== for normal force, average dot shift for shear, rotational shift for torque, then piecewise-linear calibration to control error.
- ==Learned mode switching== adds the **STS** sensor mode as a seventh policy output, labelled by the demonstrator pressing a button during force-matched replay, trained jointly with motion via ==behavioural cloning==.

## Results

- Enabling force matching, mode switching, and STS policy input together gives a **64.2%** absolute average success gain over the baseline, **46.7%** on flat handles and **81.7%** on glass knobs.
- Ablations isolate each part: force matching adds **62.5%**, mode switching adds **30.4%** over single-mode sensing, and STS data as policy input adds **42.5%**; relative pose adds **17.5%**.
- Learned mode switches occur within about one timestep (**0.1 s**) of true contact; policies without STS input plateau at **15** demonstrations while STS-based ones keep scaling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sensing force at the contact point, not the wrist, is what makes kinesthetic teaching force-aware, since a fingertip sensor naturally rejects the demonstrator's own applied wrench.
> - Any Hooke's-law sensor can drive force-matched replay: calibrating unscaled readings against control error absorbs repeatable controller and housing errors that a calibrated force-torque sensor would not.
> - Poor-quality demonstrations can make extra modalities harmful: without force matching, STS input sometimes underperformed no STS at all, a causal-confusion failure mode.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has made significant strides, yet contact-rich tasks, especially those involving subtle relative motion like slipping or sliding between the end-effector and an object, remain challenging. Conventional approaches often rely on stable, firm grasps and large arm motions, which contrast sharply with human dexterity, where relaxed grasps and fine adjustments of contact are common. This paper directly addresses this gap by seeking to learn robot policies for such dexterous manipulation, akin to human performance.

The work builds upon and extends several critical areas in robotics and machine learning:
- **Tactile Sensing:** The development of gel-based optical tactile sensors, such as GelSight and its variants, has revolutionized the acquisition of rich tactile information. The paper specifically leverages a "see-through-your-skin" (STS) multimodal optical sensor, an advanced variant combining visual and tactile capabilities. This type of sensor, discussed in prior work by some of the authors, allows for perception across the entire interaction spectrum, from approach to sustained contact.
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** IL is a dominant paradigm for training robots from expert demonstrations. The paper specifically focuses on kinesthetic teaching, where a human directly guides the robot. While kinesthetic teaching offers intuitive haptic feedback to the demonstrator and doesn't require extra devices, a major limitation is its typical inability to record or reproduce robot-environment contact forces—a crucial aspect for contact-rich tasks. Most IL work tends to favor teleoperation, which often requires proxy systems for haptic feedback and can be less direct. This paper aims to overcome the kinesthetic teaching limitation by incorporating force information.
- **Force Control and Impedance Control:** Impedance control is a standard approach for regulating robot interaction with the environment by defining a dynamic relationship between force and position. While robust, it typically relies on desired positions and stiffness parameters rather than direct force application. The paper integrates impedance control into its methodology for generating and executing trajectories, especially to enable force matching.
- **Multimodal Learning:** The use of multiple sensory modalities (vision, touch, proprioception) is increasingly recognized as vital for robust robotic perception and control. This paper explores the effective fusion of visual and tactile data from the STS sensor, alongside proprioceptive (robot pose) information, for learning manipulation policies. Critically, it also addresses the challenge of *when* to use which modality, a problem often solved with hand-crafted rules in previous multimodal systems (e.g.,), by learning a mode-switching policy.

The paper distinguishes itself from prior work by:
1. **Integrating force profiles into kinesthetic teaching:** Unlike previous methods that either don't measure forces externally or require separate demonstrations for positions and forces, this work uses a single demonstration to capture both, directly addressing the limitations of kinesthetic teaching for contact-rich tasks.
2. **Addressing human-robot interaction forces:** It highlights a crucial problem: wrist-mounted force sensors are corrupted by human forces during kinesthetic teaching (Fig. 4). By using a finger-mounted visuotactile sensor to measure contact forces, it offers a cleaner, more accurate signal for force matching.
3. **Learning sensor mode switching:** Moving beyond hand-crafted rules for multimodal sensors, this paper proposes learning the optimal switching behavior as part of the robot's policy, making the system more autonomous and adaptable.

In essence, this research pushes the boundaries of dexterous robotic manipulation by providing practical and effective solutions for acquiring and utilizing crucial force and multimodal sensory information within the imitation learning paradigm, thereby enabling robots to perform complex contact-rich tasks with greater compliance and human-like finesse.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to empower robotic systems to perform contact-rich manipulation tasks with a level of dexterity and compliance that mimics human operators, particularly in scenarios involving relative motion between the end-effector and the manipulated object. The authors specifically motivate this by observing how humans open doors with minimal arm motion, allowing fingers to slide on the handle, a stark contrast to conventional robot strategies that prioritize a firm, stable grasp.

To achieve this, the paper sets forth three primary objectives, each addressing a critical limitation in current robotic manipulation and imitation learning frameworks:

1. **To enhance kinesthetic teaching with "Tactile Force Matching":**
 - **Motivation:** While kinesthetic teaching is an intuitive and efficient method for generating robot demonstrations, it typically only records the robot's end-effector motion (poses) and neglects the crucial contact forces and torques exerted on the environment. For tasks requiring delicate interaction or controlled slipping (like opening a door knob), reproducing only the motion often leads to failure due to insufficient or excessive force. Furthermore, standard force-torque sensors at the robot's wrist are confounded by human interaction forces during teaching, making it difficult to isolate the robot-environment contact forces.
 - **Objective:** Develop a method that uses a finger-mounted visuotactile sensor to measure approximate contact forces during a human demonstration. This measured force profile is then used to adapt the robot's trajectory during replay, ensuring that the robot not only follows the demonstrated path but also recreates the intended contact forces, thereby improving the fidelity and success rate of learned policies.

2. **To introduce "Learned Mode Switching" for the See-Through Visuotactile Sensor (STS):**
 - **Motivation:** The STS sensor is a powerful multimodal tool, capable of both visual scene observation (before contact) and detailed tactile feedback (during contact). However, to fully leverage these distinct capabilities, the sensor needs an intelligent mechanism to switch between its visual and tactile modes at appropriate times. Previous work often relied on hand-crafted rules or known object information for this switching, limiting generalizability and robustness.
 - **Objective:** Integrate the sensor mode switching as a learnable output within the imitation learning policy. By allowing the human demonstrator to label the desired mode transitions during demonstration replays, the policy can learn to autonomously switch between visual and tactile modes, simplifying the crucial transition from reaching to contacting and maximizing the utility of the multimodal sensor.

3. **To conduct an extensive experimental evaluation of the benefits of STS data in multimodal control policies:**
 - **Motivation:** Despite the theoretical advantages of multimodal sensors like STS, their practical impact on challenging real-world robotic manipulation tasks, especially in comparison to simpler sensors like wrist-mounted cameras, needs rigorous empirical validation. It is important to understand when and how visuotactile data (in its different forms: visual-only, tactile-only, or mode-switched) contributes to successful task completion.
 - **Objective:** Systematically evaluate the contribution of STS visual and tactile data as policy inputs, both with and without the proposed force matching and learned mode switching. This includes comparing STS-based policies against baselines using only an eye-in-hand camera and investigating the standalone capability of an STS-only system. The experiments aim to quantify the performance gains and shed light on the task-dependent utility of each component.

By pursuing these objectives, the authors aim to develop a more robust, compliant, and human-like robotic manipulation system, particularly for tasks that have traditionally been difficult for robots due to their contact-rich nature and requirement for fine force control and adaptive sensing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an imitation learning framework to teach a robot contact-rich manipulation tasks, specifically focusing on opening and closing cabinet doors. The core of the methodology revolves around leveraging a novel multimodal visuotactile sensor, algorithmic enhancements for data collection, and a deep learning-based policy.

**A. Robotic Platform and Tasks:**
- **Hardware:** A 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda robot arm equipped with a single robotic finger mounted with a See-Through Visuotactile Sensor (STS).
- **Tasks:** Four distinct door manipulation tasks: FlatHandleOpen, FlatHandleClose, GlassKnobOpen, and GlassKnobClose. These tasks involve challenging handle geometries (flat metal handle, spherical glass knob) that necessitate fine motor control and robust contact interaction, often involving slipping.
- **Control:** Cartesian impedance control is used, allowing the robot to apply predictable forces by controlling its stiffness, damping, and desired end-effector position.

**B. Data Collection via Kinesthetic Teaching:**
- **Demonstration Generation:** Human experts generate "raw" demonstrations by physically guiding the robot arm (kinesthetic teaching). During this phase, the impedance control parameters are set to allow full compliance, making the robot easy to move.
- **Limitations of Raw Kinesthetic Teaching:** The authors identify two key limitations:
 1. **Observational Noise:** The human demonstrator's presence or shadows can corrupt camera observations.
 2. **Lack of Force Profile:** The recorded poses ($\tau_{x,raw}$) do not inherently capture the contact forces (wrenches) exerted on the environment ($\tau_{f,raw}$).
- **Addressing Limitations with Replays:**
 - The first limitation is addressed by generating replays, where the robot autonomously executes the recorded trajectory after the human has stepped away.
 - The second, more critical limitation for contact-rich tasks, is addressed by the proposed **Force Matching** method.

**C. Tactile Force Matching (Contribution 1):**
- **Measuring Unscaled Forces with STS:** The STS sensor is crucial here. Unlike wrist-mounted force sensors (which are corrupted by human interaction forces, as illustrated in Fig. 4), the finger-mounted STS measures forces at the point of contact. The method approximates unscaled wrench signals ($\tilde{F}$) (x, y, z translational forces, and z-axis torque) by tracking the movement of markers embedded in the sensor's gel membrane:
 - $\tilde{F}_z$ (normal force): Estimated from local membrane depth changes derived from marker separation.
 - $\tilde{F}_x, \tilde{F}_y$ (shear forces): Measured from the average horizontal and vertical marker movements parallel to the sensor plane.
 - $\tilde{\tau}_z$ (torque): Estimated from average marker movement perpendicular to the center of maximal normal force.
- **Calibration:** A one-time, autonomous calibration procedure is performed. The STS sensor incrementally pushes against a static environment (e.g., a glass knob) to generate a dataset of unscaled wrench readings ($\tilde{F}$) and corresponding control position errors (`e`). A piecewise linear least-squares model ($\tilde{F} = Ae + b$) is learned, establishing a relationship between the unscaled tactile wrench and the position error that would generate it under the impedance control law (F = Ke).
- **Generating Force-Matched Replays:** Using the learned `A` and `b` parameters, the raw demonstrated poses ($\tau_{x,raw}$) and the recorded unscaled wrenches ($\tilde{F}_{raw,t}$) are combined to calculate a new set of "desired replay poses" ($x_{d_{rep,t}}$) for each timestep. These modified poses, when fed to the impedance controller, aim to reproduce both the original trajectory and the original force profile (Eq. 12). This ensures that the robot not only moves similarly to the demonstration but also interacts with the environment with similar forces.

**D. STS Sensor Mode Labelling and Learned Mode Switching (Contribution 2):**
- **Multimodal Sensor Modes:** The STS sensor has a visual mode (LEDs off, transparent membrane for scene observation) and a tactile mode (LEDs on, for contact feedback via gel deformation).
- **Learning to Switch:** The decision of *when* to switch modes is treated as an output of the learned policy. During the replay phase of data collection, the human demonstrator provides a mode switch label by pressing a button at the appropriate time (e.g., just before or at initial contact). This label is then added to the expert dataset.
- **Advantage:** This approach allows the policy to learn the optimal switching behavior specific to the task, making the system more flexible and robust than hand-crafted rules.

**E. Policy Training (Behavioral Cloning):**
- **Observations (Inputs):** The robot's state (`s`) can include:
 - Wrist camera RGB images (212x120 pixels).
 - Raw STS images (212x120 pixels, either visual or tactile mode depending on the active mode).
 - Current and previous relative end-effector poses (position, quaternion). Initial pose is randomized but treated as `{0,0,0,0,0,0,1}` relative to the start.
- **Actions (Outputs):** The robot's action (`a`) consists of:
 - 6-DOF relative end-effector position and orientation changes (as a rotation vector).
 - STS mode command (visual or tactile).
- **Architecture:** A neural network combining a ResNet-18 (pretrained on ImageNet) with spatial soft-argmax for image data, and a small fully connected network for relative pose data. Features from all modalities are concatenated.
- **Learning Algorithm:** Standard mean-squared-error behavioral cloning loss (`L(π) = Σ (π(s) - a)^2`).
- **Training Details:** Adam optimizer, learning rate schedule, weight decay (0.1) to prevent overfitting. Policies trained for 20k gradient steps.

**F. Experimental Design:**
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Success rate for door opening (fully open within time limit) and closing (closes without slamming/finger slipping off).
- **Ablation Studies:** Extensive experiments are conducted to isolate the benefits of each proposed contribution:
 - Comparing full system (force matching, mode switching, all sensor data) against a baseline with none.
 - Ablating force matching (with and without).
 - Ablating mode switching (mode switching vs. visual-only vs. tactile-only).
 - Ablating sensor inputs (all data vs. no STS; all data vs. no wrist camera; all data vs. only relative pose).
 - Investigating expert data scaling (5, 10, 15, 20 demonstrations).
- **Reproducibility:** Three random seeds per configuration, 10 test episodes per seed (30 episodes per task/configuration).

This comprehensive methodology allows for a robust investigation into how multimodal visuotactile sensing and advanced data collection techniques can significantly improve robot learning for challenging manipulation tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provides strong evidence for the utility of the proposed multimodal and force-matched imitation learning approach across the four challenging door manipulation tasks.

- **Overall System Performance:** The collective inclusion of **force matching, learned mode switching, and STS data as a policy input** resulted in a remarkable **64.2% average absolute increase in success rate** across all tasks compared to a baseline without any of these additions. This improvement was particularly pronounced for the GlassKnob tasks (81.7% gain), which inherently demand higher precision and compliant interaction compared to the FlatHandle tasks (46.7% gain). This highlights the synergistic benefits of the integrated system.

- **Contribution of Force Matching:**
 - Force matching alone delivered an average **62.5% increase in policy success rates**. This was a significant gain across all tasks, notably for `FlatHandleClose` and `GlassKnobOpen`.
 - Qualitative analysis (Fig. 10) demonstrated that force-matched replays more accurately reproduced both the expert's positions and force profiles, preventing common failure modes like the end-effector slipping off the handle/knob due to insufficient or incorrect force application.
 - An interesting observation was that, in some cases (`FlatHandleClose`, `GlassKnobOpen`), using STS data *without* force matching actually performed worse than the full baseline (without STS and force matching). The authors hypothesize this is due to "causal mismatch," where suboptimal, non-force-matched demonstrations lead the policy to learn to switch modes or initiate task phases prematurely without firm contact. This underscores the critical importance of high-quality, force-informed demonstrations for effective learning.

- **Contribution of Learned Mode Switching:**
 - The learned mode switching mechanism improved average policy success rates by **30.4%** compared to keeping the STS sensor in a single mode (visual-only or tactile-only).
 - Similar to force matching, the gains were higher for the `GlassKnob` tasks (38.3%) than for `FlatHandle` tasks (22.5%), indicating that tasks requiring more delicate and precise contact benefit more from dynamic mode switching.
 - Analysis of mode switching timing (Fig. 14) showed that learned policies effectively converged to switch modes very close to the moment of contact, often smoothing out the reactive or predictive timing errors present in human demonstrations. Qualitatively, switching allows for clear visual scene understanding during approach and rich tactile information during contact (Fig. 15).

- **Value of STS Data as a Policy Input:**
 - Including STS data (both visual and tactile, with force matching enabled) yielded a **42.5% average increase in performance** compared to policies that excluded STS data and relied solely on a wrist-mounted eye-in-hand camera and relative pose information. This clearly demonstrates the substantial benefit of direct, contact-rich visuotactile feedback for these tasks.
 - Further investigation showed that including relative pose information alongside visual and tactile data still provided an average **17.5% improvement**, indicating its complementary value even with rich image data.
 - Comparing STS-only policies (no wrist camera) to wrist-camera-only policies (no STS) revealed that STS-only policies achieved a **17.5% higher success rate** on average. This is a crucial finding: for these contact-rich tasks, a multimodal STS sensor alone proves to be more valuable than a traditional eye-in-hand camera alone. While including the wrist camera generally improved performance (25.0% average increase over STS-only), the STS sensor itself provides sufficient information for adequate performance in many cases.

- **Expert Data Scaling:**
 - Performance generally improved with an increasing number of demonstrations (from 5 to 20).
 - However, policies without STS input data (relying only on the wrist camera and relative pose, even with force matching) tended to plateau at around 15 demonstrations, indicating an inherent limitation in their ability to solve these tasks without direct contact sensing, regardless of data quantity.
 - Conversely, STS-enabled methods (with force matching and mode switching) showed continued improvement with more data, and for some tasks, achieved surprisingly good performance even with only 5 demonstrations. This suggests that the quality and richness of STS data allow for more efficient learning.

- **Decoupling Human Forces:**
 - The paper provided clear validation (Fig. 22) that the finger-mounted STS sensor successfully measures only the robot-environment contact forces, effectively decoupling them from the human interaction forces present during kinesthetic teaching. This is a critical advantage over wrist-mounted force-torque sensors for generating accurate force profiles for imitation learning.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that the proposed force matching technique and learned mode switching, combined with the multimodal information from the STS sensor, significantly enhance the robot's ability to perform complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks. The STS sensor, both as a data source for force matching and a direct input to the policy, is shown to be highly valuable, often outperforming or complementing traditional visual sensors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents significant advancements in robotic manipulation, particularly for contact-rich tasks that have historically been difficult for autonomous systems. The contributions have substantial potential impact across various domains.

**A. Addressing a Core Challenge in Robotics:**
The ability to perform dexterous manipulation, involving compliant contact and controlled relative motion (like slipping and sliding), is fundamental for robots to operate effectively in unstructured human environments. Current robots often struggle with tasks like opening doors or manipulating deformable objects without precise grasps. This work directly tackles this challenge by enabling robots to learn and replicate human-like compliant interactions.

**B. Novel Algorithmic Contributions with Broad Applicability:**
1. **Tactile Force Matching:** This is a crucial innovation for imitation learning. By integrating force profile reproduction into kinesthetic teaching, the paper overcomes a major limitation of this intuitive demonstration method.
 - **Impact:** It allows human experts to naturally demonstrate tasks requiring fine force control without requiring specialized force-torque sensors that would be corrupted by human interaction. This makes the demonstration process more accessible, efficient, and robust, leading to higher-fidelity learned policies. The method's validity for any sensor following Hooke's law (e.g., visuotactile, pressure, strain-gauge sensors) extends its potential application beyond the specific STS sensor.
2. **Learned Mode Switching for Multimodal Sensors:** Treating sensor mode switching as a learnable policy output is a generalizable solution for managing multimodal perception.
 - **Impact:** It eliminates the need for complex, hand-crafted rules that are often brittle and task-specific. By allowing the policy to dynamically choose the most relevant sensor modality (visual for approach, tactile for contact), it maximizes the utility of advanced multimodal sensors like STS, improving robustness and adaptability in varying task phases.

**C. Validation and Promotion of See-Through Visuotactile Sensors (STS):**
The extensive experimental evaluation clearly demonstrates the power and necessity of the STS sensor for these types of tasks.
- **Impact:** It empirically shows that STS data, encompassing both visual and tactile feedback, provides a substantial performance boost (42.5% increase) over relying solely on a wrist-mounted camera. Furthermore, the finding that an STS-only policy can outperform a wrist-camera-only policy highlights its unique value in contact-rich scenarios. This strengthens the case for incorporating such advanced multimodal sensors in next-generation robotic end-effectors, especially for tasks requiring dexterous manipulation and interaction with unknown objects.

**D. Enabling More Robust and Human-like Manipulation:**
By combining these elements, the research leads to policies that are significantly more successful (64.2% average gain) in opening and closing various door types.
- **Impact:** This capability is crucial for applications in domestic robotics (e.g., household chores), service robotics (e.g., assisting in hospitals or warehouses), and even manufacturing (e.g., delicate assembly). The ability to manage relative motion and adapt to challenging handle geometries makes robots more versatile and less prone to failures from minor misalignments or unexpected contact dynamics. It moves robots closer to the fluidity and adaptability of human manipulation.

**E. Foundation for Future Research:**
The identified limitations (sensor degradation, lighting sensitivity, and residual control/sensing errors) provide clear avenues for future work, such as improving force sensing accuracy, incorporating self-improvement mechanisms for failure detection, and exploring more robust materials for tactile sensors. The methodology also offers a strong baseline for exploring other contact-rich tasks or integrating with reinforcement learning for continuous self-improvement.

In conclusion, this paper significantly advances the state-of-the-art in robotic imitation learning by introducing novel methods for incorporating crucial force information and intelligent multimodal sensor management. The empirical validation with real-world, contact-rich tasks underscores the practical utility and paves the way for a new generation of robots capable of more dexterous, compliant, and human-like interactions with their environment.
