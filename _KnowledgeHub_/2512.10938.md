# Stronger Normalization-Free Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Princeton, NYU, and Carnegie Mellon introduced Dynamic erf (Derf), a point-wise function that replaces traditional normalization layers in Transformers. Derf consistently achieved higher accuracy or lower error rates across various modalities, including an average of 0.6 percentage points higher top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-1K for ViT-Base/Large models and improved FID scores for Diffusion Transformers.

## Problem

- Traditional normalization layers (e.g., Batch Norm, Layer Norm) introduce computational overhead (memory, synchronization) and exhibit sensitivity to batch size, hindering efficiency and scalability.
- Previous normalization-free methods primarily aimed to match the performance of normalization layers rather than surpass them, limiting their impact.
- The underlying mathematical properties that make point-wise functions effective replacements for normalization were not fully understood.

## Method

- A systematic analysis of ==point-wise function properties== (zero-centeredness, boundedness, center sensitivity, monotonicity) was conducted to derive design principles for stable training.
- An extensive empirical search was performed for optimal point-wise functions based on these principles, leading to the identification of the ==error function (erf) with learnable parameters==, termed ==Dynamic erf (Derf)==.
- Derf was rigorously evaluated as a ==drop-in replacement for normalization layers== across diverse Transformer architectures and tasks, including vision, speech, DNA, and language models.

## Results

- Derf consistently outperformed LayerNorm, RMSNorm, and Dynamic Tanh across all evaluated architectures and tasks, including ViT (ImageNet-1K), DiT (image generation), wav2vec 2.0 (speech), HyenaDNA/Caduceus (DNA), and GPT-2 (language).
- On ViT-Base/Large models, Derf achieved up to **0.6 percentage points** higher top-1 classification accuracy on ImageNet-1K compared to LayerNorm.
- For Diffusion Transformers (DiT-L/4), Derf yielded a Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) score of **43.94**, compared to **45.91** for LayerNorm, indicating superior image generation quality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective normalization-free point-wise functions must be near zero-centered, bounded, center-sensitive, and monotonic to ensure stable convergence and high performance.
> - Point-wise functions like Derf exhibit reduced fitting capacity (higher training loss) compared to normalization layers, yet achieve superior downstream performance due to stronger generalization.
> - The specific mathematical form of the error function (erf), beyond a simple S-shape, contributes unique beneficial properties for model performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Normalization layers, such as Batch Normalization (BN), Layer Normalization (LN), and Root Mean Square Normalization (RMSNorm), have been established as integral components of modern deep neural networks. Their primary functions include stabilizing training dynamics and accelerating model convergence by regulating the distribution of intermediate activations.

Despite their benefits, normalization layers introduce several practical challenges. They rely heavily on activation statistics, which can incur additional memory access and synchronization overhead during training. Furthermore, some normalization methods exhibit sensitivity to batch size, potentially leading to training instability with suboptimal batch configurations. These issues have motivated research into normalization-free training methods.

Recent advancements in this area include the introduction of Dynamic Tanh (DyT) by Zhu et al. (2025). DyT is a point-wise function that applies an independent scalar mapping to each element, demonstrating that such functions can achieve performance comparable to traditional normalization layers while alleviating some of their inherent drawbacks. DyT's success established the viability of point-wise functions as drop-in replacements for normalization.

This current work extends this line of research. While DyT showed that point-wise functions can *match* normalization performance, the question of whether they could *surpass* it remained largely unexplored. This paper aims to systematically investigate the design space of point-wise functions to identify configurations that not only stabilize training but also yield superior empirical performance across various Transformer architectures and modalities, thereby pushing the capabilities of normalization-free models beyond previous benchmarks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to identify and develop point-wise functions that can serve as more effective replacements for normalization layers in deep neural networks, specifically targeting Transformer architectures. The work aims to demonstrate that such functions can not only stabilize training and achieve performance comparable to existing normalization methods but can also surpass them across various domains.

The motivation for this research stems from several factors:
- **Addressing Limitations of Traditional Normalization:** Traditional normalization layers, while effective, introduce complexities such as reliance on activation statistics, which can lead to increased memory usage, synchronization overhead, and sensitivity to batch size. These factors can impede scalability and efficiency, particularly in distributed training or scenarios with small batch sizes. The researchers are motivated to find alternatives that mitigate these operational drawbacks.
- **Building on Dynamic Tanh (DyT):** The recent introduction of Dynamic Tanh (DyT) demonstrated the feasibility of using simple point-wise functions to achieve normalization-level performance without the statistical dependencies. This prior work laid a foundation by showing that alternatives are possible. This paper is motivated to explore further, specifically seeking designs that can build upon DyT's success and *exceed* its performance, along with that of traditional normalization layers.
- **Systematic Understanding of Point-wise Function Properties:** A detailed understanding of how intrinsic properties of point-wise functions influence training dynamics and final model performance has been largely absent. A key motivation is to systematically analyze fundamental properties such as zero-centeredness, boundedness, center sensitivity, and monotonicity. This analysis is intended to derive concrete design principles for effective normalization-free Transformer architectures, moving beyond empirical discovery.
- **Enhancing Transformer Architectures:** Given the prevalence of Transformer models across diverse applications (e.g., vision, language, speech, genomics), the development of stronger and more efficient foundational components, such as normalization layers, can lead to overall architectural improvements. The goal is to contribute to the advancement of Transformer designs by providing a superior and simpler alternative to existing normalization schemes.
- **Improving Generalization and Efficiency:** The researchers seek to develop a solution that not only offers computational benefits by being statistics-free but also potentially improves model generalization, rather than merely enhancing fitting capacity. This pursuit aims for a more robust and broadly applicable architectural component.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research methodology involved a multi-stage approach, combining systematic property analysis with a broad empirical search and comprehensive evaluation.

#### 3.1. Function Property Analysis

The initial phase focused on understanding the impact of four intrinsic properties of point-wise functions on Transformer training:
- **Setup:** All experiments in this phase utilized a ViT-Base model, with each normalization layer replaced by a point-wise function of the general form `y = γ * f(αx) + β`. The performance metric was top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-1K. Three base functions were selected: `tanh(x)`, `erf(x)`, and `arctan(x)`.
- **Zero-centeredness:** The effect of input and output centering was investigated by applying horizontal shifts (`f(x + λ_horiz)`) and vertical shifts (`f(x) + λ_vert`) to the base functions. Various `λ` values (e.g., {±0.5, ±1, ±2}) were tested to observe how deviations from zero-centeredness affected performance and convergence.
- **Boundedness:** This property was studied using two methods:
 1. Clamping inherently unbounded S-shaped functions (e.g., `arcsinh(x)`, `logsign(x)`) within a finite range `clip(f_u(x), -λ_u, λ_u)`.
 2. Gradually transitioning bounded functions (e.g., `erf(x)`) towards an unbounded linear form `(1 - λ_b)f_b(x) + λ_b x`.
 Additionally, a family of unbounded functions with varying growth rates (e.g., `linear(x)`, `power23(x)`, `logquad(x)`) was evaluated to determine the acceptable growth rate limit.
- **Center Sensitivity:** To assess responsiveness around zero, base functions were modified to include a symmetric flat region around the origin. For inputs `x ∈ [-λ, λ]`, `f(x)` was enforced to be zero, with `λ` controlling the extent of this region. Different `λ` values (e.g., {0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0}) were tested.
- **Monotonicity:** Monotonic (e.g., `erf(x)`, `-erf(x)`) and non-monotonic functions (e.g., hump-shaped `dampx(x)`, oscillatory `sin(x)`) were compared. Non-monotonic functions were rescaled to match the output range of monotonic counterparts to isolate the effect of monotonicity.

#### 3.2. Function Search

Based on the principles derived from the property analysis (i.e., preference for functions that are near zero-centered, bounded, center-sensitive, and monotonic), a large-scale empirical search was conducted.
- **Candidate Generation:** A diverse set of point-wise functions was constructed, including widely used scalar functions, cumulative distribution functions (CDFs), and variants created through transformations like translation, scaling, mirroring, rotation, and clipping.
- **Formulation:** Each candidate function `f(x)` was implemented in a unified form: `y = γ * f(αx + s) + β`. Here, `α` (scale) and `s` (shift) are learnable scalar parameters, while `γ` and `β` are learnable per-channel affine parameters, analogous to those in standard normalization layers.
- **Evaluation Settings:** The search was conducted on two representative vision architectures: Vision Transformer (ViT-Base) for ImageNet-1K classification (top-1 accuracy) and Diffusion Transformer (DiT-B/4 and DiT-L/4) for ImageNet-1K image generation (Fréchet Inception Distance, FID).

#### 3.3. Introduction and Comprehensive Evaluation of Derf

- **Identification:** The search identified `erf(x)` (error function), with the introduced learnable parameters, as the most performant design. This function was named Dynamic erf (Derf).
- **Derf Formulation:** `Derf(x) = γ * erf(αx + s) + β`. The parameters `α` and `s` are initialized to 0.5 and 0, respectively, while `γ` and `β` are initialized to all-ones and all-zeros, similar to standard normalization layers.
- **Integration:** Derf layers systematically replaced all normalization layers (pre-attention, pre-FFN, and final normalization) in the evaluated Transformer architectures.
- **Cross-Domain Evaluation:** Derf's effectiveness was evaluated across a wide range of tasks and modalities:
 - **Vision:** ViT-Base, ViT-Large (ImageNet-1K classification); DiT-B/4, DiT-L/4, DiT-XL/2 (ImageNet-1K image generation).
 - **Speech:** wav2vec 2.0 Base, Large (LibriSpeech speech representation learning).
 - **DNA:** HyenaDNA, Caduceus (GenomicBenchmarks DNA sequence modeling).
 - **Language:** GPT-2 (124M) (OpenWebText language modeling).
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against LayerNorm (LN), Dynamic Tanh (DyT), and, where applicable, RMSNorm and GroupNorm.

#### 3.4. Analysis of Performance Gains

To understand the source of Derf's superior performance, a comparison of evaluation-mode training loss was conducted across models using normalization layers, DyT, and Derf.
- **Procedure:** After training, models were switched to evaluation mode, stochastic regularization (e.g., stochastic depth) was disabled, and test-time preprocessing was applied. The loss was then computed on the training set to obtain an estimate of fitting capacity.

#### 3.5. Ablation Studies and Approximation

- **Effect of `s`:** Ablation experiments were performed to quantify the contribution of the learnable shift parameter `s` to Derf's performance. The impact of using a scalar `s` versus a per-channel vector `s` was also investigated.
- **Derf Approximation:** An attempt was made to approximate `erf(x)` by scaling `tanh(x)` as `tanh(εx)`, where `ε` was numerically optimized to minimize the integral difference between `tanh(εx)` and `erf(x)`. This variant was then evaluated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the properties of effective point-wise functions and demonstrated the consistent superior performance of the proposed Dynamic erf (Derf).

#### 4.1. Function Property Analysis

- **Zero-centeredness:** The study indicated that zero-centeredness is critical for stable convergence and effective training. Performance consistently degraded with increasing horizontal or vertical shifts from the origin, with training failure occurring for large shifts (e.g., `|λ| ≥ 2`).
- **Boundedness:** Boundedness was identified as essential for preventing signal explosion and maintaining gradient stability. Clipped versions of inherently unbounded functions consistently outperformed their unbounded baselines. Conversely, gradually removing boundedness from intrinsically bounded functions led to performance degradation or training divergence. An upper limit on the acceptable growth rate for unbounded functions was observed, with `logquad(x)` being the fastest-growing function that still allowed convergence, while `linear(x)` and `power23(x)` caused early divergence.
- **Center Sensitivity:** High sensitivity around the origin was found to be important for effective signal propagation. Introducing a flat region around zero (decreasing center sensitivity) consistently led to performance degradation, with larger flat regions causing training divergence. Best performance was observed when no such flat region was present.
- **Monotonicity:** Monotonicity was identified as a key property for effective learning. Both monotonically increasing and decreasing functions achieved stable training and high accuracy. In contrast, non-monotonic functions (hump-shaped or oscillatory) consistently resulted in lower accuracy and higher training loss.
- **Derived Principle:** The analysis concluded that effective point-wise functions for normalization-free Transformers should be near zero-centered, bounded, center-sensitive (responsive near zero), and monotonic.

#### 4.2. Function Search

- Among a large set of candidate point-wise functions satisfying the derived principles, `erf(x)` (error function), when augmented with learnable parameters, demonstrated the strongest performance. It consistently surpassed other candidates and the baseline LayerNorm on both ViT-Base (ImageNet-1K classification) and DiT models (ImageNet-1K image generation). For instance, `erf(x)` achieved 82.8% top-1 accuracy on ViT-Base, compared to 82.3% for LayerNorm. On DiT-L/4, it achieved an FID of 43.94, compared to 45.91 for LayerNorm.

#### 4.3. Dynamic erf (Derf) Performance

- **Vision Transformers:** Derf consistently achieved higher top-1 classification accuracy than both LayerNorm and DyT across ViT-Base (82.8% for Derf vs. 82.3% for LN and 82.5% for DyT) and ViT-Large (83.8% for Derf vs. 83.1% for LN and 83.6% for DyT).
- **Diffusion Transformers:** Derf yielded lower (improved) FID scores compared to LayerNorm and DyT across all tested DiT model sizes. For DiT-L/4, Derf achieved an FID of 43.94, significantly lower than 45.91 for LN and 45.66 for DyT.
- **Speech Models:** Derf resulted in lower validation loss on wav2vec 2.0 Base (1.93 for Derf vs. 1.95 for LN and DyT) and Large models (1.90 for Derf vs. 1.92 for LN and 1.91 for DyT), indicating improved representation quality.
- **DNA Models:** Derf surpassed both the default normalization layers (LayerNorm for HyenaDNA, RMSNorm for Caduceus) and DyT in averaged classification accuracy on the GenomicBenchmarks dataset (e.g., HyenaDNA: 85.7% for Derf vs. 85.2% for LN and DyT).
- **Language Models:** Derf achieved comparable validation loss to LayerNorm on GPT-2 (124M) (2.94 for Derf vs. 2.94 for LN), while outperforming DyT (2.97).
- **Additional Normalization Comparisons:** Extended evaluations (Appendix D) confirmed that Derf also consistently outperformed RMSNorm and GroupNorm across various tasks and architectures.

#### 4.4. Stronger Generalization or Better Fitting?

- Evaluation-mode training loss analysis revealed that both Derf and DyT models exhibited *higher* training loss compared to normalization-based models across all architectures and scales. This indicates that point-wise functions, including Derf, do not achieve their performance gains through improved fitting capacity (i.e., they do not fit the training data as well as normalization layers).
- Despite higher training loss, Derf consistently achieved superior downstream performance, suggesting that its benefits primarily stem from enhanced generalization. Derf also showed slightly lower training loss than DyT, implying stronger fitting capacity than DyT, while still acting as an implicit regularizer compared to normalization layers.

#### 4.5. Analysis: Effect of `s` and Approximation

- **Effect of `s`:** The learnable scalar shift parameter `s` consistently improved overall training performance, although its impact varied across different point-wise functions. The performance difference between `erf(x)` and `tanh(x)` was not solely attributable to the presence of `s`.
- **Scalar vs. Vector `s`:** No significant performance difference was observed when comparing a scalar `s` with a per-channel vector `s`, leading to the adoption of the more efficient scalar form.
- **Approximating Derf:** Scaling `tanh(x)` to approximate `erf(x)` (i.e., `tanh(εx)` with `ε ≈ 1.205`) yielded comparable or slightly better performance than the original `tanh(x)` but still performed worse than `erf(x)`. This suggests that the specific functional form of `erf(x)`, beyond simple scaling, contributes to its efficacy.

#### 4.6. Computation Cost

- Benchmarking showed that Derf and DyT exhibited nearly identical runtimes for both forward and backward passes.
- In the forward pass, Derf and LayerNorm had comparable runtimes. In the backward pass, Derf was slightly slower than LayerNorm at small hidden dimensions but became consistently faster as hidden dimensions increased, which the authors attribute to reduced reduction and synchronization overheads in point-wise functions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents findings that challenge the long-standing view of normalization layers as indispensable components in deep neural networks, particularly in Transformer architectures. By introducing Dynamic erf (Derf), the work demonstrates that carefully designed point-wise functions can not only replace normalization layers but also consistently outperform them across a broad spectrum of tasks and modalities.

The significance and potential impact are as follows:
- **Performance Advancement:** Derf consistently achieves superior results (higher accuracy, lower FID, lower validation loss) compared to LayerNorm, RMSNorm, GroupNorm, and DyT across diverse applications, including vision classification and generation, speech representation learning, DNA sequence modeling, and language modeling. This indicates a generalizable performance improvement.
- **Improved Generalization:** The analysis suggesting that Derf's benefits stem from stronger generalization, rather than increased fitting capacity, provides a new perspective on model design. This implicit regularization offered by point-wise functions could lead to more robust models that perform better on unseen data.
- **Operational Efficiency and Stability:** As a normalization-free method, Derf inherently avoids the memory access and synchronization overheads, as well as the batch-size sensitivity, associated with statistics-dependent normalization layers. This can contribute to more efficient training, particularly in distributed computing environments or scenarios with varying batch sizes. While runtime benchmarks show comparable or slightly improved efficiency, the architectural simplicity offers fundamental advantages.
- **Simplification of Architectures:** Derf is a simple, parametric point-wise function that can be directly swapped for normalization layers. Its simplicity makes it a practical drop-in replacement, potentially streamlining the design and implementation of future Transformer architectures.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The systematic study of intrinsic function properties (zero-centeredness, boundedness, center sensitivity, monotonicity) provides concrete design principles for developing and evaluating new normalization-free methods. These principles offer a framework for exploring the vast design space of point-wise functions, guiding future efforts to create even more effective alternatives.
- **Broad Applicability:** The comprehensive evaluation across various data modalities (images, speech, DNA, text) and tasks (classification, generation, sequence modeling) suggests that Derf is a broadly applicable component, enhancing the performance and stability of diverse deep learning models. This breadth of validation increases confidence in its utility beyond specific niche applications.
