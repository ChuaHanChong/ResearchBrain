# Chain-of-Action: Trajectory Autoregressive Modeling for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Chain-of-Action (CoA) introduces a Trajectory Autoregressive Modeling approach for robotic manipulation that generates action sequences by reasoning backward from a task-specific keyframe. This method effectively mitigates compounding errors in long-horizon tasks, achieving state-of-the-art performance in simulation and real-world scenarios while demonstrating strong spatial generalization.

## Problem

- Conventional visuo-motor policies relying on a "forward prediction paradigm" suffer from compounding errors, where small deviations accumulate over long task horizons.
- Existing methods struggle with robust performance in complex, long-horizon tasks and exhibit limitations under distribution shifts, particularly in spatial variations.
- Remedies like action chunking or image-goal conditioning primarily address the symptoms of myopic step-wise prediction rather than its root cause.

## Method

- Introduces "**Chain-of-Action** (CoA)," a ==Trajectory Autoregressive Modeling== approach that generates action sequences through explicit backward reasoning, starting from a task-specific keyframe (goal).
- Employs a continuous action token representation regularized by a latent consistency loss and incorporates multi-token prediction (MTP) for local action coherence during training.
- Implements a dynamic stop mechanism for variable-length trajectory generation during inference and a reverse temporal ensemble strategy to improve keyframe accuracy and reduce variance.

## Results

- Achieved state-of-the-art performance on the RLBench-60 benchmark, outperforming ACT by an average of **16.3%** and Diffusion Policy by **23.2%** across 60 tasks.
- Demonstrated superior real-world manipulation capabilities on a Fetch robot, achieving a **15%** higher average success rate than ACT across 8 kitchen manipulation tasks.
- Exhibited robust spatial generalization, with performance gains over baselines widening as object spatial variance increased, and experienced significantly less performance degradation under extrapolation scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Backward reasoning from a task-specific keyframe enforces global-to-local consistency, fundamentally addressing and mitigating the compounding error problem in long-horizon robotic tasks.
> - Unifying keyframe detection and autoregressive trajectory generation within a single, end-to-end framework, operating directly from RGB inputs, simplifies the policy compared to hierarchical or 3D-based methods.
> - Complementary architectural designs (latent consistency loss, MTP, dynamic stop, reverse temporal ensemble) are crucial for the practical viability and stable training of continuous action, backward autoregressive policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of visuo-motor policies for robotic manipulation has witnessed substantial progress, particularly with the availability of large-scale demonstrations and the development of powerful neural architectures. Current state-of-the-art methods typically learn end-to-end mappings from raw visual inputs to low-level robot control. Approaches like ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers) and Diffusion Policy have advanced the field by modeling multi-modal action distributions and mitigating compounding errors through techniques like action chunking and denoising processes, respectively. Further developments have explored richer sensory inputs, improved network architectures, expanded datasets (e.g., Open X-Embodiment), and the trend towards Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

However, the vast majority of these methods, including prominent ones like ACT and Diffusion Policy, adhere to a "forward prediction paradigm." This means they are optimized to predict the *next* action based on the *current* observation. While intuitive, this approach suffers from a critical limitation: the accumulation of "compounding errors" during long-horizon task execution. Small errors at each step propagate and amplify, leading to significant deviations from the intended goal. Existing remedies, such as action chunking or image-goal conditioned behavioral cloning, primarily address the symptoms rather than the root cause—the inherently myopic nature of forward prediction.

"Chain-of-Action (CoA)" presents a fundamental conceptual and practical shift by reversing the action generation process. Instead of predicting actions in a forward, step-wise manner, CoA constructs action sequences in reverse, forming a chain that starts from a task-specific keyframe action (encoding the goal) and reasons backward toward the initial state. This "backward reasoning" paradigm enforces a global-to-local consistency, anchoring each local action prediction to the ultimate goal. This directly counters the compounding error problem by ensuring that generated trajectories are inherently guided by the desired final state.

This work also distinguishes itself from other related paradigms:
- **Hierarchical modeling:** Methods like PerAct, RVT, and ChainedDiffuser also utilize keyframes but often operate in an open-loop manner between keyframes and frequently rely on high-fidelity 3D inputs and separate motion planners. CoA, in contrast, unifies keyframe detection and trajectory generation within a single autoregressive framework, enabling environment-aware, closed-loop execution directly from RGB inputs, without requiring explicit 3D reconstruction or motion planning.
- **CoT-style VLA agents:** These methods incorporate intermediate semantic representations (e.g., imagined image goals, bounding boxes, gripper poses) as guidance. CoA is orthogonal to these, focusing on modeling the reasoning process *directly between actions* without relying on extra modalities as intermediate representations, making it broadly compatible with diverse sensory inputs and policy architectures.

By introducing a "Trajectory Autoregressive Modeling" approach with explicit backward reasoning, CoA positions itself as a competitive and fundamentally different alternative to address a core challenge in visuo-motor policy learning: robust long-horizon task completion and enhanced generalization under distribution shifts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel visuo-motor policy paradigm that effectively addresses the compounding error problem inherent in conventional forward-prediction methods for robotic manipulation. The core motivation stems from the recognition that while existing policies have made significant strides, their step-wise, forward prediction logic leads to an accumulation of errors, hindering performance in complex, long-horizon tasks and under distribution shifts.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:
- **To overcome compounding errors:** The central motivation is to develop a policy that ensures successful task completion by aligning local action decisions with a global task goal, thereby mitigating the accumulation of errors.
- **To introduce a "backward reasoning" paradigm:** Propose Chain-of-Action (CoA) as a novel visuo-motor policy built upon Trajectory Autoregressive Modeling, which generates an entire trajectory by explicit backward reasoning, starting from a task-specific goal.
- **To enforce global-to-local consistency:** By generating actions in reverse from a keyframe (goal), the model aims to intrinsically constrain each local action to be consistent with the final objective, enhancing reliability and robustness.
- **To unify keyframe detection and trajectory generation:** Integrate the process of identifying a keyframe (task-specific goal) and subsequently generating the action sequence within a single, end-to-end autoregressive structure.
- **To ensure practical viability:** Develop four complementary architectural designs—continuous action token representation, multi-token prediction for local coherence, dynamic stopping for variable-length trajectories, and reverse temporal ensemble—that are crucial for stable training and reliable closed-loop execution of the backward reasoning paradigm.
- **To demonstrate strong spatial generalization:** Aim to show that this novel modeling paradigm leads to improved performance in scenarios involving significant variations in object position and pose, which is critical for real-world adaptability.
- **To achieve state-of-the-art performance:** Empirically validate the effectiveness of CoA by demonstrating superior performance on a comprehensive suite of simulated RLBench tasks and real-world robotic manipulation tasks compared to existing strong baselines.

In essence, the motivation is to fundamentally rethink how action sequences are generated in visuo-motor policies to produce more robust, goal-conditioned, and generalizable robot behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Chain-of-Action (CoA) introduces a novel **Trajectory Autoregressive Modeling** approach that generates action sequences through **explicit backward reasoning**. This method unifies the entire reverse generation process into a single autoregressive framework, addressing the limitations of forward prediction.

**Formulation of Backward Reasoning:**
The core idea is to model trajectory generation in reverse order, starting from a task-specific goal and predicting actions backward. This formulation imposes a global-to-local structure, anchoring the rollout to the final intent (`a_T`) and mitigating compounding errors.
- **Keyframe Definition:** CoA adopts a simple heuristic from C2F-ARM to define a keyframe (`a_T`) as a time step where the gripper state changes or joint velocities approach zero. This keyframe action implicitly encodes the task-specific goal.
- **Autoregressive Model:** The policy models the trajectory distribution `p(a_1:T | O)` by decomposing it in reverse: `p(a_T | O) * p(a_{T-1} | a_T, O) ... p(a_1 | a_{2:T}, O)`. Here, `O` represents the observation context (visual input `I` and proprioceptive state `S`).
- **Training Sample Construction:** A sub-trajectory `a_1:T` is sampled from an expert demonstration, starting from a random time step and ending at the next keyframe. `O` is taken from the initial step.

**Four Complementary Designs for Practical Viability:**

1. **Continuous Action Token Representation:**
 - **Problem:** Discretizing actions leads to resolution loss and error accumulation in long-horizon autoregressive generation, especially when reasoning backward from a goal.
 - **Solution:** CoA uses a continuous action representation. To stabilize training with continuous latent tokens, a novel **latent consistency loss** (`L_consistency = ||x_hat_t - f_enc(a_t)||^2`) is introduced. This loss regularizes the latent space by aligning the predicted latent (`x_hat_t`) with the encoded ground-truth action (`f_enc(a_t)`), preventing encoding errors from propagating and amplifying.

2. **Locality Action Modeling (Multi-Token Prediction - MTP):**
 - **Problem:** While backward autoregression propagates high-level intent, it doesn't explicitly model local action dependencies within a sub-trajectory.
 - **Solution:** CoA incorporates MTP during training. The last `K` layers of the Transformer decoder are assigned to predict `K` future steps (`x_hat_{t+k}`) simultaneously. This encourages the model to jointly predict short action chunks, enhancing local coherence and training stability without disrupting the global-to-local causal structure during inference (where it's removed).

3. **Dynamic Stop Mechanism:**
 - **Problem:** In continuous action spaces, there's no discrete "end-of-sequence" token. Variable-length trajectory generation requires knowing when to terminate decoding during execution.
 - **Solution:** A **distance-based stop criterion** is implemented. Decoding terminates when the predicted action (representing the gripper state at time `t`) becomes sufficiently close (Euclidean distance) to the current actual gripper state. This prevents over-generation and improves execution efficiency.

4. **Reverse Temporal Ensemble:**
 - **Problem:** Standard ensembling strategies are designed for forward temporal assumptions and are not directly applicable to backward generation.
 - **Solution:** CoA develops a **reverse-compatible ensembling strategy**. Multiple backward rollouts are aligned by their predicted keyframe action (`a_T`), which acts as an anchor point. Ensembling these aligned rollouts improves the accuracy of the keyframe action and reduces variance during closed-loop execution.

**Network Architecture and Training:**
- **Architecture:** Similar to ACT, it uses a 4-layer Transformer encoder and a 7-layer Transformer decoder.
 - Multi-view RGB images are processed by a ResNet-18 vision encoder.
 - Gripper state is projected to a token.
 - Encoded visual and proprioceptive tokens are concatenated and passed to the Transformer encoder.
 - The Transformer decoder, initialized with a learnable "start-of-sequence" (SOS) token corresponding to the final keyframe action, autoregressively generates previous actions in reverse.
- **Loss Function:** The total loss `L_total` combines a regression loss in the action space and the latent consistency loss, both applied with MTP regularization. Padding for varying trajectory lengths is masked to prevent affecting gradients.

**Execution:**
- CoA generates an entire trajectory segment, typically executed in **closed-loop mode** to allow continuous refinement using reverse temporal ensembling. The dynamic stop mechanism determines trajectory termination.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of Chain-of-Action (CoA) demonstrates its superior performance and robustness across a wide range of tasks in both simulation and the real world.

**Overall Performance:**
- **Simulation (RLBench-60):** CoA achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming ACT by an average of 16.3% and Diffusion Policy (DP) by 23.2% across 60 RLBench tasks. This comprehensive benchmark, the largest conducted to date for such policies, highlights CoA's broad applicability.
- **Real-world Manipulation:** Deployed on a Fetch robot, CoA achieved an average success rate of 0.613 across 8 kitchen manipulation tasks, outperforming ACT (0.463) by a significant margin of 15%.
- **Comparison with Octo:** On the RLBench-10 subset, CoA also surpassed the finetuned generalist robot policy Octo (0.756 average success rate vs. 0.644).
- **Comparison with 3D-based methods (RLBench-18):** While a substantial gap remains compared to 3D-based hierarchical methods that leverage high-fidelity 3D inputs and motion planners, CoA demonstrates the highest average success rate among image-based visuomotor policies (37.33% vs. ACT's 24.44% and DP's 17.33%).

**Spatial Generalization Capabilities:**
CoA exhibits strong spatial generalization, which is crucial for real-world adaptability:
- **Correlation with Spatial Distribution:** Analysis across 60 RLBench tasks revealed that while all methods saw a decrease in success rate with increasing object spatial variance (difficulty), CoA consistently outperformed ACT and DP. Crucially, the *performance gap widened* as spatial variance increased, indicating that CoA is more robust to challenging spatial perturbations.
- **Interpolation vs. Extrapolation (Push Button task):** In a controlled study, CoA demonstrated significantly higher success rates in both in-distribution (interpolation) and out-of-distribution (extrapolation) settings. While performance naturally degrades under extrapolation, CoA experienced a notably *smaller drop* compared to ACT and DP, suggesting its reverse autoregressive modeling provides more robust generalization under spatial distribution shifts.
- **Attention-based Analysis of Action Chain:** Visualization of self-attention maps within the Transformer decoder revealed two key patterns: (1) a dominant local "chain-like" structure, where action tokens attend to recent predecessors, directly reflecting the autoregressive modeling; and (2) occasional "long-range dependencies" where later tokens strongly attend to the initial keyframe action, confirming that the model leverages the goal-conditioned keyframe to guide the entire trajectory.

**Ablation Studies on Architectural Components:**
The ablation studies rigorously confirm the necessity and effectiveness of CoA's core modeling paradigm and its four complementary designs:
- **Modeling Paradigm:** The "Reverse ordering" of CoA (0.756 Avg. SR) was superior to "Forward ordering" (0.668 Avg. SR) and "Hybrid ordering" (0.600 Avg. SR). This unequivocally demonstrates that both the reverse autoregressive structure and goal anchoring via keyframes are essential for optimal performance.
- **Latent Consistency Loss:** Replacing this loss with direct action reconstruction led to a drastic performance drop (0.756 to 0.212 Avg. SR) and unstable trajectories, highlighting its critical role in regularizing the continuous latent space.
- **Reverse Temporal Ensemble:** The ensemble strategy significantly improved performance (0.660 to 0.756 Avg. SR), validating its benefit in aggregating multiple backward rollouts for inference.
- **Multi-Token Prediction (MTP):** An optimal configuration of 5 MTP heads achieved the highest score (0.756 Avg. SR), indicating a balance between capturing local action dependencies and preserving global causal structure.
- **ACT+KF (Appendix E):** Adding a keyframe to ACT (ACT+KF) yielded only marginal improvements over standard ACT (0.516 vs. 0.488 Avg. SR), reinforcing that simply injecting keyframe information is not enough; the holistic, continuous, and backward trajectory modeling of CoA is the key differentiator.

In summary, CoA consistently outperforms baselines, with gains stemming from its principled shift in modeling paradigm, particularly its ability to enforce global-to-local consistency and its enhanced spatial generalization, all supported by its innovative architectural components.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Chain-of-Action (CoA) research introduces a highly significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, offering a novel paradigm that effectively addresses one of the long-standing challenges: the accumulation of compounding errors in visuo-motor policies. Its potential impact is substantial, promising more robust, reliable, and generalizable robot control systems.

**Key Significance:**

- **Fundamental Paradigm Shift:** CoA moves beyond the conventional forward prediction paradigm by proposing "trajectory autoregressive modeling" with explicit "backward reasoning." This is not merely an incremental improvement but a conceptual re-evaluation of how action sequences should be generated, anchoring decisions to the task's ultimate goal rather than relying on myopic next-step predictions.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The core benefit of CoA's global-to-local reasoning is its demonstrated ability to mitigate compounding errors significantly. This translates into superior performance in complex, long-horizon tasks and, critically, strong spatial generalization capabilities. The consistent outperformance over baselines, especially in extrapolation settings and tasks with high spatial variance, indicates a notable leap towards more adaptive and less brittle robot policies.
- **Practical Applicability for Real-World Robotics:** The validation on real-world Fetch robot tasks underscores CoA's practical value. The ability to generalize effectively from raw visual inputs in dynamic, unstructured environments is a prerequisite for widespread robotic deployment in areas like service robotics, logistics, and manufacturing.
- **Efficiency and Simplicity:** Despite its sophisticated reasoning mechanism, CoA maintains the desirable properties of an end-to-end visuo-motor policy, operating directly on RGB inputs without requiring complex 3D reconstruction, explicit motion planners, or additional intermediate semantic modalities often found in other hierarchical or CoT-style approaches. Its architectural alignment with ACT, while achieving superior results, highlights that the performance gains stem from the modeling paradigm itself.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** CoA offers a competitive alternative to existing behavioral cloning and diffusion-based methods, opening new avenues for research in robotic policy learning. The paper explicitly points to future work, such as learning keyframe structures in an unsupervised manner, which could further reduce reliance on heuristics and enhance the autonomy and versatility of the framework.

**Potential Impact:**

- **More Reliable Robotic Systems:** By reducing compounding errors and improving generalization, CoA can lead to more reliable and trustworthy robotic systems that can consistently perform tasks despite variations in environment or object placement.
- **Accelerated Development of Generalist Robots:** The enhanced generalization capabilities contribute directly to the goal of developing generalist robot policies that can perform a wide array of tasks without extensive re-training, which is a key driver in modern robotics research (e.g., VLA models).
- **Broader Adoption in Industry:** The robust performance in real-world settings makes CoA a strong candidate for industrial applications where precise and dependable manipulation is crucial, potentially accelerating automation in various sectors.
- **New Directions in AI Research:** The backward reasoning paradigm could inspire similar approaches in other sequential decision-making problems beyond robotics, where long-term consistency and goal-alignment are critical.

In conclusion, Chain-of-Action represents a significant step forward in visuomotor policy learning, offering a principled and empirically validated solution to a fundamental problem in robotic control. Its impact could reshape how robotic manipulation tasks are approached, fostering the development of more intelligent, adaptable, and autonomous robots.
