# CRISP: Contact-Guided Real2Sim from Monocular Video with Planar Scene Primitives

> [!summary] Summary
> The CRISP pipeline converts monocular videos of human-scene interactions into simulation-ready assets, reconstructing 3D human motion and scene geometry that are physically plausible. It achieved a 93.1% real-to-sim success rate and a 43% faster RL simulation throughput compared to prior methods.

## Problem

- Existing 3D human-scene reconstruction methods often produce noisy, non-watertight geometry with artifacts, making them unsuitable for robust physics simulation.
- Crucial interaction surfaces in a scene (e.g., chair seats) are often occluded by the human body in monocular videos, hindering complete and accurate reconstruction.
- Dense mesh reconstructions are computationally expensive for collision detection, leading to inefficient physics simulations and limiting scalability for downstream reinforcement learning tasks.

## Method

- **CRISP** reconstructs 3D human motion and scene geometry from monocular video by employing ==normal-based== planar primitive fitting to generate compact, convex, and simulation-efficient scene geometry.
- The pipeline incorporates human-scene contact modeling, using InteractVLM and temporal-kinematic filtering, to infer and reconstruct occluded scene elements like support surfaces based on human interactions.
- A reinforcement learning framework with a fully-constrained motion-tracking policy validates and refines the physical plausibility of the combined human and scene reconstructions, ensuring stable and realistic interactions.

## Results

- CRISP achieved a **93.1%** real-to-sim success rate, a substantial increase over the **44.8%** of the concurrent VideoMimic pipeline, and supported a **43%** faster RL simulation throughput (**23K** FPS vs. **16K** FPS).
- On the EMDB dataset, the method set a new state-of-the-art for global human motion estimation, reducing WA-MPJPE_100 to **70.60 mm** and W-MPJPE_100 to **175.93 mm** with RL refinement.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that contact-guided scene completion improved geometric alignment, leading to lower Chamfer distances and significantly enhancing the RL success rate by providing more faithful support surface reconstructions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing complex scenes into compact, convex planar primitives significantly enhances simulation efficiency and robustness, outperforming traditional dense mesh representations for physics-based interactions.
> - Explicitly leveraging human-scene contact cues effectively addresses occlusions, enabling the reconstruction of hidden interaction surfaces vital for physically accurate simulations.
> - Integrating a physics-based reinforcement learning controller not only validates the physical plausibility of reconstructed assets but also refines global human motion estimation, reducing pose drift and temporal jitter.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the challenge of reconstructing 3D human motion and simulatable scene geometry from monocular video, a subfield within computer vision and graphics that is crucial for applications in embodied AI, robotics, physically-plausible character animation, and AR/VR.

Prior work in this domain has explored various approaches to 3D human motion estimation, often formulated as recovering parameters of parametric human models like SMPL. Early methods relied on optimization, while more recent feed-forward human mesh recovery (HMR) methods use deep neural networks. A growing focus has been on recovering metric human trajectories jointly with scene geometry and contacts in a world frame, with methods like TRAM and WHAM incorporating camera pose estimation and contact likelihood for refinement. However, existing human-scene reconstruction methods frequently integrate multiple data-driven priors and geometric foundation models without explicit physics in the loop. These approaches often produce noisy 2.5D geometry with artifacts (e.g., duplicate structures, missing regions) that are unsuitable for robust physical simulation, particularly in scenarios involving parallax and occlusions caused by the human body.

Scene reconstruction, while advanced by methods like visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) and neural fields, often yields dense meshes that are computationally expensive for collision detection in physics simulators. The interaction between humans and scenes, particularly the accurate modeling of contact, is a critical component for realistic simulation. Physics-based control methods, such as those using reinforcement learning (RL) to imitate motion capture or video trajectories, have shown promise in producing natural and physically plausible interactions. However, many of these methods either focus on simplistic environments with limited scene interaction or require pre-defined scene geometry, rather than reconstructing it directly from video. Concurrent work like VideoMimic has attempted joint reconstruction of humans and environments for control policies, but has faced limitations related to simulation stability, efficiency, and RL success rates due to less accurate modeling of humans, scenes, and contacts.

This work, named CRISP, aims to bridge the gap between monocular video reconstruction and physics-based simulation by generating "simulation-ready assets." It differentiates itself by explicitly focusing on recovering clean, convex, and watertight scene geometry suitable for physics engines, incorporating contact modeling to handle occlusions, and using reinforcement learning to ensure physical plausibility of the combined human and scene reconstruction. This approach attempts to overcome the limitations of noisy geometry and inefficient dense meshes, positioning CRISP as a method for generating physically-valid human motion and interaction environments at scale.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a "vid2sim" pipeline that converts casual monocular videos of human-scene interactions into simulation-ready assets, specifically focusing on reconstructing 3D human motion and scene geometry that are physically plausible and suitable for downstream reinforcement learning.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several challenges and unmet needs in the current state of human and scene reconstruction from video:

- **Physical Understanding:** The authors argue that a comprehensive understanding of human video extends beyond space-time reconstruction or activity recognition; it requires a physical interpretation of interactions with the environment. Humans constantly engage with their surroundings (e.g., sitting on chairs, climbing stairs), and accurate simulation of these interactions requires reconstructions that obey physical laws, such as avoiding inter-penetrations or floating geometry.
- **Limitations of Prior Reconstruction Methods:** Existing methods for joint human-scene reconstruction often rely heavily on data-driven priors and joint optimization without incorporating physics. These methods frequently yield noisy geometry, characterized by artifacts, duplicate structures, or missing regions, especially in videos with parallax and occlusions (e.g., portions of a chair occluded by a sitting person). Such inaccuracies can lead to failures in motion tracking policies and instability in physics simulations.
- **Precision Requirements for Simulation:** Physical simulation demands a high degree of precision in reconstructed geometry. Even minor inaccuracies in scene geometry, such as a noisy ground plane, can cause simulated humanoids to fail or exhibit unstable behavior. Prior work on driving humanoid simulators from video often circumvents this by focusing on simplistic environments with minimal human-scene interaction.
- **Efficiency of Simulation:** Collision detection, a core component of physics simulation, is computationally intensive. It is significantly more efficient when scene geometry is well-approximated by convex primitives. The large, intricate meshes typically generated by conventional reconstruction pipelines (e.g., TSDF fusion followed by Marching Cubes) lead to high simulation costs and lower throughput.
- **Scalable Learning for Downstream Applications:** A robust real-to-sim pipeline is essential for scalable learning in fields such as embodied AI and robotics, physically-plausible character animation, and AR/VR. The ability to automatically generate high-fidelity, physically-valid assets from unconstrained videos would unlock new possibilities for training and deploying autonomous agents and virtual characters.

To address these motivations, CRISP sets out to achieve the following specific goals:
1. Recover **convex, clean, and simulation-ready geometry** by fitting compact planar primitives to point cloud reconstructions, thereby improving simulation efficiency and robustness to noise.
2. Utilize **human-scene contact modeling** to reconstruct scene geometry that might be occluded during interactions, informed by human posture and detected interaction types (e.g., inferring a chair seat from a seated pose).
3. Ensure the **physical plausibility** of both human and scene reconstructions by employing them to train and drive a humanoid controller via reinforcement learning, validating that the interactions respect physical laws.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CRISP pipeline integrates several components to transform monocular RGB video into simulation-ready human motion and scene geometry. The overall process begins with video input, proceeds through scene and human reconstruction, refines the scene geometry using contact cues, and concludes with physics-based motion tracking via reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Human, Scene, and Camera Initialization

The pipeline starts by inferring fundamental scene and human parameters.
- **Camera Parameters and Scene Point Cloud:** Camera intrinsics, per-frame camera poses, and a dense depth map are recovered from the monocular video using MegaSAM. To enhance geometry quality, the depth estimator in MegaSAM's optimization is replaced with MoGe, yielding a scale-invariant dense point cloud and calibrated camera parameters.
- **3D Human Pose Estimation:** The estimated camera intrinsics are passed to GVHMR to obtain an SMPL mesh in camera space. This 3D human model is then lifted to the world frame using the estimated camera poses, ensuring a unified coordinate system for human, scene, and camera.
- **Metric Scaling:** The global scene point cloud, initially scale-invariant, is scaled to a metric scale by matching the depth of the human in the scaled point cloud with the known depth of the 3D SMPL mesh from GVHMR.

#### 3.2. Normal-Based Planar Primitive Fitting

A core component of CRISP is its method for generating compact, simulation-efficient scene geometry.
- **Rationale:** Traditional methods converting point clouds to meshes (e.g., TSDF fusion, Marching Cubes) often result in very large, noisy, and non-watertight meshes that are inefficient for physics simulation and prone to artifacts. CRISP addresses this by decomposing the scene into a small set of convex planar primitives, which are efficient for collision detection and regularize the reconstruction against low-level noise. This choice is based on the assumption that many human-scene interactions involve planar surfaces.
- **Pipeline:**
 1. **Per-frame Segmentation:** For each video frame, normals are estimated from the dense point map. K-means clustering is applied to these normals to produce candidate planar segments. Within each segment, DBSCAN is used for spatial clustering on 3D points, splitting segments further if necessary.
 2. **Cross-frame Association:** To ensure temporal consistency and merge physical planes that might appear as multiple segments across different frames, segments are associated across frames. For pairs of frames with optical flow and covisibility masks, segments from one frame are warped to the other. An overlap ratio and normal cosine similarity are computed to link similar segments. These links are aggregated over time to identify global planar groups.
 3. **Primitive Fitting:** For each global planar group, a plane is fitted using RANSAC to the aggregated 3D points. The inliers are then projected onto this plane, and a minimum-area rectangle is fitted to define in-plane axes, determining the plane's rotation. The size of the planar primitive is set based on in-plane coverage and normal-direction spread, and its center is adjusted. A default thickness of 0.05m is assigned, forming a planar cuboid. Optionally, if a primitive's footprint is not rectangular, it can be split along a principal in-plane axis and refitted.

#### 3.3. Contact as a Cue for Scene Completion

CRISP incorporates human-scene contact modeling to address occluded scene geometry, which is critical for realistic interactions.
- **Occlusion Handling:** Key interaction surfaces (e.g., chair seats, stair treads) may be occluded by the human or other scene elements in a monocular video.
- **Contact Prediction:** Given an input frame and the estimated SMPL mesh, InteractVLM is used to predict per-vertex contact probabilities, generating a binary contact mask on the SMPL vertices.
- **Temporal-Kinematic Filtering:** To reduce false positives from InteractVLM (e.g., "near-contact" frames), a filtering step is applied. Only contact predictions with consistently high confidence over a defined window of `L` frames and minimal human motion within that window are retained. The frame with the smallest human motion is selected from this window.
- **Scene Augmentation:** These temporally-filtered contact predictions provide cues to infer and reconstruct occluded interaction surfaces. For example, a seated pose can indicate the presence and geometry of a chair seat, even if it is not directly visible. The planar fitting algorithm is then reapplied to these predicted contact points (in world coordinates) to augment the existing scene planes, ensuring a minimum thickness.

#### 3.4. Physics-Based Motion Tracking

To validate and refine the human and scene reconstructions for physical plausibility, CRISP employs a reinforcement learning (RL) framework.
- **Humanoid Controller:** A fully-constrained motion-tracking policy (π_FC) is trained to imitate the 3D human motion sequence extracted by the pipeline. The simulated character is based on the SMPL human model.
- **Observation Space:** The policy's input includes the character's current state (joint orientations, positions, linear/angular velocities relative to the root) and the next `K` target poses from the reference motion.
- **Action Space:** Actions are parameterized as desired joint targets for a Proportional–Derivative (PD) controller, which controls the simulated humanoid's movements.
- **Reward Function:** The RL agent is trained with a reward function that encourages minimizing the difference between the simulated humanoid's state (position, rotation, linear/angular velocity, root height) and the reference motion at each timestep. An energy penalty is also included to promote smoother, less jittery motion.
- **Training Details:** The policy is modeled using a transformer encoder, while the critic uses a multi-layer perceptron. Training is enhanced with Reference State Initialization (RSI) and Early Termination (ET) strategies to improve stability and efficiency. Isaac Gym is used for GPU-accelerated physics simulation at 120Hz, with policies operating at 30Hz. Optimization is performed using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Generalized Advantage Estimator (GAE). A separate control policy is trained for each motion clip to assess how well the reconstructed assets perform in simulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CRISP focuses on three key areas: world-grounded HMR accuracy, human–scene interaction fidelity, and RL-based motion tracking performance. The results demonstrate improvements across these dimensions compared to baseline methods.

#### 4.1. Overall Real-to-Sim Performance

- **Success Rate and Throughput:** CRISP achieves a 93.1% real-to-sim success rate, which is a substantial increase compared to the concurrent VideoMimic pipeline's 44.8%. This indicates a significant improvement in the reliability of generating physically plausible simulations from monocular video. Furthermore, CRISP supports a 43% faster RL simulation throughput, reaching 23K FPS compared to VideoMimic's 16K FPS.
- **Qualitative Comparison:** Qualitative comparisons (Figure 4) highlight that VideoMimic's dense mesh reconstructions often lead to simulation failures due to non-physically plausible geometry. These issues include agent penetrations into "ghost surfaces," bouncing off protruding artifacts, contact errors from over-smoothed structures, and agents getting trapped in bumpy terrains or local dents. In contrast, CRISP's planar primitives yield simulation-ready assets that facilitate stable policy rollouts and preserve contact-faithful human-scene interactions.
- **Ablation on Scene Representation:** An ablation study (Table 1) evaluates the impact of different scene representations. Replacing VideoMimic’s dense mesh with a TSDF mesh already improves RL success, but TSDF still suffers from over-smoothing and duplicate structures. Using Neural Kernel Surface Reconstruction (NKSR) further sharpens surfaces, improving non-penetration and lowering Chamfer distance. CRISP's planar primitive representation, while having a slightly higher bidirectional Chamfer distance (CD_bi) than NKSR due to potentially missing fine-grained, non-contact details, achieves the lowest one-way Chamfer (CD_one, Recon→GT) error. This indicates that where CRISP reconstructs geometry, it is highly accurate relative to ground truth. Crucially, planar primitives yield the highest non-penetration score and the best RL success rate, demonstrating their suitability for contact-rich simulations where accurate support surfaces are critical.

#### 4.2. Global Human Motion Estimation

- **Accuracy on EMDB:** On the EMDB dataset, CRISP significantly outperforms prior state-of-the-art HMR methods (WHAM, TRAM, GVHMR, VideoMimic) in global motion estimation metrics (Table 2). Without RL refinement, CRISP shows competitive WA-MPJPE_100 and W-MPJPE_100 values. With RL-based motion tracking, these errors are further reduced to 70.60 mm (WA-MPJPE_100) and 175.93 mm (W-MPJPE_100), establishing CRISP as the most accurate method among those compared.
- **Trajectory Consistency:** The method also demonstrates improved global trajectory consistency, exhibiting the lowest root translational error and substantially reduced temporal jitter compared to previous approaches. This suggests that the RL refinement not only reduces pose drift but also stabilizes temporal dynamics, resulting in smoother and more physically consistent motions. Conversely, VideoMimic exhibits large joint errors and high drift, indicating that its lower-quality reconstructions negatively impact world-grounded motion estimation.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study on Contact Cues

- **Impact on Geometric Alignment:** The ablation study on contact-guided scene completion (Table 3) shows that explicitly incorporating contact signals improves the geometric alignment of reconstructed scenes, leading to lower Chamfer distances (both GT→Recon and Recon→GT directions).
- **Support Surface Refinement:** The inclusion of contact priors helps refine surfaces near human interaction regions, yielding more faithful reconstructions of crucial support structures. Qualitatively (Figure 5), sequences without contact-guided planar reconstructions often cause downstream RL policies to fail, as occluded support surfaces are omitted. The addition of contact-guided geometry enables stable simulations and motions that better track the reference human motion.
- **Limitations:** The efficacy of contact guidance is dependent on the accuracy of HMR and contact estimation. Inaccuracies in these upstream components can lead to slightly tilted or offset planar primitives.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of CRISP demonstrates a notable advancement in the field of 3D human motion and scene reconstruction from monocular video, particularly in its focus on generating simulation-ready assets.

The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

- **Bridging the Real-to-Sim Gap:** CRISP provides an effective pipeline for converting unconstrained monocular human videos into high-quality, physically valid assets that can be directly used in physics-based simulators. This addresses a critical need for scalable data generation in simulation-driven research.
- **Enhanced Simulation Reliability and Efficiency:** By using compact, convex planar primitives and integrating contact-guided scene completion, CRISP substantially improves the reliability of physical simulations, evidenced by an 8x reduction in motion-tracking failure rates (from 55.2% to 6.9% compared to prior art). It also significantly increases RL simulation throughput, making training processes more efficient.
- **Improved Reconstruction Quality:** The research indicates that integrating physical reasoning through RL can improve the quality of both human and scene reconstructions. The planar fitting approach provides robust, noise-resistant scene geometry, while contact cues enable the recovery of occluded interaction surfaces crucial for realistic simulations.
- **Enabling Scalable Learning for Embodied AI and Robotics:** The ability to generate physically plausible human motion and interaction environments at scale from readily available video data has direct implications for embodied AI and robotics. It can facilitate the training of robust humanoid controllers, enable the learning of complex interaction skills, and support the development of agents capable of navigating and interacting with diverse environments.
- **Advancements in Character Animation and AR/VR:** For character animation, CRISP could enable the generation of more natural and physically consistent human motions and interactions with virtual environments. In AR/VR, it could support the creation of immersive experiences where virtual avatars interact realistically with reconstructed real-world scenes.
- **Validation on Diverse Data:** The validation of CRISP on human-centric video benchmarks (EMDB, PROX) and its demonstrated ability to process in-the-wild videos, including casually-captured, Internet, and Sora-generated content, indicates its robustness and broad applicability.

In summary, CRISP offers a robust and efficient approach to real-to-sim transfer, which is expected to advance research and applications requiring physically-grounded human-scene interactions derived from common video sources.
