# GHOST: Hierarchical Sub-Goal Policies for Generalizing Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The GHOST framework introduces a hierarchical approach to robot manipulation that predicts 3D end-effector sub-goals using human demonstrations and executes low-level actions, improving task robustness and enabling generalization to novel objects and task variations. It achieves higher success rates on complex, long-horizon tasks and demonstrates cross-embodiment skill transfer.

## Problem

- Existing imitation learning approaches require extensive and costly robot teleoperation data to generalize across diverse manipulation tasks and environments.
- Flat, end-to-end policies often struggle with the robustness and long-horizon planning required for complex multi-stage manipulation tasks.
- Leveraging readily available human video demonstrations for robot learning is challenging due to embodiment mismatches and the difficulty of accurately retargeting human actions to robot grippers.

## Method

- **GHOST** factors robot control into a ==high-level policy== (π_hi) that predicts 3D end-effector sub-goals from multi-view RGB-D observations and a low-level goal-conditioned policy (π_lo) for embodiment-specific action execution.
- The high-level policy is trained on an embodiment-agnostic 3D keypoint representation of end-effector poses, allowing it to incorporate human video demonstrations for generalization without requiring action labels.
- A spatial interface projects 3D predicted sub-goals into 2D end-effector heatmaps, which are then used to condition an image-based low-level Diffusion Policy for precise, reactive control.

## Results

- Hierarchical GHOST (robot data only) improved final success rates for in-distribution tasks, achieving **80.0%** on 'fold-onesie' compared to **10.0%** for a flat Diffusion Policy, and **50.0%** on 'hammer-pin' versus **16.7%**.
- Integrating human demonstrations enabled generalization to novel out-of-distribution tasks, with GHOST achieving **63.3%** success on 'mug-on-table' (novel object) compared to **13.3%** for the flat policy, and **36.7%** on 'fold-towel' (novel category).
- An oracle high-level policy, with an ideal visual domain, boosted the 'fold-towel' success rate from **40%** (human-trained π_hi) to **90%**, demonstrating the zero-shot capability of the low-level policy when guided by accurate sub-goals.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly decomposing manipulation tasks into hierarchical sub-goal prediction and low-level action execution significantly enhances robustness and performance for complex, long-horizon tasks.
> - Using an embodiment-agnostic 3D sub-goal representation allows human video data to effectively inform the high-level policy, enabling cross-embodiment transfer and adaptation to novel objects and task variations.
> - The low-level controller exhibits zero-shot generalization to novel object categories when provided with accurate sub-goals, indicating that the visual domain gap between human and robot observations in the high-level policy is a primary bottleneck for out-of-distribution transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation aims to develop policies that enable robots to interact with their environment to perform complex tasks. A prevalent approach to teaching robots these behaviors is imitation learning (IL), which involves training policies on expert demonstration trajectories. While IL has demonstrated success in learning precise and intricate behaviors when provided with substantial data, a primary limitation is the considerable data requirement for generalization to novel objects, environments, and task variations. This data is typically collected through expensive and labor-intensive robot teleoperation.

Within this context, researchers are exploring alternative strategies to improve data efficiency and generalization capabilities. One such strategy involves the explicit modeling of task hierarchy, where complex manipulation tasks are decomposed into a sequence of simpler sub-goals or phases. This hierarchical structure has been recognized for its potential to improve robustness and data efficiency by separating high-level planning (sub-goal selection) from low-level execution (embodiment-specific actions). Prior work in hierarchical imitation learning has focused on learning both high- and low-level policies from robot data, often defining sub-goals in end-effector space or conditioning controllers on intermediate targets through visual or linguistic cues.

A complementary area of research seeks to leverage diverse data sources beyond robot teleoperation, particularly human demonstrations. Human videos offer a potentially vast and cost-effective source of supervision. However, directly translating human actions to robot actions (action retargeting) presents challenges due to noisy pose estimates and fundamental embodiment mismatches between human hands and robot grippers. Some approaches attempt to learn latent representations or dynamics from human video corpora, which are then mapped to robot actions using additional robot data.

GHOST situates itself within this landscape by addressing the challenges of data efficiency, generalization, and cross-embodiment transfer. It leverages hierarchical factorization to enhance in-distribution performance and explicitly incorporates human demonstrations to facilitate generalization to out-of-distribution tasks, specifically by using human data for embodiment-agnostic sub-goal prediction rather than action retargeting.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop visuomotor manipulation policies that exhibit generalization capabilities beyond their initial training distribution. This overarching goal is motivated by the recognized limitations of current imitation learning paradigms in robotics, which typically require extensive and costly robot teleoperation data to achieve sufficient generalization.

The authors identify two key levers to address this challenge:
1. **Hierarchy in Policy Design:** Many manipulation tasks can be naturally segmented into distinct phases, each culminating in a specific sub-goal (e.g., "grasp," "transport," "place"). The research is motivated by the hypothesis that explicitly modeling this inherent task hierarchy can lead to improvements in robustness and data efficiency. By separating the selection of sub-goals from the execution of low-level actions, the system can manage long-horizon behaviors more effectively and potentially reduce the complexity of individual policy components. Specifically, defining sub-goals as end-effector poses at phase boundaries provides a compact and intuitive interface for composing skills.

2. **Incorporation of Human Demonstrations:** Given the high cost and labor intensity of collecting robot teleoperation data, the research is motivated to explore alternative, more scalable sources of supervision. Human video demonstrations are identified as a promising complementary data source. The challenge with human data, particularly for action learning, often lies in the "embodiment mismatch" between human hands and robot grippers, and the difficulty of accurately retargeting human actions to robots. The authors propose that if sub-goals can be represented in an embodiment-agnostic manner, human demonstrations could be used to inform the high-level policy without requiring noisy action retargeting, thus enabling cross-embodiment transfer.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives for the GHOST framework are:
- To factorize robot control into a high-level policy (π_hi) that predicts 3D end-effector sub-goals from multi-view RGB-D observations, and a low-level goal-conditioned policy (π_lo) that executes embodiment-specific actions.
- To introduce a spatial interface for goal conditioning that projects 3D predicted sub-goals into the image plane as end-effector heatmaps, thereby enabling effective conditioning of image-based low-level policies.
- To demonstrate that this hierarchical factorization improves in-distribution performance and robustness compared to flat policies, even when trained solely on robot demonstrations.
- To establish that the embodiment-agnostic nature of the sub-goal interface allows for the incorporation of human video data to train the high-level policy, facilitating adaptation to novel objects and task variations without requiring action labels from human demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GHOST framework employs a hierarchical approach to visuomotor manipulation policy learning, decoupling high-level goal prediction from low-level action execution.

**A. Overview of GHOST**
GHOST learns a hierarchy over 3D end-effector sub-goals. During training, sub-goal boundaries from demonstrations provide supervision for the high-level policy (π_hi), while full robot trajectories provide action supervision for the low-level controller (π_lo). At test time, π_hi predicts a sub-goal (as a distribution) based on observations and language instruction, and π_lo executes goal-conditioned control to achieve that sub-goal. This separation aims to assign long-horizon reasoning to π_hi and precise, embodiment-specific control to π_lo.

**B. Data Collection and Preprocessing**
- **Robot Data:** Demonstrations are collected via teleoperation across various task variants. Sub-goals are automatically extracted by identifying timesteps where the gripper state changes (open to close, or vice versa).
- **Optional Human Data:** Human demonstrations are collected for the same skills in novel settings (new objects or applications). 3D hand poses (H_t) are estimated using off-the-shelf hand pose trackers. Scale ambiguity in hand pose detection is resolved using Grounded-SAM and observed depth. Sub-goals are manually annotated at the termination of semantic transitions. Human data does not provide action labels, used only for high-level policy training on estimated end-effector poses.
- **End-Effector Representation:** End-effector poses for both robot and human demonstrations are represented as a sparse set of four 3D keypoints: gripper base, tips of two fingers, and the grasping center. For robots, these points are sampled from the gripper mesh; for humans, from the MANO mesh of the hand pose. This unified representation aims to bridge the embodiment gap.

**C. Policy Learning**

1. **High-Level Goal Prediction Policy (π_hi):**
 - **Objective:** Predicts a distribution over the 3D end-effector pose (e_s) at a sub-goal timestep (s).
 - **Architecture:** A decoder-only transformer processes multi-view RGB-D observations.
 - **Visual Input:** Each RGB-D image is processed by a frozen DINOv3 encoder, yielding patch tokens. Each patch token is augmented with an MLP-processed 3D coordinate [x, y, z] of its center.
 - **Context Input:** Additional tokens encode the current gripper pose (through an MLP), a task language embedding (from Flan-T5), and a learnable token indicating the demonstration's embodiment (human or robot).
 - **Output:** The transformer's output for each patch token predicts the parameters of a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM). Specifically, each patch predicts a mixing weight (w_i) and four 3D residual vectors ({δ_i,1} to {δ_i,4}) relative to its center (p_i). These residuals are trained to predict the 3D points of the gripper. The global goal distribution is a mixture of K × C isotropic Gaussians (one per patch) with fixed variance.
 - **Training:** π_hi is trained by minimizing the negative log-likelihood on sub-goal transitions, using a combined dataset of robot and human data (D'_robot ∪ D_human). Multiple fixed variances are used for the NLL loss to capture sub-goal distributions at different scales.
 - **Inference:** A patch is sampled from the categorical distribution defined by mixing weights, and its corresponding mean prediction (p_i + δ_i) yields the predicted end-effector pose.

2. **Low-Level Goal-Conditioned Policy (π_lo):**
 - **Objective:** Execute embodiment-specific actions towards a predicted sub-goal.
 - **Base Policy:** Instantiated as a Diffusion Policy, which generates action chunks by denoising conditioned on observations.
 - **Goal Conditioning:** The 3D sub-goal (e_s) predicted by π_hi is projected onto the image plane of each camera to yield sparse 2D coordinates (p_i). These 2D coordinates are then converted into dense end-effector heatmaps (H). Each channel of H encodes the pixel distance field from a specific keypoint, providing a dense supervisory signal. Three heatmap channels, corresponding to non-collinear keypoints (gripper wrist and two finger points), are selected and passed through standard 3-channel image encoder networks (ResNets).
 - **Architecture:** Images from each camera and the processed end-effector heatmap images are independently encoded with ResNet-18 backbones. These features are concatenated along with proprioceptive input into a global conditioning vector for the Diffusion Policy's U-Net.
 - **Training:** π_lo is trained using the standard diffusion loss, exclusively on robot-only data (D_robot).
 - **Inference:** During deployment, π_hi and π_lo are executed sequentially. π_hi predicts a sub-goal, and then π_lo predicts an action chunk conditioned on the heatmap representation of this sub-goal prediction. This allows for reactive replanning.

The high-level and low-level policies are trained independently. The system uses a single multi-task policy with language conditioning, where language is primarily used by the high-level policy in hierarchical methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to evaluate the performance benefits of the hierarchical policy and its ability to generalize using human demonstrations.

**A. In-Distribution Performance (Q1)**
- **Comparison with Flat Policy:** For complex, long-horizon tasks, the hierarchical factorization in GHOST (trained with robot data only) consistently improved performance compared to a flat Diffusion Policy (DP).
 - On the `fold-onesie` task (5 folds), GHOST (Robot Only) achieved an 80.0% final success rate, a substantial increase from DP's 10.0%. This indicates that explicit hierarchical decomposition improves robustness and long-horizon task completion.
 - For `hammer-pin`, GHOST (Robot Only) achieved a 50.0% success rate, compared to DP's 16.7%.
 - For simpler tasks like `plate-on-table`, most methods, including GHOST (Robot Only) and DP, achieved high success rates (83.3% and 80.0% respectively), suggesting saturation of performance with sufficient data.
- **Visual Backbone Ablation (Appendix D):** An ablation study confirmed that the hierarchical sub-goal factorization itself is a significant contributor to performance. GHOST, when using a tiny ViT trained from scratch (instead of the pre-trained DINOv3 backbone), still substantially outperformed the flat Diffusion Policy (50% vs. 20% final success on `fold-onesie`). However, the pre-trained DINOv3 backbone provided additional performance gains (90% final success), indicating the importance of a strong visual encoder for accurate goal prediction, especially over longer horizons.

**B. Generalization (Q2)**
- **Cross-Embodiment Transfer with Human Data:** The inclusion of human demonstrations for training the high-level policy enabled meaningful out-of-distribution (OOD) transfer to novel objects, object categories, and skill compositions.
 - On `mug-on-table` (a novel object combination not seen in robot demos), GHOST (with human data) achieved 63.3% success, significantly outperforming DP (13.3%) and MimicPlay (28.3%). GHOST (Robot Only) also showed improvement (55.0%), indicating benefits of hierarchy even without human data on this OOD task, but human data further boosted it.
 - For `fold-onesie-ood` (a novel object instance), GHOST achieved 56.7% final success, surpassing GHOST (Robot Only)'s 43.3% and MimicPlay's 0.0%.
 - On `fold-towel` (the most challenging OOD task involving a novel object category and skill composition), GHOST achieved 36.7% success, outperforming the MimicPlay baseline (16.7%).
 - For `hammer-pin`, GHOST (with human data) achieved 70.0% success, further improving upon GHOST (Robot Only)'s 50.0%.
- **Oracle High-Level Policy Ablation (Appendix E):** An oracle experiment on `fold-towel` (where π_hi was trained on a small set of robot demonstrations of towel folding, while π_lo remained unchanged and had never seen towel-folding data) showed that success rates increased from 40% (human π_hi) to 90% (oracle π_hi). This finding suggests two points:
 1. The low-level policy (π_lo) demonstrates zero-shot generalization to novel object categories when provided with accurate sub-goals.
 2. The primary bottleneck for OOD generalization in the human-data-assisted approach is the visual domain gap between human and robot observations within the high-level policy (π_hi), rather than the low-level controller's inability to execute on unseen objects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GHOST framework introduces a hierarchical imitation learning paradigm that offers several contributions to robot manipulation.

Firstly, the work demonstrates that factorizing control into high-level sub-goal prediction and low-level action execution significantly improves in-distribution performance and robustness, particularly for complex, long-horizon tasks. This suggests that explicitly modeling task hierarchy is a valuable strategy for enhancing the reliability and capability of visuomotor policies, even without external data sources. The spatial interface, which converts 3D goals into 2D end-effector heatmaps for goal conditioning, provides a practical mechanism to integrate 3D planning with image-based control.

Secondly, GHOST addresses a fundamental challenge in robot learning: the scarcity and expense of robot demonstration data. By leveraging embodiment-agnostic 3D end-effector sub-goals, the framework effectively incorporates human video demonstrations to train the high-level policy. This capability enables cross-embodiment skill transfer to novel object instances, categories, and compositional task variations without the need for complex and often noisy action retargeting from human to robot. This mechanism expands the potential data sources for robot learning, making it more feasible to scale manipulation skills to a wider array of real-world scenarios.

The empirical results, including improved success rates on out-of-distribution tasks, indicate that GHOST facilitates generalization, which is crucial for the deployment of versatile robotic systems. The work also highlights the complementary roles of a robust hierarchical structure and powerful pre-trained visual encoders for accurate goal prediction.

Despite these advancements, the study also identifies areas for future work, which could further amplify the framework's impact. These include improving the quality of estimated human hand poses, developing methods for automatic sub-goal discovery (rather than relying on heuristics or manual annotation), and, critically, addressing the visual domain gap between human and robot observations for the high-level policy. Overcoming this domain gap, as indicated by the oracle ablation, holds potential to unlock even greater zero-shot generalization capabilities for the low-level controller across novel object categories. The computational cost of the hierarchical approach is also noted to be higher than a flat policy, which is a practical consideration.

In summary, GHOST presents a hierarchical framework that enhances the generalization and robustness of robot manipulation policies by strategically integrating task decomposition with heterogeneous demonstration data, particularly human videos. Its impact lies in offering a pathway toward more data-efficient and adaptable robot learning, moving closer to robots that can acquire and transfer skills across diverse manipulation contexts.
