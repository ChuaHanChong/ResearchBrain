# Physics-Aware End-to-End Deep Reinforcement Learning for Quadcopter Control with Actuator Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at National Cheng Kung University developed a physics-aware end-to-end Deep Reinforcement Learning framework for quadcopter control, integrating detailed actuator dynamics and thrust/torque allocation into the training process. This approach enables DRL agents to command low-level body inputs while accounting for motor lags and allocation nonlinearities, with off-policy methods like TD3 and SAC demonstrating more stable and sample-efficient learning compared to DDPG and PPO in achieving stable hover and single-axis translation tasks.

## Problem

- Existing DRL research for quadcopter control often simplifies physical dynamics, assuming idealized rigid-body inputs and omitting real-world factors like rotor aerodynamics and actuator lags.
- These simplifications create a "sim-to-real gap," causing DRL policies trained in simulation to perform poorly when deployed on actual hardware.
- A lack of DRL frameworks that directly incorporate detailed actuator dynamics and thrust/torque allocation in a physics-aware manner.

## Method

- An end-to-end DRL framework was developed, integrating with a high-fidelity MATLAB/Simulink quadcopter simulator that includes a ==12-state rigid-body model==.
- The framework incorporates a ==physics-aware design== with ==action-to-RPM allocation== using the ==Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse== and ==first-order motor dynamics== to model actuator lag.
- DRL agents were trained on low-level body inputs (total thrust and body torques) using ==DDPG==, ==TD3==, ==PPO==, and ==SAC== in a two-stage curriculum (hover, then single-axis translation).

## Results

- TD3 achieved the most consistent and highest training return in the thrust-only hover task, closely followed by SAC, while DDPG showed oscillations and PPO failed to converge.
- In the more complex thrust plus single-axis pitch torque task, TD3 maintained its lead in sample efficiency, with SAC accelerating to comparable or superior performance, outperforming DDPG.
- The best-performing SAC agent demonstrated stable and accurate goal attainment in simulated trajectories, with minimal lateral excursion and bounded overshoot in altitude before settling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Incorporating explicit thrust/torque allocation and first-order rotor/motor dynamics into DRL training significantly increases the difficulty of the learning problem for algorithms.
> - Off-policy DRL algorithms (TD3 and SAC) demonstrated superior sample efficiency and more stable convergence compared to DDPG and PPO in the physics-aware simulation environment.
> - Detailed observation and reward shaping, along with a staged learning protocol, are crucial for effectively training DRL agents on actuator-constrained UAVs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) control, particularly for quadcopters, presents inherent challenges due to their underactuated nature, requiring four control inputs to manage six degrees of freedom. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising approach for autonomous control, demonstrating capabilities in both high-level navigation and low-level attitude/thrust control. Prominent DRL algorithms such as DDPG, TD3, PPO, and SAC are frequently applied in continuous control tasks.

Existing DRL research in UAV control often operates under simplified assumptions regarding the physical dynamics of the vehicle. A significant portion of this work assumes idealized rigid-body inputs, such as instantaneous application of total thrust and body torques ($T, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z$), thereby omitting crucial real-world factors. These factors include rotor aerodynamics, actuator lags (delays in motor response), and nonlinearities in thrust/torque allocation. While such simplifications can expedite the learning process, they introduce a notable gap between simulation and real-world performance, commonly referred to as the "sim-to-real gap." Issues like yaw authority, cross-coupled responses, and bandwidth limits of the control system can be obscured when these physical aspects are not adequately modeled.

Prior work has explored DRL for attitude stabilization and thrust control, cascaded designs, disturbance robustness, and sim-to-real demonstrations. However, these often still rely on commanding body moments and total thrust with instantaneous actuation, neglecting the intricacies of allocation and motor dynamics. Similarly, efforts in aggressive flight and architectural priors (e.g., equivariance) contribute to DRL for UAVs but do not consistently expose policies to realistic actuation mechanisms and mixing.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by specifically addressing the identified gap: the lack of physics-aware DRL frameworks that directly incorporate detailed actuator dynamics and thrust/torque allocation. By doing so, it aims to develop and evaluate DRL policies in an environment that more closely mimics hardware constraints, providing a testbed for developing controllers that are more likely to transfer effectively to physical quadcopters. The work distinguishes itself by exposing DRL policies to a realistic actuation pathway, including the mapping of body-wrench actions to per-rotor speeds via an allocation process that accounts for thrust/torque coupling and first-order motor dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate a physics-aware, end-to-end Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) approach for quadcopter control. This approach aims to train DRL agents that can act directly on low-level body inputs—total thrust ($T$) and body torques ($\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z$)—while explicitly modeling the underlying physical processes that translate these commands into rotor speeds and ultimately into vehicle motion.

The key motivations for this work stem from a recognized limitation in much of the existing DRL research for UAVs:
- **Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap:** A significant portion of DRL studies for UAV control simplifies the quadcopter's dynamics by assuming idealized rigid-body inputs. This simplification often means omitting critical physical phenomena such as rotor aerodynamics, actuator lags (time delays in motor response), and the nonlinearities involved in mapping high-level thrust/torque commands to individual rotor speeds. These omissions can lead to policies that perform well in simplified simulations but fail or perform poorly when deployed on real hardware, where these physical constraints are unavoidable. The motivation is to bridge this gap by incorporating a higher fidelity plant model into the DRL training environment.
- **Developing More Realistic and Robust Controllers:** By exposing DRL policies to a more realistic actuation pathway—where body-level actions are mapped to per-rotor commands through explicit thrust/torque allocation and first-order motor dynamics, including gyroscopic coupling—the authors aim to train controllers that are inherently more robust and stable under real-world conditions. This "Simulink-in-the-loop" design is intended to preserve control authority and bandwidth constraints typically encountered on actual hardware.
- **Providing a Standardized Benchmark:** The study seeks to offer a reproducible benchmark for quadcopter DRL by evaluating common DRL algorithms (DDPG, TD3, PPO, SAC) on the same physics-aware plant. This controlled comparison aims to highlight the exploration, stability, and sample-efficiency trade-offs of these algorithms when confronted with detailed physical dynamics.
- **Detailing Practical Training Considerations:** The authors are motivated to provide practical training details, including observation design, reward shaping, and termination logic, specifically tailored to actuator-constrained UAVs. This aims to complement existing research on robust and realistic controllers by offering a framework that integrates DRL with a physically informed model.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to move beyond idealized DRL control for UAVs towards a framework that can learn effective policies while being acutely aware of the physical limitations and dynamics of the platform, thereby facilitating better transferability to real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an end-to-end Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) framework integrated with a high-fidelity quadcopter simulator developed in MATLAB/Simulink. The core of the approach lies in its "physics-aware" design, which incorporates detailed models of the quadcopter's dynamics and actuation system.

**A. System Model:**
A 12-state rigid-body quadcopter model is implemented as a Level-2 S-Function within Simulink. The model includes:
- **Frames and ZYX Rotation:** Defines inertial and body-fixed frames, with roll, pitch, and yaw following ZYX Euler angle convention.
- **States, Inputs, Outputs:** The 12-dimensional state vector ($\mathbf{x}$) includes angular rates ($\mathbf{P, Q, R}$), Euler angles ($\phi, \theta, \psi$), linear velocities ($\mathbf{U, V, W}$), and inertial positions ($\mathbf{X, Y, Z}$). The low-level input ($\mathbf{u}$) from the DRL agent consists of total thrust ($T$) and body torques ($\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z$).
- **Kinematics:** Equations mapping Euler-angle rates to body angular rates are included.
- **Forces and Moments:**
 - Thrust: Acts along the body +z-axis, proportional to the sum of squared rotor speeds ($w_i^2$).
 - Rotor Gyroscopic Torques: Accounts for the gyroscopic precession effect caused by rotating motors, depending on motor inertia and angular rates.
 - Total Body Moments: Calculated from rotor speeds through a coefficient matrix ($D$) and augmented by gyroscopic torques and commanded torques.
- **Rigid-Body Dynamics:** Euler's equations for angular dynamics (incorporating inertia and cross-product terms) and Newton's second law for translational dynamics (considering thrust, external disturbances, gravity, and Coriolis forces) are used. Position kinematics are also included.
- **Action-to-RPM Allocation:** The DRL agent outputs a 4-dimensional action vector ($a_T, a_x, a_y, a_z$) that scales the total thrust and body torques.
 - Total thrust ($T$) is derived from a nominal hover thrust ($mg$) and a scaled action component.
 - Body torques ($\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z$) are scaled by maximum torque values.
 - These commanded $T, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z$ are then mapped to individual squared rotor speeds ($w_i^2$) using the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse of a coefficient matrix ($M$). This matrix incorporates thrust and drag coefficients and quadcopter geometry. The resulting $w_i$ values are clipped to enforce minimum and maximum actuator limits.
- **Motor First-Order Dynamics:** Each commanded rotor speed ($w_i$) passes through a first-order lag model (transfer function $1/(T_s s + 1)$) with a time constant $T_s = 0.076 s$. This models the delay between a motor command and the actual rotational speed of the rotor, which then generates forces and moments.

**B. DRL Methodology:**
- **Observation Space:** The agent receives a structured observation vector including position error to the target, Euler angles ($\phi, \theta, \psi$), body-frame linear velocities ($\mathbf{U, V, W}$), body angular rates ($\mathbf{P, Q, R}$), and the previous action (to provide control history).
- **Action Space:** The actions are continuous and directly correspond to the total thrust and body torques ($T, \tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z$), which are then processed by the Action-to-RPM allocation layer and motor dynamics.
- **Reward Design:** A shaped reward function is used to balance goal-reaching and stability:
 - **Goal Reaching:** An exponential positive term based on the Euclidean distance to the target position, switching to a linear penalty if the distance exceeds a threshold.
 - **Stability:** Penalties for large Euler angles (roll, pitch, yaw), and quadratic costs for body-frame linear and angular velocities.
 - **Survival Bonus:** A small positive constant reward for each timestep the agent remains active.
- **Training Pipeline:** The DRL training is implemented in MATLAB/Simulink. The agent interacts with the S-function plant, observations are generated, actions are produced and fed into the physical models, and rewards are collected to update the policy.
- **Algorithms Evaluated:** Four continuous control DRL algorithms are compared: DDPG (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient), TD3 (Twin Delayed DDPG), PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), and SAC (Soft Actor-Critic). All algorithms share identical network structures and hyperparameters (e.g., replay buffer size, mini-batch size, discount factor, learning rate).
- **Staged Learning Protocol:** A two-stage curriculum is employed to progressively increase the complexity of the control task:
 - **Stage 1 (Thrust-only hover):** The agent learns to hover using only thrust control, with roll, pitch, and yaw torques disabled. Initial states are randomized for X and Z positions.
 - **Stage 2 (Thrust plus single-axis torque):** One additional torque channel (pitch torque $\tau_y$) is enabled, and the agent learns to reach a translated goal (e.g., (1, 0, 1)m). Initial positions are randomized around the origin.
- **Hardware and Parallelization:** Training is conducted using MATLAB's Parallel Computing Toolbox with up to 8 workers, utilizing GPUs when available. CPU saturation is limited to reduce non-determinism.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluates four DRL algorithms (DDPG, TD3, PPO, SAC) in a physics-aware quadcopter simulation environment across two stages of increasing complexity.

**A. Stage 1: Thrust-Only Hover**
- **Performance Comparison:** Off-policy DRL baselines (DDPG, TD3, SAC) consistently improved their performance in this stage.
 - **TD3** demonstrated the most consistent and highest training return, exhibiting a near-monotonic increase after approximately 1000 episodes, achieving the highest final reward.
 - **SAC** closely followed TD3, showing slightly slower initial learning but reaching comparable terminal performance. The evaluation rollouts confirmed SAC's strong performance.
 - **DDPG** improved rapidly initially but then displayed pronounced oscillations in its learning curve and converged to a lower asymptotic performance compared to TD3 and SAC.
 - **PPO** proved to be the least sample-efficient, remaining near zero reward throughout the training and showing no significant or persistent improvement within the allocated training budget.
- **General Observation:** The evaluation metrics for noise-free rollouts mirrored the trends observed in the training curves, indicating that the performance gains were not merely artifacts of exploration noise or replay dynamics.

**B. Stage 2: Thrust Plus Single-Axis Torque (Pitch Torque Enabled)**
- **Increased Complexity:** With a translated target and the pitch-torque ($\tau_y$) channel activated, the learning problem became more challenging, evidenced by lower initial returns and a more gradual increase in performance.
- **Performance Comparison:**
 - **TD3** maintained its lead in sample efficiency, demonstrating a steady ascent in cumulative reward throughout the training period.
 - **SAC** showed a more conservative exploration phase initially, consistent with its entropy-regularized approach. However, it accelerated in the latter half of training, eventually achieving performance comparable to, and occasionally surpassing, TD3 during evaluation rollouts.
 - **DDPG** continued to exhibit noisy learning and did not achieve performance levels comparable to the twin-critic methods (TD3 and SAC) in this more complex setting.
 - **PPO** was excluded from Stage 2 due to its poor convergence behavior observed in Stage 1.

**C. Trajectory-Level Assessment (Best SAC Agent from Stage 2)**
- **Goal Reaching:** Analysis of the best-performing SAC agent's trajectories revealed a direct approach toward the target goal.
- **Lateral Stability:** The agent maintained lateral stability, with the Y-position remaining essentially constant, indicating negligible lateral excursion.
- **Vertical and Horizontal Control:** The X-position smoothly advanced towards the 1m target, while the Z-position (altitude) exhibited a bounded overshoot followed by damped oscillations, eventually settling near the 1m reference. A mild corrective phase was observed near arrival for the X-axis.
- **Quantification:** Quantitative metrics further supported these observations, confirming stable and accurate goal attainment.

**D. Overall Implications of Physics-Aware Modeling:**
- A key finding highlighted in the conclusion is that incorporating explicit thrust/torque allocation and first-order rotor/motor dynamics, while enhancing physical fidelity, makes the learning problem *harder*. This increased difficulty is attributed to the introduction of actuator lags and allocation nonlinearities, which increase variance in the learning process and slow down policy improvement compared to environments based on idealized body-level abstractions.
- Despite the increased difficulty, off-policy methods (TD3 and SAC) were found to converge more reliably than DDPG and PPO.

**E. Limitations:**
- The study reports that training remained "brittle," and the authors were unable to obtain a stable Stage-3 policy for fully coupled 3D attitude and position control within the scope of this work.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of UAV control and Deep Reinforcement Learning by integrating a high-fidelity physical model of a quadcopter, including detailed actuator dynamics and thrust/torque allocation, directly into the DRL training loop. This "physics-aware" approach has several significances and potential impacts:

- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The primary significance lies in its effort to bridge the simulation-to-real-world performance gap. By exposing DRL policies to realistic hardware constraints such as actuator lags and allocation nonlinearities, the learned controllers are potentially more robust and transferable to physical quadcopters than those trained in simplified environments. This has direct implications for the practical deployment of DRL-based UAV control systems.
- **Enhanced Realism in DRL Benchmarking:** The study provides a reproducible benchmark for DRL algorithms on a more physically representative quadcopter model. This allows for a more meaningful comparison of algorithms like SAC, TD3, DDPG, and PPO, revealing their strengths and weaknesses in scenarios closer to real-world operations. The finding that physics-aware modeling increases learning difficulty underscores the importance of such realistic benchmarks for evaluating DRL techniques.
- **Guidance for Algorithm Selection:** The empirical comparison, which indicates that off-policy methods like TD3 and SAC exhibit superior stability and exploration efficiency compared to DDPG and PPO in this detailed simulation, offers practical guidance for researchers and practitioners in selecting appropriate DRL algorithms for similar actuator-constrained continuous control problems.
- **Improved Low-Level Control:** By focusing on end-to-end control from body-wrench actions to motor dynamics, the research contributes to the development of more sophisticated low-level controllers. These controllers can manage the inherent underactuated nature of quadcopters while respecting the physical limitations of their propulsion systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework and findings establish a foundation for future work in several areas:
 - **Robust 3D Control:** The identified difficulty in achieving stable Stage-3 policies for fully coupled 3D attitude and position control highlights a clear direction for subsequent research. This could involve exploring richer observation and reward formulations, advanced DRL architectures, or curriculum learning strategies to handle the increased complexity.
 - **Multi-Agent Coordination:** The methodology could be extended to multi-agent systems, where realistic actuator dynamics are crucial for coordinated flight and interaction.
 - **Adaptive and Robust Control:** Policies trained in this physics-aware environment might be more amenable to further fine-tuning or adaptation in real-world settings, potentially reducing the need for extensive real-world data collection.

In conclusion, this work provides a framework for developing DRL controllers that are more attuned to the underlying physics of UAVs. While acknowledging the increased challenge introduced by higher fidelity modeling, the study's findings offer insights into the performance of current DRL algorithms in realistic scenarios and set a valuable precedent for future research aimed at achieving reliable and robust autonomous quadcopter flight.
