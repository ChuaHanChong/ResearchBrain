# MiraBench: Evaluating Action-Conditioned Reliability in Robotic World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> MIRABENCH, a new benchmark from Peking University, evaluates the action-conditioned reliability of robotic world models by assessing physics adherence, action-following fidelity, and optimism bias. The research demonstrates that current world models often generate visually plausible outcomes while failing to accurately simulate physical consequences or preserve failure states, revealing a pervasive optimism bias.

## Problem

- Existing world model evaluation metrics focus primarily on visual quality, which is insufficient for determining their reliability as faithful simulators for robot learning.
- World models can produce visually convincing predictions that nevertheless violate physical laws, disregard commanded actions, or predict success when actions should lead to failure (termed 'Optimism Bias').
- Acquiring large-scale, high-quality interaction data for real-world robotics, especially for rare failures or long-horizon tasks, is resource-intensive and difficult.

## Method

![[2605.29360-method.png]]
*The MIRABENCH framework evaluates world models across three levels: Physical Adherence, Action-Following Fidelity, and Optimism Bias Detection.*

- Developed **MIRABENCH**, a ==hierarchical benchmark== to assess ==action-conditioned reliability== through three levels: ==Physics Adherence==, ==Action-Following Fidelity==, and ==Optimism Bias Detection==.
- Curated a test corpus by creating ==failure-inducing counterfactual action sequences== alongside nominal successful ones for robotic manipulation tasks.
- Constructed ==human-grounded automated evaluators== by leveraging a human-annotated corpus (16,704 decisions) to supervise and validate ==Vision-Language Models (VLMs)== as judges for diagnostic granularity.

## Results

- MIRABENCH exposed a significant discrepancy between models' nominal action-following capabilities and their ability to preserve failure outcomes.
- Scaling models (e.g., DreamDojo from 2B to 14B) sometimes led to decreased failure preservation (e.g., from **48.7%** to **12.8%**) despite improvements in other physical consistency metrics.
- Post-training on success-only data substantially improved nominal task completion (e.g., DreamDojo-2B from **14.0%** to **92.0%**) but concurrently reduced failure preservation (e.g., from **48.7%** to **23.1%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual fidelity is a weak indicator of a world model's reliability as an action-conditioned simulator for robot learning.
> - Current world models exhibit a pervasive 'optimism bias,' often hallucinating successful outcomes even when conditioned on actions designed to cause failure.
> - Increasing model scale or fine-tuning on success-dominated datasets does not consistently improve overall reliability and can exacerbate optimism bias.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied intelligence, particularly in robotics, is experiencing rapid advancements, leading to the deployment of autonomous robots in diverse sectors such as manufacturing, healthcare, and daily assistance. A significant challenge in this domain is the acquisition of large-scale, high-quality interaction data. Real-world data collection for robots is often resource-intensive, time-consuming, and presents difficulties in generalization across varying environments. Furthermore, critical scenarios such as rare failures, long-horizon tasks, and safety-critical interactions are particularly difficult to capture in sufficient quantities.

World models have emerged as a potential solution to this data bottleneck. By learning to predict future observations conditioned on specific actions, these models can function as scalable simulators, generating synthetic trajectories that extend beyond the scope of practically collectible real-world data. This promise has led to an increase in the development of action-conditioned world models for robotic applications, as well as in related fields such as autonomous driving.

However, existing evaluation practices for these world models primarily focus on visual quality metrics, such as Fréchet Video Distance (FVD), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), or general human preference scores. While benchmarks like VBench, WorldSimBench, WorldModelBench, and WorldArena provide foundations for assessing perceptual quality and, in some cases, physical plausibility or instruction following, they often prioritize how generated videos appear rather than their faithfulness to the action-conditioned consequences required of a reliable simulator. Previous research has shown that generative models frequently exhibit physics violations that standard visual metrics fail to detect. Furthermore, model-based reinforcement learning relies heavily on world model fidelity, with poor fidelity often leading to suboptimal policy transfer from simulation to reality.

This paper addresses a critical gap in the existing research landscape by explicitly shifting the evaluation focus from visual realism to "action-conditioned reliability." It highlights that a world model can produce visually plausible futures while simultaneously violating physical laws, disregarding commanded actions, or predicting success even when actions should lead to failure. The latter phenomenon, termed "Optimism Bias," is particularly relevant given that robot learning datasets are often biased towards successful demonstrations, leading models to develop a strong prior for successful task completion that can override contradictory action inputs. MIRABENCH aims to provide a diagnostic framework to systematically evaluate these crucial aspects of world model reliability, distinguishing simulator faithfulness from generic visual fidelity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a comprehensive and hierarchical benchmark, named MIRABENCH, for evaluating the "action-conditioned reliability" of robotic world models. The motivation stems from the observation that current evaluation metrics primarily focus on visual fidelity, which is insufficient for determining whether a world model can function as a trustworthy simulator for robot learning.

The detailed motivations and objectives are as follows:

1. **Address the limitations of appearance-centric evaluations:** Existing benchmarks largely measure the visual quality of generated videos. This approach fails to ascertain if predicted futures are physically plausible, accurately reflect commanded actions, or correctly forecast failure when appropriate. For robot learning, these aspects are considered critical.
2. **Define and operationalize "action-conditioned reliability":** The paper aims to formalize what constitutes a "faithful simulator" by ensuring its rollouts are physically admissible, semantically consistent with specified actions, and calibrated to failure when actions imply such outcomes. This property is termed action-conditioned reliability.
3. **Decompose reliability into a diagnostic hierarchy:** MIRABENCH proposes a three-level hierarchical structure to evaluate reliability, allowing for fine-grained diagnosis of failure modes:
 - **Level 1: Physics Adherence:** This level assesses whether predicted futures maintain physical coherence. It includes sub-components for "Physical Consistency" (e.g., object persistence, coherent motion, identity preservation through occlusion) and "Physics Law Compliance" (e.g., quantitative kinematic validity under gravity or friction). The evaluation at this level is reference-free, meaning physical validity is judged from the generated rollout itself.
 - **Level 2: Action-Following Fidelity:** This level evaluates whether physically plausible rollouts genuinely reflect the conditioning action. It measures "Task Completion Rate" (whether the task goal is achieved), "Object Preservation Score" (whether objects maintain plausible appearance), and "Generalizability" (performance on unseen episodes). This addresses cases where a model might generate a coherent video but complete the wrong task or ignore the intended action.
 - **Level 3: Optimism Bias Detection:** This is a core objective, formalizing and detecting a systematic failure mode where world models hallucinate successful outcomes even when conditioned on failure-inducing actions. This bias is hypothesized to stem from training datasets dominated by successful demonstrations, leading models to develop a strong prior for success. The objective is to quantify how well models preserve failure outcomes.
4. **Develop human-grounded evaluation methodologies:** To move beyond subjective visual preference scores, a key objective is to curate a fine-grained human-annotated corpus. This corpus, consisting of judgments on various aspects of world model outputs, is used to validate and supervise the development of module-specific Vision-Language Model (VLM) evaluators. This approach seeks to combine the diagnostic granularity of human judgment with the scalability of automated evaluation.
5. **Conduct a broad evaluation to reveal systemic gaps:** The research aims to apply MIRABENCH to a diverse set of representative world model configurations to identify systematic patterns and limitations. This includes assessing models of different scales, conditioning modalities (vector vs. text), and access types (open-weight vs. closed-source). The underlying motivation is to demonstrate that appearance-centric evaluation significantly mischaracterizes the actual reliability of current robotic world models as simulators.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MIRABENCH employs a hierarchical evaluation pipeline designed to assess action-conditioned reliability in robotic world models. The methodology encompasses the construction of test cases, the definition of three progressively demanding evaluation levels, and the development of human-grounded automated evaluators.

#### 3.1. Construction of Test Cases

The benchmark is built upon manipulation episodes sourced from two datasets:
- **GR-1 humanoid corpus:** This dataset focuses on contact-rich tasks such as pick-and-place, pouring, and folding, often involving humanoid robots.
- **Lingchu bimanual dataset:** This dataset contributes real-world bimanual manipulation data, including tasks like bimanual handover and sliding.

For each episode, two types of inputs are generated:
- **Nominal inputs:** These consist of an initial observation (first frame) and a successful action sequence (either raw motor commands or natural language task instructions).
- **Failure-inducing counterfactual inputs:** These are created by perturbing a nominal successful action into a physically interpretable failure-inducing action, while keeping the initial scene constant. For descriptive-action models, these failure modes are expressed as natural language instructions. Six perturbation families are defined: insufficient grip force, premature release, carry slip, contact oscillation, wrist tilt, and approach overshoot. These perturbations are designed to necessarily prevent task completion under true physics.

The generated corpus includes 906 videos and 16,704 human annotation decisions across four evaluation modules.

#### 3.2. Hierarchical Evaluation Levels

MIRABENCH decomposes action-conditioned reliability into three levels:

**Level 1: Physics Adherence**
This level evaluates the physical meaningfulness of a generated rollout independently of action fidelity. It comprises two modules:
- **Physical Consistency:** Measures whether manipulated objects remain coherent over time, assessing aspects like shape, material, size, color, and identity preservation through occlusion. This is scored by presenting paired frames to a VLM (InternVL3-78B) which judges consistency on object attributes and identity, with a penalty for any inconsistency.
- **Physics Law Compliance:** Targets videos that appear coherent but contain implausible motion. For object-motion episodes, object trajectories are extracted using SAM2.1 and analyzed for simple kinematic cues such as uniformly accelerated motion, impact behavior, post-contact drift, and bounce decay. A 10% VLM-based video quality score component is included to filter out rollouts lacking scorable translational motion. The kinematic scoring involves fitting degree-2 polynomials to motion segments and evaluating factors like acceleration sign, magnitude, and uniformity. This component does not use LLM for judgment within the kinematic computation itself.

**Level 2: Action-Following Fidelity**
This level assesses whether a physically plausible rollout accurately reflects the conditioning action and task intent. It evaluates three dimensions:
- **Task Completion Rate (TCR):** A VLM (InternVL3-78B) judges if the robot successfully completes the task instruction by observing 16 uniformly sampled frames of the predicted video, without ground-truth reference frames.
- **Object Preservation Score (OPS):** A VLM (InternVL3-78B) evaluates whether objects maintain physically plausible appearance throughout the predicted video, checking for distortion, disappearance, or unnatural pop-in/out events. This is done by comparing 16 temporally aligned frame pairs from the predicted and ground-truth videos.
- **Generalizability (GEN):** Measures the gap between TCR performance on in-distribution (GR1) and out-of-distribution (unseen) episodes, quantified by an exponential decay function.

**Level 3: Optimism Bias Detection**
This level specifically probes whether a model preserves failure outcomes when conditioned on failure-inducing actions.
- Six perturbation families are used (insufficient grip, premature release, etc.). For each episode, a nominal rollout and a counterfactual (perturbed) rollout are generated.
- A VLM (InternVL3-78B) acts as an automated judge, comparing the late-phase frames (81-97% progress) of the baseline and perturbed videos. The VLM determines if the manipulated object is in the "same general location/state" in both videos, indicating whether the model ignored the perturbation. A majority vote across 7 sampled frames determines the final judgment. Different prompt variants are used for vector-conditioned and text-conditioned models to account for rendering style differences.

#### 3.3. Human-Grounded Evaluator Construction

A key aspect of MIRABENCH is its reliance on human annotations to supervise and validate its automated evaluators.
- **Annotation Corpus:** A corpus of 906 generated videos was curated with 16,704 structured human annotation decisions. These decisions span 16 indicators for physical consistency, a single overall physics-law grade with 10 anomaly tracks, structured action-following judgments, and 18 optimism-bias diagnostic questions.
- **VLM Evaluator Validation:** Frontier-scale VLMs, specifically InternVL3-78B, are deployed as zero-shot judges. Their outputs are stabilized through multi-frame voting (e.g., 20 evenly spaced frames for Physical Consistency, 16 for Action-Following, 7 late-phase frames for Optimism Bias) and structured prompting designed to elicit specific judgments. The evaluators achieved agreement above 85% with human annotations on held-out expert-labeled samples.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 12 representative world model configurations, encompassing both vector-conditioned and text-conditioned generative models from various research and industrial groups, yielded several consistent patterns:

1. **Visual Fidelity is a Weak Proxy for Action Fidelity:**
 - MIRABENCH revealed a notable discrepancy between a model's performance on nominal actions (action-following) and its reliability under failure-inducing actions (failure preservation).
 - For instance, DreamDojo-GR1-14B exhibited high task completion and object preservation scores, yet its failure-preservation score was among the lowest. Similarly, Happy Horse, which performed strongly on physics adherence and action following, showed only moderate failure preservation.
 - This indicates that models can produce visually plausible and seemingly successful rollouts without accurately representing the causal relationship between actions and outcomes, particularly when actions should lead to failure.

2. **Increasing Model Scale Does Not Reliably Improve Action Following or Failure Preservation:**
 - Comparisons within model families, such as DreamDojo (2B vs. 14B) and Cosmos-GR1 (2B vs. 14B), showed inconsistent improvements with scale.
 - While some physical consistency metrics improved with scaling in DreamDojo, failure preservation notably decreased from 48.7% to 12.8% when scaling from 2B to 14B parameters.
 - DreamDojo-GR1 showed nearly unchanged nominal action-following performance with increased scale, while failure preservation again decreased.
 - Additional fine-tuning steps (e.g., Cosmos-GR1 6000 vs. 8000 steps) also produced non-monotonic changes across benchmark levels, suggesting that more training or larger capacity alone may not resolve these fundamental reliability issues.

3. **Success-Only Post-Training Enhances Nominal Task Execution but Weakens Failure Preservation:**
 - Post-training on success-dominated data (e.g., GR1 post-training) substantially improved nominal action-following capabilities. For DreamDojo-2B, task completion increased from 14.0% to 92.0% and object preservation from 90.0% to 98.0% after post-training.
 - However, this improvement in nominal performance often came at the cost of failure preservation, which dropped from 48.7% to 23.1% for DreamDojo-2B after post-training.
 - This finding suggests that training primarily on successful demonstrations reinforces a model's success prior, making it more prone to optimism bias when confronted with actions intended to cause failure.

4. **Optimism Bias is Pervasive Across Current Systems:**
 - A high prevalence of optimism bias was observed across the evaluated models. Many models exhibited a tendency to predict successful outcomes even when conditioned on failure-inducing actions.
 - Different model families and conditioning modalities displayed distinct failure profiles. Some text-conditioned models (e.g., Wan2.1) achieved stronger failure preservation, while some vector-conditioned models excelled in nominal action-following.
 - No single evaluated model consistently dominated across all three levels of reliability (Physics Adherence, Action-Following Fidelity, and Failure Preservation). For instance, Wan2.1 showed the strongest failure preservation but was not uniformly strong in physics or nominal action-following, while Happy Horse performed well on physics and action-following but had a gap in failure preservation.
 - This heterogeneity underscores the necessity of MIRABENCH's hierarchical design for diagnosing specific types of failures rather than relying on a single aggregate score.

These findings collectively indicate that the ability to generate visually appealing videos does not equate to learning a faithful action-conditioned simulator. Current world models struggle with maintaining action-outcome contingency, particularly in failure scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of MIRABENCH represents a shift in the evaluation paradigm for robotic world models, moving the focus from superficial visual plausibility to the more fundamental aspect of simulator faithfulness and action-conditioned reliability. This work offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Diagnostic Foundation for World Model Development:** By decomposing reliability into Physics Adherence, Action-Following Fidelity, and Optimism Bias Detection, MIRABENCH provides a diagnostic framework. This hierarchical structure enables researchers to pinpoint the specific level at which a world model fails (e.g., physical incoherence, action insensitivity, or success-biased prediction). This diagnostic capability can guide targeted improvements, moving beyond general scaling or optimization efforts that may not address core reliability issues.
2. **Addressing "Optimism Bias":** The formalization and measurement of Optimism Bias highlight a critical and previously under-characterized failure mode. World models' tendency to hallucinate success under failure-inducing actions poses a significant risk for robot learning, as policies trained on such models may operate with an unrealistic understanding of failure contingencies in the real world. MIRABENCH provides a tool to identify and quantify this bias, prompting the development of mitigation strategies.
3. **Informing Training Data and Objectives:** The findings suggest that current training regimes, often dominated by successful demonstrations, contribute to optimism bias. This implies a need for failure-aware training data, novel learning objectives that contrast nominal and failure-inducing actions, and potentially physics-informed modeling approaches to explicitly enforce physical priors and rule out impossible trajectories. MIRABENCH can serve as a testbed for evaluating the efficacy of such interventions.
4. **Enhancing Trustworthiness and Safety in Embodied AI:** Reliable world models are crucial for developing safer and more robust embodied intelligent agents. By ensuring that world models accurately simulate the consequences of actions, including failures, MIRABENCH contributes to building robot learning systems that are better calibrated to real-world complexities and risks. This could lead to a reduction in unexpected behaviors or accidents in safety-critical robotic applications.
5. **Community Resource and Reproducibility:** The release of the MIRABENCH evaluation toolkit, the human-annotated corpus (906 videos, 16,704 structured decisions), pre-generated evaluation videos, and post-training scripts provides a valuable open resource for the research community. This standardizes evaluation, lowers the barrier for benchmarking new models, and promotes transparency and reproducibility in assessing world model capabilities.
6. **Broader Applicability:** While currently focused on tabletop manipulation and short-horizon contact dynamics, the principles of action-conditioned reliability and hierarchical evaluation articulated by MIRABENCH could be extended to other embodied domains, such as navigation, locomotion, deformable object interaction, and long-horizon planning. Future work could expand the perturbation taxonomy and explore more advanced methods for generating failure demonstrations.

In summary, MIRABENCH is significant for providing a foundational framework to build more faithful and reliable world models, which are essential for advancing robot learning and ensuring the safe and effective deployment of embodied AI systems.
