# Towards Proactive Safe Human-Robot Collaborations via Data-Efficient Conditional Behavior Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed a data-efficient, model-based Conditional Behavior Prediction (CBP) framework for proactive human-robot collaboration, which enables robots to adaptively assist and ensure long-term safety without requiring extensive interaction data. This approach achieved comparable performance to data-intensive baselines in simulations and reduced human hesitation in user studies.

## Problem

- Existing Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) models often treat the robot as a passive observer, failing to account for how a robot's actions influence human behavior.
- Current Conditional Behavior Prediction (CBP) models, which consider robot influence, typically require vast datasets of human-robot interactions, making them impractical for data-scarce HRC domains.
- Ensuring long-term probabilistic safety in HRC is challenging when human intentions are uncertain and can be dynamically influenced by robot actions.

## Method

- A model-based **CBP** framework that decouples prior and posterior human goals, utilizing a hand-designed score function to estimate human goal shifts conditioned on robot actions, bypassing the need for large training datasets.
- A multi-stage robot objective that adaptively switches between 'courtesy' (minimizing interference with clear human intent) and 'influence' (guiding uncertain humans toward efficient team strategies) based on detected human uncertainty.
- Integration of the **CBP** model with a long-term probabilistic safe control strategy that accounts for uncertain and influenced human intentions to generate trajectories maintaining a high probability of safety over time.

## Results

- In simulations, the model-based proactive robot demonstrated comparable performance to a data-intensive learning-based baseline, causing human goal changes in **18%** of rollouts (vs. **17%**) and achieving 7.5 goals (vs. 7.7).
- A user study showed participants hesitated significantly less with the proactive robot (0.87 seconds) compared to a reactive robot (1.2 seconds) and preferred the proactive robot (**47%** ranked as most preferred).
- The framework maintained an empirical safety probability of **99.96%** across user interactions, adhering to the theoretical long-term safety guarantees even with human uncertainty.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling human intention adaptation through a score function allows for data-efficient Conditional Behavior Prediction, bridging the gap for HRC in data-sparse environments.
> - Adaptive collaborative strategies that dynamically adjust between courteous and influential behaviors based on human certainty can improve team fluency and efficiency.
> - Long-term probabilistic safety in HRC can be robustly achieved by incorporating the robot's understanding of its own influence on human behavior into the safety controller.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of robots into industrial, social, and home environments necessitates seamless and proactive human-robot collaboration (HRC). A significant challenge in HRC is the closed-loop nature of interactions, where a robot's actions can influence human behavior, and vice versa. Understanding and accounting for this mutual influence is crucial for efficient and safe interactions.

Existing approaches to HRI often rely on observational models, which treat the robot as a passive observer of human behavior. These models typically predict human intentions without considering how the robot's own decisions might alter those intentions, potentially leading to inefficient or suboptimal interactions. Game-theoretic methods attempt to address this influence but can be constrained by assumptions about hierarchical information structures or may not accurately predict human decision-making.

A more recent development, particularly in autonomous driving and pedestrian prediction, is Conditional Behavior Prediction (CBP). CBP models predict an agent's future behavior conditioned on the ego agent's planned actions. However, current CBP models are predominantly data-driven and require extensive datasets of human-human or human-robot interactions. Such large datasets are frequently unavailable in many HRC domains, such as flexible industrial manufacturing or home robotics, due to the diversity of tasks and environments. This data scarcity limits the direct applicability of existing CBP techniques to broader HRI contexts.

This paper addresses the gap by developing a data-efficient, model-based CBP approach for HRC. It aims to enable robots to reason about the closed-loop nature of human-robot collaboration in environments where vast amounts of interaction data are not available, thereby extending the utility of CBP to a wider range of HRI applications. Furthermore, the work integrates this predictive capability with objectives for adaptive collaboration and mechanisms for long-term safety, moving beyond reactive safety measures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to collaborate proactively and safely with human partners, particularly in environments characterized by data scarcity. The work is motivated by several key challenges in current human-robot interaction paradigms:

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Conditional Behavior Prediction**: Many current methods for predicting human behavior, especially those accounting for robot influence (Conditional Behavior Prediction), rely on large datasets. These datasets are often impractical or impossible to collect in new or specific HRC domains (e.g., manufacturing, home assistance) where task variations are high. The authors aim to develop a **model-based CBP formulation** that allows robots to infer human intentions, conditioned on the robot's future plan, without requiring extensive pre-existing data. This addresses a fundamental limitation for deploying proactive robots in novel scenarios.

2. **Enabling Adaptive Proactive Collaboration (Courtesy and Influence)**: A robot should not passively react to a human but should also be able to proactively assist or influence the human. However, the manner of influence needs to be context-dependent. Sometimes, a robot should be "courteous" and avoid interfering with a human's clear intent, while at other times, it should "influence" a human, particularly when the human is uncertain, to guide them towards a more efficient team strategy. The research aims to design an **integrated framework for robot goal selection** that utilizes the proposed CBP model to adaptively switch between courteous and influential behaviors, thereby improving collaboration efficiency and fluency.

3. **Ensuring Long-Term Probabilistic Safety**: In collaborative settings, robot safety is paramount. Traditional safety mechanisms often focus on short-term collision avoidance or reactive responses. However, for seamless and proactive HRC, robots need to ensure safety over a longer planning horizon, especially when human intentions are uncertain and subject to robot influence. The objective is to demonstrate how the developed CBP model can be incorporated into a **long-term probabilistic safe control strategy**, allowing the robot to generate trajectories that maintain a high probability of safety while accounting for the dynamic and influenced nature of human behavior.

In essence, the paper seeks to bridge the gap between sophisticated human behavior prediction models and their practical application in data-sparse HRI environments, while simultaneously enabling adaptive collaborative strategies and assuring long-term safety.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a framework for proactive and safe human-robot collaboration based on a data-efficient, model-based Conditional Behavior Prediction (CBP) model.

**HRI System Formulation:**
The system comprises one robot and one human, sharing a predefined set of goals (Θ). The human's dynamics are noisy and dependent on both agents' previous states and the human's true, but unknown, goal (`θ*_H`). The robot's dynamics are deterministic. Both agents are modeled as double-integrator LTI systems. The human's control policy is inspired by social force models, exhibiting attraction to their goal and repulsion from the robot. The robot's objective is to minimize a task-specific cost function `J` while adhering to a probabilistic safety constraint: the probability of collision with the human must remain below a small threshold `ϵ` over a given trajectory horizon `H`.

**Model-Based Conditional Behavior Prediction (CBP):**
The core innovation is a model-based CBP that decouples the human's *prior* goal (`θ_prior_H`), inferred from observational Bayesian inference, from their *posterior* goal (`θ_post_H`), which accounts for the robot's planned actions.
1. **Observational Inference:** The robot first computes a belief `b_t_R(θ_prior_H)` over the human's goal based on past observations of human and robot states and human actions.
2. **Conditional Inference:** To account for the robot's influence, the robot estimates `p(θ_post_H | o_0:t, θ_R)`, the probability of the human's posterior goal given past observations and the robot's *future goal* (`θ_R`). This is achieved by marginalizing over the prior goal:
 `p(θ_post_H | o_0:t, θ_R) = Σ_θ_prior_H p(θ_post_H | θ_prior_H, o_0:t, θ_R)p(θ_prior_H | o_0:t)`.
3. **Score Function for Influence:** The crucial term `p(θ_post_H | θ_prior_H, o_0:t, θ_R)` is modeled using a softmax distribution whose parameters are derived from a hand-designed *score function* `s(θ_H, θ_R; o_0:t, θ_prior_H)`. This score function incorporates factors such as the human's proximity to a goal, the distance between the human's and robot's goals, and the consistency between the human's potential new goal and their prior goal. This explicit modeling, rather than learning from large datasets, enables data-efficient operation.

**Robot's Multi-Stage Objective (Courtesy and Influence):**
The robot's objective function `J(·)` adaptively switches between two modes:
1. **Detecting Human Uncertainty:** The robot maintains a joint belief over the human's prior intention and its own model confidence parameter (`β_R`). If the peak probability for `β_R` across all human goals is below a threshold `δ`, the human is deemed uncertain about their intention.
2. **Courtesy Objective (`J_c`):** When the human is *not* uncertain, the robot acts courteously. Instead of minimizing KL-divergence (which empirically led to unstable goal selection), the objective is to maximize the probability that the human maintains their most likely *prior* intention (`^θ_H`). This is achieved by minimizing `J_c(θ_R) = -b_t+1_R(θ_post_H = ^θ_H | θ_R)`.
3. **Influence Objective (`J_i`):** When the human *is* uncertain, the robot aims to influence them towards an efficient team strategy. The objective is to minimize the sum of distances the robot and human would travel to their respective goals, where the human's goal is the most likely *posterior* goal influenced by the robot's choice: `J_i(θ_R) = ||x_t_R - θ_R|| + ||x_t_H - ^θ_H||`.
The overall objective `J(·)` then switches between `J_i` and `J_c` based on the detected human uncertainty.

**Safe Trajectory Generation:**
The CBP model is integrated with a long-term probabilistic safe control strategy to ensure `p(x_t ∈ Z, ∀t = 1..., H) ≥ 1 − ϵ`.
1. **Safety Definition:** Safety is defined as maintaining a minimum distance `d_min` between the human and robot, represented by a barrier function.
2. **Integrating CBP:** The robot maintains a mental model of the human's inference about the robot's goal. Combining this with its own CBP belief, the robot computes an overall probability distribution `p(θ_post_H)` for the human's posterior goal.
3. **Probabilistic Safe Controller:** This `p(θ_post_H)` distribution is fed into an existing long-term safe controller that samples from the uncertain human intention to generate safe trajectories. The controller uses potential field methods to repel the robot from potential collision regions, considering both the human's possible movements under different goals and synthetic obstacles to diversify trajectories. This ensures that the robot's low-level actions respect the long-term safety constraint, accounting for the dynamic nature of human intention.

**Simulation Environment and Baselines:**
The methodology was evaluated in a 2D multi-goal task. The score function weights for the model-based CBP were fixed. Two simulated human behaviors were used: "Uncertain Human" (infers robot's goal and avoids it) and "Stubborn Human" (always pursues the closest goal). Baseline robot controllers included a "Naive Robot" (chooses closest goal), a "Reactive Robot" (chooses closest goal not inferred as human's), and a "Proactive-NN Robot" (a learning-based CBP model trained on 3.5 million data points, serving as a data-intensive benchmark).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research findings are presented through simulations with synthetic humans and a user study with real participants.

**Simulation Results:**
Simulations involved 100 games where the human was randomly either "uncertain" or "stubborn," unknown to the robot.
- **Courtesy:** The proactive controllers (Proactive-NN and Proactive-Model) demonstrated higher courtesy, resulting in fewer human goal changes. The Proactive-NN robot caused humans to change their goal in 17% of rollouts (average 0.27 changes per trajectory), while the Proactive-Model robot yielded similar results at 18% (average 0.31 changes). This compares to 61% (0.8 changes) for the Naive robot and 42% (0.7 changes) for the Reactive robot. The Proactive-Model performed comparably to the data-intensive Proactive-NN, indicating its data-efficiency.
- **Flexibility and Efficiency:** Proactive models achieved the highest average number of goals reached by the team. Proactive-NN reached 7.7 goals, and Proactive-Model reached 7.5 goals, showcasing their adaptability to different human behaviors. The Reactive robot achieved 5.6 goals, and the Naive robot achieved 5.44 goals. Notably, the Naive robot performed well with the "uncertain" human, possibly because its immediate commitment allowed for quick human responses.
- **Long-Term Safety:** The integration of the CBP model with the long-term safe controller maintained a safety probability above 90% (for a desired 1-ϵ=0.9), while a nominal goal-seeking controller without this safety modification failed to do so.

**User Study Results:**
An IRB-approved study involved 21 participants interacting with the Naive, Reactive, and Proactive-Model robots in a 2D diamond collection task.
- **Objective Measures:**
 - **Goals Reached:** Participants achieved the highest number of goals with the Naive robot (18.9), followed by the Proactive-Model (17.1), and then the Reactive robot (15.2). Statistical analysis indicated significant differences between all robot types (p < 0.0001). The Naive robot's performance was attributed to users adapting to its predictable, immediate goal selection.
 - **Hesitation Time:** Users hesitated significantly less with the Proactive-Model robot (0.87 seconds) compared to the Reactive robot (1.2 seconds) (p = 0.0004). Hesitation with the Naive robot (0.95 seconds) was also lower than with the Reactive robot (p = 0.004). This suggests the proactive robot reduced human cognitive load in goal selection.
 - **Empirical Safety:** Over 37,800 timesteps across all user interactions, there were 13 collisions, resulting in an empirical safety probability of 99.96%, which aligns with the theoretical guarantees.
- **Subjective Measures:**
 - **Accounted for Goals:** Participants rated that both Proactive (3.7/5) and Reactive (4.0/5) robots accounted more for their desired goals than the Naive robot (2.1/5) (p < 0.0001).
 - **Changed Initial Goals:** Participants reported changing their goals less with the Reactive (1.7/5) and Proactive (2.7/5) robots compared to the Naive robot (3.7/5) (p < 0.0001).
 - **Overall Preference:** When forced to rank the robots, a plurality of participants (47%) ranked the Proactive-Model robot as their most preferred interaction partner, followed by the Reactive (33%) and Naive (19%) robots. This indicated a preference for the proactive controller despite the Naive robot sometimes leading to higher objective performance. Subjective ratings on other questions, such as "scored as many points as we could," sometimes favored the Reactive robot despite its lower objective performance, suggesting that factors like perceived responsiveness might influence subjective preferences more than raw task efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of human-robot interaction by introducing a data-efficient model-based Conditional Behavior Prediction (CBP) framework. This framework addresses a critical limitation of existing CBP approaches, which often require extensive datasets unavailable in many practical HRC domains.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:
- **Enabling Data-Efficient Proactive HRC:** The model-based CBP formulation demonstrates performance comparable to data-intensive learning-based models in simulations. This indicates that proactive human-robot collaboration, where robots anticipate and influence human behavior, can be realized even in data-sparse environments common in industrial manufacturing or domestic robotics. This broadens the applicability of advanced HRI techniques.
- **Improving Collaborative Fluency and Efficiency:** The proposed multi-stage objective, which allows robots to dynamically switch between courteous and influential behaviors based on human certainty, enhances the fluidity of interactions. Simulation results indicate improved team efficiency (more goals reached) and reduced human goal changes when using the proactive controllers.
- **Reducing Human Cognitive Load:** The user study showed that the proactive robot led to significantly less human hesitation compared to the reactive robot. This suggests that the robot's ability to account for and adapt to human intent can reduce the mental effort required from human partners, potentially leading to a more natural and less taxing collaborative experience.
- **Assuring Long-Term Probabilistic Safety:** By integrating the CBP model with a long-term probabilistic safe controller, the research demonstrates a method for maintaining safety guarantees over an extended horizon, even with uncertainty in human intentions. This moves beyond reactive, short-term safety measures, which is crucial for building trust and enabling continuous, unconstrained human-robot collaboration in shared workspaces.
- **Insights into Human Perception of Robots:** The user study provided valuable insights into human preferences and perceptions. While the Naive robot sometimes yielded higher objective performance, the proactive robot was generally preferred by participants. This highlights that factors beyond raw task efficiency, such as perceived understanding, responsiveness, and reduced cognitive burden, play a significant role in user satisfaction and acceptance of collaborative robots.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as adapting the score function weights online, suggests avenues for further refining the model's performance and adaptability to individual human partners, potentially retaining the data-efficiency benefits while further increasing real-world collaboration effectiveness. Overall, this work advances the development of more intelligent, adaptive, and safe robots for a wider range of collaborative applications.
