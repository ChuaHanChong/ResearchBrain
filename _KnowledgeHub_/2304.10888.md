# Learning Robust, Agile, Natural Legged Locomotion Skills in the Wild

> [!summary] Summary
> The framework from Tsinghua University and the Shanghai Qi Zhi Institute enables quadrupedal robots to acquire robust, agile, and natural locomotion capabilities for diverse, challenging real-world terrains. It integrates sim-to-real reinforcement learning with adversarial motion priors, allowing robots to perform complex gaits like galloping using only proprioceptive sensing.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning for legged robots often produces robust but unnatural and conservative gaits, requiring laborious manual reward engineering to achieve fluidity.
- Existing Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) methods generate natural, animal-like gaits but are limited to flat, controlled environments and lack robustness for challenging terrains.
- Legged robots need to reliably and efficiently navigate diverse, unpredictable real-world environments while maintaining natural, agile movements, a capability current methods struggle to achieve simultaneously.

## Method

- A teacher policy is trained in simulation using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with both proprioceptive and privileged information, incorporating an adversarial training branch for motion style matching with real animal data.
- A student predictor is trained to infer the teacher's latent privileged information from historical proprioceptive observations, enabling real-world deployment without direct access to privileged data.
- Sim-to-real transfer is enhanced through extensive domain randomization, a progressive terrain curriculum, and an action filtering mechanism applied to the policy's output actions.

## Results

- The method achieved significantly higher success rates, longer time-to-fall, and better command tracking across challenging simulated terrains (e.g., stairs, discrete obstacles) compared to baseline methods.
- Real-world experiments on a Unitree A1 robot demonstrated superior performance on diverse, untrained terrains, including slippery surfaces, 8-cm high stairs, and 30-degree slopes, exhibiting dynamic gait adaptation.
- The robot successfully performed dynamic gaits, including galloping, in unstructured outdoor environments, marking the first learning-based demonstration of a quadrupedal robot 'galloping in the wild.'

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining a task reward with a style reward from an adversarial motion prior effectively guides reinforcement learning to produce both robust and natural gaits for challenging terrains.
> - Privileged information, unavailable in the real world, can be effectively inferred from historical proprioceptive observations by a trained predictor, facilitating robust sim-to-real transfer.
> - Comprehensive domain randomization, an adaptive terrain curriculum, and a low-pass action filter are critical components for achieving stable learning, high robustness, and smooth, energy-efficient locomotion on real-world, diverse terrains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robot locomotion has seen significant advancements, traditionally relying on model-based control approaches. While these methods have achieved success, they often require extensive human engineering and struggle with adaptation to complex, unstructured environments. The emergence of reinforcement learning (RL) has revolutionized this area, offering a paradigm where control policies are trained in simulation to optimize task-specific rewards and then transferred to real robots. This approach has led to impressive results in terms of robustness, enabling robots to traverse challenging terrains, recover from unhealthy states, and navigate complex environments, as exemplified by works like those by Lee et al. (2020) and Kumar et al. (2021).

However, a persistent challenge in RL-based locomotion is that policies optimized purely for task rewards often result in gaits that are overly conservative, unnatural, and energy-inefficient. Achieving natural and agile movements typically involves laborious reward engineering, which remains a significant hurdle. To address the challenge of naturalness, the concept of Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), introduced by Peng et al. (2021), has gained traction. AMP leverages real animal motion capture data and adversarial imitation learning to guide policy training, yielding highly natural and stylized movements. Escontrela et al. (2022) successfully demonstrated the transfer of AMP-learned policies to real quadrupedal robots.

The key limitation of existing AMP methods, as identified by the authors, is their confinement to traversing relatively flat, laboratory-like environments. They lack the inherent robustness required to handle challenging terrains such as stairs, rocky ground, or slippery surfaces. This paper addresses a critical gap by combining the strengths of both paradigms: the robustness and adaptability of sim-to-real reinforcement learning on challenging terrains with the naturalness and agility imparted by adversarial motion priors. It seeks to develop a unified framework that overcomes the limitations of previous AMP approaches while simultaneously mitigating the unnaturalness of purely task-driven RL policies. Furthermore, the paper claims to be the first learning-based method to enable quadrupedal robots to "gallop in the wild," pushing the boundaries of high-speed, dynamic locomotion in unstructured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a novel framework that enables quadrupedal robots to learn locomotion skills that are **robust, agile, and natural** when traversing **challenging terrains in real-world, uncontrolled environments (the "wild")**.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several identified limitations in current legged locomotion research:
- **Unnatural and Conservative Gaits:** While modern reinforcement learning methods have produced robust robot controllers, the resulting gaits are often stiff, conservative, and lack the fluidity and efficiency observed in real animals. This is a common consequence of optimizing for task completion without explicit consideration for motion aesthetics or biomechanical naturalness.
- **Laborious Reward Engineering:** Crafting complex reward functions to encourage natural and agile gaits is a notoriously difficult and time-consuming process. Minor adjustments can significantly alter behavior, and the process often requires extensive trial and error, making it less scalable for diverse movement patterns.
- **Limitations of Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP):** Although AMP has shown great promise in generating natural, animal-like gaits by leveraging motion capture data, its current implementations have been restricted to relatively benign, flat terrains. Extending these natural gaits to unstructured, challenging environments remains an unsolved problem, as directly combining AMP with challenging terrain training often leads to suboptimal performance even in simulation.
- **Need for "Wild" Locomotion:** For legged robots to be truly useful in real-world applications (e.g., search and rescue, exploration, delivery), they must be able to reliably and efficiently navigate diverse, unpredictable, and challenging terrains outside of laboratory settings. This necessitates controllers that are not only robust to environmental uncertainties but also adaptable and energy-efficient.
- **Enabling Advanced Gaits:** The ability to execute a diverse repertoire of gaits, including high-speed and dynamic patterns like galloping, is crucial for versatile and efficient locomotion. Learning such complex gaits, especially in real-world challenging environments, represents a significant step towards more advanced and biologically inspired robotic movement.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to advance the state of the art in legged locomotion, enabling robots to move with a level of agility, naturalness, and robustness previously unattainable in complex, real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework combines a teacher-student training paradigm with an adversarial training branch, augmented by several techniques to enhance sim-to-real transfer. The approach can be broken down into two main phases:

**Phase 1: Teacher Policy Training with Adversarial Motion Style Matching**
1. **Teacher-Student Training Framework:** Inspired by prior work, the teacher policy is trained in simulation with access to both **proprioceptive observations** (e.g., joint positions, velocities, projected gravity, foot contact states, last actions) and **privileged information** about the environment and robot dynamics (e.g., base velocity, friction coefficient, measured terrain heights, external torque, added mass, contact forces, motor gains). This privileged information allows the teacher to achieve high performance in challenging simulated environments. The privileged information is encoded into a latent vector `l_t` by an encoder `μ(x_t)`, which is then concatenated with the proprioceptive observation `O_t` to form the full state `s_t = (l_t, O_t)`. A base policy `π` maps this state to actions `a_t`.
2. **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used as the backbone RL algorithm to optimize the teacher policy.
3. **Adversarial Motion Style Matching:** To achieve natural and agile gaits, an adversarial training branch is incorporated simultaneously with the teacher policy training.
 - **Hybrid Reward Function:** The overall reward `r_t` for the RL agent is a weighted sum of a **task reward** (`r_g_t`) and a **style reward** (`r_s_t`). The task reward incentivizes tracking of linear and angular velocity commands, defined by an exponential function to penalize deviations. The style reward is crucial for naturalness.
 - **Discriminator for Style Reward:** A discriminator `D_ϕ` is trained to distinguish between state transition samples from a real animal motion capture dataset and those generated by the robot policy. The discriminator is optimized to output 1 for real motion data and -1 for policy-generated data, incorporating a gradient penalty for training stability.
 - **Policy as Generator:** The robot policy acts as a generator, trained via RL to maximize the style reward, effectively tricking the discriminator into believing its movements are from the real animal dataset, thus encouraging natural gaits.
 - **Motion Capture Data:** High-quality dog motion capture data (Zhang et al., 2018) is used as reference. This data is retargeted to the robot's morphology using inverse kinematics (similar to Peng et al., 2020) and augmented by mirroring to enhance diversity, particularly for learning galloping gaits.

**Phase 2: Student Policy Training for Real-World Deployment**
1. **Predictor Training:** Since privileged information is not available on a real robot, a separate encoder, called the "predictor" (`ˆμ`), is trained. This predictor takes a sequence of historical proprioceptive observations (`O_t-T..., O_t`) as input and is trained via supervised learning to predict the latent vector `l_t` that the teacher policy used. The objective is to minimize the squared error `||ˆl_t - l_t||^2`. This leverages the partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) assumption that unobservable states can be inferred from historical observations.
2. **Real-World Deployment:** The trained predictor `ˆμ` infers `ˆl_t` from sensor history, and this `ˆl_t` is then fed, along with the current proprioceptive observation `O_t`, to the *same* base policy `π` that was trained in Phase 1. The resulting actions are directly applied to the real robot's actuators.

**Enhancing Sim-to-Real Transfer (Applied during Training):**
1. **Domain Randomization:** Critical for bridging the reality gap, this involves randomizing various parameters during simulation training:
 - **Robot Dynamics:** Mass, motor gains, etc.
 - **Environment Dynamics:** Friction coefficients.
 - **Perturbations:** Linear/angular velocity perturbations, actuator torque disturbances.
 - **Sensor Noise:** Added to observations to simulate real-world sensor imperfections.
2. **Terrain Curriculum:** To enable generalization to diverse and challenging terrains, the training environment dynamically adjusts terrain difficulty. The robot starts on simpler terrains and progresses to more complex ones (plane, uniform noise, discrete obstacles, stairs) once it achieves a satisfactory task reward (successful crossing, high velocity tracking). If performance drops, the robot is reset to easier terrains, ensuring stable learning.
3. **Action Filtering:** A low-pass filter is applied to the output actions of the neural network policy (`u_t = 0.2 * u_t-1 + 0.8 * a_t`). This empirically observed technique significantly improves motion smoothness and contributes to better sim-to-real transfer and energy efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed framework was rigorously evaluated through extensive experiments on both simulation and a real quadruped robot, the Unitree A1. The controllers were compared against two baselines:
- **Complex rewards:** A policy trained with typical model-free reinforcement learning using a complex, hand-designed reward function, akin to methods in Rudin et al. (2022).
- **AMP:** A policy trained using adversarial motion priors for style reward, similar to Escontrela et al. (2022), adapted by the authors for comparison on diverse terrains.

**Simulation Experiments:**
Experiments were conducted using the Isaac Gym simulator across three challenging terrain types: continuous stairs, discrete obstacles (randomly placed obstacles or pits), and uniform noise (uneven ground). Key metrics included success rate (no fall), time to fall (TTF), command tracking accuracy, and energy efficiency (average motor power).

- **Robustness:** The proposed method demonstrated significantly higher success rates and TTF values across all challenging terrains compared to baselines. Notably, the AMP baseline failed entirely on stairs and discrete obstacles, while the Complex Rewards baseline also struggled considerably on these terrains. This highlights the framework's superior ability to robustly navigate highly complex environments.
- **Agility and Naturalness:** The controller achieved better command tracking accuracy, indicating more agile responses to velocity commands. Qualitatively, the learned policy could smoothly transition between diverse gait patterns (pace, trot, gallop) depending on velocity commands and terrain type (Figure 4). The paper explicitly claims this is the first learning-based method to enable a quadrupedal robot to gallop in the wild.
- **Energy Efficiency:** The proposed method exhibited better energy efficiency, particularly at higher speeds on uniform noise terrain, compared to baselines. An ablation study confirmed that the low-pass action filter significantly improved energy efficiency, showcasing the importance of motion smoothness.

**Real-World Experiments:**
Real-world deployments on the Unitree A1 robot were conducted on even more challenging and diverse terrains, including a 13-cm high floating barrier on slippery ground, natural grassland with bumps and pits, an extremely slippery surface (oil on plastic sheets), an 8-cm high staircase, and untrained 30-degree slopes. Metrics included success rate, TTF, and distance traversed.

- **Superior Performance:** The proposed method consistently outperformed both baselines across all real-world scenarios, demonstrating higher success rates, longer TTF, and greater traversed distances (Table III). Baselines often failed or traversed very short distances on these difficult terrains.
- **Adaptive Behavior:** The robot showcased intelligent and natural adaptation:
 - **High Obstacles:** When encountering a 13-cm barrier, the robot naturally lifted one front leg while extending the other, tilting its body to smoothly climb over, demonstrating dynamic gait switching based on proprioceptive feedback.
 - **Grassland:** It quickly adjusted its posture to maintain balance on uneven grassland, moving robustly in both walking and galloping gaits. This confirmed the "gallop in the wild" capability.
 - **Slippery Ground:** Upon transitioning to an extremely slippery surface, the robot immediately adapted to a more conservative, "glide-like" gait with reduced foot lift, effectively maintaining balance, unlike baselines that struggled.
 - **Staircase:** Successfully climbed 8-cm stairs without visual information, exhibiting an agile and natural gait.
 - **Generalization:** Remarkably, the robot successfully adapted and traversed untrained slopes of approximately 30 degrees, showcasing strong generalization capabilities.
- **Impact of Action Filtering:** Real-world results further validated the positive effect of the low-pass action filter on energy efficiency (Table IV), contributing to smoother and more effective real-world locomotion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of legged robot locomotion, offering a framework that effectively addresses critical limitations of current learning-based approaches.

**Significance:**
- **Bridging Robustness and Naturalness:** The most profound contribution is successfully combining the robustness of sim-to-real reinforcement learning on challenging terrains with the agility and naturalness derived from adversarial motion priors. This resolves the long-standing issue of RL-learned policies being robust but often unnatural, and simultaneously extends the applicability of AMP beyond flat, controlled environments.
- **Proprioceptive-Only Adaptation:** The framework enables robots to adapt to highly complex and unknown real-world terrains using only proprioceptive sensing. This is crucial for practical deployment, as it reduces reliance on complex and often failure-prone vision systems, which can be computationally expensive and sensitive to environmental conditions (e.g., lighting, dust).
- **First Learning-Based Gallop in the Wild:** The demonstration of a quadrupedal robot performing a gallop gait in unstructured outdoor environments is a landmark achievement for learning-based control. Galloping requires precise timing, high dynamic stability, and significant adaptability, pushing the boundaries of what learned policies can accomplish.
- **Comprehensive Sim-to-Real Solution:** The integration of teacher-student learning, robust domain randomization, an adaptive terrain curriculum, and action filtering provides a highly effective and holistic pipeline for achieving robust real-world transfer, offering valuable insights and techniques for future research.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Real-World Robot Deployment:** The ability to move robustly, agilely, and naturally on diverse, challenging terrains fundamentally enhances the utility of legged robots for real-world applications. This could accelerate their adoption in fields such as search and rescue (navigating debris, stairs), industrial inspection (uneven factory floors, construction sites), defense, and last-mile delivery.
- **Reduced Development Effort:** By effectively leveraging motion capture data and minimizing the need for intricate manual reward engineering, the framework can significantly reduce the development time and expertise required to design high-performance locomotion controllers.
- **Inspiration for Biologically Inspired Robotics:** The ability to mimic animal-like gaits, including galloping, and adapt them to varied environments provides a strong foundation for more biologically inspired robotic design and control. This could lead to robots that are not only more capable but also more energy-efficient and resilient, learning from millions of years of natural selection.
- **Advancements in Reinforcement Learning for Robotics:** The success of this integrated framework serves as a strong testament to the power of combining different RL techniques (imitation learning, adversarial training, teacher-student) and sim-to-real methodologies. It opens new avenues for future research, such as exploring methods to decouple style matching and task completion for even more flexible and efficient learning.
- **Platform for Complex Behaviors:** A robust and agile locomotion base is foundational for developing higher-level cognitive and manipulative skills in robots. This work lays the groundwork for robots that can not only move effectively but also interact intelligently with their environment in complex, unstructured settings.
