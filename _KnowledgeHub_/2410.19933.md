# Enhancing Safety in Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback via Rectified Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ShanghaiTech University, SenseTime Research, and Washington State University introduced Rectified Policy Optimization (RePO), an algorithm that enforces critical safety constraints on large language models (LLMs) to prevent individual unsafe responses. RePO achieved a 96.08% model-based safety rate for Alpaca-7B while maintaining helpfulness, significantly improving upon baselines that rely on average safety.

## Problem

- Existing LLM safety alignment methods, often based on expected safety constraints, suffer from a 'safety compensation' pitfall, where models can generate individual unsafe responses while maintaining a low average harmfulness.
- This limitation leads to LLMs producing specific harmful outputs, such as ethical violations or misinformation, despite meeting aggregate safety metrics.
- The primary challenge is to ensure per-instance safety, requiring that nearly every generated prompt-response pair adheres to safety conditions rather than just overall expectations.

## Method

- The paper redefines LLM safety alignment using '==critical safety constraints==,' which mandate that every prompt-response pair generated by the policy must satisfy the safety condition.
- It introduces Rectified Policy Optimization (**RePO**), an actor-critic algorithm that transforms the critically constrained problem into an unconstrained min-max formulation using a 'rectification operator' (`max{C(x, y), 0}`).
- RePO's policy update mechanism differentiates between safe and unsafe samples within a batch, maximizing helpfulness for safe outputs and applying a rectified penalty, scaled by an iteratively updated `λ`, for unsafe ones.

## Results

- RePO consistently achieved superior safety, demonstrating significantly higher safety rates (e.g., **96.08%** model-based for Alpaca-**7B**) and lower average costs compared to state-of-the-art baselines like SafeRLHF and SACPO.
- The algorithm effectively reduced the generation of unsafe samples during fine-tuning and maintained or improved helpfulness, demonstrating its capability to balance both objectives.
- The performance improvements were robust across different LLM architectures (Alpaca-**7B** and Llama3.2-**3B**) and validated by both model-based and GPT-4 evaluations, confirming generalizability and genuine gains in perceived safety.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Relying solely on expected safety constraints in LLM alignment is insufficient, as it permits 'safety compensation' where models can be statistically safe but still produce critically unsafe individual outputs.
> - Enforcing per-instance 'critical safety constraints' is essential for robust and trustworthy LLM behavior, preventing the generation of harmful content on a case-by-case basis.
> - Optimizing under strict per-instance safety requirements can be effectively achieved through a rectified re-formulation, which selectively penalizes only actual safety violations, coupled with a dynamic penalty mechanism.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the rapidly evolving landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) alignment, a crucial area of AI research focused on ensuring that powerful LLMs operate in a manner consistent with human intentions, values, and ethical guidelines. The paper specifically targets the "safety alignment" aspect, which is often considered alongside "helpfulness."

The current paradigm for LLM alignment often leverages Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), where a reward model is trained on human preferences to guide an LLM's behavior. Within RLHF, two main approaches exist: explicit reward modeling followed by RL optimization (e.g., PPO), and direct policy optimization (DPO) which implicitly learns from preferences. This paper primarily builds upon the RLHF framework, drawing connections to constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs).

Previous works, such as those by Dai et al. (2023), Huang et al. (2024), and Wachi et al. (2024), have attempted to improve LLM safety by decoupling helpfulness and harmlessness objectives. They frame safety as a constraint within a CMDP, maximizing helpfulness while keeping the *expected* safety cost below a certain threshold. This approach, often implemented using PPO-Lagrangian or DPO-like objectives, has been the state-of-the-art for incorporating safety.

However, the authors identify a critical flaw in this widely adopted "expected safety constraint" formulation, which they term "safety compensation." This pitfall allows an LLM to satisfy the safety constraint on average (i.e., its expected harmfulness is low) while still generating individual responses that are overtly unsafe, provided other responses are sufficiently "safe" to balance the average. This problem is particularly acute in real-world applications where a single unsafe generation can have severe negative consequences, regardless of the model's average performance.

By proposing "critical safety constraints" – where *every* prompt-response pair must satisfy the safety condition – Peng et al. aim to address this fundamental limitation. Their work thus represents a significant step towards more robust and reliable safety guarantees for LLMs, moving beyond aggregate metrics to ensure per-instance safety. This aligns with a growing need in the AI community for more stringent safety measures as LLMs become increasingly integrated into sensitive applications where even rare unsafe outputs are unacceptable. The paper's contribution is therefore an advancement in the theoretical formulation and algorithmic implementation of safety guarantees within the RLHF paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel method for enhancing the safety of Large Language Models (LLMs) by addressing a critical limitation in existing safety alignment techniques. The authors are motivated by the inherent risks associated with LLMs generating undesirable content, such as violating ethics, propagating bias, spreading misinformation, or breaching privacy. While LLMs offer immense benefits, their potential for harm necessitates robust safety mechanisms.

The core motivation stems from identifying a specific flaw in current safety alignment approaches, which typically rely on "expected safety constraints." The authors term this flaw "safety compensation," explaining that:
- **The Problem of Safety Compensation:** When safety is framed as an expected value (e.g., the average safety score across many responses must be below a threshold), the model can "compensate" for unsafe responses with overly safe ones. This means that an LLM might satisfy the average safety criterion, yet still produce individual responses that are highly unsafe or harmful for specific prompts. This is unacceptable for real-world deployments where even a single unsafe interaction can be detrimental.
- **The Research Question:** This observation directly leads to the central question guiding their work: "Can we guarantee safety for nearly all prompt-response pairs?" This question emphasizes a shift from statistical averages to per-instance safety guarantees.

To achieve this, the key objectives are:
1. **Introduce Critical Safety Constraints:** Replace the prevalent "expected safety constraints" with "critical safety constraints," which demand that *every* generated prompt-response pair must satisfy the safety condition (i.e., `C(x, y) ≤ 0` for all pairs, not just on expectation).
2. **Develop Rectified Policy Optimization (RePO):** Propose a novel reinforcement learning algorithm, RePO, that can effectively optimize LLMs under these stricter critical safety constraints. RePO needs to be capable of:
 - **Utilizing a Rectification Operator:** Incorporate the `max{·, 0}` operator to specifically penalize positive (unsafe) costs, treating zero or negative costs as satisfactory.
 - **Balancing Helpfulness and Safety:** Enable the model to maximize helpfulness while strictly adhering to the per-instance safety requirements.
 - **Addressing Computational Challenges:** Develop an efficient optimization strategy for the notoriously difficult problem of optimizing with strict, non-differentiable constraints over high-dimensional and combinatorial output spaces.

In essence, the authors are driven by the need to build LLMs that are not just "safe on average," but "safe almost always," ensuring reliability and trustworthiness in diverse applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology lies in the **Rectified Policy Optimization (RePO)** algorithm, designed to address the "safety compensation" pitfall by imposing critical safety constraints rather than expected ones. The approach can be broken down into several key components:

#### 3.1. Formalizing Critical Safety Constraints

The paper reformulates the LLM safety alignment problem as a constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP). Unlike prior work that aims to maximize helpfulness (`R(x, y)`) while constraining the *expected* cost (`E[C(x, y)] ≤ 0`), RePO imposes a stricter **critical safety constraint**: `C(x, y) ≤ 0` for *all* prompt-response pairs `(x, y)` generated by the policy `πθ`. This means every single output must be harmless.

#### 3.2. Rectified Re-formulation and Min-Max Problem

Directly optimizing with hard, per-instance constraints is computationally challenging. Inspired by online convex optimization with hard constraints, the authors propose a **rectified re-formulation** that transforms the critically constrained MDP into an equivalent "min-max" unconstrained problem:
`min_πθ max_λ≥0 [-E[R(x, y)] + βKL(πθ || πref) + λE[{C(x, y)}+]]`
Here, `{C(x, y)}+ = max{C(x, y), 0}` is the **rectification operator**. This operator effectively penalizes only *positive* costs (i.e., safety violations), leaving negative or zero costs (safe responses) unpenalized. `λ` is a non-decreasing rectified penalty, *not* a Lagrange multiplier in the traditional sense, which is a key distinction from primal-dual methods that assume strong duality.

#### 3.3. RePO Algorithm - Actor-Critic Framework

RePO adopts an actor-critic architecture, common in reinforcement learning:
- **Actor (Policy Model `πθ`):** The LLM itself, which generates responses.
- **Critics (Value Models `Vrϕ` and `Vcψ`):** Two separate networks that learn to estimate the expected cumulative reward and cost, respectively. These are crucial for calculating advantage functions and guiding policy updates.

#### 3.4. Policy Update Mechanism - Rectified Policy Gradient

The central innovation in RePO's policy update involves a nuanced approach to handling safe and unsafe samples within a training batch:
1. **Batch Sampling:** A batch of prompts `B` is sampled. For each prompt `x`, a response `y` is generated by the current policy `πθt`. Reward `R(x, y)` and cost `C(x, y)` are computed using pre-trained preference models (reward model and cost model).
2. **Token-level Decomposition:** Trajectory-level rewards and costs are decomposed into token-level values, incorporating the KL divergence regularization term (`βKL(πθ || πref)`) to prevent deviation from the reference model (`πref`).
3. **Clipped Surrogate Objectives:** Similar to PPO, clipped surrogate objectives (`L_CLIP_r` and `L_CLIP_c`) are used for reward and cost, respectively, to ensure stable policy updates. These use importance sampling weights and generalized advantage estimation (GAE) for variance reduction.
4. **Batch Classification:** The batch `B` is split into two subsets based on the safety constraint:
 - `B_safe`: Contains prompt-response pairs `(x, y)` where `C(x, y) ≤ 0` (i.e., safe samples).
 - `B_unsafe`: Contains prompt-response pairs `(x, y)` where `C(x, y) > 0` (i.e., unsafe samples).
5. **Rectified Policy Gradient Estimation:**
 - For `B_safe` samples, the objective is solely to maximize helpfulness (`L_CLIP_r`).
 - For `B_unsafe` samples, a penalty structure is applied: `[L_CLIP_r - λt * L_CLIP_c] / (1 + λt)`. This balances helpfulness with a penalty proportional to the cost, scaled by the rectified penalty `λt`. The normalization `(1 + λt)` keeps the two objectives on the same scale.
 - The overall rectified policy gradient is computed by combining these two objectives.
6. **Policy Update:** The LLM's parameters `θ` are updated using this estimated rectified policy gradient.

#### 3.5. Rectified Penalty (`λ`) Update

The rectified penalty `λ` is updated iteratively. Crucially, it only increases when safety violations occur. Specifically, `λt+1 = min{λt + αt * E[{C(x, y)}+], λmax}`. This ensures that `λ` grows in response to persistent safety violations, compelling the policy to prioritize safety. `λmax` is an upper limit to prevent excessive growth.

#### 3.6. Critic Model Updates

The parameters `ϕ` and `ψ` of the reward and cost critic models are updated by minimizing the Mean Squared Temporal Difference (MSTD) error, which helps them accurately predict future rewards and costs.

This sophisticated approach allows RePO to directly address the per-instance safety requirement, effectively training LLMs that are not only helpful but also critically safe by rectifying violations at the prompt-response pair level.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by Peng et al. provide compelling evidence that Rectified Policy Optimization (RePO) significantly enhances the safety alignment of LLMs, outperforming existing state-of-the-art baselines. The evaluation was performed on two LLM architectures (Alpaca-7B and Llama3.2-3B) and utilized both model-based and GPT-4-based evaluations to ensure robust assessment.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Superior Safety Enhancement:**
 - **Lower Harmlessness Score (Average Cost):** RePO consistently achieved the lowest (most negative) average cost, indicating a higher level of harmlessness. For Alpaca-7B, RePO resulted in an average cost of -13.85, compared to -12.50 for SafeRLHF and -8.32 for SACPO. For Llama3.2-3B, RePO achieved -12.43, while SafeRLHF managed -6.92, and SACPO surprisingly had a positive average cost of 4.12, suggesting it was not consistently safe.
 - **Higher Safety Rate (S.R.):** RePO demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of outputs satisfying safety constraints (`C(x,y) ≤ 0`). For Alpaca-7B, RePO achieved a 96.08% safety rate (model-based) and 72.03% (GPT-4 evaluation), surpassing SafeRLHF (90.58%, 51.16%) and SACPO (80.72%, 59.50%). Similar trends were observed for Llama3.2-3B, with RePO achieving 91.46% (model-based) and 68.60% (GPT-4 evaluation). This is a direct testament to RePO's ability to mitigate "safety compensation" by enforcing critical safety constraints.
 - **Reduced Unsafe Sample Generation:** Visual evidence (Figure 1 and Appendix A) clearly illustrates that RePO drastically reduces the number of unsafe samples generated per batch during fine-tuning, pushing models towards generating almost exclusively safe responses, even when starting from an "expected safe" but "not critically safe" state.

2. **Preservation of Helpfulness:**
 - Crucially, RePO achieved these significant safety improvements *without sacrificing helpfulness*. For Alpaca-7B, RePO even showed a positive `∆ Helpfulness` (+1.01), indicating an improvement over the initial model, while SafeRLHF and SACPO both showed negative changes. For Llama3.2-3B, RePO maintained a positive `∆ Helpfulness` (+0.16). This demonstrates RePO's ability to balance both objectives effectively, which is a major challenge in LLM alignment.

3. **Robustness Across Models and Evaluation Methods:**
 - RePO's consistent outperformance on both Alpaca-7B and Llama3.2-3B, two different base LLMs, indicates its generalizability.
 - The positive results across both model-based evaluation (using pre-trained reward/cost models) and GPT-4 evaluation (a more human-like assessment) bolster the credibility of the findings, suggesting that the improvements are not due to overfitting to a specific reward model but reflect genuine gains in perceived safety and helpfulness.
 - GPT-4 preference comparisons (Figure 2) further confirmed that GPT-4 rated RePO-optimized models as safer and better than those from baselines.

4. **Empirical Justification of "Safety Compensation" Pitfall:**
 - The experiments effectively justify the authors' initial hypothesis regarding the "safety compensation" pitfall. Baselines optimizing expected safety constraints either failed to achieve critical safety or, in the case of SACPO on Llama3.2-3B, even led to a regression in safety (positive average cost). RePO, by explicitly penalizing individual violations, successfully navigated this pitfall.

5. **Role of the Rectified Operator:**
 - The comparison between RePO and SafeRLHF (which is algorithmically similar but lacks the rectified operator and critical constraints) serves as an ablation study, highlighting the direct impact of the rectified operator in enhancing model safety. The superior performance of RePO validates this key algorithmic design choice.

In summary, the main findings strongly support RePO as a more effective and reliable method for LLM safety alignment, capable of pushing models towards a state of "critical safety" that is essential for trustworthy AI deployments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Enhancing Safety in Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback via Rectified Policy Optimization" carries substantial significance and holds considerable potential impact across the field of artificial intelligence, particularly for the deployment and trust-building of large language models.

#### 5.1. Addressing a Fundamental Limitation in AI Safety

The most significant contribution of this work is its explicit identification and algorithmic solution to the "safety compensation" pitfall inherent in traditional expected safety constraints. This is a fundamental limitation in current AI safety research, where aggregate metrics can mask critical failures at the individual instance level. By shifting the paradigm from "expected safe" to "critically safe" (or "nearly all prompt-response pairs are safe"), the authors provide a more rigorous and trustworthy framework for developing safe AI systems. This has profound implications for user trust and the responsible deployment of LLMs in sensitive applications.

#### 5.2. Advancing Constrained Reinforcement Learning

RePO introduces a novel approach within constrained reinforcement learning (CRL). While CRL has existed for some time, applying it effectively to the high-dimensional, combinatorial action spaces of LLMs, especially with strict per-instance constraints, is challenging. The rectified re-formulation and the proposed actor-critic algorithm, with its differential treatment of safe and unsafe samples and a non-traditional rectified penalty, represent a significant algorithmic advancement. This methodology could inspire new research directions in CRL for other complex AI systems facing similar safety-critical demands.

#### 5.3. Enabling More Reliable LLM Deployments

The practical impact of RePO is immense. In domains such as healthcare, finance, legal services, or critical infrastructure, even a single unsafe or inaccurate response from an LLM could have severe consequences, including financial loss, legal liability, or physical harm. RePO's ability to ensure per-instance safety significantly reduces these risks, paving the way for LLMs to be deployed with greater confidence in applications where reliability and harmlessness are non-negotiable. This could accelerate the adoption of LLMs in industries currently hesitant due to safety concerns.

#### 5.4. Improving the Alignment of AI with Human Values

This work directly contributes to the broader goal of aligning AI systems with human values. The tension between helpfulness and harmlessness is a central challenge in this endeavor. RePO demonstrates that it is possible to achieve both simultaneously, by prioritizing safety while still optimizing for utility. This balanced approach is crucial for creating AI that is not only intelligent but also beneficial and ethical.

#### 5.5. Potential for Broader Application Beyond LLMs

While demonstrated on LLMs, the core principle of critical safety constraints and the RePO algorithm could potentially be adapted to other safety-critical reinforcement learning domains. For instance, in autonomous driving, robotic control, or intelligent agents interacting with the physical world, ensuring safety in every instance, rather than on average, is paramount. The rectified operator and policy optimization framework could provide a blueprint for developing more robust safety mechanisms in these areas.

In conclusion, "Enhancing Safety in Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback via Rectified Policy Optimization" offers both a critical theoretical insight into AI safety and a practical, empirically validated solution. By moving beyond mere expected safety to nearly guaranteed per-instance harmlessness, this research significantly strengthens the foundations for developing truly trustworthy and responsible artificial intelligence.
