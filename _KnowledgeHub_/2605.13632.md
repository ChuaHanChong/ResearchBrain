# Guide, Think, Act: Interactive Embodied Reasoning in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> GTA-VLA introduces an interactive Vision-Language-Action framework that allows human users to provide spatially steerable visual cues, such as points, boxes, or traces, to guide robot policies. This integration improves robot robustness in out-of-domain conditions and facilitates human recovery from policy failures, leading to enhanced task completion rates.

## Problem

- Existing "Sense-to-Act" VLA models exhibit brittleness in out-of-domain visual or semantic shifts and lack transparency, making failure diagnosis and intervention difficult for humans.
- Current embodied Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning is largely internal and not easily modifiable by human input, which can propagate initial grounding errors into incorrect action plans.
- Robot policies lack a precise interface for conveying human spatial intent, hindering effective correction of visual ambiguities or re-grounding of robot attention during failures.

## Method

- An interactive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework, GTA-VLA, integrates optional human spatial guidance (points, boxes, traces) directly into a structured "Guide, Think, Act" reasoning process.
- The system employs an asynchronous "slow reasoning, fast action" design to enable real-time robotic control while leveraging expressive Vision-Language Model (VLM) reasoning.
- An automated pipeline synthesizes large-scale interactive spatial Chain-of-Thought annotations from existing robot datasets, creating the Interact-306K dataset for scalable training.

## Results

- GTA-VLA achieved strong in-domain performance, with 98.6% average success on LIBERO and 81.2% on SimplerEnv, performing competitively with or surpassing existing baselines.
- The framework demonstrated enhanced robustness under out-of-distribution shifts on SimplerEnv-Plus, achieving 58.3% success for unseen objects compared to 36.2% for baselines, and maintaining stronger performance across visual, robot-state, and language shifts.
- Human-provided spatial guidance consistently improved success rates in ambiguous scenarios, recovering an average of 20% of policy failures and increasing overall success from 81.2% to 86.1% on SimplerEnv-Bridge.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating optional human spatial guidance directly into a Vision-Language Model's structured reasoning process significantly improves robot robustness and allows for human correction of visual ambiguities.
> - An asynchronous architecture, separating slow VLM-based reasoning from fast continuous action generation, is crucial for achieving practical, responsive interactive robotic control.
> - Automating the generation of interactive spatial annotations from existing robot datasets provides a scalable method for training complex interactive embodied policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust generalist robotic agents capable of operating in open-world environments is a central aim of embodied AI. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a significant step in this direction, leveraging large pre-trained vision-language models to enable robot learning across diverse tasks and embodiments.

Existing VLA models primarily function via an implicit "Sense-to-Act" mapping, directly translating multimodal observations into robot actions. While effective within their training distributions, these tightly coupled policies often exhibit brittleness when encountering out-of-domain (OOD) visual or semantic shifts. Furthermore, their black-box nature provides limited transparency, making it difficult for human operators to diagnose failures or provide targeted corrective guidance when issues arise from perception failures, such as clutter, lighting variations, or unseen objects.

A recent evolution in this field involves Embodied Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, shifting towards a "Sense, Think, and Act" paradigm. These approaches predict intermediate representations, such as task decomposition or motion plans, aiming to improve interpretability and expose elements of the policy's decision-making process. However, a limitation of current embodied CoT systems is that their reasoning process remains largely internal, generated solely from the model's self-belief. This internal reasoning, though visible, cannot be easily modified or re-grounded by human input, particularly when initial grounding errors propagate into incorrect plans in OOD scenarios.

Concurrently, advancements in interactive perception and visual prompting in computer vision, exemplified by models like SAM and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) such as Ferret, have significantly enhanced spatial grounding capabilities. These systems allow conditioning on geometric prompts (points, boxes, marks) for precise 2D perception tasks like segmentation and detection. However, extending these 2D perception capabilities directly to continuous motor actions in embodied control presents challenges, as they do not inherently address the temporal dynamics, control interfaces, or action generation requirements of robotic manipulation.

This research addresses the gap between self-contained embodied reasoning and explicit human-robot interaction by introducing a framework that integrates spatially steerable human guidance directly into the policy's reasoning process. It combines the interpretability benefits of embodied CoT with the precision and correctability offered by human-provided visual cues, extending interactive perception beyond 2D tasks to direct robotic action generation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an interactive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework, termed GTA-VLA (Guide, Think, Act), that enables spatially steerable embodied reasoning by allowing users to guide robot policies with explicit visual cues.

This objective is motivated by several challenges observed in current VLA systems:

1. **Brittleness and Lack of Transparency in "Sense-to-Act" Policies:** Existing VLA models typically learn a direct mapping from observations to actions. While proficient within their training data distribution, these policies are prone to failure under out-of-domain (OOD) visual or semantic shifts. When failures occur, the implicit nature of these policies offers limited transparency, making it difficult for humans to understand the cause of error or intervene effectively. There is no explicit interface to re-ground the robot's attention or provide targeted corrections when perception fails due to factors like clutter, lighting, or unseen objects.

2. **Limitations of Existing Embodied Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Approaches:** Recent embodied CoT methods aim to improve interpretability by exposing intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., task decomposition, grounding cues, motion plans). However, these reasoning processes remain largely self-contained and are generated solely from the model's internal belief. This means that while reasoning is visible, it cannot be easily corrected by human input. This limitation is particularly critical in OOD settings where initial errors in object grounding or contact localization can propagate into subsequent, incorrect action plans.

3. **Need for Precise Human Spatial Guidance:** In scenarios involving visual ambiguity or policy failures, human users can often resolve issues immediately through simple, precise spatial cues, such as pointing to a target, marking a grasp region, or sketching a desired path. These spatial signals are often more precise and natural for conveying visual intent than language-only corrections. The motivation is to create an interaction interface that allows such human spatial guidance to directly condition and refine the robot's reasoning process.

4. **Practical Deployment Challenges of Autoregressive Reasoning:** Autoregressive generation of explicit reasoning tokens by large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can introduce substantial inference latency, which is often slower than the control frequencies required for responsive closed-loop manipulation. A key motivation is to mitigate this latency mismatch to ensure practical deployment.

5. **Scalable Data Generation for Interactive Systems:** Training VLA models with interactive capabilities typically requires large datasets of human interventions. A motivation is to develop an automated method for synthesizing interactive annotations from existing robot datasets, thereby reducing the need for costly manual human intervention data collection.

6. **Comprehensive Evaluation under Distribution Shift:** To adequately assess the robustness and interactive capabilities of the proposed framework, there is a motivation to introduce a benchmark that systematically evaluates interactive embodied policies under diverse OOD conditions and spatial ambiguities.

By addressing these motivations, GTA-VLA aims to create robot policies that are not only autonomous by default but also naturally correctable and steerable by human users when ambiguities or failures arise, enhancing both performance and user trust.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GTA-VLA employs an interactive Vision-Language-Action framework structured around a "Guide, Think, Act" paradigm. The system integrates optional human spatial guidance into a structured reasoning process, which then informs a downstream action execution module.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries and Overall Architecture

The system formulates robotic manipulation as a conditional sequence modeling problem. It extends the standard VLA input, which includes multi-view RGB observations ($I_t$), a natural language instruction ($L$), and robot proprioceptive state ($s_t$), by introducing an optional spatial prior ($P_{spatial}$). This prior provides sparse geometric guidance in image space.

The core architecture consists of:
- **VLM Backbone:** Qwen3-VL-2B is utilized for its multimodal understanding and spatial grounding capabilities. This backbone generates a structured spatial-visual reasoning sequence ($C$) and associated latent reasoning states ($H_{reasoning}$). It primarily consumes the main image, language instruction, and optional spatial prior.
- **Downstream Continuous Control Module:** A Flow-Matching action head is employed for action generation. This module consumes current control observations (main image, wrist-view image, proprioceptive inputs) along with the latest latent reasoning states ($H_{reasoning}$) to predict continuous action chunks, primarily end-effector poses.

#### 3.2. The “Guide” Phase: Multimodal Spatial Priors

This phase focuses on incorporating human guidance:
- **Spatial Prior Interface:** An optional $P_{spatial}$ serves as an additional input for sparse geometric guidance in image space. It complements, rather than replaces, the language instruction.
- **Source and Timing:** Spatial priors can originate from scripted simulator annotations, external perception models (e.g., Grounding-DINO, Gemini), or direct human intervention. They can be provided either upfront at the episode start or mid-episode when a human or failure detector identifies mis-grounding, incorrect affordances, or undesirable motion paths.
- **Spatial Formulations:** Three types of guidance are supported:
 - **Affordance Guide ($P_{point}$):** A single 2D image coordinate (x, y) indicating a task-relevant interaction point (e.g., grasp point).
 - **Box Guide ($P_{bbox}$):** A bounding box (x_min, y_min, x_max, y_max) specifying a target region.
 - **Trace Guide ($P_{trace}$):** An ordered sequence of 2D points representing a coarse image-space path.
- **Serialization and Tokenization:** $P_{spatial}$ inputs are serialized into the VLM's coordinate token space and concatenated with the textual instruction. Qwen3-VL's native grounding interface is used for point and box encoding, and trace guidance is represented as a sequence of point coordinates.
- **Fusion:** The spatial prior, language instruction, and visual observation are jointly provided to the VLM, allowing unified attention and conditioning.

#### 3.3. The “Think” Phase: Conditioned Spatial-Visual CoT

Instead of direct action prediction, the VLM generates a structured spatial-visual reasoning sequence ($C$) autoregressively. This sequence is organized into three functional segments:
- **Task CoT ($C_{task}$):** High-level semantic rationale, decomposing the instruction into sub-tasks and identifying relevant objects and interactions.
- **Vision CoT ($C_{vision}$):** Prediction of visually grounded intermediate targets (target regions, affordance locations) in image space, conditioned on observations and task rationale.
- **Robot CoT ($C_{robot}$):** Prediction of a coarse image-space motion sketch for the end-effector, based on grounded visual targets.
The key aspect is that this reasoning process is explicitly conditioned on the optional $P_{spatial}$. When $P_{spatial}$ is absent, the model reasons autonomously; when present, it acts as a geometric constraint. The hidden states associated with these reasoning tokens ($H_{reasoning}$) form a dense latent representation passed to the action head.

#### 3.4. The “Act” Phase: Asynchronous Flow-Matching

To mitigate the latency of autoregressive VLM reasoning, an asynchronous slow-fast design is adopted:
- **Slow Reasoning Module (VLM Backbone):** Executes the Guide and Think phases at a lower update frequency (e.g., 2 Hz), producing $C$ and $H_{reasoning}$. These states are cached as the latest reasoning memory.
- **Fast Action Module (Flow-Matching Head):** Operates at a higher control frequency (e.g., 10 Hz). It consumes the current control observation (primary image, wrist image, proprioceptive state) along with the latest cached $H_{reasoning}$ to predict continuous action chunks. The action head accesses $H_{latest\_reasoning}$ via cross-attention, allowing responsive low-level control while reusing high-level reasoning.
The action generation is parameterized by a flow-matching policy head, modeling continuous action chunks by learning a time-dependent vector field over actions.

#### 3.5. Data Construction and Training Recipe

- **Interact-306K Dataset:** A multi-embodiment dataset for guided spatial reasoning, built from approximately 306K real-world manipulation trajectories from sources like Open X-Embodiment (OXE), DROID, and RoboMind.
- **Automated Spatial-CoT Supervision:** For each trajectory, a structured reasoning target ($C = [C_{task}, C_{vision}, C_{robot}]$) is automatically constructed. Task decomposition is inferred from keyframes and language, visual grounding from localizing/tracking task-relevant objects, and robot-centric supervision from projecting end-effector motion into 2D image space. Stochastic noise is applied to generated spatial annotations to create synthetic affordance points, boxes, and traces for training the Guide interface.
- **Training Recipe:** A two-stage training process:
 1. **Stage 1:** The VLM backbone is trained on Interact-306K using stochastic spatial conditioning, exposing it to both guided and unguided inputs. Subsequently, the Flow-Matching action head is trained to map $H_{reasoning}$ and control observations to action chunks.
 2. **Stage 2:** The full policy is jointly fine-tuned on domain-specific robot data (e.g., BridgeData V2) to adapt to target embodiments and environments.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

Evaluation is conducted on LIBERO (sim-to-sim) and SimplerEnv (real-to-sim) for in-domain performance. For OOD generalization and guidance efficacy, SimplerEnv-Plus is introduced, featuring systematic perturbations across visual, robot-state, language, and object-centric factors. Protocols are defined for evaluating guidance in unseen object ambiguity and distractor-based ambiguity scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides experimental results demonstrating the performance of GTA-VLA across in-domain benchmarks, out-of-distribution conditions, and interactive guidance scenarios, including real-world deployment.

#### 4.1. In-Domain Performance (Table 1)

- **LIBERO Benchmark:** GTA-VLA achieved an average success rate of 98.6%, performing competitively with or marginally surpassing established baselines. This indicates that the integration of explicit spatial reasoning does not negatively impact the policy's fundamental manipulation capabilities or multi-task execution within familiar environments.
- **SimplerEnv (WidowX) Benchmark:** The framework recorded an average success rate of 81.2%, outperforming the strongest reported baselines. This suggests that explicit spatial reasoning is particularly beneficial in bridging the visual and semantic disparities between large-scale open-world training data and the conditions encountered during simulated robot execution. By explicitly grounding task-relevant regions and affordances prior to action generation, the policy demonstrated improved alignment between semantic understanding and executable control.

#### 4.2. Out-of-Distribution Generalization (Table 2)

On the SimplerEnv-Plus benchmark, GTA-VLA exhibited enhanced robustness under systematic distribution shifts:
- **Visual Shift:** The model maintained stronger performance under sensor viewpoint changes (39.6% success vs. 27.1% for X-VLA) and lighting variations (76.1% success vs. 68.8% for X-VLA), indicating that explicit intermediate grounding reduces reliance on low-level visual correlations that may be spurious.
- **Robot State Shift:** Performance remained robust despite randomized initial states and execution noise (79.2% success vs. 68.7% for X-VLA), suggesting that the combination of explicit reasoning and stable action generation contributes to resilience against embodiment uncertainty.
- **Language Shift:** The model preserved competitive performance under lexical variations in instructions (68.1% success vs. 66.3% for X-VLA), implying that the introduction of spatial supervision does not substantially weaken language understanding.
- **Object Shift:** The largest relative advantage was observed in scenarios with unseen objects (58.3% success vs. 36.2% for X-VLA) and distractor-heavy scenes (50.0% success vs. 46.9% for X-VLA). This indicates that explicit task decomposition and grounded intermediate reasoning are particularly beneficial when target identification is ambiguous.
Overall, these results suggest that the "Think and Act" design improves both in-domain execution and robustness under distribution shift while maintaining autonomous operation without external guidance.

#### 4.3. Guidance Efficacy (Table 3)

Explicit visual guidance consistently improved performance in ambiguous scenarios:
- **Unseen Object Ambiguity:** Visual guidance (point or box) significantly improved success rates. For instance, box guidance achieved an average of 56.9% compared to 27.8% with dense language instructions alone.
- **Distractor-based Ambiguity:** Point guidance was particularly effective, reaching an average of 54.2% success compared to 37.5% with dense language instructions.
- **Complementary Guidance:** Point guidance provided precise affordance-level supervision, effective for fine-grained spatial ambiguities (e.g., same-category distractors). Box guidance offered stronger object-level localization, beneficial when object identity was the primary challenge (e.g., unseen objects).
- **Trace Guidance:** On obstacle-avoidance and constrained-path tasks, trace guidance improved success from 27.8% to 30.5%, demonstrating its value for conveying path-level preferences.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study (Table 4)

An ablation study on SimplerEnv-Bridge tasks highlighted the contribution of each CoT field:
- Removing $C_{vision}$ (visual grounding) resulted in the largest performance drop (average -14.5%), confirming its criticality for cluttered scenes where target localization is challenging.
- Removing $C_{task}$ (task decomposition) also substantially reduced performance (average -12.4%), emphasizing the importance of semantic decomposition for mapping instructions to actions.
- Removing $C_{robot}$ (robot-oriented motion reasoning) had a smaller but measurable effect (average -3.1%), indicating that the action head can recover some low-level motion intent from cached visual reasoning but still benefits from explicit motion sketches.
- A free-form CoT variant underperformed the structured format (average -15.6%), suggesting that structured fields provide a more stable and controllable mechanism for aligning task decomposition, visual grounding, and motion intent with pseudo-label supervision in current robot datasets.

#### 4.5. Human-in-the-loop Failure Recovery (Table 5)

Re-running 10 failed episodes per task with human-provided spatial priors demonstrated failure recovery:
- Human guidance recovered an average of 20% of failures, increasing the overall end-to-end success rate from 81.2% to 86.1%.
- Interventions primarily resolved failures related to target grounding, affordance localization, and path selection, rather than low-level control errors (e.g., gripper orientation, approach angle).

#### 4.6. Real-World Robot Deployment (Figure 4)

GTA-VLA was successfully deployed on an AgileX Piper manipulator.
- The system demonstrated success in cluttered seen-object settings, as well as in more challenging unseen-object and referring expression scenarios.
- Point guidance provided notable gains in unseen and reference-ambiguous settings (e.g., 75% success for unseen multiple targets with guidance vs. 20% baseline), indicating effective transfer of the guidance and reasoning mechanism to real-world spatial ambiguity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents GTA-VLA, an interactive Vision-Language-Action framework that contributes to the field of embodied AI in several ways:

- **Enhanced Robot Robustness and Correctability:** By enabling users to guide robot policies with explicit visual cues, GTA-VLA addresses a limitation of traditional "Sense-to-Act" VLA models: their brittleness in out-of-domain scenarios and lack of a mechanism for real-time human correction. This interactive capability offers a pathway toward more robust robotic systems that can operate reliably in diverse and unpredictable environments, mitigating failures arising from visual ambiguities or distribution shifts.

- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** The framework facilitates a more intuitive and precise communication of human spatial intent to autonomous robots. By allowing users to directly influence the robot's reasoning process through affordance points, bounding boxes, or traces, it helps align human visual understanding with the robot's autonomous decision-making, potentially leading to more effective and trustworthy human-robot collaborative tasks.

- **Increased Interpretability of Robot Actions:** GTA-VLA leverages a structured spatial-visual Chain-of-Thought (CoT), which exposes intermediate reasoning steps. This transparency can make robot policies more understandable to human operators, aiding in diagnosing why a robot might fail and building greater confidence in autonomous systems.

- **Scalable Training for Interactive Robotics:** The development of an automated data pipeline for synthesizing large-scale interactive annotations from existing robot datasets is significant. This approach addresses a major challenge in training interactive robot policies, which typically demand extensive and costly manual human intervention data. By generating interaction-style supervision automatically, it makes guided spatial reasoning more scalable and accessible.

- **Practical Deployment in Real-Time Control:** The asynchronous "slow reasoning, fast action" architecture effectively decouples the latency of autoregressive Vision-Language Model (VLM) reasoning from the high-frequency requirements of closed-loop manipulation. This design allows for responsive real-world control while retaining the expressive power of rich VLM-based reasoning, making interactive embodied reasoning viable for practical robotic applications.

- **Advancement in Evaluation Benchmarking:** The introduction of SimplerEnv-Plus, a benchmark specifically designed for evaluating interactive embodied policies under various out-of-domain conditions, provides a valuable tool for future research. It enables systematic assessment of robustness against visual, robot-state, language, and object-centric perturbations, fostering development of more generalized and robust robot learning methods.

A current limitation noted by the authors is the primary formulation of reasoning and guidance in 2D image space. Extending these capabilities to 3D will be an important future direction, potentially enabling richer geometric grounding and more versatile interaction in complex real-world environments. Overall, GTA-VLA represents a step towards creating more adaptable, interpretable, and human-steerable robotic agents.
