# Dynamic Search for Inference-Time Alignment in Diffusion Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Dynamic Search for Diffusion (DSearch) introduces a gradient-free method for inference-time alignment in diffusion models, effectively maximizing non-differentiable reward functions while preserving sample naturalness and diversity. The approach consistently outperforms existing gradient-free techniques, improving DNA enhancer activity scores to 7.245 and drug-likeness (QED) to 0.946, among other quantitative gains across various domains.

## Problem

- Many real-world scientific applications require optimizing non-differentiable or black-box reward functions in diffusion models, a challenge for gradient-based alignment methods.
- Existing gradient-free guidance methods (e.g., best-of-N, SMC, SVDD) often yield suboptimal reward alignment or are computationally inefficient in complex search spaces.
- Achieving high reward while simultaneously maintaining the naturalness and diversity of generated samples poses a significant balance challenge.

## Method

- The research frames ==inference-time alignment== in diffusion models as a ==dynamic search problem== over the inherent tree structure of the denoising process.
- It introduces **DSearch** (Dynamic Search for Diffusion), a ==gradient-free algorithm== employing a ==dynamic beam search== strategy that adjusts beam and tree widths across diffusion steps.
- **DSearch** incorporates a ==multi-step Monte Carlo look-ahead heuristic== to accurately estimate future rewards from intermediate states, guiding the search efficiently.

## Results

- DSearch consistently achieved superior reward performance across image generation, biological sequence design (e.g., **7.245** for DNA enhancer activity), and molecular optimization (e.g., **0.946 QED** and **13.7 Parp1 docking affinity**).
- The method maintained competitive naturalness and diversity in generated samples, with lower NLL values for molecular generation compared to SVDD (e.g., **911 vs. 1049** for QED).
- DSearch demonstrated more significant gains in reward scores with increasing computational budgets, efficiently utilizing resources and showing **~35% faster reward-per-second scaling** than baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Framing inference-time alignment as a search problem enables robust, gradient-free optimization of non-differentiable reward functions in diffusion models.
> - Dynamic scheduling of search expansion, which concentrates search efforts in later diffusion steps, significantly improves reward optimization while reducing computational costs by approximately 35%.
> - A look-ahead heuristic function, which estimates future rewards from intermediate states, enhances the accuracy of beam selection and leads to superior final rewards.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Diffusion models have demonstrated capabilities in generating diverse data across various domains, including image synthesis and molecular design. A significant challenge in their application is aligning the generated outputs with specific, often complex, reward functions. This alignment process aims to optimize desirable properties in the generated samples beyond general data distribution adherence.

Existing approaches to inference-time alignment in diffusion models include gradient-based and gradient-free methods. Gradient-based techniques, such as classifier guidance, rely on the differentiability of the reward function. However, many real-world scientific and engineering applications involve non-differentiable or black-box reward functions. Examples include molecular docking scores, molecular fingerprints, and rewards derived from physical simulations or biological structure prediction algorithms (e.g., AlphaFold3, DSSP) that do not support gradient computation. The difficulty or infeasibility of learning accurate differentiable surrogates for these scientific rewards limits the applicability of gradient-based guidance.

Gradient-free guidance methods have emerged to address these limitations. These typically involve generating multiple samples and selecting the best (best-of-N sampling) or using sequential Monte Carlo (SMC)-based techniques that resample from weighted distributions. Other approaches, such as soft value-based decoding (SVDD), perform importance sampling using approximated soft value functions. While these methods have shown some effectiveness, they often do not achieve optimal inference-time alignment, particularly in complex search spaces or when computational resources are constrained.

This work introduces a novel perspective by framing inference-time alignment in diffusion models as a search problem. This framing leverages the inherent tree-like structure of the denoising process in diffusion models. Given the success of search algorithms in optimizing rewards in fields like biochemical design and language models, applying such methods to diffusion models represents a logical extension. The paper aims to develop a more sophisticated gradient-free inference-time alignment method that can navigate this search space efficiently and effectively, potentially overcoming the sub-optimality observed in prior gradient-free approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel and efficient gradient-free method for inference-time alignment in diffusion models, particularly for scenarios where reward functions are non-differentiable. The authors aim to maximize desired reward functions while maintaining the naturalness and diversity of generated samples.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges associated with aligning diffusion model outputs:

- **Non-Differentiable Reward Functions:** A critical limitation of many existing guidance methods (e.g., classifier guidance) is their reliance on differentiable reward functions. In scientific applications such as drug discovery or biological sequence design, reward functions often involve black-box simulations, physical models, or discrete molecular descriptors that do not provide gradients. Learning differentiable surrogates for these rewards is frequently difficult, inaccurate, or infeasible due to data scarcity or the complexity of the underlying scientific phenomena.
- **Sub-optimality of Existing Gradient-Free Methods:** While methods like best-of-N sampling, SMC-based approaches, and SVDD offer gradient-free alternatives, they may not achieve optimal reward alignment. Best-of-N is computationally inefficient as it only intervenes at the final state. SMC and SVDD, though more advanced, do not explicitly leverage a structured search over the generation trajectory, potentially leading to suboptimal paths.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The direct application of search algorithms to diffusion models can be computationally expensive due to the large, continuous action spaces and the sequential nature of denoising. A naive search across the entire induced tree structure (which can be of size $O(|X|^T)$ where $X$ is the design space and $T$ is the number of diffusion steps) is impractical. Therefore, there is a need for efficient search strategies that manage computational budgets effectively.
- **Balancing Multiple Objectives:** In generative tasks, achieving high reward must often be balanced with maintaining sample quality (naturalness, fidelity to the data distribution) and diversity. Over-optimizing for reward without considering these factors can lead to unrealistic or repetitive outputs.
- **Unified Framework for Continuous and Discrete Models:** The authors seek a method applicable to both continuous (e.g., image generation) and discrete (e.g., biological sequence, molecule generation) diffusion models, where gradient computation challenges can be pronounced.

To address these motivations, the authors propose to frame the inference-time alignment problem as a search problem over the implicit tree structure induced by the diffusion denoising process. Their key objective is to develop a "Dynamic Search for Diffusion (DSearch)" algorithm that can efficiently explore this tree, identify high-reward generation paths, and dynamically adapt its search strategy based on the characteristics of the denoising process and intermediate reward estimations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of DSearch involves reframing inference-time alignment in diffusion models as a search problem over a dynamically constructed search tree. This approach combines elements of beam search, heuristic-guided exploration, and dynamic resource allocation.

#### 3.1. Search Tree Formulation

A pre-trained diffusion model inherently defines a tree structure where each node $x_t$ represents a candidate sample at time step $t$, and its children $x_{t-1}$ are states sampled from the reverse denoising process $p_{\text{pre}}(x_{t-1}|x_t)$. The leaf nodes correspond to the final generated samples $x_0$. The objective is to find a path through this tree that maximizes a downstream reward function $r(x_0)$.
To make the search tractable, the authors introduce a constraint on the tree width by subsampling from the denoising process. Instead of exploring the entire support of $p_{\text{pre}}$, children nodes $Ch(x_t)$ are sampled empirically, limiting the number of branches to a specified tree width $w(t)$ at each time step $t$. Even with this constraint, a static tree width can lead to $O(w^T)$ states, which is still computationally expensive. This motivates the use of heuristic functions to guide the search and prune suboptimal branches.

#### 3.2. Heuristic Functions for Intermediate Node Evaluation

To guide the search effectively without fully traversing the tree, DSearch employs estimated value functions as heuristic functions $\hat{v}(x_t)$ for intermediate nodes $x_t$. This aligns with the concept of soft value functions $v_{t-1}(\cdot)$ from optimal policies in diffusion models, which predict future rewards from intermediate states.
A common heuristic in existing methods (e.g., DPS, SVDD) is to use $\hat{v}_t(x_t) = r(\hat{x}_0(x_t))$, where $\hat{x}_0(x_t)$ is the one-step prediction of the clean data $x_0$ from the noisy state $x_t$. This approximation is based on replacing the expectation over $p_{\text{pre}}(x_0|x_t)$ with a delta function at its mean, which is approximated by $\hat{x}_0(x_t)$.
DSearch refines this by introducing a **look-ahead heuristic function estimation** (Algorithm 1). This method performs a multi-step Monte Carlo estimation:
1. From an intermediate state $x_t$, it runs $M$ particles for $K$ steps ahead using the pre-trained diffusion model, yielding $\{x^{(s)}_{t-K}\}_{s=1}^M$.
2. For each $x^{(s)}_{t-K}$, it maps to an estimated clean sample $\hat{x}_0(x^{(s)}_{t-K})$.
3. The reward $r(\hat{x}_0(x^{(s)}_{t-K}))$ is then evaluated for each particle.
4. The overall estimated value $\hat{v}_t(x_t)$ is computed as the average of these $M$ rewards.
This look-ahead estimation is more accurate because it considers multiple future trajectories (addressing approximation A in Eq. 4) and evaluates $\hat{x}_0$ closer to $t=0$ (where $\hat{x}_0$ is more accurate, addressing approximation B).

#### 3.3. Dynamic Search Tree Expansion (DSearch Algorithm)

DSearch (Algorithm 2) implements a dynamic beam search strategy, which adjusts the beam width $b(t)$ (number of paths retained) and tree width $w(t)$ (number of children explored per retained path) across time steps. This contrasts with static beam search, which can waste resources on unpromising paths.
The key principle is to maintain a constant computational budget $C = w(t)b(t)$ at each active search step. This means that as the beam width decreases, the tree width can increase, allowing for broader exploration when fewer promising paths are retained.
The algorithm proceeds as follows:
1. Initialize with a beam of candidate samples (e.g., $x_T$ from Gaussian noise).
2. For each time step $t$ from $T$ down to $0$:
 - **Search Scheduling (Line 3):** Determines whether to perform an active search step at time $t$ (if $t \in A$) or a simpler denoising step.
 - **Dynamic Beam Width Adjustment (Line 5):** If $t \in A$:
 - For each beam (current sample) $x_t^{(j)}$, expand it by sampling $w(t)$ children $x_{t-1}^{(j)}[i]$ from $p_{\text{pre}}(\cdot|x_t^{(j)})$.
 - Evaluate each child using the look-ahead heuristic $\hat{v}(x_{t-1}^{(j)}[i])$.
 - Perform a greedy selection: $z_{t-1}^{(j)} = \text{argmax}_{x \in Ch(x_t^{(j)})} \hat{v}(x)$.
 - From the set of all $z_{t-1}^{(j)}$ candidates (from all beams), select the top $b(t-1)$ samples based on their heuristic values to form the next set of beams $\{x_{t-1}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{b(t-1)}$. This step effectively prunes weaker beams and focuses resources on promising ones.
 - **Simple Denoising (Line 7):** If $t \notin A$, simply sample one child $x_{t-1}^{(j)}$ from $p_{\text{pre}}(\cdot|x_t^{(j)})$ for each beam, without extensive search.

#### 3.4. Scheduling Mechanisms

Two key scheduling mechanisms are integrated into DSearch:

- **Beam Scheduling:** Controls how the beam width $b(t)$ changes over time. DSearch proposes exponential scheduling, where $b(t)$ decreases exponentially from an initial $b(0)$ to a final $b(T)$. This allows for broader exploration in early stages and more focused refinement in later stages. Other strategies like linear, quadratic, and sigmoid reductions are also explored.
- **Search Scheduling:** Determines *when* to activate the full search expansion (i.e., defining the set $A$ in Algorithm 2). It is motivated by the observation that early diffusion steps contain sparse information, while later steps are more critical for defining the final sample. Exponential scheduling is proposed, where search operations are more frequent in later time steps. This reduces computational cost from $O(TC)$ to $O(|A|C)$, where $C^\dagger = |A|/T$ is the effective search frequency.

#### 3.5. DSearch-R (Variant: Dynamic Beam Resample)

An alternative strategy, DSearch-R (Algorithm 3), is also explored. Instead of dynamically adjusting beam width, DSearch-R maintains fixed beam and tree widths. At each active search step, it discards a certain percentage ($r_r$) of suboptimal beams and resamples replacements from the remaining high-quality beams, weighted by their heuristic values. This aggressively maximizes reward but may reduce diversity compared to the main DSearch algorithm.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of DSearch was conducted across a diverse set of domains: image generation (compressibility, aesthetic score, human preference score), biological sequence design (DNA enhancer activity, 5'UTR MRL, 5'UTR stability), and molecular optimization (drug-likeness (QED), synthetic accessibility (SA), Parp1 binding affinity). DSearch was compared against several baselines: pre-trained diffusion models, best-of-N sampling, DPS (a gradient-based method adapted for gradient-free settings), SMC, and SVDD. Computational budgets ($\bar{C}$) were matched across methods for fair comparison.

#### 4.1. Superior Reward Optimization

- DSearch consistently demonstrated superior reward performance across all evaluated tasks (Table 1).
- For image tasks, DSearch achieved compressibility scores of -35.7 (vs. SVDD -37.3), aesthetic scores of 6.54 (vs. SVDD 6.37), and HPS scores of 0.3133 (vs. DPS 0.2971).
- In biological sequence generation, DSearch showed particularly pronounced improvements, with HepG2 enhancer activity scores of 7.245 (vs. SVDD 7.040), 5'UTR MRL of 1.521 (vs. SVDD 1.356), and 5'UTR stability of 0.533 (vs. SVDD 0.469).
- For molecular optimization, DSearch achieved QED of 0.946 (vs. SVDD 0.931), SA of 1.000 (vs. SVDD 0.986), and Parp1 docking scores of 13.7 (vs. SVDD 12.7).
- The variant DSearch-R generally achieved even higher rewards, demonstrating a more aggressive optimization strategy at the cost of diversity.

#### 4.2. Naturalness and Diversity

- DSearch maintained competitive or improved naturalness (measured by NLL for discrete data, BRISQUE for images) compared to baselines. For example, in molecular generation, DSearch achieved lower NLL values (911 for QED, 892 for SA, 731 for Docking) than SVDD (1049, 1068, 993 respectively), suggesting the generation of chemically realistic molecules.
- DSearch exhibited a balance between reward optimization and sample diversity (measured by pairwise distances/similarities). While SMC showed a marked drop in diversity, DSearch generally maintained reasonable diversity levels, often comparable to or better than SVDD and Best-of-N for similar reward levels. DSearch-R, however, showed reduced diversity as expected due to its aggressive beam replacement strategy.

#### 4.3. Performance Scaling with Computational Budget ($\bar{C}$)

- As the computational budget $\bar{C}$ increased, DSearch demonstrated more significant gains in reward scores compared to all other methods (Figure 4, Figure 7, Figure 8). This indicated that DSearch effectively utilized additional computational resources for alignment, highlighting the efficiency of its dynamic tree search.

#### 4.4. Effectiveness of Scheduling Search Expansion

- **Search Scheduling:** Experimental results indicated that exponential search scheduling, which prioritizes search efforts in later diffusion steps, yielded better rewards while reducing computational cost by approximately 35% compared to no scheduling (Figure 5a, 5c, Figure 23). Linear and uniform scheduling also improved efficiency but did not achieve the same performance level.
- **Beam Scheduling:** Exponential beam scheduling, which progressively prunes weaker samples and reduces beam width in earlier steps, proved most effective (Figure 5b, 5d, Figure 23). This strategy allowed for wider exploration earlier and more refined focus later, enhancing alignment performance without increasing computational overhead.

#### 4.5. Effectiveness of Look-Ahead Mechanism

- Increasing the look-ahead horizon $K$ (number of future steps sampled) generally improved performance, indicating that more informed intermediate state selection leads to better final rewards (Figure 6a, Figure 24). The gains tended to saturate beyond a certain threshold for $K$.
- Choosing intermediate states based on the maximum reward from multiple look-ahead evaluations (M-Max) generally outperformed averaging, suggesting that focusing on the most promising potential future path is beneficial.
- The number of forward evaluations $M$ had a more nuanced effect, with higher $M$ sometimes being crucial for tasks requiring extensive exploration.
- Reward estimation analysis showed that the look-ahead heuristic's correlation with the ground-truth final reward significantly improved as the diffusion process approached $t=0$, becoming nearly perfect in late steps (Figures 11, 12, 13).

#### 4.6. Runtime and Computational Complexity

- Empirical runtime analysis (Table 3, Figure 10) indicated that DSearch's execution time was competitive with or superior to baselines, especially at higher computational budgets. DSearch showed faster reward-per-second scaling compared to DPS and Best-of-N, confirming its cost-effectiveness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Dynamic Search for Diffusion (DSearch) holds significant implications for generative modeling and various application domains, particularly those involving scientific discovery and design.

#### 5.1. Addressing the Non-Differentiable Reward Challenge

DSearch offers a robust solution for optimizing non-differentiable or black-box reward functions, which are prevalent in real-world applications but pose a fundamental challenge to gradient-based guidance methods. This capability makes diffusion models more accessible and applicable in high-stakes fields like drug discovery (optimizing binding affinity with docking software) and biomolecular engineering (designing sequences with specific functional properties where reward functions are based on complex simulations or domain-specific lookup tables).

#### 5.2. Enhanced Performance and Efficiency

By framing inference-time alignment as a search problem and employing dynamic beam search with heuristic guidance, DSearch consistently outperforms existing gradient-free methods (best-of-N, SMC, SVDD) and even gradient-based methods adapted for gradient-free contexts (DPS) in reward optimization. The dynamic scheduling of tree expansion and beam width contributes to this efficiency by focusing computational resources on promising paths and critical time steps, rather than wasting them on suboptimal explorations. This "intelligent" resource allocation is crucial for practical applications where computational budgets are often limited.

#### 5.3. Balanced Generative Outcomes

DSearch effectively balances reward maximization with the generation of natural and diverse samples. This is important for ensuring that high-reward outputs are also realistic, novel, and representative of the underlying data distribution. For instance, in molecular design, it yields chemically plausible molecules with high reward, rather than merely optimizing a score at the expense of chemical validity or novelty. This balance expands the utility of generated designs.

#### 5.4. Broad Applicability

The methodology is applicable to both continuous (e.g., image generation) and discrete (e.g., biological sequence, molecule generation) diffusion models without requiring model-specific modifications. This generalizability allows DSearch to be a versatile plug-and-play inference-time strategy for a wide range of generative tasks.

#### 5.5. Foundations for Future Research

This work establishes a novel search framework for inference-time alignment in diffusion models, opening new avenues for research. Future work could explore more advanced search algorithms (e.g., Monte Carlo Tree Search variants adapted for diffusion), adaptive learning methods for optimizing scheduling hyperparameters, or integrating DSearch with differentiable guidance when available to further enhance performance. The insights into the utility of look-ahead heuristics and dynamic resource allocation can inform the design of future generative models and alignment strategies.

In summary, DSearch represents a step forward in inference-time alignment for diffusion models, particularly for property-driven generation with complex, non-differentiable objectives. Its ability to achieve superior reward optimization while maintaining sample quality and diversity, all within an efficient computational framework, makes it a valuable tool for scientific discovery and advanced generative AI applications.
