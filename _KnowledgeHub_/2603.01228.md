# Towards Policy-Adaptive Image Guardrail: Benchmark and Method

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Fudan University, Tencent, and Peking University developed SafeGuard-VL, a reinforcement learning-based method that enables vision-language models to dynamically adapt to diverse image safety policies, moving beyond static content moderation. The approach, paired with the new SafeEditBench benchmark, achieved the highest F1-score of 72.2 on UnsafeBench while preserving general VQA capabilities.

## Problem

- Existing VLM-based image safety guardrails often overfit to fixed safety policies, requiring complete retraining for policy shifts.
- This overfitting leads to a degradation of general instruction-following abilities and poor generalization to unseen safety policies.
- A lack of comprehensive benchmarks to systematically evaluate the cross-policy generalization capabilities of VLM safety systems.

## Method

- Introduction of **SafeGuard-VL**, a ==two-stage training== paradigm combining supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for unsafe semantics learning and policy-aware reinforcement learning (RL).
- Development of SafeEditBench, a vision-centric benchmark featuring semantically aligned safe-unsafe image pairs and five distinct safety policies to evaluate cross-policy generalization.
- A self-recaption pipeline is employed in the SFT stage to explicitly generate detailed descriptions of unsafe elements in images, enhancing semantic understanding.

## Results

- SafeGuard-VL-Full achieved the highest overall F1-score of **72.2** on UnsafeBench, outperforming both general-purpose and specialized safety VLMs.
- SFT-only models demonstrated severe cross-policy overfitting on SafeEditBench, highlighting their inability to generalize to varying policy definitions.
- SafeGuard-VL-RL maintained strong general VQA capabilities (**57.02** General Overall) while achieving competitive safety performance (**71.78** F1 on LlavaGuardBench), indicating better generalization without compromising overall functionality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current SFT-based VLM guardrails exhibit significant overfitting to static safety policies, making them ineffective when policies evolve or differ.
> - Effective policy-adaptive safety requires models to reason about subtle contextual cues and policy nuances, rather than relying on fixed categories or general common sense.
> - A decoupled training approach that first imparts fine-grained unsafe semantic understanding and then aligns with diverse policies via RL can foster robust cross-policy generalization while maintaining core VLM functionalities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement and widespread deployment of multimodal AI systems, particularly Vision-Language Models (VLMs), have introduced new challenges related to content safety. VLMs are increasingly foundational for applications like image captioning and visual question answering, but their deployment in open environments necessitates robust mechanisms for rejecting sensitive or harmful visual content, a capability referred to as the "harmful image guardrail." This requirement is crucial for preventing misuse and ensuring compliance with evolving safety standards across various domains and jurisdictions.

Traditionally, image-based detectors employed fixed taxonomies of harm (e.g., "sexual," "violence"). While effective in static settings, these systems are limited by their predefined categories and require complete retraining when policies shift, making them inflexible. VLMs, with their advanced world knowledge, instruction-following capabilities, and semantic understanding, offer a potential solution for more dynamic and policy-aware guardrails.

However, a significant limitation identified in the broader landscape is that existing VLM-based guardrail methods, despite their inherent semantic capacity, are typically trained and evaluated under a single, fixed safety policy. This supervised fine-tuning (SFT) approach causes models to overfit to the seen policy, leading to a failure in generalizing to new or unseen policies and, in some cases, a degradation of their basic instruction-following ability and general knowledge. Current evaluation frameworks primarily focus on static classification under universally accepted safety standards, rather than assessing a model's adaptability to evolving or custom regulatory instructions. Prior work has largely overlooked the policy-dependent nature of safety judgments, thus failing to systematically evaluate cross-policy generalization.

This research positions itself within the exogenous safety paradigm, where a separate model acts as a guardrail to identify and reject unsafe content. It directly addresses the identified gap in policy adaptability and cross-policy generalization for VLM-based safety guardrails. By introducing a new benchmark and a novel training methodology, the work aims to move beyond static, fixed-category content moderation towards systems that can dynamically interpret and adhere to diverse and evolving safety policies, enhancing the trustworthiness and robustness of multimodal AI in real-world applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from a critical challenge in deploying VLM-based safety guardrails: the inherent policy-dependent and continuously evolving nature of what constitutes "safe" or "unsafe" visual content. Current systems, whether traditional classifiers or VLM-based methods, are largely confined to fixed safety policies, necessitating frequent and costly retraining when new guidelines are introduced. This limitation results in a lack of adaptability and robustness in dynamic real-world environments.

The paper identifies two specific problems with existing VLM-based safeguarding methods:
1. **Overfitting to Fixed Policies:** These models are typically trained and evaluated under a single, static safety policy using supervised fine-tuning (SFT). This process causes models to heavily overfit to the training policy's distribution, making them ineffective when confronted with unseen policies.
2. **Degradation of General Capabilities:** The rigid alignment to a fixed policy can lead to a loss of the model’s general instruction-following ability and broader world knowledge, compromising its overall utility beyond the specific safety task.

To address these issues, the research sets forth two primary objectives, each supported by a key contribution:

1. **Objective 1: Systematically Benchmark Cross-Policy Generalization.**
 - **Motivation:** The lack of a comprehensive evaluation framework for assessing how well guardrail models adapt to *unseen* or *changing* safety policies. Existing benchmarks focus on single-policy performance, failing to capture the dynamic nature of real-world safety requirements.
 - **Contribution:** Introduction of **SafeEditBench**, a novel evaluation suite designed to benchmark the cross-policy generalization performance of existing VLMs. This benchmark aims to provide a fine-grained assessment of policy-aware generalization by leveraging image-editing models to create policy-aligned datasets.

2. **Objective 2: Develop a Policy-Adaptive and Robust Method for Unsafe-Image Guardrails.**
 - **Motivation:** To overcome the limitations of SFT-based methods that suffer from policy overfitting and knowledge degradation. A method is needed that can promote verifiable adaptation across evolving policies while preserving general multimodal capabilities.
 - **Contribution:** Introduction of **SafeGuard-VL**, a reinforcement learning-based method with verifiable rewards (RLVR). This method explicitly optimizes the model with policy-grounded rewards, aiming to foster adaptability to diverse and shifting safety definitions.

In summary, the core motivation is to enhance the adaptability and robustness of harmful image guardrails, moving beyond static classification to dynamic, policy-aware decision-making. The objectives are to accurately measure this adaptability and to develop a method that can achieve it effectively.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed **SafeGuard-VL** is a two-stage training paradigm designed to equip vision-language models with robust and policy-aware safety capabilities, minimizing degradation of the model’s original generalization ability. The framework allows the VLM to function as a flexible safety guardrail, evaluating content against specific policy constraints.

#### 3.1. Stage 1: SFT for Unsafe Semantics Learning

The initial stage focuses on enhancing the model's understanding of potentially harmful visual content through supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Unlike conventional approaches that train models for direct "safe" or "unsafe" classification, this stage teaches the model to *describe* the unsafe elements present in images. This is motivated by the observation that baseline models often produce vague responses to harmful content due to built-in safety protocols, lacking explicit semantic understanding of risks.

- **Dataset:** Approximately 100,000 diverse, internet-sourced images containing various categories of unsafe content (e.g., sexual, violence, illegal-related).
- **Self-Recaption Pipeline:** A two-step process is used to generate augmented captions for each image (illustrated in Fig. 3):
 1. **Initial Caption Generation:** A baseline model (Qwen2.5-VL) generates an initial caption. Due to its safety protocols, this caption typically omits explicit sensitive details.
 2. **Recaptioning for Unsafe Details:** A separate, more permissive model (Gemma 27B) is then used to "recaption" the image. This step recovers suppressed unsafe details, producing a caption with explicit harmful descriptions while retaining the original syntactic structure. The recaption model is constrained to only *add* unsafe semantic descriptions without altering neutral or factual components.
- **Objective:** This stage aims to inject critical safety knowledge and semantic understanding of harmful content into the model, preserving its core descriptive abilities.

#### 3.2. Stage 2: Policy-Aware Reinforcement Learning (RL)

Following the SFT stage, the model is further optimized using reinforcement learning (specifically, Group Relative Policy Optimization, GRPO) to learn policy-specific discrimination. Crucially, the model has not been exposed to classification tasks in Stage 1, thus it learns to *reason* why an image complies with or violates a given policy.

- **Data:** The LlavaGuard training set is repurposed for policy-conditioned RL.
- **Reward Signal:** For each image-policy pair, the ground truth label (safe/unsafe) serves as the reward signal.
- **Mechanism:** The model is encouraged to generate responses that justify its decisions based on the provided policy text. This promotes internal reasoning and adaptability to varying policy definitions, rather than relying on rote memorization or a fixed rule set. For instance, a policy permitting "sexual" content would lead the model to allow images previously flagged as unsafe under stricter rules.
- **Objective:** This stage enables the model to generalize across different policy definitions, maintain general multimodal ability, and achieve adaptive and verifiable safety behavior.

#### 3.3. SafeEditBench: A Vision-Centric Benchmark for Unsafe Image Guardrail

To evaluate policy adaptability and generalization, the paper introduces SafeEditBench. This benchmark is designed to test a model’s ability to reason under varying policy constraints, moving beyond static definitions of "unsafe."

- **Dataset Construction:**
 - **Source:** Built from the LlavaGuard test set, comprising 128 images across nine harmful categories.
 - **Image-Editing for Safe-Unsafe Pairs:** For each unsafe image, minimal, semantically-preserving edits are applied using image-editing models (e.g., Nano Banana, Gemini’s AI image generator). These edits transform unsafe images into "safe" versions that differ only in localized regions, for example, by replacing a weapon with a camera or reinterpreting semantic elements. This design creates visually consistent safe-unsafe pairs that require fine-grained discrimination based on subtle contextual cues rather than global features.
- **Policy-Level Definition:** SafeEditBench incorporates five distinct safety policies (L1 to L5) applied to the same set of 62 image pairs (see Fig. 4).
 - **L1 (Most Permissive):** Treats nearly all human expression as safe, only flagging egregious violations.
 - **L5 (Most Restrictive):** Imposes maximal restrictions, deeming even innocuous physical contact as unsafe.
 - **L3 and L4:** Align with mainstream societal expectations.
 - **L1 and L5:** Represent counterintuitive regimes specifically designed to test policy adherence.
 - **Key Principle:** The benchmark demonstrates that safety judgments are fundamentally policy-dependent, not common-sense-dependent (Fig. 5), as the same image can be safe or unsafe depending on the policy.
- **Evaluation Metric:** F1-score for binary classification (safe/unsafe) is used for each policy, except for Policy L1 (where all images are safe, thus accuracy is used). The final metric is the macro-averaged F1-score across all five policy settings.

#### 3.4. Model Variants

For experimental clarity, four model variants are defined:
- **SafeGuard-VL-SFT:** Stage-1 SFT only.
- **SafeGuard-VL-Full:** Stage-1 SFT + Stage-2 RL (the complete proposed pipeline).
- **SafeGuard-VL-RL:** Stage-2 RL only, without initial SFT (trained on identical data as QwenGuard for comparison).
- **SafeGuard-VL-RL+SafeEditTrain:** RL trained on SafeEdited data (constructed by applying the SafeEditBench image-edit procedure to the LlavaGuard training set).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results evaluate the performance of SafeGuard-VL and baseline models across three safety-focused benchmarks: UnsafeBench, SafeEditBench, and LlavaGuardBench, in addition to general VQA benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Results on UnsafeBench

- **Overall Performance:** SafeGuard-VL-Full achieved an overall F1-score of 72.2, which was the highest among evaluated models. This performance represents a substantial improvement over general-purpose VLMs like Qwen2.5-7B (41.7) and safety-specialized models such as QwenGuard-7B (43.6).
- **Category-Specific Gains:** Notable performance gains for SafeGuard-VL-Full were observed in categories such as Hate, Sexual, and Spam, indicating a broader and more robust understanding of various harmful content types.

#### 4.2. Results on SafeEditBench (Policy Adaptability)

- **Overfitting in SFT Models:** Models trained exclusively with SFT under specific policy levels (L1–L5) exhibited a significant lack of cross-policy generalization. For instance, an SFT model trained solely on the most permissive policy (L1) degenerated into an "always-safe" classifier, achieving 0% F1-score on all other policy levels. Similarly, training on the most restrictive policy (L5) resulted in severe performance drops on L1 and L2. These findings indicate that existing SFT-based guardrail approaches struggle to generalize across different policy definitions.
- **RL Alleviation:** Reinforcement learning (RL) was observed to mitigate some of the overfitting issues seen with SFT. However, models, even with RL, still demonstrated a degree of policy-dependency, highlighting the inherent challenge of true policy adaptivity.
- **Effectiveness of SafeEdited Data:** SafeGuard-VL-RL+SafeEditTrain, which was trained on data created using the SafeEditBench image-editing procedure, achieved an overall F1-score of 49.43 on SafeEditBench. This was higher than SafeGuard-VL-RL trained on original LlavaGuard data (45.59), suggesting that training with carefully constructed edited pair data can improve the model's ability to learn subtle semantic boundaries defined by policy.

#### 4.3. Results on LlavaGuardBench and General VQA Benchmarks

- **QwenGuard-7B Limitations:** QwenGuard-7B, while achieving a high F1-score on its proprietary LlavaGuardBench (84.57), demonstrated significant performance degradation on other safety benchmarks (UnsafeBench: 43.56) and general Visual Question Answering (VQA) tasks (e.g., BLINK: 12.05; General Overall: 35.98). This indicates strong overfitting to the specific annotation style and policy assumptions of its training data, limiting its generalization capacity.
- **SafeGuard-VL-RL Generalization:** In contrast, SafeGuard-VL-RL, trained on the same data as QwenGuard-7B, not only achieved competitive performance on LlavaGuardBench (71.78) but also substantially improved results on UnsafeBench (from 41.71 for Qwen2.5-7B to 62.39 for SafeGuard-VL-RL on this benchmark). Crucially, SafeGuard-VL-RL maintained competitive general VQA capabilities (General Overall: 57.02), suggesting better generalization without sacrificing overall functionality.
- **Qualitative Advantages:** Qualitative comparisons (Fig. 7) illustrated SafeGuard-VL-RL's ability to perform context-aware policy interpretation (e.g., correctly classifying a museum exhibit of a firearm as safe under a policy allowing historical displays, which QwenGuard incorrectly rejected). It also demonstrated robust instruction following by adhering to requested output formats rather than defaulting to fixed JSON-style safety responses.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Recaption Effectiveness:** Removing the self-recaption step from SafeGuard-VL-SFT resulted in a notable drop in UnsafeBench performance (53.22 vs. 66.96 F1-score when recaption was included). This confirms the importance of the meticulously designed captions in enabling the model to learn fine-grained and context-dependent harmful patterns.
- **RL Stage Contribution:** The addition of the Stage 2 RL (SafeGuard-VL-Full) after SFT (SafeGuard-VL-SFT) yielded a further performance improvement of +5.2 on UnsafeBench (from 66.96 to 72.16). This validates the two-stage training strategy, demonstrating that RL effectively enhances the model’s policy-specific judgment beyond the general safety knowledge acquired during SFT.
- **Preservation of General Capabilities:** Across all ablation variants, the model's general VQA capabilities remained stable (ranging from 53.09 to 56.92), indicating that the safety enhancements were achieved without compromising overall functionality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a fundamental limitation in the field of VLM-based safety guardrails: the prevalent lack of policy-aware generalization. The work highlights that current approaches, despite leveraging sophisticated VLMs, often overfit to static safety policies, making them brittle and inflexible in dynamic real-world deployments.

The introduction of **SafeEditBench** represents a significant contribution to the research community. By creating a novel benchmark based on semantically aligned safe-unsafe image pairs and diverse policy levels, it provides a tool for systematically evaluating cross-policy generalization. This moves beyond traditional, static safety benchmarks and establishes a framework for assessing how well models can adapt to evolving or nuanced policy definitions, which is critical for future development in AI safety. The benchmark's design, using minimal, locality-preserving edits, poses a challenge for models to reason about specific unsafe elements rather than relying on coarse visual cues.

The proposed **SafeGuard-VL** method offers a practical solution to the identified problem. Its two-stage training paradigm, which decouples initial semantic understanding (via recaptioned SFT) from policy-specific alignment (via RL with verifiable rewards), is demonstrated to significantly improve cross-policy robustness. This approach not only enhances the model's ability to adhere to varying safety policies but also preserves its general multimodal capabilities, mitigating the risk of catastrophic forgetting or over-specialization observed in previous methods.

The ability of SafeGuard-VL to interpret policy context, differentiate subtle visual cues, and maintain instruction-following fidelity, as shown in the qualitative results, indicates a step towards more trustworthy and reliable AI systems. The findings demonstrate that RL can effectively align models with complex, dynamic policy constraints, fostering a deeper, verifiable adaptation compared to rigid SFT approaches.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. By enabling VLMs to adapt to diverse and evolving safety policies, it paves the way for the deployment of more flexible, continuously adaptive, and socially responsible multimodal AI systems across various industries and cultural contexts. This can include applications in content moderation, ethical AI development, and user-facing systems where safety guidelines are subject to frequent updates or specific regional requirements. The public release of the code and data (under controlled-access protocols for responsible use) further facilitates future research and development in this critical area of AI safety.
