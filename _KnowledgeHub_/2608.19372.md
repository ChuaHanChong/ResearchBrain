# The Missing Touch: Spatially Distributed Tactile Feedback Brings Teleoperation Closer to Human Dexterity

> [!summary] Summary
> A teleoperation system pairs a 32-DoF electro-osmotic cutaneous shape display with a 2-DoF bilateral force-feedback telemanipulator, relaying spatially localized GelSight fingertip deformations to the operator's finger. Full-resolution tactile feedback pulls teleoperated trajectories 29-79% closer to natural human movement across button-press and peg-rolling tasks, versus off, uniform, or two-region feedback.

## Problem

- Teleoperated demonstrations remain slower, less accurate, and less natural than direct manipulation despite decades of bilateral kinesthetic force-feedback research in telemanipulation.
- Prior cutaneous rendering approaches (vibrotactile, electrotactile, skin-stretch, uniform tilting platforms) collapse spatially distributed contact into a single low-dimensional cue, discarding location information.
- It remains unknown whether restoring spatial contact structure narrows the teleoperation gap in movement naturalness rather than just task speed and accuracy, which prior work measured.

## Method

- Custom 2-DoF leader-follower telemanipulator with brushless-DC-motor ==bilateral kinesthetic force feedback==, paired with a GelSight vision-based tactile sensor on the robot fingertip.
- Algorithm mapping GelSight contact images to a 32-DoF electro-osmotic cutaneous shape display via ==frame differencing==, ==Gaussian blur==, ==Otsu thresholding==, and per-pixel contact-region inflation.
- Four systematically varied feedback-resolution conditions (Off, 1D uniform, 2D two-region, Full localized) evaluated with ==dynamic time warping== against blindfolded direct-manipulation baselines.

## Results

- Full spatial feedback reduced DTW distance to natural trajectories by **29-79%** versus Off, 1D, and 2D conditions across button-discrimination and peg-rolling tasks (**p<0.001**).
- Full feedback cut mean button-overshoot and switchback counts significantly (**p<0.001**, **p<=0.007**) and produced significantly longer peg-rolling strokes with fewer strokes per trial (**p<=0.014**).
- Full feedback lowered within- and between-operator inter-trial DTW variability versus all coarser conditions (**p<=0.005**) and reduced self-reported mental demand (NASA-TLX Q2).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task naturalness and task speed are separable performance dimensions; feedback resolution can improve trajectory naturalness even when speed gains plateau.
> - Spatially distributed cutaneous cues are complementary to kinesthetic feedback, not redundant, whenever kinesthetic cues alone cannot localize contact.
> - Higher-resolution tactile feedback compresses demonstration state-space coverage and cross-operator variability, a property linked to better imitation-learning outcomes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic teleoperation aims to enable human operators to control remote robots with a level of intuitiveness and dexterity comparable to direct manipulation. Despite advancements and its increasing application in collecting demonstration data for training autonomous robot policies, teleoperated robot performance consistently falls short of natural human dexterity, even for fundamental tasks. This performance disparity impacts the quality of learned policies, as autonomous robots trained on these demonstrations tend to inherit inefficiencies, resulting in slower, less accurate task execution and trajectories that deviate from efficient human movements.

Human manipulation is characterized by its speed, efficiency, and robustness, largely attributed to the continuous, spatially distributed tactile information received from fingertips regarding contact location, pressure distribution, and incipient slip. When this cutaneous feedback is absent, such as during fingertip anesthesia, human manipulation performance degrades significantly, leading to increased grip forces, higher object drop rates, and greater reliance on visual compensation.

In teleoperation, despite decades of research into kinesthetic (force) feedback, operators often experience a sensory deficit analogous to fingertip anesthesia. They receive information about interaction forces but lack the local contact patterns that typically guide natural manipulation. Consequently, teleoperators exhibit compensatory behaviors, producing demonstrations that are slower, less accurate, and involve more corrective actions than direct human manipulation.

Existing research on haptic feedback in teleoperation has often focused on conveying low-dimensional force information through various cutaneous channels, such as vibrotactile, electrotactile, or skin-stretch displays, or uniformly tilting platforms. While these methods have demonstrated improvements in task speed and accuracy, they generally collapse spatially distributed signals into simplified cues, leaving the underlying movement patterns and their naturalness largely unexamined. Modern robotic hands are increasingly equipped with high-resolution tactile sensors capable of capturing dense contact geometry, which has proven valuable for autonomous manipulation. However, this rich tactile information is seldom conveyed to human operators. This paper addresses this gap by investigating whether the reproduction of spatially distributed fingertip deformations can significantly enhance the naturalness of teleoperated behavior, thereby bridging the dexterity gap and improving the quality of data for robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to investigate whether reproducing spatially distributed fingertip deformations can make teleoperated behavior more closely resemble natural human manipulation. The authors hypothesized that the absence of spatially distributed tactile feedback is a key factor contributing to the observed performance gap between human and teleoperated dexterity.

Specific objectives motivated by this hypothesis included:

1. **Quantifying Naturalness**: To develop a method for comparing teleoperated trajectories with those produced during direct human manipulation, utilizing metrics such as Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance to assess the "naturalness" of movement patterns.
2. **Assessing Impact of Spatial Resolution**: To systematically evaluate how varying the spatial resolution of conveyed tactile signals influences operator performance, specifically focusing on task speed, accuracy, and the naturalness of movements (e.g., minimizing corrective actions, reducing overshoots, and maintaining smooth trajectories).
3. **Evaluating Consistency**: To determine if spatially distributed cutaneous feedback improves the consistency of teleoperated demonstrations, both within and across operators, and to examine its effect on the state-space distribution of teleoperated motions. Such consistency and compactness of state-space distribution have been associated with improved training outcomes for autonomous robot policies.
4. **Subjective Operator Experience**: To measure the cognitive load, perceived difficulty, and frustration experienced by operators under different feedback conditions, thereby providing insights into the intuitiveness and effort required for control.

The motivation for this work stems from two main areas: firstly, the desire to improve the inherent performance of human operators in teleoperation systems, enabling them to control remote robots with greater dexterity and efficiency. Secondly, and critically, to enhance the quality of demonstration data collected via teleoperation for training autonomous robot policies. Current teleoperated demonstrations often contain inefficiencies that are subsequently inherited by learned policies. By making teleoperated movements more natural and consistent, the research aims to provide higher-quality supervisory signals for imitation learning, ultimately contributing to the development of more capable and robust autonomous robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a custom 2-degree-of-freedom (DoF) leader-follower telemanipulation system with bilateral kinesthetic force feedback. This system allowed operators to move in vertical and horizontal directions. The robot "finger" was a vision-based tactile sensor (GelSight Mini), which captured spatially distributed contact information during interaction with the environment. This rich tactile data was then conveyed to the human operator via a 32-DoF cutaneous shape display (Fluid Reality) under their fingertip, which reproduced localized deformation patterns through controlled inflation of small elastic domes. This configuration represented a novel approach to relaying localized deformation data from robot to human fingertips.

**Tactile Mapping Algorithm:**
The mapping from the vision-based tactile sensor to the cutaneous shape display involved several steps:
1. A reference image ($I_0$) was captured upon sensor initialization.
2. Subsequent frames ($I$) were differenced with $I_0$ to yield a difference image ($I_d$), which was then converted to grayscale and smoothed with a Gaussian blur.
3. If the Frobenius norm of $I_d$ exceeded a noise floor threshold ($T_{noise\_floor} = 800$), Otsu's method was applied to binarize $I_d$. Otherwise, no pixels were inflated.
4. Each of the 32 display pixels was mapped to a corresponding image coordinate ($x_i, y_i$). If the Frobenius norm of pixel intensities within a $2r \times 2r$ region ($r=10$) centered at $(x_i, y_i)$ exceeded a contact threshold ($T_{contact} = 1200$), the corresponding display pixel was inflated on the operator's fingertip.

**Experimental Design:**
Two behavioral studies were conducted to evaluate the effects of high-resolution, spatially distributed tactile feedback: a button discrimination task and a peg-rolling task. Participants provided informed consent under Northwestern University's IRB protocol.

- **Participants:** 12 participants (7 male, 5 female, mean age 28.6) for the button task, and 10 separate participants (7 male, 3 female, mean age 25.9) for the peg-rolling task. All had varying levels of prior teleoperation experience.
- **Feedback Conditions:** Four distinct cutaneous feedback conditions were tested in a randomized order, with kinesthetic force feedback active throughout all 48 trials (12 per condition):
 - **Off:** No cutaneous feedback.
 - **1D:** All elastic domes inflated uniformly (in-phase) if any contact was detected.
 - **2D:** Domes in the upper or lower half of the display inflated uniformly if contact was detected in that respective half.
 - **Full:** Only the specific domes corresponding to the localized contact pattern on the robot finger were inflated, faithfully reproducing the deformation.
- **Baseline Measurements:** Before teleoperation trials, participants performed each task directly with their bare dominant index finger, blindfolded to match the experimental conditions. These trajectories served as "natural human movement" baselines.
- **Post-Experiment Questionnaire:** After each task, participants completed a modified NASA-TLX questionnaire, rating perceived performance, mental demand, task difficulty, and frustration on a 7-point scale.

**Experimental Tasks:**
- **Button Discrimination Task:** Participants, blindfolded, were instructed to press one of two adjacent buttons, spaced 1 cm apart. The robot finger was 2.5 cm across, allowing it to depress both buttons simultaneously if positioned inaccurately. Button locations were randomized between trials to prevent memorization.
- **Peg-Rolling Task:** Participants were instructed to roll a peg forward until it reached a hard stop, then roll it backward to its starting position. This task involved continuous contact and repositioning, requiring a sense of the peg's position relative to the finger.

**Data and Statistical Analysis:**
Trajectory data were collected at 50 Hz.
- **Naturalness:** Two-dimensional Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance was calculated between each teleoperated trajectory and the corresponding direct-manipulation baseline trajectories. DTW distances were normalized by each participant's mean DTW distance across all trials.
- **Task-Specific Metrics:**
 - Button task: Mean overshoot distance beyond the target button and total number of direction reversals ("switchbacks").
 - Peg-rolling task: Mean stroke length, mean number of strokes per trial, and the number of slips (when the finger rolled off the peg onto the underlying surface).
- **Consistency and Variability:**
 - **Within-participant consistency:** Standard deviation of DTW distance and completion time.
 - **Inter-trial variability (within-participant):** Pairwise DTW distances between trials from the same participant within each feedback condition.
 - **Aggregated dataset consistency (multi-operator):** Pairwise DTW distances between all trials combined from all participants for each feedback condition.
- **Statistical Tests:** Linear mixed-effects models (LME) with random intercepts were used for normalized DTW distances and completion times, followed by ANOVA and post-hoc coefficient tests (Holm-Bonferroni corrected). One-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for task-specific metrics, variability measures, and questionnaire responses. Mann-Whitney U tests were used for aggregated inter-trial DTW distances.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the influence of spatially distributed tactile feedback on teleoperated manipulation across two distinct tasks.

**Spatially Resolved Feedback and Natural Movement:**
- **Button Discrimination Task:**
 - Fully localized (Full) cutaneous feedback brought teleoperated movements closer to natural human performance. It significantly reduced both the average overshoot distance ($p < 0.001$) and the number of direction reversals ("switchbacks", $p \le 0.007$) compared to the Off, 1D, and 2D conditions (Tables S1, S2).
 - Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance, a measure of naturalness, was significantly modulated by feedback resolution ($p < 0.001$). All levels of cutaneous feedback (1D, 2D, Full) resulted in significantly smaller DTW distances than the Off condition ($p < 0.001$). Crucially, the Full condition produced significantly smaller DTW distances than the 1D condition ($p = 0.014$), indicating that higher spatial resolution improved naturalness beyond uniform feedback (Table S3).
 - Within-participant variability of DTW distance was significantly lower in the Full condition compared to 2D ($p = 0.013$), 1D ($p < 0.001$), and Off ($p < 0.001$) conditions, indicating more consistent natural movements (Table S4).
 - Task completion time was also affected ($p < 0.001$), with any level of cutaneous feedback accelerating execution relative to the Off condition ($p < 0.001$). However, the difference in completion times between 1D and Full conditions did not reach statistical significance after correction ($p_{adj} = 0.074$), suggesting that naturalness gains were proportionally larger than speed gains at higher resolutions (Table S5).
 - Variability in task completion time was significantly lower with Full feedback compared to 2D ($p = 0.003$), 1D ($p = 0.003$), and Off ($p < 0.001$) conditions (Table S6).

- **Peg-Rolling Task:**
 - The Full condition produced significantly fewer roll-offs onto the underlying surface ($p \le 0.039$), significantly longer strokes ($p \le 0.004$), and significantly fewer strokes per trial ($p \le 0.014$) compared to any other condition (Tables S7, S8, S9). These metrics indicate improved dynamic manipulation and a better sense of peg position.
 - DTW distances were strongly modulated by feedback resolution ($p < 0.001$). Both 2D and Full feedback resulted in significantly smaller DTW distances than the Off condition ($p < 0.001$). The Full feedback produced more natural trajectories than both the 1D ($p < 0.001$) and 2D ($p = 0.003$) conditions (Table S10).
 - Within-participant variability of DTW distance under Full feedback was significantly lower than in any other condition ($p \le 0.005$) (Table S11).
 - Completion time was strongly affected by feedback resolution ($p < 0.001$). Full feedback led to significantly faster completion times than 2D and 1D ($p < 0.001$), and 2D was faster than 1D ($p = 0.001$). 2D and Full were faster than Off ($p < 0.001$), while 1D was not significantly faster than Off ($p_{adj} = 0.112$) (Table S12).
 - Completion times under Full feedback were significantly more consistent than those under coarser feedback conditions ($p = 0.003$) (Table S13).

**Subjective Experience and Mental Demand:**
- Post-experiment questionnaires revealed that participants reported progressively lower mental demand (Q2) as feedback resolution increased across both tasks. Significant reductions in mental demand were observed in comparisons between adjacent feedback conditions (e.g., Off to 1D, 1D to 2D, 2D to Full) ($p \le 0.047$) (Tables S14, S15). This suggests that higher-resolution feedback allowed operators to engage more intuitive sensorimotor control strategies.

**Implications for Learning from Demonstration:**
- Spatially distributed cutaneous feedback improved the consistency and quality of datasets relevant for training autonomous robot policies. Trajectories collected with higher-resolution feedback exhibited a more compact state-space distribution, as visually demonstrated by occupancy heat maps and overlaid trajectories.
- Within-participant inter-trial DTW distance (a measure of trajectory variability) was significantly lower under spatially distributed cutaneous feedback compared to no cutaneous feedback ($p \le 0.003$) for both tasks (Tables S16, S17).
- When combining demonstrations from all participants to form multi-operator datasets, trajectory variability in the spatially distributed feedback dataset was substantially lower than in all other conditions ($p < 0.001$) (Tables S18, S19). This indicates that high-resolution feedback produces more consistent aggregated demonstrations, which are generally more suitable for policy learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides evidence that the spatial resolution of cutaneous feedback critically influences not only the speed of teleoperated task completion but also the naturalness of the operator's movements. This distinction between task speed and movement naturalness is a significant contribution, indicating that previous haptic feedback systems, while improving speed, may not have fully addressed the underlying movement quality.

**Enhancing Robot Learning from Demonstration:**
The findings have substantial implications for robot learning from demonstration. By promoting more natural, consistent, and less variable teleoperated trajectories (e.g., fewer overshoots, reduced direction reversals, and concentrated state-space coverage), spatially distributed tactile feedback can generate higher-quality datasets. Such datasets, characterized by reduced within- and between-operator variability, are beneficial for training autonomous robot policies, potentially leading to more robust, efficient, and human-like learned behaviors. This addresses a known challenge in imitation learning where diffuse state-space coverage or excessive variability can hinder policy acquisition.

**Improving Operator Experience and Proprioceptive Awareness:**
The observed reduction in mental demand with increasing feedback resolution suggests that spatially distributed feedback allows operators to utilize their natural sensorimotor control strategies, making teleoperation more intuitive and less cognitively taxing. This could lead to reduced operator fatigue and support longer, more productive teleoperation sessions in applications such as surgical robotics or hazardous environment manipulation. Furthermore, the peg-rolling task results hint at a potential enhancement of proprioceptive awareness, where richer cutaneous feedback may improve the operator's state estimation regarding the manipulator's position relative to the environment, thereby aiding in movement planning.

**Task-Dependent Design Considerations:**
The study highlights that the value of feedback resolution is not uniform across all tasks. The benefits of spatially distributed feedback are most pronounced when the tactile information provides cues distinct from kinesthetic feedback and when the relevant contact features can be effectively represented by the display. This suggests that future teleoperation systems should consider matching the tactile rendering resolution to the specific spatial information requirements of the task, rather than assuming higher resolution is always universally optimal. For instance, in tasks where kinesthetic feedback already conveys most relevant information or where features are too small for the display, the benefits of very high spatial resolution might diminish.

**Future Directions:**
Several avenues for future research are identified. These include exploring alternative metrics for assessing natural behavior (e.g., action sequences, individual joint motions), generalizing these findings to higher-DoF systems and multi-finger telemanipulation, and investigating more complex dexterous tasks. Establishing the precise resolution required for optimal natural manipulation and identifying where diminishing returns occur would provide valuable design targets for future tactile displays and teleoperation systems. Additionally, further studies are needed to establish a causal link between rendered cutaneous resolution and improvements in proprioceptive awareness.

In conclusion, this work demonstrates that providing spatially distributed tactile feedback is essential for bridging the gap between human and teleoperated dexterity, yielding benefits not only for immediate operator performance but also for the long-term advancement of autonomous robot learning.
