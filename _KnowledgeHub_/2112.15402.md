# Relational Experience Replay: Continual Learning by Adaptively Tuning Task-wise Relationship

> [!summary] Summary
> Relational Experience Replay (RER) introduces a bi-level learning framework that dynamically adjusts sample weights to balance new knowledge acquisition and old knowledge retention in continual learning. It uses a lightweight neural network to generate adaptive weights based on task relationships and sample importance, achieving average accuracy improvements of 1.61% to 5.81% on CIFAR-100 across various baselines and effectively reducing catastrophic forgetting.

## Problem

- Deep neural networks suffer from catastrophic forgetting in continual learning when incrementally learning new tasks, overwriting previously acquired knowledge.
- Rehearsal-based continual learning methods often rely on fixed hyperparameters to balance new and old task losses, failing to adapt to dynamic task relationships and evolving data distributions.
- Existing methods typically treat all samples within a task or class equally, neglecting the varying importance of individual samples, such as those near decision boundaries.

## Method

- Proposes **Relational Experience Replay (RER)**, a ==bi-level optimization== framework comprising a Main Net for learning and a lightweight ==Relation Replay Net (RRN)== for adaptive weight generation.
- The **RRN** dynamically estimates sample weights for both new task data and memory buffer samples, using their ==loss values== and ==logit norms== to capture semantic and distributional similarities.
- Employs a ==bi-level optimization== process where the inner loop updates the Main Net using **RRN**-generated weights (plasticity), and the outer loop optimizes the **RRN** to ensure stable consolidation of old knowledge by minimizing ==empirical risk== on memory buffer data (stability).

## Results

- RER consistently improved the average accuracy of integrated rehearsal-based baselines (ER, ER-ACE, DER++) by **1.61%** to **5.81%** on CIFAR-100 and up to **3.08%** on CIFAR-10, outperforming state-of-the-art methods.
- The method effectively mitigated catastrophic forgetting, demonstrating better retention of old task knowledge and reduced Backward Transfer (BWT) compared to baselines across CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet datasets.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of the bi-level optimization framework, with the RRN's adaptive weights enhancing generalization and managing the stability-plasticity trade-off more effectively than end-to-end training or fixed weighting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically generated sample weights, derived from task relationships and individual sample importance, are more effective than fixed hyperparameters in balancing the stability-plasticity dilemma in continual learning.
> - A bi-level optimization structure, where a meta-network learns to generate weights for the main learning network, is crucial for effectively adapting to evolving task relationships and mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
> - The Relation Replay Net implicitly models task-wise relationships by leveraging gradient-based similarity between new and old task samples, allowing it to adaptively prioritize learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep neural networks have achieved notable successes across various computer vision tasks, including classification, semantic segmentation, and object detection, when trained with static, offline datasets. However, these models typically struggle in Continual Learning (CL) scenarios. In CL, knowledge is incrementally acquired from data streams with non-stationary distributions, leading to a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting, where the model overwrites previously learned knowledge when new tasks are introduced. Addressing this challenge is a central problem in the field of continual learning.

Existing CL algorithms are generally categorized into three types: model-based approaches, which dynamically modify network architectures; regularization-based approaches, which impose constraints on important parameters to preserve prior knowledge; and rehearsal-based approaches, which store a small subset of data from old tasks in a memory buffer and replay them during the learning of new tasks. Rehearsal-based methods are often favored for their relatively stable and effective performance in mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

Despite their advantages, current rehearsal-based methods face limitations. Many of these approaches, such as Experience Replay (ER) and DER++, commonly treat each new task equally without adequately considering the dynamic relationship or similarity between old and new tasks. The balance between learning new information (plasticity) and retaining previous knowledge (stability), often referred to as the 'stability-plasticity' dilemma, is typically managed by manually tuning fixed hyperparameters (e.g., $\lambda$ in ER, or $\lambda_M$, $\gamma_M$ in DER++). This static approach fails to account for the continually varying data distributions and task relationships throughout the learning process. Furthermore, these methods often overlook the varying importance of individual samples within the same class, neglecting that some samples might be more difficult or informative for model training (e.g., samples near decision boundaries).

This work positions itself within the rehearsal-based continual learning paradigm, specifically aiming to overcome the limitations of fixed hyperparameters and the neglect of dynamic task relationships and sample importance. By proposing an adaptive, automated weighting strategy, the research seeks to enhance the model's ability to navigate the stability-plasticity dilemma more effectively than existing methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a continual learning framework that can adaptively balance the acquisition of new knowledge (plasticity) with the retention of previously learned information (stability), thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This is achieved by explicitly addressing two critical factors often overlooked in existing rehearsal-based continual learning methods:
1. **Dynamic Task-wise Relationships:** The relationship between new and old tasks is not static but changes as new data streams arrive. For instance, new tasks involving semantically related classes might interfere more significantly with old knowledge than unrelated tasks. Current methods often use fixed hyperparameters to balance new and old task losses, which cannot adapt to these dynamic relationships. The objective is to enable the model to dynamically adjust its focus on old versus new tasks based on their evolving relationships.
2. **Sample Importance within Each Task:** Not all data points contribute equally to model training. "Easy" samples may represent core class features, while "hard" samples (e.g., near decision boundaries) are crucial for refining classification. Existing methods typically treat all samples equally within a task or class, overlooking this intrinsic difference in sample importance. The objective is to assign appropriate weights to individual samples to optimize their contribution to learning.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the shortcomings of current rehearsal-based continual learning approaches. Conventional methods often sum the losses from new tasks and memory buffer samples with manually predetermined and fixed weights. This approach assumes a constant trade-off between stability and plasticity, which is not realistic given the dynamic nature of data in continual learning. Fixing these weights at the outset of training prevents the model from adapting its learning strategy as task relationships evolve. For example, a new task closely related to a previous one might cause severe forgetting if not adequately counteracted by emphasizing the old task's samples. Conversely, an unrelated new task might not require significant re-emphasis on old knowledge. By addressing these issues, the authors aim to develop a more flexible and effective continual learning system capable of accumulating new knowledge while robustly consolidating existing knowledge.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

To address the identified challenges, the authors propose **Relational Experience Replay (RER)**, a bi-level learning framework. The core idea of RER is to assign appropriate, dynamically adjusted weights to training samples based on task relationships between new and old data, and the importance of individual samples within each task.

The RER framework comprises two main components:
1. **Main Net ($f(\cdot; \theta)$):** This is the primary classification neural network responsible for continual learning (e.g., a ResNet-18 backbone). Its parameters ($\theta$) are updated to learn from the stream of tasks.
2. **Relation Replay Net (RRN, $h(\cdot; \phi)$):** A lightweight neural network parameterized by $\phi$, whose function is to estimate and generate the sample weights for both new task samples and memory buffer samples.

The training process is structured as a bi-level optimization problem:

**Inner-Loop Optimization (Addressing Plasticity):**
The inner loop focuses on updating the Main Net's parameters ($\theta$) to learn from the newly presented task while considering the relationship with old tasks. For each training iteration, a batch of new task data ($B_D$) and a batch of data from the memory buffer ($B_M$) are sampled. These are combined to form a paired training batch ($B_{tr}$). The Main Net is trained using a weighted loss function:
$L_{tr}(\mathcal{B}_{tr}; \theta, \phi) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \lambda^D_i(\phi)L_{CE}(x^D_i; \theta) + \lambda^M_i(\phi)L_{CE}(x^M_i; \theta)$
Here, $L_{CE}$ is the cross-entropy loss. Crucially, the sample weights ($\lambda^D_i(\phi)$ for new task samples and $\lambda^M_i(\phi)$ for memory buffer samples) are dynamically generated by the RRN, rather than being fixed hyperparameters. This allows the Main Net to adapt its learning based on the current task relationships and sample importance. The Main Net is updated using one-step gradient descent: $\theta_{k+1}(\phi) = \theta_k - \eta_\theta \nabla_\theta L_{tr}(\mathcal{B}_{tr}; \theta_k, \phi_k)$.

**Outer-Loop Optimization (Addressing Stability):**
The outer loop is responsible for optimizing the RRN's parameters ($\phi$). The objective of the outer loop is to ensure that the RRN generates weights that lead to a stable consolidation of previously learned knowledge, acting as a meta-optimizer for the Main Net. The RRN is optimized by minimizing the empirical risk of a separate batch of data ($B_{bf}$) sampled from the memory buffer. This memory buffer is considered a relatively balanced dataset of old tasks, guiding the RRN to emphasize stability for existing knowledge. The outer-loop loss is defined as:
$L_{bf}(\mathcal{B}_{bf}; \theta^*(\phi)) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} L_{bf}(x_i; \theta^*(\phi))$
where $\theta^*(\phi)$ represents the Main Net parameters after the inner-loop update (i.e., $\theta_{k+1}(\phi)$). The RRN parameters are updated using one-step gradient descent: $\phi_{k+1} = \phi_k - \eta_\phi \nabla_\phi L_{bf}(\mathcal{B}_{bf}; \theta_{k+1}(\phi_k))$.

**Relation Replay Net (RRN) Design:**
The RRN is designed to take "information abundant" inputs to generate meaningful weights. For each paired sample ($x^D_i$ from new task, $x^M_i$ from memory buffer), the RRN takes their respective loss values ($L_{tr}(x^D_i)$ and $L_{tr}(x^M_i)$) and logit norms ($\|z^D_i\|_2$ and $\|z^M_i\|_2$, where $z$ are predicted logits) as input. This combination of label and feature information allows the RRN to capture semantic and distributional similarities, which are then mapped to the output weights [$\lambda^D_i(\phi)$, $\lambda^M_i(\phi)$] via a two-hidden-layer neural network.

**Theoretical Analysis:**
The paper theoretically demonstrates that the RRN implicitly models task-wise relationships. The updating formulation of $\phi$ (Eq. 7) is shown to depend on the coefficient $G(j)$ (Eq. 9 and Eq. 16), which represents the mean dot product similarity between the gradient of buffer samples and that of training samples within each class. This gradient-based similarity captures the relationship between the new task and previously seen classes.

**Implementation Details and Adaptability:**
- **Warm-up Stage:** During the initial phases of a new task (Iter$_{warm}$), the RRN is updated, but the Main Net is trained with preset fixed weights. After this warm-up, the RRN-generated weights guide Main Net training.
- **Update Interval:** To manage computational cost, the RRN is updated once for every `Interval` steps of Main Net updates.
- **Baseline Integration:** The RER framework is demonstrated to be adaptable to various rehearsal-based baselines, including ER, ER-ACE, and DER++, by modifying how the inner-loop loss function is constructed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of the Relational Experience Replay (RER) method was evaluated through extensive experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny ImageNet datasets, comparing it against multiple state-of-the-art continual learning methods and its base rehearsal-based counterparts.

**Overall Performance Improvement:**
- RER consistently improved the performance of the three integrated rehearsal-based baselines: Experience Replay (ER), ER with Asymmetric Cross-Entropy (ER-ACE), and Dark Experience Replay++ (DER++). These enhanced versions are termed Relational-ER (RER), Relational-ER-ACE (RER-ACE), and Relational-DER (RDER), respectively.
- **CIFAR-10:** RDER achieved a 3.08% absolute performance gain over its baseline DER++ in the Class-IL setting with a memory buffer of 200 samples. It also demonstrated higher classification accuracy and reduced Backward Transfer (BWT) compared to other state-of-the-art methods across various memory buffer sizes and both Class-IL and Task-IL settings (Table I).
- **Tiny ImageNet:** Similar improvements were observed on Tiny ImageNet, with RER-enhanced baselines generally achieving the best results, improving both Average Accuracy (ACC) and BWT (Table I). This suggests a better trade-off between plasticity and stability.
- **CIFAR-100:** The method consistently showed significant ACC improvements across different buffer sizes for all three baselines. For instance, in the Class-IL setting with a buffer size of 100, RER, RER-ACE, and RDER improved their respective baselines by 2.63%, 1.61%, and 5.81% (Table II).

**Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting:**
- Visualizations of accuracy changes for each task during the continual learning process (Fig. 4 and Fig. 8 in Appendix II-C) indicated that RER-enhanced methods better mitigated forgetting for previous tasks compared to their baselines. This suggests a more effective consolidation of old knowledge.

**Adaptive Task-wise Relationship Modeling:**
- An experiment on CIFAR-10 investigating task similarity effects (Fig. 7) revealed that RER dynamically adjusts sample weights based on the semantic relationship between new and old tasks.
 - When the new task was semantically relevant to the old task (Setup 1), the RRN tended to assign larger weights to old task samples and smaller weights to new task samples. This indicated RER's ability to prioritize stability to counteract potential interference from similar new knowledge.
 - When the new task was semantically irrelevant (Setup 2), the sample weights for old and new tasks were more dispersed, suggesting that the model dynamically determined importance without strong bias, as interference was lower. This adaptive weighting led to a smaller performance gap between semantic-relevant and irrelevant setups for RER compared to ER, signifying reduced interference.

**Effectiveness of Bi-level Optimization:**
- An ablation study (Table III) compared RDER with a "Vanilla" approach (end-to-end training of Main Net and RRN without bi-level optimization). The Vanilla method's performance was inferior to both RDER and the baseline DER++, sometimes even impairing performance. This empirically validated that the bi-level optimization framework is crucial for generating meaningful, informative weights that enhance generalization and effectively manage the stability-plasticity trade-off.

**Outer-Loop Training Set Construction:**
- Experiments exploring different strategies for constructing the outer-loop training set (Table IV) demonstrated that using the entire memory buffer (RDER) for RRN training was more effective than splitting the buffer (Split RDER) into separate inner and outer loop sets. The full buffer approach led to better overall performance, especially with limited buffer sizes, by providing a richer dataset to guide the RRN.

**Impact of Small Buffer Sizes:**
- RER demonstrated its effectiveness even with very small memory buffer sizes (e.g., 50 or 100 samples on CIFAR-10, Table V). RDER, for instance, improved DER++ by approximately 3.05% with a buffer size of 50 in the Class-IL setting, highlighting its capability to extract maximum information from limited memory.

**Ablation Study on Hyperparameters:**
- **Warm-up Stage (Iter$_{warm}$):** An adequate warm-up stage for the RRN was found to be critical (Table VI). Using a very short warm-up (e.g., 17 iterations) led to significant performance deterioration, indicating insufficient learning of task relationships. An overly long warm-up also showed a slight performance drop, likely due to prolonged use of preset, potentially suboptimal weights. An Iter$_{warm}$ value around half the total iteration epochs per task was found to perform optimally.
- **RRN Updating Interval (Interval):** The frequency of RRN updates also impacted performance (Table VII). A small interval (e.g., 1) caused performance degradation due to training oscillations and computational overhead. A large interval (e.g., 10) led to suboptimal weights due to inadequate RRN training. An interval of 5 was identified as a balanced choice, providing good accuracy and computational efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method that addresses a fundamental challenge in continual learning, the stability-plasticity dilemma, through an adaptive and automated approach. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Automated Weighting Strategy:** The primary contribution is the development of an automated sample weighting strategy for continual learning. Unlike previous methods that rely on fixed, manually tuned hyperparameters, RER's Relation Replay Net (RRN) dynamically generates weights for samples from new tasks and the memory buffer. This removes the burden of manual tuning and allows the model to adapt in real-time to evolving data distributions and task relationships.
- **Improved Stability-Plasticity Trade-off:** By adaptively tuning task-wise relationships and sample importance, the proposed framework demonstrates a more effective balance between retaining old knowledge and acquiring new knowledge. This capability is evidenced by consistent improvements in both average accuracy and backward transfer metrics across multiple benchmarks and baselines.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The method's ability to dynamically adjust focus means it can better handle scenarios where new tasks are either highly related (and thus potentially interfering) or unrelated to previous knowledge. This leads to more generalized and robust continual learning systems that are less susceptible to catastrophic forgetting.
- **Theoretical Foundation for Adaptive Weighting:** The theoretical proof demonstrating that the RRN implicitly models task relationships through gradient similarity provides insight into the underlying mechanisms of the proposed adaptive weighting. This can encourage further theoretical exploration into dynamically weighting samples in complex learning environments.
- **Broad Applicability and Extensibility:** The RER framework is designed to be easily integrated with existing rehearsal-based continual learning methods (e.g., ER, ER-ACE, DER++). Its demonstrated effectiveness across diverse baselines and datasets suggests its potential for widespread adoption and adaptation in various continual learning applications.
- **Resource Efficiency:** The method's ability to achieve significant performance gains even with small memory buffer sizes (e.g., 50 samples) indicates its efficiency in leveraging limited stored data, which is crucial for real-world continual learning scenarios where memory constraints are common.
- **Future Research Direction:** This work can serve as a foundation for future research in meta-learning for continual learning, particularly concerning adaptive learning strategies, dynamic curriculum learning, or sophisticated weighting mechanisms that consider other contextual factors in streaming data.

In conclusion, Relational Experience Replay contributes to the field of continual learning by offering a flexible and robust framework that systematically addresses the stability-plasticity dilemma. Its practical applicability and strong empirical performance suggest a valuable step towards developing more intelligent and adaptive machine learning systems capable of lifelong learning.
