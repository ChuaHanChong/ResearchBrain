# Learning Aerodynamics for the Control of Flying Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a control framework for jet-powered humanoid robots, like iRonCub-Mk1, that enables stable flight in windy environments by integrating data-driven aerodynamic models derived from CFD simulations and wind tunnel experiments into a whole-body controller.

## Problem

- Controlling complex, non-aerodynamic humanoid robots in flight, especially with external winds, due to highly turbulent and separated flows.
- Lack of explicit, accurate aerodynamic models for articulated, bluff-body humanoid robots.
- Limitations of single-mode locomotion for humanoids in diverse and challenging environments.

## Method

- Designed and built iRonCub-Mk1, a 43.3 kg jet-powered humanoid robot with custom jetpack and reinforced forearms for thrust and attitude control.
- Developed a comprehensive methodology combining wind tunnel experiments and extensive CFD simulations to generate an aerodynamic dataset for the robot's complex shape.
- ==Trained Deep Neural Network== and simpler Axisymmetric models for real-time aerodynamic force prediction and integrated them into an aerodynamic-aware whole-body controller.

## Results

- The mechanical design of iRonCub-Mk1 was successfully validated through FEM analysis, confirming structural integrity for jet engine integration.
- Aerodynamic models (DNN and Axisymmetric) were accurately derived from the CFD dataset, with the DNN achieving a maximum Relative Mean Error of 3x10^-6 on validation data, and CFD models validated against wind tunnel data with an average error below **10%**.
- The aerodynamic-aware controller enabled the robot to complete all designed flight maneuvers in simulation with external wind, while a baseline controller failed to stabilize, demonstrating the necessity of aerodynamic compensation. Ground experiments also showed appropriate compensatory responses on the physical hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Changes in robot joint configurations significantly impact aerodynamic forces (up to 20% variation) more than airspeed, suggesting Reynolds-independence for modeling in certain operating ranges.
> - Combining CFD simulations and wind tunnel data with machine learning effectively models complex aerodynamic forces on highly non-aerodynamic, articulated robot bodies.
> - Explicitly accounting for aerodynamic forces via an aerodynamic-aware controller is crucial for stable flight of humanoid robots in external wind conditions, unlike baseline controllers that neglect these effects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the growing field of multi-modal locomotion robotics, particularly focusing on aerial-terrestrial systems. Existing multi-modal robots often prioritize lightweight designs and low thrust-to-weight propulsion mechanisms, which can limit their payload capacity and overall task versatility. The paper positions itself by addressing the goal of equipping heavier humanoid robots, known for their bipedal locomotion and manipulation capabilities, with flight capabilities. This approach aims to combine the benefits of humanoids with aerial mobility, offering potential for applications in diverse and challenging environments such as transportation, exploration, and search and rescue missions.

The work builds upon and extends several distinct areas of literature:
- **Task-driven mechanical design of multi-modal flying platforms**: While previous research has explored various designs from tailsitter drones to bio-inspired robots, this paper specifically focuses on integrating jet engines into a humanoid robot to achieve a high thrust-to-weight ratio. This differentiation is critical for enabling heavier platforms with substantial payload capacities to fly, moving beyond the lightweight designs common in current multi-modal aerial robots.
- **Aerial systems control with and without aerodynamic modeling**: Much of the literature on Vertical Take-Off and Landing (VTOL) systems, especially for indoor or low-speed operations, tends to disregard aerodynamic forces. When aerodynamics are considered, they are often treated as external disturbances or modeled using simplified approaches (e.g., Blade Element Momentum theory for propellers, simplified models for fixed-wing aircraft). This paper addresses the significant challenge of modeling aerodynamic effects on a flying humanoid robot, a platform characterized by its non-aerodynamic, multi-link bluff body shape and the resulting complex aerodynamic interference and separated flow regions. It moves beyond disturbance estimation to explicit modeling for potential aggressive maneuvers.
- **Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations and wind tunnel tests of bluff bodies**: Studies on bluff body aerodynamics often concentrate on simpler shapes. As body complexity increases, tailored models are required to address the unsteady and multi-scale nature of turbulent flows. The paper leverages established CFD techniques (RANS equations with turbulence models like SST k-ω) and validates them through empirical wind tunnel experiments. Its novelty in this context lies in applying these detailed methodologies to a full-scale humanoid robot and integrating the derived aerodynamic models into a whole-body control framework, which represents a comprehensive approach not typically found in the existing literature for such complex platforms.

In essence, this work integrates technological advancements in robot design with scientific contributions in fluid dynamics and control theory to enable robust flight for humanoid robots in the presence of aerodynamic forces, bridging a gap in the current research landscape where detailed aerodynamic modeling for complex, heavy, multi-modal robots is less common.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable flying humanoid robots to operate effectively in environments with external winds by developing a comprehensive approach for modeling and controlling aerodynamic forces. This objective is addressed from both a technological and a scientific perspective.

The detailed motivations and specific objectives include:

- **Enabling Aerial Capabilities for Humanoid Robots**: The core motivation is to enhance the versatility of humanoid robots by endowing them with flight capabilities. Humanoid robots typically excel in terrestrial locomotion and manipulation tasks. By adding aerial mobility, the goal is to overcome the limitations of single-mode locomotion, allowing these robots to navigate diverse and challenging environments that might involve obstacles or terrains impassable by walking alone.
- **Addressing Challenges of Aerodynamic Forces on Humanoids**: Flying humanoid robots, unlike streamlined aerial vehicles, possess complex, non-aerodynamic shapes with numerous links. This geometry leads to highly turbulent, separated flows and significant aerodynamic interference between links. The lack of an explicit, accurate model for these forces poses a substantial challenge for stable and effective flight control, especially in the presence of non-zero relative wind.
- **Technological Contribution: Hardware Development**:
 - **Design of iRonCub-Mk1**: A specific objective was the mechanical design and development of iRonCub-Mk1, a jet-powered humanoid robot. The motivation here was to integrate powerful, compact jet engines to achieve a high thrust-to-weight ratio. This is crucial for a 40 kg robot, differentiating it from lighter multi-modal platforms and allowing for potentially heavy payloads. The design focused on optimizing jet engine integration into the existing iCub v2.7 platform, including the creation of a jetpack and custom forearms.
 - **Hardware for Wind Tunnel Experiments**: Another technological objective involved modifying the iRonCub-Mk1 and the wind tunnel setup to enable precise measurements of overall aerodynamic forces, moments, and surface pressure distributions. This was essential for empirical data collection and validation of simulation models.
- **Scientific Contribution: Modeling and Control Methodology**:
 - **Comprehensive Aerodynamic Modeling**: The central scientific motivation was to develop a comprehensive methodology to model aerodynamic forces acting on a flying humanoid robot. This involved leveraging Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations and validating them through wind tunnel experiments. The aim was to create an extensive aerodynamic dataset.
 - **Learning-Based Aerodynamic Models**: A key objective was to train both classical (linear regression) and learning-based (Deep Neural Network) models from this dataset. These models aim to predict the distributed aerodynamic forces based on the robot's attitude and joint configurations, enabling real-time force estimation.
 - **Aerodynamic-Aware Control Design**: The ultimate scientific objective was to integrate these learned aerodynamic models into a dynamics simulator to design and validate aerodynamic-aware controllers. The motivation was to develop a control strategy that can actively compensate for or potentially exploit aerodynamic forces, leading to more stable, precise, and robust flight compared to controllers that neglect these effects.
 - **Robustness and Generalizability**: The methodology also sought to evaluate the robustness of the control strategy to modeling errors and to establish a framework that could be generalized to other shape-shifting drones under aerodynamic disturbances.

In summary, the research is motivated by the desire to expand the capabilities of humanoid robots into the aerial domain, driven by the need for robust control in aerodynamically complex environments, and achieved through a rigorous integration of mechanical engineering, fluid dynamics, and advanced control and learning techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-faceted methodology encompassing mechanical design, empirical experimentation, computational fluid dynamics, machine learning for modeling, and control system design and simulation.

#### 3.1. iRonCub-Mk1 Mechanical Design

The iRonCub-Mk1, an adaptation of the iCub v2.7 humanoid robot, was designed for jet propulsion.
- **Actuator Selection**: JetCat P220-RXi jet engines were chosen for primary lift (maximum thrust ~22 kg each) due to their high thrust-to-weight ratio and fuel efficiency compared to electric propellers, especially for a 40 kg robot aiming for multi-minute flights.
- **Engine Placement**: Two P220-RXi engines were positioned on the robot's back via a custom-designed jetpack for primary lift. Two smaller P100-RX JetCat engines (maximum thrust ~15 kg each) were integrated into custom forearms, replacing the robot's original forearms and hands, to provide attitude control through thrust vectoring and arm joint movements.
- **Structural Integrity**: A stainless steel spine was permanently installed on the robot's back to reinforce its mechanical structure and distribute jet engine reaction forces. Both the jetpack and forearm assemblies were subjected to Finite Element Method (FEM) analysis using Ansys Mechanical software to ensure structural integrity, with maximum stresses calculated to be at least five times lower than the ultimate tensile strength of the materials.

#### 3.2. Wind Tunnel Experiments

Experiments were conducted at the Politecnico di Milano (GVPM) large wind tunnel to characterize the robot's aerodynamic properties.
- **Facility and Setup**: Tests were performed in a 4m x 3.84m low-turbulence test section, capable of wind speeds up to 55 m/s. The full-scale iRonCub-Mk1 was mounted on a remotely driven pylon and turntable, allowing for automatic adjustment of pitch and yaw angles (angles of attack α and side-slip β). The setup ensured a low blockage ratio (≤3%).
- **Data Collection**: 400 measurements across 54 tests were recorded. This included global aerodynamic forces and moments using a six-component strain gauge and distributed surface pressure data from 124 pressure taps embedded in custom nylon covers. Measurements were averaged over 10-second intervals.
- **Test Configurations**: Experiments included "hovering tests" (fixed α=90°, varying β from -90° to 90°) and "flight tests" (fixed β=0°, varying α from 25° to 65°), as well as tests with different robot joint configurations.
- **Key Observations**: Initial analysis indicated that changes in robot joint configurations resulted in more significant variations (up to 20%) in global force areas compared to variations induced by different airspeeds (Reynolds number). This observation informed the decision to prioritize joint configuration over Reynolds number variations in subsequent CFD dataset generation, assuming Reynolds-independence for the studied flow conditions.

#### 3.3. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) Simulations

CFD simulations were used for validation against wind tunnel data and for generating a large aerodynamic dataset.
- **Fluid Flow Assumptions**: Due to the robot's operational speed and altitude, the flow was assumed to be incompressible. The steady Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations were employed with two turbulence models (Realizable k-ε and SST k-ω) to model turbulent flow, balancing computational efficiency with accuracy.
- **Geometry Simplification**: Non-regular surfaces, small gaps, and cavities in the CAD models of both the robot and the wind tunnel support were simplified using PTC Creo and Ansys SpaceClaim to facilitate mesh generation while preserving main geometric features.
- **Validation Simulations**: A first set of 104 simulations included both the robot model and the wind tunnel support geometry. These were compared against wind tunnel experimental data, showing an average error in aerodynamic force area magnitude below 10%, with the SST k-ω model exhibiting higher fidelity. This model was subsequently chosen for dataset generation.
- **Dataset Generation Simulations**: A second set of approximately 8500 simulations focused solely on the robot model in a spherical fluid domain (free airstream). To manage computational cost, a mesh sensitivity analysis was performed, leading to the adoption of a coarser mesh with approximately 0.33 x 10^6 elements (a 97% reduction from the validation mesh), which still maintained overall aerodynamic force accuracy within 15% of experimental data.
- **Automated Framework**: An automated CFD framework, built using the Ansys pyFluent open-source API, was developed. This allowed for automatic generation of meshes and execution of simulations for 24 distinct robot joint configurations, 19 pitch angles (0° to 180°), and 18 yaw angles (-160° to 180°), encompassing all possible relative wind velocity directions. The dataset was augmented by mirroring non-symmetric configurations to enforce symmetry.

#### 3.4. Aerodynamic Models for Learning and Online Control

Two distinct aerodynamic models were developed from the CFD dataset.
- **Deep Neural Network (DNN) Model**: A DNN was designed to predict the three aerodynamic force components acting on each robot link's Center of Mass (CoM).
 - **Inputs**: Robot joint positions (19 DoFs) and relative wind velocity direction.
 - **Architecture**: 9 fully connected internal linear layers, each with 1048 neurons and ReLU activation functions. Input and output layers matched the dimensions of input and output vectors (22 and 39, respectively).
 - **Training**: The network was trained on 80% of the augmented CFD dataset for 60,000 epochs using Mean Squared Error (MSE) as the loss function, the Adam optimizer, and 10% dropout regularization to prevent overfitting.
- **Axisymmetric Links Model**: A simpler, more interpretable model was developed for real-time control integration and robustness analysis.
 - **Assumptions**: Each robot link was considered isolated and axisymmetric, neglecting aerodynamic interference and Reynolds number effects.
 - **Formulation**: The model expresses drag (CDA) and normal force (CNA) coefficients as sinusoidal functions of the local angle of attack, derived from literature models for spheres and cylinders.
 - **Coefficient Identification**: Coefficients for these sinusoidal functions (up to 3rd order) were identified using a combination of Lasso and Least Squares linear regression on the CFD dataset, enhanced by mirroring for symmetric links.

#### 3.5. Aerodynamic Control Design and Simulation Environment

A whole-body momentum-based controller was designed and integrated into a simulation environment.
- **Aerodynamic Force Integration**: Aerodynamic forces predicted by either the DNN or Axisymmetric Model were transformed into generalized total aerodynamic wrenches acting on the robot. These wrenches were then incorporated into the robot's dynamics equations within the simulator.
- **Flight Control Design**: The control strategy extended previous work by accounting for aerodynamic effects in the centroidal momentum dynamics. It used relative degree augmentation and feedback linearization to control momentum. The control input (jet thrust rates and joint velocities) was optimized through a Quadratic Programming (QP) problem, which also incorporated a postural task to resolve actuation redundancy and hyperbolic tangent-based constraints on input variables.
- **Simulation Environment**: The control strategy was validated using a custom "Whole-Body Aerodynamic Simulator" implemented in MATLAB/Simulink, leveraging the iDynTree physics engine. This simulator could incorporate either the DNN or Axisymmetric Model for aerodynamics.
- **Validation Methodology**:
 - **Flight Simulations**: Compared a "Baseline Controller" (neglecting aerodynamics) with an "Aerodynamic-aware Controller" (using feedback from aerodynamic models) under a defined flight envelope with external wind.
 - **Robustness Analysis**: Tested the Aerodynamic-aware Controller's performance when the controller received feedback from the simpler Axisymmetric Model while the simulator used the more realistic DNN model for robot dynamics.
 - **Ground Experiments**: Conducted on the physical iRonCub-Mk1 (jets off). Artificial wind profiles were injected directly into the controller as feedback (without physical aerodynamic forces), and the robot's balancing responses (CoM adjustments, joint torques) were observed to evaluate the controller's reliability on hardware.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings across the technological and scientific aspects of developing flying humanoid robots.

#### 4.1. iRonCub-Mk1 Mechanical Design

The mechanical design process successfully produced iRonCub-Mk1, a 43.3 kg jet-powered humanoid robot. The integration of four jet engines (two P220-RXi on the back, two P100-RX on the forearms) was achieved through a custom jetpack and reinforced forearms. Finite Element Method (FEM) analysis confirmed the structural integrity of these modifications, demonstrating that developed stresses were at least five times lower than the ultimate tensile strength of the materials.

#### 4.2. Wind Tunnel Experiments

- **Aerodynamic Characteristics**: 400 measurements were conducted on the full-scale iRonCub-Mk1. The experiments confirmed that the robot, in its current non-aerodynamically optimized form, exhibited low aerodynamic efficiency, characteristic of bluff bodies.
- **Dominant Factors**: The results indicated that changes in robot joint configurations had a more substantial impact on the global aerodynamic force areas (up to 20% variation) than variations in airspeed (Reynolds number) within the tested operating range. This observation supported the assumption of Reynolds-independence for generating the CFD dataset.

#### 4.3. Aerodynamics CFD Simulations

- **Model Validation**: RANS simulations, particularly with the SST k-ω turbulence model, showed an average error below 10% when compared to wind tunnel experimental data for aerodynamic force areas.
- **Flow Characteristics**: Simulations visually confirmed the complex flow around the robot, characterized by a large separated flow region and a significant low-velocity, low-pressure wake, consistent with bluff body aerodynamics. Pressure drag was identified as the predominant component, accounting for approximately 95% of the total aerodynamic drag. High turbulent conditions throughout the wake and on the robot surface (viscosity ratio > 100) supported the choice of turbulence models.
- **Dataset Generation Efficiency**: A mesh sensitivity analysis demonstrated that a significant reduction in mesh elements (97%) for CFD simulations (to 0.33 x 10^6 elements) still allowed for the calculation of overall aerodynamic forces with an accuracy within 15% compared to experimental data. This efficiency enabled the generation of an extensive aerodynamic dataset of approximately 8500 simulations.

#### 4.4. Performances of the Aerodynamic Models

- **Deep Neural Network (DNN)**: The trained DNN model exhibited high accuracy in predicting aerodynamic forces on the validation set, achieving a maximum Relative Mean Error (RME) of 3x10^-6. Local prediction errors were observed to remain below 20% of the maximum force value.
- **Axisymmetric Links Model**: The simpler Axisymmetric Model, while providing a physically coherent representation of aerodynamic forces, showed a lower prediction accuracy with an RME approximately one order of magnitude higher than the DNN model.

#### 4.5. Validation on iRonCub Robot

- **Flight Simulations**:
 - The "Baseline Controller," which neglected aerodynamic effects, consistently failed to stabilize the robot, leading to loss of balance during flight maneuvers in the presence of external wind.
 - The "Aerodynamic-aware Controller," incorporating feedback from either the DNN or Axisymmetric aerodynamic models, successfully enabled the robot to complete all designed flight maneuvers, maintaining balance throughout the flight envelope. This demonstrated the necessity of accounting for aerodynamic forces.
 - Jet engine thrust profiles were observed to be asymmetric, indicating that the controller successfully generated differential thrust to counteract aerodynamic moments acting on the robot.
- **Robustness Analysis**: A simulation where the system dynamics used the more accurate DNN model, but the controller relied on the simpler Axisymmetric Model for aerodynamic feedback, successfully completed the flight envelope. While some oscillations in joint positions were noted during transition maneuvers, the robot's Center of Mass (CoM) tracking error and overall joint position errors remained bounded. This result indicated a degree of robustness of the control strategy to modeling inaccuracies.
- **Ground Experiments**: On the physical iRonCub-Mk1 (with jet engines off), artificial wind profiles injected into the aerodynamic-aware controller as feedback prompted appropriate postural adjustments from the robot. For instance, a simulated frontal wind caused adjustments primarily through ankle pitch joints, while a lateral wind led to adjustments in leg hip and ankle roll joints. These responses demonstrated the controller's ability to generate appropriate compensatory motions on the real hardware to minimize estimated linear momentum derivative, even without physical aerodynamic interaction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes a significant contribution to the field of robotics by addressing a fundamental challenge in the development of multi-modal flying humanoid robots: the accurate modeling and effective control of aerodynamic forces. Its potential impact extends across several domains.

#### 5.1. Advancing Multi-modal Robotics

The work pushes the boundaries of multi-modal locomotion by demonstrating a comprehensive approach to enable heavy humanoid robots with jet propulsion to fly in the presence of external winds. This capability combines the dexterous manipulation and bipedal locomotion of humanoids with the mobility of aerial robots, opening new avenues for applications that require navigating complex, unstructured environments where both ground-based interaction and aerial traversal are necessary. Examples include advanced search and rescue operations, inspection in hazardous industrial sites, or exploration in challenging terrains.

#### 5.2. Technological Prowess

The mechanical design and realization of iRonCub-Mk1, a pioneering jet-powered humanoid, represents a substantial technological achievement. The meticulous engineering required to integrate powerful jet engines into a humanoid form factor, along with the custom hardware modifications for wind tunnel testing, provides a valuable blueprint for future heavy-payload flying robot designs. This platform itself is a significant research asset for further exploration of multi-modal capabilities.

#### 5.3. Scientific Contribution to Aerodynamics and Control

- **Integrated Methodology**: The research presents a novel, integrated methodology that combines classical fluid dynamics techniques (CFD simulations and wind tunnel experiments) with modern machine learning (Deep Neural Networks and linear regression) to generate, model, and predict distributed aerodynamic forces on a complex, articulated body. This hybrid approach offers a robust framework for understanding and quantifying aerodynamic effects on highly non-aerodynamic shapes.
- **Aerodynamic-Aware Control**: The development and validation of an aerodynamic-aware controller demonstrate the critical necessity of explicit aerodynamic compensation for stable flight in non-zero wind conditions. This moves beyond treating aerodynamics as simple disturbances, paving the way for controllers that can potentially exploit these forces for enhanced maneuverability and efficiency.
- **Generalizability**: The methodology is presented as generalizable to "any shape-shifting drone under aerodynamic disturbances," suggesting broader applicability for a range of reconfigurable aerial platforms beyond humanoids, thereby impacting the design and control of future generations of agile, versatile flying robots.

#### 5.4. Foundation for Future Research and Development

The findings lay crucial groundwork for several future research directions:
- **Aerodynamic Optimization**: The current robot design is not aerodynamically optimized. The obtained models and data will be instrumental in informing future design iterations of iRonCub, potentially incorporating aerodynamic shaping (e.g., wings, morphing covers) to improve lift-to-drag ratios, enhance flight efficiency, reduce fuel consumption, and extend flight endurance.
- **Advanced Sensing and Modeling**: The identified limitations in real-time wind estimation and the reliance on quasi-steady flow assumptions suggest future work in integrating onboard wind sensors and distributed pressure sensors. These could feed into more sophisticated Physics-Informed Neural Network architectures or turbulence-resolving CFD methods (like LES or GPU-accelerated DES with compressibility effects) to account for unsteady flow dynamics and jet-flow interactions.
- **Exploiting Aerodynamics**: With a more robust understanding and real-time estimation of aerodynamic forces, future control strategies could transition from mere compensation to actively exploiting these forces for more aggressive maneuvers and complex flight trajectories, potentially leading to more dynamic and agile robot behavior.
- **Real-world Deployment**: Addressing the current inability to conduct real flight tests due to sensor limitations and facility requirements, this research motivates the development of dedicated indoor flight facilities and more precise arm actuation for robust real-world deployment in demanding applications.

In essence, this work establishes a foundational understanding and a practical framework for integrating aerodynamics into the design and control of flying humanoid robots, significantly advancing the capabilities of multi-modal robotic systems for diverse and complex operational environments.
