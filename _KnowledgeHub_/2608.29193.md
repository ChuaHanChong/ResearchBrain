# HalluPrism: When Multimodal Uncertainty Should Diagnose, Not Decide

> [!summary] Summary
> HalluPrism is a behavioral diagnostic for multimodal LLMs that re-runs an answer under visual degradation, blank-image replacement, and grounding/relation probes, yielding a three-part perturbation-response signature over visual sensitivity, image-removal confidence retention, and alignment instability. Across 58,440 examples from four models and four benchmarks, the signature lifts pooled failure-family AUROC from 0.78 to 0.95 over scalar confidence, though naive scalarizations of it hurt abstention ranking.

## Problem

- Hallucination benchmarks show multimodal errors are heterogeneous, yet scalar confidence and calibration only rank likely correctness without saying which failure mode produced the answer.
- Selective prediction treats uncertainty as a threshold decision whose validity rests on designer-chosen scores, validation sets, and risk assumptions that may fail on open-world instances.
- Mitigation methods such as contrastive decoding, attention penalties, or post-hoc revision apply one fixed correction rule to every risky prediction, regardless of why it was risky.

## Method

- ==Perturbation-Response Signature== over visual sensitivity, image-removal retention, and alignment instability; the visual score uses blur, crop, brightness, and noise, weighting answer flips 0.6 and confidence shifts 0.4.
- The ==image-removal score== is a clipped confidence ratio after blank-image replacement, with a stricter answer-persistence variant; the alignment score blends ==grounding re-checks== with ==relation swaps== at weight 0.6.
- A ==magnitude-based diagonal-dominance test== over visual, language, and alignment-ruined inputs, plus ==intervention routing== mapping within-setting normalized dominance to re-grounding, anti-prior prompting, or relation checking.

## Results

- Pooled XGBoost failure-family AUROC rises from **0.78** with scalar confidence to **0.95** with the signature and **0.97** with confidence added; HallusionBench goes **0.634** to **0.769**, VizWiz **0.707** to **0.817**.
- Only **18** of **48** source-target diagonal checks pass (visual **11/16**, language **2/16**, alignment **5/16**); retention dominates **99.7%** of samples yet alignment carries **0.50** of tree-based importance.
- All three scalarizations lower abstention AUROC, Qwen3-VL dropping **0.81** to **0.73**; matched routing holds Qwen3-VL/VSR at **87.6%** accuracy with **2.7%** breakage versus generic prompting's **76.2%** and **15.5%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prevalence is not diagnostic value: the near-saturated coordinate dominated almost every sample, while the rarest coordinate carried most of the predictive weight.
> - Targeted perturbations rarely isolate their intended axis, so read multi-probe scores as one joint signature rather than three independent causal sources.
> - A feature that explains a failure need not improve abstention ranking; keep diagnosis, correctness ranking, and correction-path choice as separate evaluation objects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) combine visual encoders with language-model reasoning and generation. Their errors include object hallucination, visual illusion, attribute mistakes, answerability failures, and spatial or relational errors. Existing benchmarks such as POPE, HallusionBench, Visual Spatial Reasoning (VSR), and VizWiz-VQA demonstrate that multimodal failures are heterogeneous rather than reducible to a single type of “hallucination.”

Most uncertainty research treats uncertainty as a decision variable. A confidence score is used to rank likely-correct answers, determine whether the system should abstain, or trigger additional verification. Related work has examined calibration, semantic uncertainty, sampling inconsistency, hidden-state reliability, evidential conflict, and uncertainty in visual tokens. Separately, behavioral testing methods use controlled perturbations to identify weaknesses under changes to the input. Hallucination mitigation methods generally apply a fixed intervention, such as visual contrastive decoding, attention calibration, grounding prompts, or post-generation revision.

HalluPrism connects these areas but changes the primary objective. It argues that multimodal uncertainty should first be used to **diagnose the behavioral pattern associated with a prediction**, and only afterward to decide whether to abstain, correct, or route the prediction to a particular intervention. The paper distinguishes three tasks that are often combined: correctness ranking, failure-source diagnosis, and correction-path selection. Its central claim is that a representation can be useful for identifying the type of failure without improving the ranking of correct versus incorrect answers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Motivation

The paper begins with cases in which an MLLM produces a confident but incorrect answer to a visually grounded question. For example, a model may reverse the spatial relationship between a traffic cone and a parked car. Similar confidence values could arise from different underlying behavioral patterns:

1. The relevant visual evidence may be weak because the object is small, blurred, dark, or occluded.
2. The model may retain its answer when the image is replaced with a blank image, indicating confidence that persists without direct visual evidence.
3. The model may recognize the relevant objects but incorrectly bind the question to the spatial relation between them.

A scalar confidence value may indicate that the answer is risky, but it does not distinguish these patterns. This distinction matters because different failures may require different responses. Visual weakness may call for image enhancement or visual re-grounding, while language-prior reliance may require an explicit instruction not to answer from common knowledge. A relation error may require an object-and-relation verification step.

#### 2.2. Objectives

The study has four main objectives:

- To develop a behavioral uncertainty representation that characterizes MLLM responses under targeted input perturbations.
- To evaluate whether this representation improves prediction of broad failure families compared with scalar confidence.
- To test whether the representation corresponds to independent causal sources or instead reflects entangled behavioral responses.
- To determine whether the representation should be directly converted into an abstention score or used for targeted correction routing.

The paper explicitly avoids claiming that its measurements reveal the hidden mechanisms inside an MLLM. Instead, it treats them as observable response profiles.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. HalluPrism Signature

For each clean model prediction, HalluPrism computes ordinary scalar confidence \(c\) and a three-dimensional perturbation-response signature:

\[
s(x,M)=(V,L,A).
\]

The three coordinates are:

- **\(V\): visual-perturbation sensitivity.** This measures how much the answer or confidence changes when visual evidence is degraded.
- **\(L\): image-removal confidence retention.** This measures how much confidence remains after replacing the original image with a uniform blank image.
- **\(A\): grounding or relation-probe instability.** This measures response changes under prompts that require explicit visual grounding or under relation-swapped questions.

The coordinates are bounded behavioral measurements, not source labels. For example, high \(L\) does not prove that the model ignored the image; it only shows that confidence was retained after image removal.

#### 3.2. Perturbation Probes

The visual probe applies four transformations while keeping the question unchanged: Gaussian blur, a center crop covering half of each image dimension, brightness reduction to 30 percent, and Gaussian noise. \(V\) combines answer flips and absolute confidence changes, with answer flips receiving the larger weight.

For \(L\), the original image is replaced with a blank canvas. The main score is the ratio of blank-image confidence to clean-image confidence, clipped to one. The authors also test stricter variants that require the blank-image answer itself to match the clean answer or use a stabilized denominator.

For \(A\), every dataset receives a grounding re-check prompt asking the model first to describe the image and then answer the original question. VSR examples additionally receive relation-swapped prompts, such as changing “left of” to “right of.” The alignment score combines answer or confidence changes under grounding prompts with answer persistence under relation swaps.

#### 3.3. Models and Datasets

The evaluation uses four model families: Gemma 4, Qwen3-VL, LLaVA-Mistral, and LLaVA-Vicuna. The datasets are:

- **HallusionBench:** 951 image-paired examples per model involving visual illusion, attribute errors, and grounded hallucination.
- **POPE:** 9,000 object-presence examples per model across random, popular, and adversarial splits.
- **VSR:** 340 spatial-relation examples per model.
- **VizWiz-VQA:** 4,319 validation examples per model involving visual insufficiency and answerability.

This produces 14,610 examples per model and 58,440 pooled model-output instances. Models use deterministic decoding with temperature zero, disabled sampling, and a 128-token output limit.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Tasks

For diagnosis, the authors train logistic regression, XGBoost, and LightGBM classifiers using scalar confidence, the signature alone, or the signature combined with confidence. They report AUROC and Macro-F1, using stratified five-fold cross-validation.

The paper also tests whether targeted interventions isolate their intended coordinates. Visual-, language-, and alignment-ruined conditions are applied, and the authors check whether the corresponding coordinate changes more than the other two. This tests separability rather than assuming it.

For abstention, three hand-designed scalarizations penalize confidence according to \(V\), \(L\), and \(A\): a maximum penalty, a weighted penalty, and a geometric combination. For routing, normalized coordinate dominance selects among visual re-grounding, anti-prior prompting, and grounding or relation checking. A generic reasoning prompt serves as a baseline.

A blind human audit of 200 examples, with 50 from each dataset, provides a limited semantic plausibility check. Three annotators classify examples as visual, language-prior, alignment, mixed, unclear, or no failure.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Prevalence and Entanglement

Image-removal confidence retention is high across nearly all settings, with mean \(L\) values between 0.91 and 1.00. Using the default ratio, \(L\) is the largest coordinate for 58,287 of 58,440 examples, or 99.7 percent. However, this dominance is partly caused by saturation. When answer persistence is required, \(L\) dominates about 51.6 percent of examples, while \(A\) and \(V\) account for 27.9 and 20.4 percent, respectively.

The intervention tests show that the three coordinates are not cleanly separable. Only 18 of 48 source-target checks satisfy magnitude-based diagonal dominance. Visual interventions pass 11 of 16 checks, language interventions pass 2 of 16, and alignment interventions pass 5 of 16. For example, language-ruined inputs sometimes produce a larger change in \(A\) than in \(L\). The authors therefore interpret HalluPrism as a joint behavioral signature rather than a causal decomposition.

#### 4.2. Failure-Family Diagnosis

Despite entanglement, the full signature improves failure-family prediction. In fixed-dataset analyses, AUROC improves from 0.634 to 0.769 on HallusionBench and from 0.707 to 0.817 on VizWiz-VQA. Gains are smaller on POPE, from 0.823 to 0.839, and nearly absent on VSR, from 0.722 to 0.724.

In pooled XGBoost analysis, AUROC increases from 0.78 with scalar confidence to 0.95 with \(V,L,A\), and to 0.97 when confidence is added. Alignment instability receives the largest pooled XGBoost feature importance, approximately 0.50, while image-removal retention receives 0.20 and visual sensitivity 0.15. This indicates that prevalence and diagnostic value differ: the most frequent signal is not necessarily the most useful for distinguishing failure families.

Dataset and model controls reduce concerns that results merely reflect benchmark identity. Adding the signature beyond dataset identity, model identity, and confidence improves pooled Macro-F1, although leave-dataset-out transfer is weak. The paper therefore presents the signature as more useful within related task settings than as a universally transferable failure classifier.

#### 4.3. Abstention and Routing

The three tested source-aware scalarizations do not improve correctness ranking. Pooled AUROC is 0.783 for scalar confidence and 0.767 for the best source-aware scalarization. For Qwen3-VL, the score decreases from approximately 0.81 to 0.73. Risk-coverage results show particularly poor behavior for maximum and geometric penalties at low coverage.

Routing produces conditional rather than universal benefits. Across all settings, accuracy is 76.6 percent without intervention, 75.7 percent with generic prompting, and 75.9 percent with matched routing. However, matched routing reduces harmful changes to originally correct answers. On Qwen3-VL/VSR, generic prompting reduces accuracy from 87.4 to 76.2 percent and breaks 15.5 percent of correct answers. Matched routing retains 87.6 percent accuracy and lowers breakage to 2.7 percent. Across 16 model-dataset settings, matched routing outperforms generic prompting in 10 and lowers break rate in 10.

The human audit provides limited support. Agreement across six labels is modest, with Fleiss’ kappa of 0.236, and 87 of 200 examples are judged unclear. The audit nevertheless finds that the joint signature predicts clear human source labels better than scalar confidence, although agreement remains insufficient to support causal interpretation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HalluPrism’s main contribution is conceptual and methodological: it separates uncertainty-based diagnosis from uncertainty-based decision-making. The results suggest that an MLLM’s reaction to controlled perturbations can provide information about the type of failure even when a direct confidence adjustment does not improve abstention.

Potential applications include model auditing, benchmark-level error analysis, selective use of verification prompts, and safer correction routing. In particular, the method may help avoid applying a generic correction to already-correct answers. It could be deployed selectively on high-risk examples rather than on every query.

Several constraints limit its immediate deployment value. The method requires seven forward passes per example, or eight for VSR, and the full experiment used more than 500,000 forward passes. The evaluation covers four public benchmarks and four model families but does not test medical, legal, document-heavy, video, multi-image, or latency-sensitive applications. Human interpretability is limited, and cross-dataset transfer is weak. Most importantly, the signature is not causal, does not guarantee answer safety, and should not be treated as a standalone abstention certificate. Its appropriate role is as a diagnostic layer that informs subsequent review or intervention under task-specific validation.
