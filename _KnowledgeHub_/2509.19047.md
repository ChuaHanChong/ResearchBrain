# ManipForce: Force-Guided Policy Learning with Frequency-Aware Representation for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ManipForce is a handheld dual-camera gripper with a wrist force-torque sensor that records RGB at 30 Hz and gravity-compensated F/T at 200 Hz during natural human demonstrations, with ArUco-based pose tracking. Its Frequency-Aware Multimodal Transformer tokenizes both streams with learnable frequency and modality embeddings and fuses them by bi-directional cross-attention inside a transformer diffusion policy, reaching 83% average success over six contact-rich tasks.

## Problem

- Handheld demonstration interfaces capture vision and pose but no force-torque signal, so contact behaviors central to precision assembly are never modeled.
- Existing vision-plus-force collection depends on depth point clouds and visual SLAM, which blur small features, tight clearances, and degrade during close-contact interaction.
- Prior multimodal policies downsample force streams to the camera frame rate, discarding transient contact dynamics, or restrict cross-modal exchange to the slow visual branch.

## Method

- **ManipForce** hardware: rack-and-pinion trigger gripper with pin-ray compliant fingertips, dual hand-eye RGB cameras, wrist F/T sensor, 3D ArUco marker tracking, and IMU-based tool gravity compensation.
- ==Frequency-aware multimodal embeddings==: ==DINOv2== visual tokens and a ==1D CNN force encoder== are aligned by learnable spatial, frequency, and modality embeddings, with timestamp windowing binding 200 Hz force samples to each frame.
- ==Bi-directional cross-attention== fuses visual and force tokens in both directions, then concatenation plus a fully connected layer and ==LayerNorm== conditions a ==transformer diffusion policy== head for action denoising.

## Results

- Across six real tasks over 20 trials each, the model averaged **83%** success versus **22%** for the RGB-only diffusion policy, e.g. gear assembly **0.95** vs **0.35** and box flipping **0.90** vs **0.05**.
- Ablations on gear assembly, box flipping, and open lid showed drops when force was downsampled to **30 Hz**, when frequency-aware embeddings were removed, and when cross-attention was disabled.
- Gear assembly success rose monotonically with force sampling rate, from **0.40** at **30 Hz** to **0.95** at **200 Hz**; gravity compensation cut residual forces from about **8 N** to within **1 N**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Force bandwidth, not just force presence, carries the signal: transient spikes that mark jamming or latch events vanish under camera-rate downsampling.
> - Heterogeneous sensor rates can be handled by learned frequency and modality embeddings rather than resampling or dual slow-fast architectures.
> - Marker-based pose tracking plus gravity compensation keeps handheld demonstrations geometrically and force-wise consistent with robot execution, enabling transfer without task-specific calibration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of robotic manipulation, imitation learning, and multimodal sensing. It directly addresses several long-standing challenges in enabling robots to perform complex, contact-rich tasks that humans execute effortlessly.

#### 1.1. Challenges in Contact-Rich Manipulation

Contact-rich manipulation tasks, such as precise assembly, insertion, and non-prehensile handling, are fundamental to many industrial and domestic applications. These tasks are inherently difficult for robots because they demand precise control of interaction forces, subtle adjustments based on physical contact, and robust handling of small clearances and dynamic friction. Humans naturally leverage rich haptic feedback alongside vision to perform these tasks. However, traditional robotic systems often struggle to perceive and react to these fine-grained physical interactions, leading to brittleness and low success rates.

#### 1.2. Limitations of Existing Data Collection Methods

Imitation learning (IL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for teaching robots complex behaviors by observing human demonstrations. However, the quality and richness of demonstration data are paramount.
- **Teleoperation-based systems** (e.g., VR, 3D spacemouse, leader-follower systems) are widely used for data collection. While some provide intuitive control, they often require skilled operators, are time-consuming, and crucially, typically lack clear haptic feedback for the human demonstrator. This absence of haptic information makes it exceedingly difficult to accurately convey the precise force control required for contact-rich tasks.
- **Handheld data collection systems** like UMI represent a significant advance by allowing users to directly manipulate a gripper, making data collection more intuitive and natural. However, many of these systems, including UMI, primarily capture visual data and do not incorporate force-torque (F/T) information. This omission leaves out a critical sensory modality for contact-rich tasks.
- More recent attempts to combine visual and F/T data in handheld systems often rely on point clouds for scene representation, which introduces complex setup requirements, limits the perception of small objects or fine clearances, and often down-samples high-frequency F/T signals to match the lower image frame rate, thus losing vital temporal information about contact dynamics. Other specialized systems might require fingertip force sensors or fixed-wrist setups, limiting their generality.

#### 1.3. Limitations of Existing Multimodal Learning Approaches

While multimodal imitation learning, combining vision with force or tactile sensing, is an active area, it faces its own set of challenges:
- **Feature-level concatenation**, is a common strategy, but it may not optimally integrate heterogeneous, asynchronous data streams.
- **Cross-attention modules** and **dynamic modality weighting** offer more sophisticated fusion, but the fundamental challenge of managing asynchronous inputs (e.g., 30 Hz for vision vs. 200+ Hz for force/tactile) remains.
- Some approaches like Reactive Diffusion Policy introduce dual-architecture designs, but they can be complex and may restrict cross-modal interaction to the slower visual stream, thereby underutilizing high-frequency force data.

#### 1.4. The Niche for ManipForce and FMT

ManipForce directly addresses these limitations by providing a system for collecting **simultaneous, high-frequency RGB and F/T data** during natural human demonstrations. It overcomes the issues of complex setups, limited perception, and lost F/T data inherent in prior systems. Furthermore, the Frequency-Aware Multimodal Transformer (FMT) is specifically designed to leverage this rich, asynchronous data by employing novel frequency- and modality-aware embeddings and bi-directional cross-attention within a Transformer Diffusion Policy. This allows the model to effectively integrate heterogeneous sensor modalities at their native sampling rates, extracting the maximum information from both visual context and precise contact dynamics. By offering both a superior data collection system and an advanced learning architecture, ManipForce and FMT aim to set a new standard for robust imitation learning in contact-rich manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind ManipForce and FMT stems from the observation that while imitation learning has shown great promise for robotic manipulation, its effectiveness in contact-rich tasks is severely limited by the inadequacy of existing demonstration data and learning architectures. Humans naturally rely on haptic feedback for these tasks, a crucial element often missing or poorly integrated in robot learning systems.

#### 2.1. Addressing Data Collection Gaps

The primary objective for developing **ManipForce** is to bridge the gap in high-quality demonstration data for contact-rich manipulation. Current handheld systems simplify demonstration collection but typically lack force-torque (F/T) information, which is vital for understanding physical interactions. Teleoperation systems, while versatile, often lack haptic feedback for the human operator, making it difficult to demonstrate precise force control. ManipForce aims to:
- Enable the simultaneous capture of high-frequency F/T data and visual (RGB) information during natural human demonstrations.
- Provide a robust and precise data collection system that minimizes common issues like visual occlusion, pose tracking degradation during close contact, and gravity-induced F/T measurement bias.
- Ensure that the collected data is directly transferable to robotic execution without extensive task-specific calibration, promoting real-world applicability.

#### 2.2. Overcoming Multimodal Learning Challenges

The second key objective, addressed by the **Frequency-Aware Multimodal Transformer (FMT)**, is to develop a policy learning architecture that can effectively process and fuse inherently asynchronous and heterogeneous multimodal data (low-frequency RGB and high-frequency F/T signals). Existing multimodal approaches often downsample high-frequency signals or struggle with optimal integration, leading to a loss of critical temporal information. FMT aims to:
- Develop a method to represent and integrate asynchronous RGB and F/T signals at their native sampling rates, preserving the rich temporal dynamics of force interactions.
- Leverage the power of Transformer architectures for multimodal policy learning.
- Create a robust policy that can learn fine-grained control for diverse contact-rich manipulation tasks from these complex data streams.

#### 2.3. Improving Policy Robustness for Contact-Rich Tasks

Ultimately, the overarching motivation is to significantly improve the robustness and success rates of robotic policies for contact-rich manipulation. By providing richer demonstration data and a more sophisticated learning architecture, the authors aim to empower robots to:
- Perform tasks requiring precise alignment and controlled axial forces (e.g., gear assembly).
- Handle tasks demanding sub-millimeter accuracy and carefully regulated insertion forces (e.g., LAN plug insertion).
- Execute non-prehensile manipulations guided by continuous contact sensing (e.g., box flipping).
- Detect and react to subtle force cues for delicate operations (e.g., opening a lid, battery disassembly/insertion).

By addressing both the data collection and policy learning aspects comprehensively, ManipForce and FMT aspire to enable robots to acquire human-like dexterity and resilience in physical interaction tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework consists of two main components: ManipForce, a handheld system for data collection, and the Frequency-Aware Multimodal Transformer (FMT), a novel policy learning model.

#### 3.1. ManipForce: The Data Collection System

**System Design and Gripper Mechanism:** ManipForce is engineered for intuitive, precise, and stable human-guided data collection. It features a rack-and-pinion gripper with a trigger interface for natural grasping. Crucially, a deformable pin-ray structure at the fingertips enhances compliance and contact sensitivity. Integrated linear guides minimize mechanical vibration, ensuring smoother manipulation. A trigger-lock mechanism allows the user to hold the jaws closed, reducing fatigue and ensuring F/T signals primarily capture task-relevant interaction forces, not gripper-holding effort. All gripper components are mounted downstream of the F/T sensor to accurately capture all interaction forces. The hardware and software are open-source.

**RGB and Wrist-Pose Data Acquisition:** The system employs dual hand-eye cameras (Intel RealSense D455 and D405) strategically positioned to minimize occlusions and provide clear views during manipulation. To overcome the limitations of SLAM-based wrist-pose estimation, which degrades near surfaces, ManipForce utilizes 3D ArUco markers detected by an external Azure Kinect sensor. This setup achieves robust, viewpoint-independent tracking with sub-millimeter accuracy, essential for fine assembly tasks. End-effector actions are computed as 6-DOF pose deltas relative to the marker frame and then transformed to the robot’s TCP frame using CAD-derived matrices. Additional ArUco markers on the gripper jaws track jaw positions and infer open/close states. All RGB and wrist-pose data are recorded at 30 Hz.

**F/T Data Acquisition and Gravity Compensation:** An AIDIN AFT200 F/T sensor, identical to the one used on the evaluation robot, is wrist-mounted to record force/torque signals at a high frequency of 200 Hz. A critical step is gravity compensation: raw F/T measurements include the tool's weight, which must be removed to isolate true interaction forces. This is achieved by using IMU data from the D455 camera to compute the gravity vector in the F/T sensor frame. The gravitational wrench (force and torque) is then calculated using the tool's mass and center of mass and subtracted from the raw measurements. This procedure is applied consistently during both data collection and robot execution, validated by tests showing significant reduction in gravity-induced F/T bias (from ±8N to ±1N forces, and ±0.4N·m to ±0.15N·m torques).

#### 3.2. Frequency-Aware Multimodal Transformer (FMT)

FMT is a Transformer-based Diffusion Policy that learns from the asynchronous RGB (30 Hz) and F/T (>200 Hz) data collected by ManipForce, designed to fuse low-rate visual cues with high-frequency force information.

**RGB-Force Tokenization:** Visual observations from the dual cameras are preprocessed (padded, resized to 256x256) and encoded using DINOv2-B to extract visual representations. F/T data is processed by a 1D CNN encoder to capture force dynamics. This results in visual tokens (T_cam1, T_cam2 ∈ R^(T_img × L × d)) and F/T tokens (T_ft ∈ R^(T_ft × d)). To manage asynchronous inputs, a timestamp-based windowing approach aligns each visual frame with corresponding high-frequency F/T samples within the image frame's temporal boundaries, preserving F/T dynamics while maintaining visual context.

**Frequency-aware Multimodal Embedding:** To enable unified processing despite different sampling rates and modalities, three types of learnable positional embeddings are introduced:
- **Spatial embeddings (E_spatial):** Encode within-image positions for visual tokens.
- **Frequency-aware embeddings (E_freq):** Capture timestamp relationships across modalities with different sampling rates. For camera inputs, E_freq is resampled via linear interpolation to align with the camera's asynchronous timestamps.
- **Modality embeddings (E_cam1, E_cam2, E_ft):** Encode the source modality of each token.
These embeddings are added to their respective tokens and learned during training, allowing the Transformer to model the spatial, temporal, and modal context of each element in the multimodal sequence.

**Bi-directional Cross-Attention:** The architecture includes bi-directional cross-attention modules to facilitate rich information exchange. Visual tokens query F/T tokens to incorporate contact dynamics (T''_img = CA_img←ft(Q=T'_img, K=T'_ft, V=T'_ft)), and F/T tokens query visual features to understand spatial context (T''_ft = CA_ft←img(Q=T'_ft, K=T'_img, V=T'_img)). This fusion ensures that each modality's representation is enhanced by complementary information from the other, capturing interdependencies between visual scenes and physical interactions. The enhanced tokens are then concatenated, passed through a fully connected layer, and layer normalized to form a unified observation representation (T_obs).

**Transformer Diffusion Policy:** Following the Time-series Diffusion Transformer architecture, the unified multimodal tokens (T_obs) serve as conditioning input for action generation. A noise prediction network generates action sequences through iterative denoising steps. This process involves noisy action embeddings attending to multimodal observation features via cross-attention, while also maintaining causal self-attention over previous action tokens. This diffusion-based approach naturally handles variable-length sequences and different sampling rates without requiring explicit synchronization strategies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments thoroughly evaluate FMT on six contact-rich manipulation tasks, comparing it against baselines and analyzing the contribution of its individual components.

#### 4.1. Performance Gains with High-Frequency Force-Aware Policy Learning

The core finding is the substantial performance improvement of FMT over an RGB-only baseline. Across six diverse contact-rich tasks (gear assembly, LAN plug insertion, box flipping, open lid, battery disassembly, and battery insertion), FMT achieved an average success rate of **83%**, dramatically outperforming the RGB-only model's **22%** average.
- For precision tasks like **gear assembly** and **battery assembly**, where fine alignment under rapidly changing forces is critical, FMT showed superior performance, maintaining precise insertion and stable contact.
- In **box flipping**, which requires sustained contact and continuous force regulation, FMT achieved smooth and reliable manipulation, whereas the RGB-only baseline largely failed (5% success).
- For **open lid**, a task hinging on detecting and exploiting a single, transient high-frequency force spike, FMT achieved a perfect 100% success rate, significantly better than the RGB-only model's 20%.
These results conclusively demonstrate that incorporating high-frequency F/T data, processed by a frequency-aware policy, is crucial for robust and reliable contact-rich manipulation.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study: Contribution of FMT Modules

An ablation study on three representative tasks (Gear Assembly, Box Flipping, and Open Lid) confirmed the complementary contributions of FMT's three core modules: high-frequency F/T sensing, frequency-aware multimodal embeddings, and bi-directional cross-attention.
- The **full FMT model consistently achieved the highest success rates**.
- **Low-Frequency F/T (30 Hz) input** significantly degraded performance, especially in tasks reliant on transient force spikes like "open lid." This highlights the necessity of preserving high-frequency force signatures for critical event detection.
- Removing **frequency-aware multimodal embeddings** caused the steepest performance drop in tasks requiring precise alignment under rapidly changing forces (e.g., gear assembly), underscoring their importance for synchronizing heterogeneous streams.
- Disabling **bi-directional cross-attention** also led to performance reduction, indicating that adaptive fusion of visual and force information is vital for robust policy learning.
The study concluded that high-frequency sensing, proper alignment via embeddings, and adaptive fusion via cross-attention are all critical, working together to form effective frequency-aware multimodal representations.

#### 4.3. Impact of F/T Sampling Frequency

An analysis of the gear assembly task at varying F/T sampling frequencies (30 Hz, 60 Hz, 120 Hz, and 200 Hz) revealed a monotonic relationship between F/T frequency and success rate. Performance steadily increased from 40% at 30 Hz to **95% at 200 Hz**. This finding directly supports the hypothesis that higher-frequency F/T inputs provide richer information, enabling the model to detect subtle force changes, transient sticking/jamming events, and perform more precise corrective actions. It validates FMT's ability to effectively exploit high-bandwidth F/T data.

#### 4.4. Data Collection Efficiency of ManipForce

A comparison of data collection efficiency across human demonstrations, ManipForce, and the teleoperation-based Gello system highlighted ManipForce's practical advantages. ManipForce achieved efficiency comparable to direct human demonstrations for tasks like box flipping and battery disassembly. In stark contrast, teleoperation systems like Gello required dramatically longer recording times. This is attributed to the inherent lack of haptic feedback in teleoperation, making it difficult for operators to apply accurate interaction forces for contact-rich tasks. ManipForce, by leveraging natural human operation with a handheld system, ensures that demonstrations capture both task-relevant motions and the crucial interaction forces efficiently.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ManipForce framework, encompassing both a novel data collection system and an advanced policy learning architecture, presents several significant contributions and holds substantial potential impact on the field of robotic manipulation.

#### 5.1. Addressing Critical Gaps in Robotic Manipulation

The most immediate significance lies in addressing the long-standing challenge of performing robust contact-rich manipulation tasks. By enabling the capture and effective utilization of high-frequency F/T data alongside visual information, ManipForce allows robots to acquire a level of precision and adaptability that was previously difficult to achieve with vision-only or poorly integrated multimodal systems. This opens doors for reliable automation in areas requiring delicate part handling, precision assembly, and complex interaction with unstructured environments. The demonstrated success across diverse tasks like gear assembly, plug insertion, and battery operations highlights its versatility and potential for real-world deployment in manufacturing, service robotics, and even hazardous environments.

#### 5.2. Advancing Multimodal Imitation Learning

From a machine learning perspective, FMT's novel approach to handling asynchronous multimodal data is a significant step forward. The introduction of frequency-aware multimodal embeddings and bi-directional cross-attention provides a robust mechanism for fusing information from sensors operating at drastically different sampling rates. This methodology can be generalized beyond RGB and F/T to other multimodal sensor combinations (e.g., tactile, audio), paving the way for more sophisticated and human-like robotic perception. The finding that higher F/T sampling frequencies directly correlate with improved performance underscores the importance of preserving raw sensor fidelity, challenging previous assumptions that downsampling is acceptable. This encourages future research into high-bandwidth sensor integration and learning architectures capable of processing such rich data streams.

#### 5.3. Practical Implications and Future Directions

The practical impact of ManipForce is amplified by its design as an intuitive, handheld system that significantly improves data collection efficiency compared to traditional teleoperation. By making high-quality, multimodal demonstration data easier and faster to collect, ManipForce lowers the barrier to entry for training robotic policies for complex tasks. The open-source release of hardware and software components will enable broader adoption and further development by the research community, fostering collaborative progress in this critical area. The consistency in F/T sensing between the demonstration system and the robot, ensured by identical sensors and gravity compensation, is a crucial detail that enhances the sim-to-real (or human-to-robot) transferability of learned policies.

While the current study demonstrates strong performance on a diverse set of tasks, the authors acknowledge future work extending the framework to longer-horizon and more dexterous tasks requiring sophisticated gripper behaviors. This indicates a commitment to enhancing the generality and scalability of ManipForce for even more complex real-world scenarios. Ultimately, ManipForce represents a holistic framework that addresses both the data acquisition and policy learning challenges, positioning itself as a foundational contribution to building more capable, adaptable, and robust robots for physical interaction tasks.
