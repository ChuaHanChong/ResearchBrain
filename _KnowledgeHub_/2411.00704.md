# Learning to Look Around: Enhancing Teleoperation and Learning with a Human-like Actuated Neck

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MIT CSAIL developed a robotic system incorporating a 5-DOF actuated neck to enhance teleoperation and improve data quality for imitation learning. This approach yielded higher success rates for autonomous policies trained with dynamically acquired visual data compared to static camera views.

## Problem

- Existing teleoperation systems impose high cognitive load due to limited, often static, camera views, hindering operator performance in complex manipulation tasks.
- Data collected from teleoperation using static or wide-angle cameras often suffers from perceptual inconsistencies and distribution shift, leading to suboptimal performance for learned autonomous policies.
- Current robotic perception lacks the dynamic, adaptive qualities of human head movements, which are crucial for overcoming occlusions and gaining comprehensive environmental understanding.

## Method

- A mobile manipulation system was designed, integrating two UR5e arms and a 5-DOF actuated neck with a camera system on a ==Husarion Panther== mobile base.
- A teleoperation interface was developed, mapping human hand and head movements (via an Apple Vision Pro) directly to the robot's arms and actuated neck for intuitive control and synchronized perception.
- Imitation learning policies were trained on teleoperated demonstrations that included dynamic neck movements, and their performance was compared against policies trained with static wide-angle camera data.

## Results

- Qualitative evaluation showed enhanced teleoperation performance across seven complex manipulation tasks, with operators reporting improved situational awareness and dexterity due to dynamic viewpoint adjustment.
- Autonomous policies trained with actuated neck data achieved significantly higher success rates: **95%** for Cup Transfer From Bottom Shelf (vs. **0%** for static camera), **90%** for Left-to-right Pick and Place (vs. **78%**), and **82%** for Close-range Object Manipulation (vs. **68%**).
- The policy implicitly learned to reason about tasks and adjust neck movements, demonstrating robust generalization across varied object locations without explicit task labeling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a human-like actuated neck significantly reduces operator cognitive load and improves situational awareness by enabling dynamic viewpoint adjustment and 'peeking' around obstacles during teleoperation.
> - Dynamic, task-relevant visual data provided by the actuated neck mitigates distribution shift and leads to more robust and generalizable autonomous policies compared to static wide-angle camera views.
> - The actuated neck facilitates interactive perception, allowing a 'global' neck camera to guide 'local' wrist cameras, enabling both broad environmental reasoning and precise manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous robotic systems, particularly for complex manipulation tasks, relies significantly on high-quality demonstration data. The broader research landscape for data collection involves various methods, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. These methods include the use of exoskeletons for human-to-robot motion transfer, handheld manipulation devices, and the retargeting of human actions from videos into robot trajectories. While these approaches aim to translate human expertise to robots, they often introduce potential errors during the data translation or retargeting process.

Teleoperation presents an alternative by offering a more direct method of data collection, bypassing the need for complex retargeting. However, teleoperation systems commonly face challenges related to intuitiveness and ease of use for human operators. These difficulties often stem from a limited field of view, particularly with fixed camera setups, and the absence of haptic feedback, which can increase the operator's cognitive load and hinder performance in intricate environments.

This work addresses a specific aspect of the teleoperation challenge: the perception gap. Existing teleoperation systems frequently rely on static camera views or third-person perspectives, which do not align with human natural perception. Humans extensively use head and neck movements to actively explore their environment, overcome occlusions, and gain a comprehensive understanding of their surroundings during manipulation tasks. By integrating a human-like actuated neck into a teleoperation system, this research aims to bridge this perception gap, making teleoperation more intuitive and efficient. This advancement not only seeks to improve immediate teleoperation performance but also to generate higher-quality, more relevant data for training autonomous policies through imitation learning, thereby contributing to the broader goal of robust and generalizable robotic autonomy. The use of an actuated neck also touches upon interactive perception, where sensor observations and action trajectories are integrated over time, providing richer signals for learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are twofold:
1. **To enhance the intuitiveness and efficiency of remote teleoperation** by integrating a 5-degrees-of-freedom (DOF) actuated neck into a robotic system, thereby mimicking natural human head movements and perception.
2. **To investigate the role of an actuated neck in improving the quality and effectiveness of data collection for training autonomous robotic policies** through imitation learning.

These objectives are motivated by several key challenges in current robotic teleoperation and autonomous learning:

- **Cognitive Load and Limited Perception in Teleoperation:** Operators of existing teleoperation systems frequently experience high cognitive load due to an unnatural or restricted field of view. Fixed camera setups often limit the operator's ability to "look around" obstacles, change viewpoints dynamically, or gain a comprehensive understanding of the environment. This limitation directly impacts task performance, especially in complex whole-body manipulation scenarios where objects might be occluded or require observation from multiple angles. The motivation is to provide operators with a more natural, human-like perception experience, enabling behaviors such as "peeking" or "tilting," which are fundamental to human interaction with the physical world.
- **Suboptimal Data Quality for Imitation Learning:** Data collected from teleoperation often suffers from perceptual inconsistencies when cameras are static or offer wide-angle views. Static wide-angle cameras can introduce image distortion and capture irrelevant background information, which can lead to out-of-distribution perception during autonomous execution. This contributes to distribution shift, hindering the generalization and robustness of learned policies. The motivation here is to provide dynamic, task-relevant viewpoints through an actuated neck, which can reduce distortion, manage occlusions more effectively, and ensure that the visual data provided to the learning algorithm is focused and contextualized, thereby leading to better generalization for autonomous policy training.
- **Enhancing Interactive Perception and Generalization:** Human perception is inherently interactive, where head movements are used to actively gather information relevant to ongoing tasks. Current robot perception systems often lack this dynamic, adaptive quality. The motivation is to integrate an actuated neck to facilitate interactive perception, where sensor observations (from the neck camera) and action trajectories are intrinsically linked. This integration can help autonomous policies implicitly learn which objects or areas are salient for specific tasks, leading to more adaptive and generalizable behaviors across diverse environments and task variations (e.g., different heights or positions).

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between human perceptual capabilities and robotic teleoperation/learning, aiming for systems that are not only easier for humans to control but also provide richer, more focused data for robots to learn from, ultimately leading to more capable and adaptable autonomous robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves the development of a comprehensive teleoperation system incorporating a human-like actuated neck, followed by its evaluation in both direct teleoperation tasks and imitation learning scenarios.

#### 3.1. Hardware System Design

The robotic system is designed with three core principles: human-like mobility, suitability for remote teleoperation, and robustness.

- **Mobile Base:** A Husarion Panther mobile base provides untethered mobility, capable of speeds up to 2 m/s and navigation on uneven terrain. It supports both differential and holonomic drive modes for flexible maneuvering. The base is weighted at the bottom to ensure stability and prevent tipping.
- **Robot Arms:** Two Universal Robotics UR5e arms are utilized for dexterous manipulation. These arms offer a 5 kg payload capacity each, a reach of 50 cm to 170 cm, and torque-based collision detection for safety during operation. The system includes parallel-jaw grippers (Robotiq 2F-140) and can also integrate a dexterous Psyonic Ability hand for more complex manipulation.
- **Actuated Neck:** The core innovation is the Interbotix WidowX-200, a 5-DOF actuated neck. This component allows for human-like head movements, including rotation, tilting, and translational "peeking" movements to look around obstacles and adjust viewpoints dynamically.
- **Camera System:** Four USB cameras (Intel Realsense D405) are strategically placed: one on the actuated neck, one on the torso, and two on the wrists. These cameras provide real-time video streaming.
- **System Integration:** The arms transmit proprioception data (joint positions, velocities, torques) via Ethernet. The actuated neck communicates via a serial connection. These sensory inputs are recorded for imitation learning. The entire upper body, including the arms and neck, is mounted on a metal frame mimicking human torso structure, affixed to the mobile base.

#### 3.2. Teleoperation Interface

The teleoperation system comprises two main components: hand tracking and head tracking, both designed for intuitive, natural control.

- **Hand Tracking:**
 - **Ascension trakSTAR:** A 6-DOF electromagnetic tracking system is used, with nodes placed on the operator's palm, thumb, and index finger for precise capture of hand positions and finger movements, controlling the robot's grippers.
 - **Manus VR glove:** This glove provides 6-DOF tracking of 25 keypoints on the hand for fine-grained control of a dexterous robotic hand. It also offers potential for future haptic feedback integration.
 - **Apple Vision Pro (for comparison):** While tested for hand tracking, it posed challenges for close-proximity tasks due to hands moving out of the headset's field of view, leading to tracking inconsistencies and jitter.
- **Head Tracking:**
 - **Apple Vision Pro:** This device is used for 6-DOF head tracking. The operator's head pose changes are directly mapped to the robot's actuated neck camera pose, creating a synchronized perception experience.
 - **Visual Feedback:** Video from the neck camera is streamed in real-time to the Vision Pro over Wi-Fi, allowing operators to adjust the robot's viewpoint by simply moving their head. The system is designed to be self-contained, with all visual feedback exclusively through the Vision Pro, removing the need for external global cameras.

#### 3.3. Experimental Design for Evaluation

The evaluation is divided into two phases: assessing intuitive teleoperation and evaluating impact on imitation learning.

- **Actuated Neck Enabled Intuitive Teleoperation:**
 - **Task Selection:** Seven complex, whole-body manipulation tasks were designed, requiring coordinated use of dexterous hands, a mobile base, and a parallel-jaw gripper. Examples include loading dishes, hanging sweatshirts, filling a cup, arranging a dinner table, making coffee, refrigerator item transfer, and throwing trash. These tasks inherently demand dynamic perspective adjustments, handling occlusions, and base repositioning.
 - **Operator Experience:** Teleoperators operated remotely, relying solely on the robot's onboard cameras. Qualitative observations focused on how the actuated neck facilitated "peeking," situational awareness, fine-grained dexterous manipulation, and adaptation to different interaction angles.
 - **Comparison Basis:** The effectiveness was qualitatively contrasted with scenarios where a fixed wide-angle camera or software-based zooming would be used, highlighting the actuated neck's advantages in depth perception and dynamic occlusion handling.

- **Impact of an Actuated Neck on Imitation Learning:**
 - **Policy Architecture:** An imitation learning policy (ACT) was trained to predict joint configurations for the robot's right arm and the actuated neck.
 - **Input Data:** Proprioceptive input (joint positions of neck, arm, end-effector) and visual input (neck camera observations concatenated with wrist camera observations) were used. The context window was set to 1 for immediate feedback.
 - **Task Design:** Three specific tasks requiring dynamic neck adjustments were chosen:
 1. **Left-to-right Pick and Place (L2R):** Picking from a bin and placing on a top shelf, requiring horizontal neck adjustments.
 2. **Close-range Object Manipulation (CRange):** Manipulating an object close to the robot's body, requiring the neck to retract inward.
 3. **Cup Transfer From Bottom Shelf (CfB):** Picking a cup from beneath a coffee table and placing it in a box on a higher table, requiring vertical neck adjustments.
 - **Data Collection:** 120 teleoperated demonstrations were collected for each task, simultaneously controlling the right arm and the neck.
 - **Training Strategy:** A single imitation learning policy was trained on a merged dataset from all three tasks without explicit task labeling. Object locations were varied during training and testing to assess generalization.
 - **Baseline Comparison:** A separate policy was trained using data from a 160-degree diagonal ultra-wide camera (static baseline). For fair comparison, the L2R task was modified (L2Rmod) to remove explicit "peeking" behavior, eliminating occlusions that a static camera could not handle by definition.
 - **Evaluation:** 15 trials per task were conducted, with slightly altered object locations. Success rates were recorded for both the actuated neck policy and the static wide-angle camera policy. The role of the global neck camera guiding local wrist cameras was also observed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded findings in both teleoperation performance and the efficacy of imitation learning with an actuated neck.

#### 4.1. Actuated Neck Enabled Intuitive Teleoperation

The qualitative evaluation across seven complex, whole-body manipulation tasks demonstrated that the actuated neck significantly enhanced the teleoperation experience.

- **Improved Situational Awareness and Occlusion Handling:** Operators reported improved situational awareness due to the ability to dynamically adjust viewpoints, "peek" around obstacles, and observe objects from occluded angles. For tasks like hanging a sweatshirt or retrieving items from a refrigerator (as shown in Figure 1 and Figure 4), the actuated neck allowed operators to locate target objects and understand their spatial relationship to the robot in ways a fixed camera could not.
- **Enhanced Dexterous Manipulation:** During precision tasks, such as filling a cup with water or placing items accurately, the actuated neck allowed operators to refine camera angles for fine-grained observation of the dexterous hand's movements. This capability contributed to improved control and higher task success rates.
- **Facilitated Whole-Body Coordination:** The ability to dynamically adjust the viewpoint was crucial for tasks requiring the robot to reposition its mobile base relative to the workspace. For instance, optimally positioning the robot for hanging a sweatshirt necessitated coordinated base movement and viewpoint adjustments.
- **Contrast with Wide-angle Cameras:** The study highlighted that wide-angle cameras, while offering a broad field of view, suffer from distortion and lack the ability to dynamically change perspective. This limitation makes them less effective in handling occlusions or adapting to dynamic environments compared to an actuated neck that provides depth perception and targeted views.

#### 4.2. Impact of an Actuated Neck on Imitation Learning

The experiments on training autonomous policies showed a quantitative advantage for policies learned with an actuated neck.

- **Significant Performance Improvement:** As presented in Table 1, the actuated neck policy consistently outperformed the static wide-angle camera policy across all three evaluated tasks.
 - **CfB (Cup Transfer From Bottom Shelf):** The actuated neck policy achieved a 95% success rate, whereas the static wide-angle camera policy failed completely (0%). This task critically required vertical neck adjustments to keep the cup visible from under a coffee table and then to guide it into a box on a higher table (Figure 6). The static camera could not adjust its view downwards, causing the cup to be out of its effective field of view.
 - **L2Rmod (Left-to-right Pick and Place - modified):** The actuated neck policy achieved 90% success, compared to 78% for the static wide-angle camera. Even without explicit "peeking," dynamic horizontal adjustments provided an advantage.
 - **CRange (Close-range Object Manipulation):** The actuated neck policy succeeded in 82% of trials, while the static wide-angle camera managed 68%. The neck's ability to retract inward for close observation proved beneficial.
- **Reduced Distribution Shift and Enhanced Robustness:** The observed performance difference is attributed to the actuated neck's ability to provide a focused, dynamic view, which reduces distribution shift. Unlike static wide-angle cameras that may capture distorted images or irrelevant background details (even minor changes in wire positions on the floor could cause issues), the actuated neck adapts to specific areas, ensuring consistent and relevant visibility.
- **Global Camera Guides Local Wrist Cameras:** The actuated neck's camera played a critical role as a "global" guide. In tasks like CfB, it detected and localized objects initially outside the local wrist cameras' view, such as the cup on the bottom shelf or the packaging box on the top table (Figure 6a). Subsequently, the local wrist cameras provided fine-grained details for precise grasping and release. This hierarchical perception, enabled by the dynamic neck, allowed for effective reasoning over a broad visual area while maintaining precision at the manipulation point.
- **Autonomous Task Reasoning:** The policy, trained on a merged dataset without explicit task labeling, demonstrated the ability to implicitly reason about the tasks at hand and adjust its neck movements accordingly. This indicates that the active perception provided by the neck contributes to more intelligent and adaptable autonomous behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research suggest several significant implications and potential impacts across robotic teleoperation and autonomous system development.

#### 5.1. Enhanced Teleoperation Capabilities

The integration of a 5-DOF actuated neck into a teleoperation system represents a notable advancement in providing operators with a more intuitive and natural control experience. By mimicking human head movements, the system effectively reduces the cognitive load on human operators. This improved intuitiveness translates to enhanced task efficiency and broader task capabilities, particularly in complex manipulation scenarios involving occlusions, dynamic environments, or requiring precise dexterous movements. The ability to "peek" around obstacles and adjust viewpoints dynamically allows for more comprehensive situational awareness, which is crucial for remote operation where the operator lacks direct physical presence. This could lead to wider adoption of teleoperation for tasks that currently require significant operator training or are deemed too challenging due to perceptual limitations.

#### 5.2. Improved Data Collection for Autonomous Learning

One of the most significant impacts lies in the quality of data generated for imitation learning. The actuated neck provides dynamic, task-relevant visual information, which is a departure from the static or wide-angle camera views often used. This dynamic perspective reduces issues like image distortion and out-of-distribution perception, thereby mitigating distribution shift. As demonstrated by the higher success rates of policies trained with the actuated neck, particularly in challenging tasks with occlusions or varying object heights, this approach enables the collection of more robust and generalizable demonstration data. Higher-quality data can accelerate the development of autonomous policies, requiring fewer demonstrations and leading to more reliable robotic behaviors in diverse, real-world conditions.

#### 5.3. Advancements in Interactive and Adaptive Perception

The research highlights the role of the actuated neck in facilitating interactive perception. By integrating dynamic sensor observations with action trajectories, the system allows autonomous policies to implicitly learn spatial relationships and object salience relevant to tasks. This capability to actively seek and maintain visual information, mirroring human behavior, can lead to more adaptive and intelligent autonomous robots. Robots equipped with such active perception systems could potentially generalize better across different environmental layouts, object placements, and task variations without requiring extensive pre-programming or explicit task labeling. This moves towards robots that can reason more effectively about their environment through active sensory exploration.

#### 5.4. Broader Application Potential

The methodology and findings have potential implications for a wide range of applications. In remote inspection and maintenance, dynamic viewpoint adjustment could allow operators to thoroughly inspect difficult-to-reach areas. In logistics and manufacturing, more intuitive teleoperation and robust autonomous policies could lead to greater efficiency and flexibility in handling diverse items. For domestic robotics, the ability to manage household objects in varied and often cluttered environments, as demonstrated by the teleoperation tasks, is a critical step. The untethered and mobile nature of the robotic platform further broadens its applicability to dynamic and unstructured outdoor or indoor environments.

In conclusion, this work contributes to the development of more capable and intuitive robotic systems by addressing fundamental challenges in perception for teleoperation and data collection for autonomous learning. The actuated neck represents a significant step towards human-like interaction and learning capabilities in robotics, with the potential to enhance both human-in-the-loop and fully autonomous applications.
