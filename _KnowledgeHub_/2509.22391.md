# Do LLM Agents Know How to Ground, Recover, and Assess? A Benchmark for Epistemic Competence in Information-Seeking Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Duke Kunshan University and Microsoft AI developed SeekBench, a benchmark for evaluating the epistemic competence of Large Language Model (LLM) search agents, focusing on process-level groundedness, recovery, and calibration. Their findings indicate that while reinforcement learning improves agent accuracy and calibration, it often diminishes the quality of evidence-grounded reasoning.

## Problem

- Existing evaluation methods for LLM search agents primarily focus on final answer accuracy, neglecting the quality of the internal reasoning process.
- This outcome-only approach masks critical behavioral deficiencies like hallucination, ungrounded inferences, and miscalibration of confidence.
- A lack of process-level insight prevents understanding how agents reason, hindering targeted improvements and leading to potentially spurious performance assessments.

## Method

- Introduces **SeekBench**, the first benchmark designed to evaluate the ==epistemic competence== of LLM search agents through a detailed, process-level analysis.
- Develops a robust annotation schema, validated by human experts and LLM judges (GPT-5, GPT-4.1), to systematically categorize and evaluate agent reasoning and search steps.
- Formalizes three quantitative metrics: Reasoning Quality Index (RQI) for groundedness, Evidence Recovery Function (ERF) for recovery, and Calibration Error (CE) for assessing confidence alignment with evidence.

## Results

- RL-trained agents achieved higher F1 scores (e.g., ASearcher at **39.77%**) and improved calibration, reducing "overconfident answering" from **63.1%** (base models) to **35.3%** (RL agents).
- Few-shot prompting yielded the highest Reasoning Quality Index (RQI = **0.27**), outperforming all RL-trained agents, suggesting a decline in grounded reasoning due to current RL training methods.
- The study identified "REFINE" and "FOLLOW-UP" as the most effective search strategies for recovering from low-quality evidence, providing concrete guidance for agent policy design.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A significant trade-off exists where current reinforcement learning training improves final answer accuracy and calibration but often degrades the quality of evidence-grounded reasoning processes in LLM agents.
> - The success of combining specialized agents (agent synthesis) highlights the potential of modular AI architectures, where different components excel at specific epistemic tasks like evidence collection or synthesis.
> - Process-level evaluation exposes specific agent weaknesses, such as difficulties in "Plan Formation" and "State Assessment," and reveals hidden capabilities, like a base model's strong evidence gathering, that are overlooked by outcome-only metrics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has ushered in a new era of AI capabilities, rapidly shifting from models requiring explicit prompting to increasingly autonomous LLM agents. These agents, particularly "search agents," are designed to tackle complex information-seeking problems by iteratively reasoning, utilizing external search tools, and integrating retrieved evidence. Much recent work explores training these agents with reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize their decision-making strategies for tasks like open-domain question answering (QA).

However, the prevailing evaluation methodologies in this domain suffer from a critical limitation: they predominantly focus on **final answer accuracy**, employing metrics such as exact match or F1 score. While these metrics assess the outcome, they neglect the intricate *process* through which agents arrive at their answers. This oversight creates a significant blind spot, as agents might achieve high accuracy scores through superficial means, such as pattern matching or fortuitous guesses, rather than through robust, justified, and epistemically sound reasoning.

This paper directly addresses this fundamental gap by introducing **SeekBench**, the first benchmark specifically designed to evaluate the *epistemic competence* of LLM search agents. Epistemic competence refers to an agent's ability to reliably acquire, evaluate, and act upon knowledge in a justified manner. The existing research landscape has recognized the need for deeper evaluation, with some works exploring causal analysis of reasoning chains, graph-based methods for step dependencies, or intermediate step tracking. However, these prior approaches primarily focus on structural alignment or partial process analysis and lack a formal framework to assess genuine epistemic traits like evidence grounding, adaptive recovery from information gaps, or calibrated confidence under uncertainty. Concerns about LLM "hallucination," overconfidence, and lack of transparency further amplify the urgency for more comprehensive evaluation methods.

SeekBench thus represents a crucial advancement by shifting the focus from "what" an agent answers to "how" it reasons and interacts with information. It provides a new lens through which to assess the trustworthiness, reliability, and true intelligence of autonomous LLM agents, guiding the field towards developing AI systems that are not only performant but also epistemically sound and accountable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged limitations of current evaluation protocols for LLM search agents. As LLMs transition from simple prompt-response systems to autonomous agents capable of complex information-seeking and decision-making, evaluating solely on final answer accuracy becomes insufficient and potentially misleading. The authors identify several critical issues:
- **Masked Behavioral Gaps:** Accuracy-only metrics fail to reveal crucial behavioral deficiencies, such as agents hallucinating claims, making unsupported inferences, failing to identify or recover from knowledge gaps, or exhibiting overconfidence in their answers.
- **Lack of Process-Level Insight:** Without a step-level analysis, it is impossible to understand *how* agents navigate the information landscape, *why* they make certain decisions, or the true impact of algorithmic innovations on their reasoning capabilities. This can lead to spurious performance gains that do not reflect genuine improvements in reasoning or information-seeking strategies.
- **Need for Reliable AI:** For autonomous agents to be deployed in real-world applications, especially those with high stakes, their processes must be reliable, transparent, and justified. Epistemic competence – the ability to acquire, evaluate, and act upon knowledge soundly – is paramount for building trustworthy AI systems.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets forth several key objectives:
1. **Introduce SeekBench:** To establish the first benchmark specifically designed for process-level evaluation of LLM search agents' epistemic competence.
2. **Develop an Epistemic Evaluation Framework:** To create a structured framework that enables granular, step-level analysis of agent response traces, moving beyond final outcomes.
3. **Formalize Epistemic Competencies:** To rigorously define three core epistemic competencies crucial for information-seeking agents:
 - **Groundedness:** The ability to generate reasoning steps directly supported by retrieved evidence.
 - **Recovery:** The capacity to adaptively reformulate searches and overcome situations of low-quality or insufficient information.
 - **Calibration:** The skill to accurately assess whether current evidence is sufficient to provide a reliable answer, avoiding both overconfidence and overcautiousness.
4. **Design Quantitative Metrics:** To develop precise, interpretable, and measurable metrics (Reasoning Quality Index - RQI, Evidence Recovery Function - ERF, Calibration Error - CE) for quantifying these competencies.
5. **Conduct Large-Scale Evaluation:** To apply SeekBench to state-of-the-art LLM search agents and widely used QA benchmarks, identifying common behavioral gaps and unique agent specializations.
6. **Provide Development Guidance:** To leverage the findings to offer concrete guidance for developing more capable, reliable, and epistemically sound information-seeking agents in the future.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research is structured into three distinct phases, drawing on principles from qualitative research like Content Analysis to systematically connect observable agent behaviors to underlying epistemic competencies, which are then quantified through novel metrics.

**Phase 1: Observable Features and Schema Construction**
The foundation of SeekBench is a robust, extensible annotation schema. This schema was developed iteratively and data-driven:
1. **Exploratory Analysis:** The researchers first extensively examined diverse agent traces to identify key observable behaviors and common failure patterns. This included noting different functions of reasoning steps (e.g., identifying gaps, summarizing, planning) and search steps (e.g., initial exploration, verification, follow-up), as well as errors like ungrounded reasoning or repetitive queries.
2. **Schema Development:** An initial annotation schema was then developed with two main aspects for each step:
 - **Functional Type:** Categorizing the cognitive purpose of a step (e.g., *InformationSynthesis*, *PlanFormation*, *StateAssessment*, *CritiqueAndCorrection* for reasoning steps; *InitialQuery*, *RefinedQuery*, *FollowUpQuery*, *RepeatQuery* for search steps).
 - **Quality Attribute:** Evaluating the epistemic soundness (e.g., *groundedness* of reasoning).
3. **Iterative Refinement and Validation:** Three expert annotators independently coded 190 agent traces over three rounds. Inter-annotator reliability was measured using Cohen's Kappa (κ). Features with low agreement (κ < 0.5) were either pruned or merged, resulting in 8 well-defined features. Further validation involved using GPT-5 to generate adversarial edge cases, ensuring the mutual exclusivity of definitions.
4. **LLM-as-Judge Validation:** The finalized schema was evaluated by human experts and state-of-the-art LLM judges (GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-5) using standardized prompts. This process demonstrated high human agreement (κ = 0.811) and strong alignment between LLM judges and human experts (GPT-5 achieved κ = 0.754). This validation is critical as it enables the scalable, cost-effective annotation required for large-scale evaluation.

**Phase 2: Latent Constructs and Competency Definition**
Building upon the observable patterns identified in Phase 1, the researchers applied the framework of latent construct inference to identify the underlying cognitive competencies. They observed systematic behavioral differences, such as variations in reasoning quality (supported vs. unsupported), adaptive versus repetitive search strategies, and differences in decision timing (premature vs. appropriate answering). These observations led to the formal definition of three core epistemic competencies:
- **Groundedness:** Measures the alignment of reasoning steps with retrieved evidence.
- **Recovery:** Evaluates an agent's ability to adapt its search strategies after initial failures to acquire sufficient information.
- **Calibration:** Assesses whether an agent's decision to provide an answer is appropriately aligned with the quality and sufficiency of the retrieved evidence.

**Phase 3: Competency Metrics and Operationalization**
In this final phase, the abstract epistemic competencies were translated into concrete, quantitative metrics, ensuring construct validity.
1. **Evidence State Definition:** A foundational concept, `Evidence State (E_i,t)`, was formally defined. It combines two binary annotations—`Clarity (C_i,t)` (unambiguous/interpretable) and `Quality (Q_i,t)` (sufficient information)—into a three-level state: `E=0` (poor), `E=1` (partial), `E=2` (good).
2. **Reasoning Quality Index (RQI):** To quantify Groundedness, the RQI was introduced. It measures the proportion of reasoning steps whose factual content is supported by retrieved evidence (`G_i,t`). RQI is calculated at both model-level (overall) and type-level (e.g., for Information Synthesis, Plan Formation, State Assessment), and can be decomposed by evidence state to understand how grounding changes with available evidence.
3. **Evidence Recovery Function (ERF):** For Recovery, the ERF quantifies the cumulative proportion of traces where an agent successfully reaches a high-evidence state (`E=2`) or provides a correct answer by a given turn. It employs Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to handle variable-length traces and identifies the most effective search strategies (e.g., RefinedQuery, FollowUpQuery) for recovery.
4. **Calibration Error (CE):** To assess Calibration, CE measures the deviation of an agent's answering behavior from an ideal policy (answering *if and only if* evidence is good). This metric captures both "overconfident answering" (answering with insufficient evidence) and "overcautious abstention" (failing to answer despite good evidence).

**Experimental Setup:**
The evaluation involved a diverse set of models: the base Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, its few-shot prompted version, and four state-of-the-art RL-trained agents (SEARCH-R1, RESEARCH, ASEARCHER, DEEPRESEARCHER). These agents were tested on seven question-answering benchmarks (both single-hop and multi-hop). The scale of evaluation was substantial, covering 28,493 traces and over 283,950 steps, with GPT-4.1-mini used for large-scale annotation. The datasets were sanitized to remove ambiguous questions and data contamination, ensuring reliable evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The large-scale evaluation using SeekBench revealed nuanced insights into the epistemic competencies of LLM search agents, often challenging conclusions drawn from traditional accuracy-only metrics.

1. **Accuracy vs. Grounded Reasoning:**
 - While all RL-trained agents generally achieved higher final answer F1 scores than the base model (ASearcher topping at 39.77%), this did not correlate with improved *evidence-grounded reasoning*.
 - **RL Training Degrades Groundedness:** Few-shot prompting achieved the highest Reasoning Quality Index (RQI = 0.27), significantly *outperforming all RL-trained agents*. This critical finding suggests that current RL training primarily optimizes for correct final answers, often at the expense of developing epistemically sound, evidence-grounded reasoning processes.
 - **Specific Reasoning Weaknesses:** Agents universally struggled most with "Plan Formation" and "State Assessment" (RQI consistently below 0.2), indicating fundamental difficulties in strategy formulation and identifying knowledge gaps. In contrast, "Information Synthesis" was a relative strength (e.g., ASEARCHER: 0.56 RQI), meaning agents are better at summarizing existing information than at higher-order reasoning.
 - **Epistemic Misalignment:** Some agents, like SEARCH-R1, showed elevated groundedness even under insufficient evidence, hinting at spurious grounding or overconfidence in their reasoning regardless of actual evidence quality.

2. **Recovery from Insufficient Evidence:**
 - **Recovery Correlates with F1:** ASEARCHER, the agent with the highest F1 score, also demonstrated superior recovery performance (fastest Evidence Recovery Function increase), reinforcing the link between adaptive search strategies and overall success.
 - **Effective Search Strategies:** "REFINE" and "FOLLOW-UP" search queries were identified as the most effective strategies for recovering from low-quality evidence, while "REPEAT" queries offered minimal benefit. "Grounded Reasoning" also contributed to better evidence utilization. This provides concrete guidance for improving agent search policies.

3. **Evidence-Aligned Calibration:**
 - **Evidence Quality Drives Accuracy:** The research confirmed a strong positive correlation between higher evidence quality (E=2) and increased answer accuracy (RL-trained models achieved 31.6% accuracy with good evidence versus 8.4% without support).
 - **RL Training Improves Calibration:** RL-trained models showed substantial improvements in calibration. They significantly reduced "overconfident answering" (from 63.1% in base models to 35.3% in RL agents) and achieved the lowest overall Calibration Error (0.309). This indicates that RL training successfully teaches agents to be more selective and align their answering decisions with the perceived sufficiency of evidence.
 - **Persistent Calibration Deficiencies:** Despite improvements, agents still exhibited widespread "overconfident answering" (76.5% of trajectories for RL agents), meaning they often answered prematurely before acquiring sufficient evidence. Conversely, some agents, like SEARCH-R1, showed "overcautious abstention" (18.7%), failing to answer despite having good evidence.

4. **Exploiting Epistemic Competencies through Agent Synthesis:**
 - The evaluation revealed distinct specializations: ASEARCHER excelled in evidence acquisition and recovery, while SEARCH-R1 showed superior information synthesis capabilities and conservative answering.
 - **Synergistic Performance:** By combining agents (one for evidence collection, another for answer synthesis), significant performance gains were observed. SEARCH-R1 emerged as the most effective synthesizer (+1.27 F1 average gain), especially when using evidence collected by the Base model (+3.50 F1 improvement).
 - **Hidden Capabilities:** Surprisingly, the Base model, despite its lowest standalone F1 score, provided high-quality evidence that, when synthesized by other agents, led to significantly better answers. This suggests that traditional accuracy metrics can *underestimate* the underlying evidence-gathering capabilities of less performant models and *overstate* the overall gains of RL training.

5. **Metric Trade-offs:**
 - A critical inverse relationship was identified: while RL training improved final answer accuracy and calibration, it simultaneously degraded evidence-grounded reasoning quality. This reveals a fundamental trade-off that is overlooked by outcome-only evaluations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a pivotal step in the evaluation and development of Large Language Model agents, offering profound significance and potential impact across several dimensions:

1. **Paradigm Shift in LLM Agent Evaluation:** SeekBench introduces a much-needed shift from purely outcome-based (final answer accuracy) to **process-level and epistemically-informed evaluation**. This provides a more holistic and interpretable understanding of agent capabilities, moving beyond superficial performance metrics to truly assess how agents reason, learn, and interact with information. This methodological innovation is critical for building robust and trustworthy AI.

2. **Enhancing AI Reliability and Trustworthiness:** By formally defining and quantifying epistemic competence (groundedness, recovery, calibration), this work provides concrete tools to develop AI systems that are less prone to common failure modes like hallucination, overconfidence, and opaque decision-making. Future agents can be trained and optimized not just for correctness, but for the *justification* and *reliability* of their outputs, which is paramount for their adoption in high-stakes real-world applications (e.g., healthcare, finance, legal).

3. **Granular Debugging and Targeted Improvement:** The detailed step-level analysis and the breakdown of performance across specific epistemic competencies allow researchers and developers to precisely pinpoint where agents succeed and fail. For example, identifying weaknesses in "Plan Formation" or "State Assessment" highlights specific areas for targeted algorithmic improvements, rather than relying on trial-and-error based on aggregate accuracy. The ability to identify agents' specialized strengths, like SEARCH-R1's synthesis prowess, opens doors for novel agent design strategies.

4. **Guidance for Future Agent Architectures and Training:**
 - **Multi-objective Optimization:** The discovery of trade-offs, such as improved accuracy/calibration versus degraded grounded reasoning in RL-trained agents, emphasizes the need for multi-objective optimization strategies in agent training. Future reinforcement learning schemes should explicitly incorporate epistemic criteria to foster both accurate outcomes and sound reasoning processes.
 - **Modular Agent Design:** The success of "agent synthesis," where different agents' strengths are combined, points towards a future of modular AI architectures. This could involve specialized components for evidence acquisition, reasoning, synthesis, and calibration, allowing for the best-of-breed combination to create more sophisticated and capable information-seeking systems.
 - **Focus on Metacognition:** The identified weaknesses in higher-order reasoning skills like plan formation and state assessment underscore the urgent need for research into enhancing the metacognitive capabilities of LLMs.

5. **Increased Interpretability and Explainability:** By providing a structured framework for analyzing the "why" and "how" of agent actions, SeekBench contributes significantly to the interpretability and explainability of LLM agents. Understanding when and why an agent failed to ground its reasoning, or why it made an overconfident assertion, is a crucial step towards building AI systems that can be understood and audited by humans.

In conclusion, SeekBench provides a foundational benchmark that moves beyond superficial performance metrics to evaluate the deep cognitive processes of LLM agents. It not only exposes critical behavioral gaps in current models but also offers a clear roadmap for developing more transparent, reliable, and truly intelligent autonomous AI systems, thereby shaping the future direction of LLM agent research and development.
