# Imitation Learning as Return Distribution Matching

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Politecnico di Milano developed a framework for imitation learning (IL) that matches an expert's entire return distribution using Wasserstein distance, addressing the limitations of risk-neutral IL and partial risk-sensitive methods. The framework introduces a novel class of non-Markovian policies and provides provably efficient algorithms, RS-BC and RS-KT, which empirically outperform baselines in imitating risk-sensitive behaviors.

## Problem

- Traditional imitation learning (IL) methods are risk-neutral, focusing on matching only the expert's expected return and neglecting their full risk attitude (e.g., variance, tail behavior).
- Existing risk-sensitive IL approaches only match specific risk measures like Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) at a single alpha level, providing an incomplete imitation of the expert's comprehensive risk profile.
- Markovian policies, commonly used in IL, are often insufficient to capture general risk-sensitive behaviors, potentially leading to misspecification errors when imitating non-Markovian experts.

## Method

- Formulating Imitation Learning as a ==Return Distribution Matching== (**RDM**) problem, where the objective is to minimize the Wasserstein distance between the learned policy's and expert's return distributions.
- Introducing an efficient class of non-Markovian policies, "Pi(r_theta)", which condition actions on the current state, stage, and a discretized version of the cumulative reward received so far.
- Developing two provably efficient algorithms: Risk-Sensitive Behavior Cloning (RS-BC) for offline learning with unknown transitions, and Risk-Sensitive imitation with Known Transition (RS-KT) which uses a Linear Program for policy optimization when dynamics are known.

## Results

- RS-BC and RS-KT consistently outperform risk-neutral baselines (Behavioral Cloning and MIMIC-MD) in matching the expert's return distribution, particularly for non-Markovian expert policies and larger horizons.
- RS-KT achieves substantially lower sample complexity, being independent of state (S) and action (A) space sizes, and demonstrates superior performance with significantly fewer trajectories in large MDPs compared to RS-BC and baselines.
- The approximation error of RS-BC and RS-KT is directly controlled by the reward discretization parameter (theta), with larger theta leading to increased error, aligning with theoretical predictions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Markovian policies are fundamentally insufficient for general Return Distribution Matching, as demonstrated by a counterexample showing non-zero Wasserstein distance even for short horizons.
> - Wasserstein distance is a suitable metric for RDM, as it implies closeness in expected return, variance, and all levels of CVaR, while statistically stronger metrics like total variation require exponentially more samples.
> - A novel class of non-Markovian policies, conditioned on discretized cumulative reward, is sufficiently expressive to accurately approximate any expert's return distribution while maintaining a tractable, polynomial representation size.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation Learning (IL) is a prominent field in reinforcement learning (RL) that seeks to train agents by mimicking expert demonstrations, thereby circumventing the challenges of manual reward function design. Traditional IL algorithms, such as Behavioral Cloning (BC) and Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL), typically focus on matching the expert's occupancy measure. This approach primarily ensures that the imitator's expected return (average performance) is close to that of the expert. The assumption of Markovian policies being sufficiently expressive for occupancy measure matching is central to these methods.

However, a growing body of research recognizes that real-world expert behavior, particularly from humans in stochastic environments like finance or autonomous driving, often exhibits risk-sensitive attitudes. Standard IL methods, by focusing solely on expected return, are inherently risk-neutral and overlook other crucial characteristics of the return distribution, such as variance or tail behavior.

To address this limitation, some existing works have extended IL to risk-sensitive settings by attempting to match specific risk measures, such as the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) at a fixed level, in addition to the expected return. While these approaches acknowledge the importance of risk, they face two primary limitations: first, matching only a single risk measure like CVaR at a chosen alpha level provides a limited and incomplete imitation of the expert's full risk attitude; second, these methods often implicitly or explicitly rely on Markovian policies, which have been shown to be insufficiently expressive to capture general risk-sensitive behaviors, potentially leading to significant misspecification errors.

This research positions itself within this landscape by proposing a more comprehensive framework for risk-sensitive IL. It argues that a more robust imitation of expert risk attitude requires matching the *entire* expert return distribution, not just its expectation or a specific tail risk measure. Furthermore, it explicitly challenges the sufficiency of Markovian policies for this task, advocating for the necessity of non-Markovian (history-dependent) policies to accurately model complex risk-sensitive behaviors, even in tabular settings with finite horizons. By introducing a novel policy class and provably efficient algorithms, this work aims to provide a theoretical and algorithmic foundation for training agents that can truly mimic the full spectrum of an expert's performance and risk preferences.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a robust framework for training a risk-sensitive reinforcement learning agent through imitation learning. This goes beyond existing paradigms by aiming not only to match the expert's average performance (expected return) but also to accurately replicate its complete risk attitude, which is encoded in the shape of its entire return distribution.

The motivation for this comprehensive approach is multifaceted:

- **Limitations of Standard Imitation Learning:** Traditional IL algorithms are inherently risk-neutral. They prioritize matching the expert's occupancy measure, which, while ensuring similar expected returns, completely disregards the expert's risk preferences. In many practical domains, such as financial trading, medical decision-making, or autonomous driving, experts (often humans) do not solely optimize for average outcomes. Instead, they exhibit complex risk-sensitive behaviors, valuing outcomes based on their variability, potential for extreme losses, or overall distribution. An agent that only matches average performance might, for example, take excessively risky actions that lead to high variance in returns, even if the expected return is matched.
- **Incompleteness of Existing Risk-Sensitive IL Methods:** Previous attempts to introduce risk sensitivity into IL have focused on matching specific risk measures, such as the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) at a chosen percentile (α-level). While an improvement over purely risk-neutral methods, this approach offers only a partial imitation. Matching a single point (CVaR) or even a few points of the return distribution does not capture its entire shape. A policy could match the expected return and CVaR at one level but still have a drastically different and undesirable return distribution in other aspects (e.g., higher variance or different tail behavior at other α-levels).
- **Insufficiency of Markovian Policies:** A fundamental assumption in much of traditional and even some risk-sensitive IL is the sufficiency of Markovian policies. For occupancy measure matching, it is known that for any arbitrary policy, a Markovian policy exists with the same occupancy measure. However, for risk-sensitive control, particularly when considering the entire return distribution or even specific risk measures like CVaR, optimal policies are often non-Markovian. This means that an agent's optimal action may depend not only on the current state and time step but also on its accumulated return or other aspects of its history. Ignoring this necessity and restricting policy search to Markovian policies can lead to significant "misspecification error," where even the best possible Markovian policy cannot adequately imitate the expert's risk-sensitive behavior. The paper provides theoretical evidence (Proposition 3.2) demonstrating this limitation for even short horizons.
- **The Need for a General Metric:** To address the above, the authors propose Return Distribution Matching (RDM) as the objective, using the Wasserstein distance to quantify the similarity between the expert's and the agent's return distributions. Wasserstein distance is chosen because it naturally quantifies distances between distributions, implying closeness in various statistical moments (expectation, variance) and risk measures (CVaR at any level), making it a comprehensive metric for risk imitation. The paper also highlights that stronger metrics like total variation distance are often statistically intractable, requiring an exponential number of samples.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond incomplete notions of imitation towards a holistic replication of expert behavior, particularly concerning risk, necessitating a shift towards matching entire return distributions and embracing the expressivity of non-Markovian policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on formulating Imitation Learning as a Return Distribution Matching (RDM) problem and developing provably efficient algorithms for it, especially considering the necessity of non-Markovian policies.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation: Return Distribution Matching (RDM)

The paper formalizes RDM as minimizing the Wasserstein distance between the return distribution induced by the learned policy ($\eta_{\hat{\pi}}^{r_E}$) and the expert's return distribution ($\eta_{\pi_E}^{r_E}$).
- **Known-Reward Setting (Primary Focus)**: The objective is $\min_{\pi \in \Pi_{NM}} W(\eta_{\pi}^{r_E}, \eta_{\pi_E}^{r_E})$, where $r_E$ is the expert's reward function, and $\Pi_{NM}$ is the set of non-Markovian policies.
- **Unknown-Reward Setting (Robust RDM)**: For cases where $r_E$ is unknown, a robust formulation is proposed: $\min_{\pi \in \Pi_{NM}} \max_{r:S \times A \times JHK \to} W(\eta_{\pi}^{r}, \eta_{\pi_E}^{r})$. This seeks a policy that matches the expert's return distribution for all possible reward functions.

#### 3.2. Key Theoretical Insights and Challenges Addressed

1. **Generality of Wasserstein Distance**: The paper establishes that matching return distributions in Wasserstein distance is a more general objective. If the Wasserstein distance is small, it implies that the policies are close in terms of expected return, variance, and CVaR at any level (Appendix B.1).
2. **Sample Complexity for Stronger Metrics**: Theorem 3.1 demonstrates that using stronger metrics like total variation distance for return distribution matching would require an exponentially large number of expert trajectories, even if the environment model is fully known. This justifies the choice of Wasserstein distance for its favorable sample complexity.
3. **Insufficiency of Markovian Policies**: Proposition 3.2 provides a concrete counterexample where, even for a simple MDP with horizon H=3, any Markovian policy incurs a minimum Wasserstein distance of at least 0.5 from a non-Markovian expert's return distribution. This highlights the necessity of non-Markovian policies for RDM.

#### 3.3. An Efficient Class of Non-Markovian Policies

To address the intractability of optimizing over the entire set of non-Markovian policies ($\Pi_{NM}$), the paper introduces a subclass of policies, $\Pi(r)$, where action choices depend on the current state ($s$), stage ($h$), and the *cumulative reward collected so far* ($G(\omega;r)$).
- **Expressivity (Lemma 4.1)**: This class $\Pi(r_E)$ is shown to be sufficiently expressive, containing at least one policy ($\pi_{r_E}$) that perfectly matches the expert's return distribution ($\eta_{\pi_{r_E}}^{r_E} = \eta_{\pi_E}^{r_E}$).
- **The Discretization Approach**: Directly working with $G(\omega;r)$ can still lead to a state space exponential in the horizon if rewards are continuous or fine-grained. To overcome this, the authors introduce a discretization parameter $\theta \in (0,1]$. A discretized reward $r_\theta$ is defined such that $r_{\theta,h}(s,a)$ is the closest value in a $\theta$-covering grid $Y_\theta$ to the true reward $r_h(s,a)$. This leads to a policy class $\Pi(r_E,\theta)$ where actions depend on the discretized cumulative reward. The size of this augmented state space is polynomial in $H/\theta$.
- **Approximation Error (Lemma 4.2)**: The error introduced by this discretization is bounded: $W(\eta_{\pi_{r_E,\theta}}^{r_E}, \eta_{\pi_E}^{r_E}) \le H\theta$. This means the approximation error can be controlled by choosing a sufficiently small $\theta$.

#### 3.4. Algorithms for the Known-Reward Setting

1. **RS-BC (Risk-Sensitive Behavior Cloning - Algorithm 1)**:
 - **Setting**: No-interaction (offline), unknown transition model, known expert reward $r_E$.
 - **Approach**: Directly estimates the policy $\pi_{r_E,\theta}$ from the expert dataset $D_E$. It counts the occurrences of (state, discretized cumulative reward, action) tuples in the expert trajectories and uses these empirical frequencies to define the learned policy $\hat{\pi}$. This is analogous to standard Behavioral Cloning but in an augmented state space that includes cumulative reward.
 - **Theoretical Guarantee (Theorem 4.3)**: Provides a worst-case upper bound on sample complexity. With $\theta = \epsilon/(4H)$, it requires $N = \tilde{O}(SAH^6/(\epsilon^3 \delta))$ samples to achieve $W(\eta_{\pi_E}^{r_E}, \eta_{\hat{\pi}}^{r_E}) \le \epsilon$ with probability $1-\delta$.

2. **RS-KT (Risk-Sensitive imitation with Known Transition - Algorithm 2)**:
 - **Setting**: Known transition model $p$, known expert reward $r_E$.
 - **Approach**: First, it estimates the expert's return distribution $\eta_{\pi_E}^{r_E}$ (specifically $\eta_{\pi_E}^{r_E,\theta}$) from the dataset $D_E$. Second, it finds a policy $\hat{\pi} \in \Pi(r_E,\theta)$ whose return distribution is closest to this empirical estimate. This second step is formulated and solved as a Linear Program (LP) on an augmented MDP where states are tuples of (original state, cumulative discretized reward). The LP explicitly optimizes over occupancy measures to find one that induces the desired return distribution.
 - **Theoretical Guarantee (Theorem 4.4)**: Achieves a significantly lower sample complexity compared to RS-BC. With $\theta = \epsilon/(7H)$, it requires $N = O(H^2/(\epsilon^2 \delta))$ samples. Crucially, the sample complexity is independent of the size of the state ($S$) and action ($A$) spaces, highlighting its efficiency for large MDPs.

#### 3.5. Statistical Insights on the Unknown-Reward Setting

While the paper primarily focuses on the known-reward setting, it offers theoretical insights into the robust RDM problem (unknown $r_E$, seeking a policy that performs well for *all* $r$).
- **Estimating all Return Distributions (Theorem 5.1)**: Shows that a polynomial number of samples $N = \tilde{O}(SAH^3/(\epsilon^2 \delta))$ is sufficient to accurately estimate $\eta_{\pi_E}^{r}$ for *all possible* reward functions $r$ (after discretization to $r_\theta$).
- **Sample Efficiency of Robust RDM (Theorem 5.2)**: This implies that if an oracle could solve the complex minimax optimization problem (Eq. 12), the robust RDM problem itself would be sample-efficient, requiring polynomial samples. The practical algorithmic challenge of solving this minimax optimization efficiently is left for future work.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical guarantees and empirical validations for its proposed framework and algorithms.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Necessity of Non-Markovian Policies for RDM**: The paper formally demonstrates that Markovian policies are generally insufficient for Return Distribution Matching (RDM). Proposition 3.2 provides a concrete counterexample, showing a simple MDP where a non-Markovian expert's return distribution cannot be closely matched by any Markovian policy, even for a short horizon (H=3), leading to a significant Wasserstein distance. This finding underscores the motivation for moving beyond traditional Markovian policy classes in risk-sensitive IL.
- **Wasserstein Distance as a Suitable Metric**: The use of Wasserstein distance is justified as it is a more general and statistically tractable metric for comparing return distributions. It is shown to imply closeness in terms of expected return, variance, and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) across all levels (Appendix B.1), providing a comprehensive measure of risk imitation. Stronger metrics like total variation are shown to demand exponentially more samples (Theorem 3.1).
- **Efficient Non-Markovian Policy Class**: A novel subclass of non-Markovian policies, $\Pi(r_\theta)$, is introduced. These policies depend on the current state, stage, and the *discretized cumulative reward received so far*. This class is shown to be sufficiently expressive to approximate the expert's return distribution accurately (Lemma 4.2) while maintaining a tractable, polynomial representation size (in $S, A, H, 1/\theta$).
- **Provably Efficient Algorithms for Known-Reward Setting**:
 - **RS-BC (Risk-Sensitive Behavior Cloning)**: For the offline setting with an unknown transition model, RS-BC is proven to be sample-efficient, requiring a polynomial number of expert trajectories $N = \tilde{O}(SAH^6/(\epsilon^3 \delta))$ to achieve an $\epsilon$-accurate return distribution match with high probability (Theorem 4.3).
 - **RS-KT (Risk-Sensitive imitation with Known Transition)**: For the setting with a known transition model, RS-KT achieves substantially lower sample complexity, requiring $N = O(H^2/(\epsilon^2 \delta))$ samples (Theorem 4.4). Notably, this bound is independent of the state ($S$) and action ($A$) space sizes, making it highly efficient for large MDPs. The policy optimization step in RS-KT is formulated as a Linear Program, ensuring computational tractability.
- **Statistical Tractability of Robust RDM (Unknown Reward)**: The paper provides statistical insights for the more challenging unknown-reward setting. It proves that a polynomial number of samples $N = \tilde{O}(SAH^3/(\epsilon^2 \delta))$ is sufficient to accurately estimate the expert's return distribution for *any* reward function simultaneously (Theorem 5.1). This implies that the robust RDM problem is statistically tractable if an appropriate optimization oracle were available (Theorem 5.2).

#### 4.2. Empirical Findings (Numerical Simulations)

Numerical simulations compare RS-BC and RS-KT against standard IL baselines (Behavioral Cloning (BC) and MIMIC-MD) across various scenarios, providing evidence for the theoretical claims:

- **Superiority over Risk-Neutral Baselines**: For non-Markovian expert policies, RS-BC and RS-KT consistently outperform BC and MIMIC-MD in matching the expert's return distribution. The standard IL algorithms, by using Markovian policies, exhibit a persistent bias, failing to reduce the error significantly even with a large number of expert trajectories. This performance gap is more pronounced with larger horizons (H).
- **Impact of Discretization Parameter ($\theta$)**: Increasing $\theta$ (i.e., coarser discretization) leads to a larger approximation error, causing RS-BC and RS-KT to perform worse, as predicted by Lemma 4.2. RS-KT's performance is observed to be more sensitive to a larger $\theta$ than RS-BC, suggesting a trade-off between computational efficiency (larger $\theta$) and accuracy.
- **Performance with Markovian Experts**: When the expert's policy is truly Markovian, standard BC outperforms RS-BC and RS-KT. This is attributed to BC operating on a smaller, unbiased hypothesis space (Markovian policies) when the expert is indeed Markovian, leading to better sample and computational efficiency.
- **Sample Efficiency Advantage of RS-KT**: For large state and action spaces, RS-KT demonstrates a dramatic improvement in sample efficiency compared to RS-BC, confirming its theoretical advantage. It achieves good performance with significantly fewer trajectories, while RS-BC and BC struggle to converge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a paradigm shift in risk-sensitive Imitation Learning, moving from partial imitation of risk measures to comprehensive matching of the entire return distribution. The significance and potential impact of this work are substantial across theoretical, algorithmic, and applied domains:

- **Enhanced Understanding of Risk-Sensitive Imitation**: The paper rigorously highlights the fundamental limitations of traditional (risk-neutral) and prior risk-sensitive IL methods, particularly their reliance on insufficient Markovian policy classes and partial risk measures. By formally demonstrating the necessity of non-Markovian policies and the comprehensive nature of Wasserstein distance for RDM, it provides a more accurate conceptual foundation for imitating complex, human-like risk-sensitive behaviors.
- **Development of Provably Efficient Algorithms**: The introduction of RS-BC and RS-KT offers concrete, provably efficient algorithms for RDM in tabular settings. RS-BC provides a robust baseline for offline learning when environment dynamics are unknown, while RS-KT leverages known dynamics to achieve impressive sample efficiency, especially in large state-action spaces. The formulation of RS-KT's core optimization as a tractable Linear Program is a significant algorithmic contribution.
- **Broader Applicability of Imitation Learning**: The ability to match an expert's entire return distribution opens new avenues for applying IL in critical domains where risk attitude is paramount. This includes:
 - **Finance**: Training trading agents that not only maximize profit but also manage portfolio risk consistent with human experts.
 - **Autonomous Driving**: Developing self-driving systems that adhere to human-like safety margins and risk-averse behaviors in uncertain situations.
 - **Healthcare**: Assisting medical robots or diagnostic AI in replicating physician decision-making, balancing treatment efficacy with potential side effects or complications.
 - **Human-Robot Interaction**: Creating more natural and trustworthy robot partners that imitate human preferences for safety and predictability.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The work lays a strong theoretical groundwork for several future research directions.
 - **Scaling to Complex Environments**: The tabular setting is a starting point; extending these ideas to large, continuous state-action spaces (e.g., using deep learning for function approximation) is a natural next step with potentially high impact.
 - **Practical Algorithms for Unknown-Reward RDM**: The statistical tractability of robust RDM in the unknown-reward setting (Theorem 5.2) poses an open algorithmic challenge for efficiently solving the minimax optimization, which could lead to more general and robust IL systems.
 - **State-Only Observation and Partial Observability**: Extending the framework to settings where only state observations are available or the environment is partially observable, which often gives rise to non-Markovian behavior, would further enhance its practical relevance.
- **Highlighting Trade-offs**: The empirical analysis provides valuable insights into the trade-offs between policy expressivity, sample efficiency, and computational complexity, which is crucial for practitioners designing IL systems.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the field of Imitation Learning by proposing a more comprehensive, theoretically sound, and practically relevant approach to imitating risk-sensitive expert behaviors, thereby broadening the scope and impact of IL applications.
