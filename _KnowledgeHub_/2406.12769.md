# Latent Intuitive Physics: Learning to Transfer Hidden Physics from A 3D Video

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University developed a latent intuitive physics framework that infers hidden fluid properties from a single 3D video and transfers this learned physics to simulate novel scenes. The probabilistic approach significantly outperforms existing methods in generalizing fluid dynamics to unseen geometries and boundary conditions, while also providing superior short-term and long-term particle prediction accuracy when physical parameters are unknown.

## Problem

- Most deep learning physics simulators require explicit knowledge of physical properties, which are often difficult to obtain or measure.
- Prior intuitive physics models struggle with inherent stochasticity and complexity of real-world phenomena, often requiring extensive finetuning for new properties or overfitting to observed scenes.
- A lack of effective methods to transfer learned physical properties from visual observations to generalize simulations in entirely novel scenarios.

## Method

- A **Latent Intuitive Physics** framework is proposed, comprising a ==probabilistic particle transition module==, a ==physical prior learner==, a ==particle-based posterior estimator==, and a ==neural renderer==.
- A ==three-stage training pipeline== is introduced: pretraining the probabilistic fluid simulator on particle data, inferring scene-specific ==visual posterior latent features== from a single 3D video, and adapting only the physical prior learner to these visual posteriors.
- Hidden physical properties are captured in a ==probabilistic latent space==, allowing for transfer to a physics engine for simulation in novel scenes without explicit parameter estimation.

## Results

- The model achieved superior performance in simulating novel scenes, exhibiting significantly lower average Euclidean distance (e.g., **34.54±0.55** on novel geometry) compared to baselines like CConv (**52.49**) and PAC-NeRF (**51.10**).
- Demonstrated strong performance in future prediction of observed dynamics and superior particle-level predictions when physical properties were inaccessible, with a long-term average Euclidean distance (¯d) of **38.37±0.860** compared to **90.21** for DPI-NET.
- Qualitative results showed more reasonable and coherent fluid dynamics simulations on novel scenes, avoiding the noisy or physically implausible predictions often generated by baseline methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Probabilistic latent variables can effectively capture unobservable, complex, and potentially stochastic physical properties of fluids directly from visual observations.
> - A staged pretraining-inference-transfer scheme allows for robust generalization of learned physics to novel scene geometries and boundary conditions.
> - Adapting only the physical prior learner, rather than the entire transition model, helps to leverage pretraining knowledge and reduces overfitting on limited visual data, improving transferability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "Latent Intuitive Physics" addresses a challenge within the field of deep learning-based physics simulation, specifically concerning the inference and transfer of complex physical properties from visual observations. Traditional deep learning methods for simulating physical systems, such as those for rigid bodies, fluids, and deformable objects, often assume the availability of accurate physical properties. These properties typically require substantial domain knowledge or specialized measurement devices.

A distinct line of research, known as "intuitive physics," draws inspiration from human perception, where individuals can infer physical properties and predict dynamics from visual cues without explicit physical parameter estimation. Within this domain, "inverse graphics" models aim to train learning-based physical simulators by solving the inverse problem of rendering 3D scenes from raw visual data.

Previous work in learning-based fluid simulation, such as DPI-Net, GNS, TIE, CConv, and DMCF, primarily focuses on learning fluid dynamics under known physical properties. These models are generally deterministic and do not account for stochastic components or inaccessible physical characteristics. Efforts to integrate intuitive physics with neural networks, particularly for fluids, include NeuroFluid and PAC-NeRF. NeuroFluid requires finetuning a deterministic transition model for each new set of physical properties. PAC-NeRF infers explicit physical properties (e.g., viscosity) using heuristic simulators, necessitating appropriate initial guesses and fluid type specification.

This paper distinguishes itself by proposing a probabilistic, latent-variable approach that aims to infer *hidden* (unobservable and possibly stochastic) physical properties directly from a single 3D video. Unlike prior methods, it does not require explicit physical parameter estimation or extensive finetuning for new properties. Instead, it captures these properties in a latent space, enabling their transfer to a probabilistic physics engine for simulation in novel scenes. This addresses the limitations of deterministic models and the dependency on explicit parameter knowledge or finetuning, positioning it as a significant contribution to generalizing physics simulation from visual data.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a transfer learning framework for physics simulation, termed "latent intuitive physics," that can infer the hidden physical properties of fluids from a single 3D video and subsequently simulate the observed fluid in novel scenes. This framework aims to operate without explicit knowledge of the true physical parameters of the fluid.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations and challenges in current physics simulation and intuitive physics research:

- **Limitation of explicit physical property knowledge:** Most existing deep learning methods for physics simulation necessitate accurate and known physical properties, which often require specialized devices or strong domain expertise to acquire. The authors pose the question of whether physical systems can be predicted with limited knowledge of their physical properties and, if so, whether this hidden physics can be transferred from readily accessible visual observations to learning-based simulators.
- **Inspiration from human perception:** The authors are inspired by the human ability to infer how a fluid will behave simply by watching a video, even without explicitly estimating its exact physical properties (e.g., viscosity, density). This suggests that crucial dynamic information can be implicitly learned from visual cues.
- **Addressing limitations of prior intuitive physics models:** While "inverse graphics" models have been proposed for intuitive physics, existing methods like NeuroFluid require finetuning a deterministic transition model for every new set of physical properties, which is inefficient. PAC-NeRF, while inferring properties from visuals, uses heuristic simulators and demands an initial guess for optimized parameters and explicit fluid type specification. These approaches struggle with the inherent stochasticity and unobservability of many complex physical phenomena.
- **Modeling stochastic and complex systems:** Real-world physical phenomena often involve stochastic components and complex interactions that deterministic models struggle to capture. A probabilistic approach is motivated by its ability to model such systems and account for uncertainty in the data, offering greater flexibility.
- **Generalization to novel scenarios:** A core motivation is to enable learned physics to be transferred to novel scenes, including those with unseen geometries, boundary conditions, and dynamics, without re-estimating or retraining for specific physical parameters. This aims to overcome the overfitting tendencies of some previous models to observed scenes.

By using latent features drawn from a learnable prior distribution conditioned on underlying particle states, the authors aim to capture these invisible and complex physical properties. This latent space serves as a bridge between the particle and visual spaces, facilitating the inference and transfer of hidden physics through probabilistic modeling.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The "latent intuitive physics" framework consists of four main network components and a three-stage training pipeline.

**Network Components:**
1. **Probabilistic Particle Transition Module (pθ):** This module is responsible for predicting the future states of particles. It takes the previous particle state (x_t-1) and a latent feature (z_t) as input to output the next particle state (ˆx_t). It employs a deterministic architecture, as the stochastic components are intended to be captured by the latent features z_t.
2. **Physical Prior Learner (pψ):** This module learns a prior distribution over the latent features. It takes historical particle states (x_1:t-1) and the previous latent feature (˜z_t-1) to predict the parameters (mean and variance) of a Gaussian distribution from which the current prior latent feature (˜z_t) is sampled. This module is designed to reason about latent features from historical particle states.
3. **Particle-based Posterior Estimator (qξ):** This module, used primarily during initial pretraining, estimates a posterior distribution over the latent features. It takes the current particle state (x_t) and previous states/features (x_1:t-1, z_t-1) as input to predict the parameters of a Gaussian distribution for the posterior latent feature (z_t). It helps guide the prior learner during supervised particle-space training.
4. **Neural Renderer (Rϕ):** This differentiable module bridges the gap between particle states and visual observations. It is based on PhysNeRF, adapting it to generate images from particle distributions and camera viewpoints. For a sampled point along a camera ray, it uses view-independent particle encodings (e.p, capturing fictitious particle center, soft density, and radial deformation) and view-dependent encodings (e.d, capturing normalized view direction to the fictitious particle center) along with the viewing direction (d) to estimate volume density (σ) and color (c). Unlike the original PhysNeRF, the sampled point's position is excluded from the MLP input to enhance particle encoding-rendering relationships.

**Training Pipeline:**
The training process unfolds in three stages, depicted in Figure 2:

**a) Stage A: Probabilistic Fluid Simulator Pretraining**
- **Objective:** To train the probabilistic fluid simulator (pθ, pψ, qξ) on a dataset of particle states with diverse physical properties, making it capable of predicting fluid dynamics without explicit knowledge of physical parameters.
- **Mechanism:** Continuous Convolutional Networks (CConv) are used as feature encoders to represent individual particles by aggregating features from their neighbors. A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) is then employed to process historical particle information and infer the parameters of the prior (pψ) and posterior (qξ) latent distributions. Latent features (z_t or ˜z_t) are sampled from these Gaussian distributions.
- **Particle Transition:** The particle transition module (Tθ), another CConv, uses the previous state (x_t-1) and the sampled latent feature (z_t from the posterior during training) to predict the next particle state (ˆx_t).
- **Loss Function:** The simulator is trained using a combined loss: a reconstruction loss (∥ˆp_i_t - p_i_t∥_γ^2) which measures the Euclidean distance between predicted and ground-truth particle positions, weighted by neighbor count; and a KL divergence term (DKL(qξ(z_t | x_1:t, z_t-1) ∥ pψ(˜z_t | x_1:t-1, ˜z_t-1))) to align the prior and posterior latent distributions.

**b) Stage B: Visual Posterior Inference**
- **Objective:** To infer scene-specific visual posterior latent features (ˆz) from a single sequence of multi-view visual observations of a specific fluid, without ground truth particle data.
- **Mechanism:** This stage uses the pretrained particle transition module (Tθ) in conjunction with the differentiable neural renderer (Rϕ).
 - **Initial States Estimation:** First, the initial particle states (ˆx_t=1) are estimated from the first frame's multi-view observations using a voxel-based neural rendering technique and an occupancy cache.
 - **Neural Renderer Pre-finetuning:** The neural renderer (Rϕ) is finetuned on the current visual observation using a photometric loss (∥ˆC(r, t) - C(r, t)∥) with the estimated initial states.
 - **Visual Posterior Optimization:** With Rϕ and Tθ frozen, a set of particle-dependent Gaussian distributions N(ˆμ_i, ˆσ_i) are initialized for each particle. Latent features (ˆz_i_t) are sampled from these distributions. The system then simulates subsequent states (ˆx_t = Tθ(ˆx_t-1, ˆz)) and renders images (ˆC(r, t)). The parameters (ˆμ_i, ˆσ_i) of these visual posterior distributions are optimized by backpropagating the photometric error over the entire observed sequence.

**c) Stage C: Physical Prior Adaptation**
- **Objective:** To transfer the hidden physical properties encoded in the scene-specific visual posteriors (ˆz) to the physical prior learner (pψ), enabling generalization to novel scenes.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of finetuning the entire transition model, only the prior learner (pψ) is adapted. The learned visual posteriors (N(ˆμ_i, ˆσ_i)) from Stage B serve as the target distribution.
- **Loss Function:** The prior learner (pψ) is finetuned by minimizing a combined loss: the photometric error (∥ˆC(r, t) - C(r, t)∥) and a KL divergence term (DKL(q(ˆz) ∥ pψ(˜z_t | ˆx_1:t-1, ˜z_t-1))) that encourages the prior learner's output distribution to match the converged visual posteriors. The particle transition module (Tθ) and neural renderer (Rϕ) remain fixed during this stage.
- **Transfer:** Once pψ is adapted, it is embedded into the probabilistic fluid simulator, allowing for novel simulations on unseen geometries, boundaries, and dynamics with identical inferred physical properties, effectively transferring knowledge from the visual domain back to the particle simulation domain.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research paper evaluates the proposed "latent intuitive physics" model across three main experimental settings: novel scene simulation, future prediction of observed dynamics, and supervised particle simulation.

**Evaluation of Visual Physical Inference:**
- **Settings:** A single 3D video of a Cuboid fluid (unseen during Stage A pretraining) falling in a default container was used for visual inference (first 50 time steps) and future prediction validation (last 10 time steps). Evaluations were conducted across three distinct sets of fluid physical properties (density ρ, viscosity ν) on novel scenes: (1) unseen fluid geometries (Stanford Bunny, Sphere, Dam Break) in the default container, and (2) unseen boundary conditions (a container with a central pillar) with Dam Break fluid. The primary metric was the average Euclidean distance (¯d) from ground truth particles to the closest predicted particles.
- **Novel Scene Simulation (Table 1):** The proposed model (OURS) demonstrated superior performance in simulating novel scenes with unseen fluid geometries and boundary conditions across all tested physical property sets. For instance, with ρ=2000, ν=0.065, OURS achieved an ¯d of 34.54±0.55 for geometry and 39.86±0.90 for boundary, significantly outperforming baselines like CConv (52.49, 64.29), NeuroFluid (65.01, 73.55), Sys-ID (156.59, 179.11), and PAC-NeRF (51.10, 59.61). The model successfully generalized to unseen boundary conditions despite being trained on scenes with a default fluid boundary. Baselines like Sys-ID underperformed, attributed to the difficulty of accurate explicit parameter inference from visual observations. PAC-NeRF, while using an inherently more stable MPM simulator, tended to overfit observed scenes, leading to degraded performance on novel scenes.
- **Future Prediction of Observed Scenes (Table 2):** OURS generally exhibited strong performance in predicting the future dynamics (10 time steps) of the *observed* Cuboid scene. For ρ=2000, ν=0.065, OURS achieved 32.41±0.17, outperforming CConv (107.27), Sys-ID (37.25), and PAC-NeRF (44.42), and closely matching NeuroFluid (85.45). In the case of ρ=500, ν=0.2, NeuroFluid (33.22) slightly outperformed OURS (41.15±0.71). This slight advantage for NeuroFluid was attributed to its joint optimization of the entire transition model and renderer on the observed scene, which can lead to overfitting locally but limits generalization as seen in Table 1. Our model's approach of adapting only the prior learner leveraged pretraining knowledge, reducing overfitting and improving generalization.
- **Qualitative Results (Figure 4, 10-15):** Qualitative visualizations supported the quantitative findings, showing that the proposed model produced more reasonable and coherent fluid dynamics simulations compared to baselines, which often generated noisy, sporadic, or physically implausible predictions on novel scenes.

**Evaluation of Probabilistic Fluid Simulator:**
- **Settings:** The pretrained probabilistic fluid simulator (Stage A) was evaluated on a particle dataset simulated with DFSPH, featuring fluids with various inaccessible physical parameters (viscosity, density) falling in a cubic box. The task involved short-term prediction (d_t+1, d_t+2) and long-term prediction (¯d over 190 time steps given initial 10 states).
- **Results (Table 3):** The proposed probabilistic fluid simulator demonstrated superior performance in both short-term and long-term particle predictions when physical properties were inaccessible. It achieved d_t+1 = 0.31±0.003, d_t+2 = 0.94±0.011, and ¯d = 38.37±0.860. These results were better than DPI-NET (0.95, 2.99, 90.21), CConv (0.34, 1.03, 44.79), DMCF (0.54, 1.23, 39.70), and TIE (0.52, 1.36, 41.82). This indicates the effectiveness of the probabilistic approach in capturing hidden physics within the latent space by leveraging spatiotemporal correlations in particle data.

**Generalization to Dynamics Discrepancies:**
- **Settings:** A more complex scenario involving two interacting heterogeneous fluids was used. The model was adapted to a visual scene with these fluids and then tested on novel scenes with unseen fluid geometries.
- **Results (Table 4, Figure 5):** OURS exhibited robust generalization. For example, on observed scenes, OURS achieved an ¯d of 36.03±0.80, and on unseen dynamics, 44.25±1.36. This demonstrated the model's capacity to handle visual scenes with dynamics significantly diverging from the pretraining dataset. A variant using a scene-specific, time-invariant global latent ("Global Latent") performed worse (60.63 observed, 90.51 unseen), indicating that optimizing time-varying, particle-dependent latent features is more effective than global latents.

**Ablation Study (Table 5):**
- **Impact of Stage B (Visual Posterior Inference):** Removing Stage B ("w/o Stage B") and directly finetuning the prior learner on visual observations resulted in higher prediction errors on both observed and unseen scenes compared to the full model. This confirmed that the visual posterior distribution learned in Stage B contributes to training stability by constraining the latent space.
- **Impact of Stage C (Physical Prior Adaptation):** Removing Stage C ("w/o Stage C") meant that the model only had scene-specific visual posteriors (ˆz) attached to particles, preventing simulation of novel scenes with varying particle numbers. The reported observed scene errors were comparable to the full model, but generalization to novel scenes was not possible. This highlights the necessity of transferring knowledge to the prior learner for novel scene simulation.
- **Initial State Estimation:** Models using estimated initial states showed comparable performance to those using ground truth initial states, indicating the robustness of the initial state estimation module.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The "Latent Intuitive Physics" framework presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the fields of deep learning, physics simulation, and artificial intelligence.

**1. Inference of Hidden Physical Properties from Visual Data:**
The core significance lies in the model's ability to infer latent, unobservable, and complex physical properties of fluids directly from a single 3D video. This moves beyond traditional approaches that require explicit knowledge of physical parameters (e.g., density, viscosity) or extensive finetuning. By representing these properties as probabilistic latent states, the framework can capture intricate fluid behaviors that might be difficult to parameterize or measure explicitly. This capability has implications for tasks where detailed physical parameters are unavailable or challenging to obtain.

**2. Probabilistic Modeling for Complex and Stochastic Systems:**
The introduction of a probabilistic particle-based fluid simulation network is a notable advancement. Many real-world physical phenomena inherently involve stochastic components and uncertainties. A probabilistic model can better capture this inherent variability and complexity compared to deterministic counterparts. This enables more flexible and robust simulations, particularly in scenarios where data is noisy or incomplete.

**3. Effective Transfer Learning for Generalization:**
The proposed pretraining-inference-transfer optimization scheme is crucial for the framework's practical utility. By learning scene-specific visual posteriors and then adapting a general physical prior learner to these posteriors, the model effectively transfers hidden physics from the observed visual world to a simulator that can operate in the particle space. This allows for simulation of novel scenes with unseen geometries, boundary conditions, and dynamics, without requiring retraining or finetuning for each new scenario. This enhanced generalization capability, demonstrated through superior performance on novel scenes, addresses a key limitation of prior methods that tend to overfit observed data.

**4. Reduced Training Burden:**
Unlike some prior inverse graphics methods that jointly optimize the entire transition model and renderer, this framework adapts only the physical prior learner. By leveraging knowledge from a comprehensive pretraining stage, the core particle transition model is less prone to overfitting on limited visual data and the training burden for adapting to new visual scenes is significantly reduced.

**5. Bridging the Gap between Visual Perception and Physical Simulation:**
The model effectively connects the visual observation space, the latent space, and the particle state space. This approach, inspired by human intuitive physics, enables a more holistic understanding and prediction of physical systems from raw perceptual input, potentially paving the way for AI agents with more sophisticated physical reasoning abilities.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Robotics and Control:** Improved physics simulators that can infer properties from observation could enhance robotic manipulation of fluids (e.g., pouring liquids, mixing) or deformable objects, especially in unstructured or unknown environments.
- **Scientific Discovery and Engineering:** The ability to simulate complex systems without explicit parameter tuning could aid in modeling natural phenomena, drug discovery (e.g., fluid dynamics in microfluidics), or material science, where identifying underlying physical laws is challenging.
- **Computer Graphics and Virtual Reality:** More realistic and dynamic fluid simulations for games, movies, and virtual environments could be generated with less manual tuning of physical parameters.
- **AI for Science:** The framework provides a method for implicit system identification from observational data, a crucial step toward building AI systems that can independently discover and model scientific principles.

The authors acknowledge challenges in real-world application, such as dealing with visual noise (reflection, refraction) and the need for high frame-rate, synchronized multi-view camera setups for dynamic scenes. However, the foundational learning scheme and the promising results on synthetic data indicate a significant step towards practical intuitive physics for complex fluid dynamics.
