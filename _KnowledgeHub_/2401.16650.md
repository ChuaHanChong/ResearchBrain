# Augmenting Replay in World Models for Continual Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Monash University researchers introduced WMAR (World Models with Augmented Replay), an algorithm that extends DreamerV3 with a memory-efficient augmented replay buffer for Continual Reinforcement Learning. WMAR substantially reduced catastrophic forgetting (average 0.071 vs 0.665 for baseline) in tasks without shared structure, while also demonstrating backward transfer in tasks with shared structure using approximately half the memory of a standard DreamerV3 setup.

## Problem

- Catastrophic forgetting in Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL) when agents sequentially learn new tasks, leading to degradation of performance on previously acquired knowledge.
- Traditional replay-based methods in CRL often demand large memory capacities to store past experiences, limiting their scalability and computational feasibility.
- A need for robust model-based RL approaches for CRL that effectively leverage learned world models to mitigate forgetting and facilitate knowledge transfer without requiring explicit task identifiers.

## Method

- **WMAR** extends the ==DreamerV3== algorithm by integrating a ==memory-efficient augmented replay buffer== designed for Continual Reinforcement Learning.
- The augmented replay buffer consists of two parallel buffers: a short-term ==FIFO buffer== for recent experiences and a long-term ==global distribution-matching== (LTDM) buffer that uses ==reservoir sampling== to maintain diversity of older experiences.
- Rollouts are ==spliced into smaller, fixed-size chunks== (512 states) before being added to the LTDM buffer, ensuring a more representative sample distribution and efficient memory usage.

## Results

- WMAR drastically reduced catastrophic forgetting in tasks without shared structure (Atari games), achieving an average forgetting score of **0.071**, a substantial improvement over the **0.665** observed in the DreamerV3 baseline.
- For tasks with shared structure (OpenAI Procgen CoinRun variants), WMAR demonstrated backward transfer, achieving an average forgetting score of **-0.077**, indicating performance improvements on earlier tasks after learning new, similar tasks.
- WMAR achieved these enhanced continual learning capabilities with an augmented replay buffer requiring approximately **half the memory capacity** compared to a typical single DreamerV3 buffer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a dual replay buffer strategy, comprising both short-term (FIFO) and long-term (distribution-matching) components, significantly enhances a world model's ability to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in continual learning scenarios.
> - Replaying experiences to update the world model itself, rather than solely the policy, serves as an effective mechanism for continuous adaptation and knowledge preservation across diverse tasks.
> - Memory-efficient replay, achieved through techniques like spliced rollouts and reservoir sampling, can provide robust continual learning performance with reduced memory footprint, addressing a key scalability challenge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) traditionally focuses on an agent mastering a single, static task. However, real-world applications often demand agents that can learn new tasks sequentially without forgetting previously acquired knowledge, a challenge known as Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL). A significant hurdle in CRL is "catastrophic forgetting," where learning new tasks degrades performance on old ones. Another objective in CRL is to leverage prior knowledge to accelerate learning of new tasks, referred to as forward transfer.

Common approaches to CRL, particularly in model-free RL, often utilize replay buffers to store past experiences, which helps mitigate catastrophic forgetting. However, these methods typically require large memory capacities, limiting their scalability and accessibility.

This work draws inspiration from Complementary Learning Systems (CLS) observed in mammalian brains, where the hippocampus replays recent observations to the neocortex, understood to form a "world model." While CLS suggests replay to a world model, traditional RL replay has predominantly been applied in model-free settings to improve policies directly.

Model-based RL (MBRL), which involves learning a "world model" to predict environment dynamics, offers potential benefits for CRL. World models provide an environment representation independent of the policy, inherently supporting off-policy learning and data augmentation. Recent advancements in MBRL, such as the Dreamer series (DreamerV1 to V3), have demonstrated strong performance in single-task benchmarks. Previous research has also explored the application of world models to continual learning, noting their potential in curbing catastrophic forgetting even with standard replay buffers. However, these approaches often demand large replay buffers, maintaining the memory scalability challenge.

This paper addresses the memory efficiency and forgetting aspects in MBRL for CRL. It extends the state-of-the-art DreamerV3 algorithm, which traditionally uses a simple First-In-First-Out (FIFO) buffer and was not designed or extensively tested for CRL, by incorporating a memory-efficient, distribution-matching replay buffer. The work positions itself at the intersection of model-based RL and continual learning, aiming to develop a scalable and effective approach to CRL that mitigates catastrophic forgetting and facilitates knowledge transfer without requiring explicit task identifiers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop and evaluate a model-based Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithm, named WMAR (World Models with Augmented Replay), that demonstrates robust continual learning capabilities. This involves learning new tasks sequentially without catastrophically forgetting previously learned information, while simultaneously enhancing performance on both past and future tasks.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and opportunities in the field of Continual RL (CRL):

- **Addressing Catastrophic Forgetting:** A central problem in CRL is that neural networks tend to forget old tasks when learning new ones. The authors aimed to mitigate this phenomenon, often referred to as catastrophic interference, particularly within a model-based RL framework.
- **Improving Scalability and Accessibility:** Traditional replay-based methods for CRL, especially in model-free settings, often require substantial memory capacity to store experiences from all past tasks. This limits their scalability and makes them computationally demanding. A key motivation was to introduce a memory-efficient replay mechanism that could reduce these requirements without compromising performance.
- **Leveraging World Models for CRL:** The biological inspiration of Complementary Learning Systems suggests replay to a "world model" (a neocortex-like structure). World models in model-based RL provide a representation of the environment dynamics independent of the agent's policy, naturally supporting off-policy learning and data augmentation. The authors sought to investigate and demonstrate the advantages of applying such a model-based approach, specifically extending the DreamerV3 architecture, to the continual learning paradigm.
- **Enhancing Knowledge Transfer:** Beyond preventing forgetting, a successful continual learning agent should also be able to effectively use previously learned knowledge to learn new tasks more efficiently (forward transfer) and potentially improve performance on old, similar tasks after learning new ones (backward transfer). The research aimed to evaluate WMAR against these transfer metrics.
- **Developing a Task-Agnostic Solution:** Many CRL methods rely on explicit task identifiers, which may not be available in real-world scenarios. The authors aimed for a solution that does not require such identifiers, allowing for more flexible adaptation to changing environments.
- **Evaluating Performance in Diverse Settings:** The research aimed to test the proposed WMAR algorithm in two distinct, challenging scenarios: tasks with shared structure (where learned knowledge can be more readily leveraged, e.g., a robot adapting to variations of a cleaning task) and tasks without shared structure (where environments and rewards can differ significantly, e.g., an agent learning various arcade games). This comprehensive evaluation was intended to assess the algorithm's general applicability and robustness.

By integrating a memory-efficient, distribution-matching replay buffer into a powerful world model architecture, the authors hypothesized that the world model's accuracy could be maintained across different tasks, thereby preserving performance on past environments while enabling adaptation to new ones.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduced WMAR (World Models with Augmented Replay), an algorithm that extends DreamerV3, a state-of-the-art model-based RL algorithm. WMAR integrates a memory-efficient replay buffer design to enhance its capabilities for Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL).

The WMAR architecture comprises three primary components: a world model, an actor-critic controller, and an augmented replay buffer.

#### 3.1. World Model

WMAR utilizes a Recurrent State-Space Model (RSSM), a core component of DreamerV3, to model the environment dynamics.
- **Functionality:** The RSSM maintains a deterministic hidden state ($h_t$) and models a stochastic representation of the next state ($z_{t+1}$) given current and previous observations ($x_{1:t}$) and actions ($a_{1:t}$).
- **Dynamics Modeling:** A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) network predicts the deterministic state ($h_{t+1} = \text{GRU}(h_t, z_t, a_t)$). The stochastic state ($z_t$) is either inferred by a variational autoencoder ($z_t \sim q_\theta(z_t | h_t, x_t)$) or predicted by the dynamics model ($z_t \sim p_\theta(\hat{z}_t | h_t)$) during "dreaming" (open-loop prediction).
- **Architecture:** A standard GRU with Tanh activation is used. The stochastic state ($z_t$) consists of 32 discrete stochastic units, each with 32 categorical classes, consistent with DreamerV3.
- **State Representation:** The world model state at timestep $t$ is the concatenation of $h_t$ and $z_t$, forming a Markovian representation.
- **Training:** The world model is trained to reconstruct input images and rewards. KL balancing is employed to model state transitions.

#### 3.2. Actor-Critic Controller

- **Components:** The actor and critic are Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) that map the world model's state to actions and value estimates, respectively.
- **Training:** These components are trained exclusively on trajectories generated stochastically by the world model, a process referred to as "dreaming." The actor and critic are trained on-policy using REINFORCE. This approach allows for efficient training as imagined trajectories are computationally inexpensive and do not require interaction with the actual environment.

#### 3.3. Augmented Replay Buffer

This is a key contribution of WMAR, designed for memory efficiency and distribution matching to mitigate catastrophic forgetting.
- **Structure:** The augmented replay buffer consists of two equally sized buffers operating in parallel:
 - **Short-term FIFO (First-In-First-Out) buffer ($D_1$):** This buffer has a capacity of $2^{18}$ samples (~262,000 observations) and stores the most recent rollout experiences. Its purpose is to allow the world model to train on incoming data, biasing training samples towards recent experiences to improve convergence on the current task. This is similar to the single buffer used in DreamerV3 but is half the size of the total augmented buffer.
 - **Long-term global distribution matching (LTDM) buffer ($D_2$):** Also with a capacity of $2^{18}$ samples, this buffer aims to match the global training data distribution over time. It contains a uniform random subset of "spliced rollouts" (explained below). Reservoir sampling is used to maintain diversity by assigning a random value to each rollout chunk, preserving those with the highest key values. This component is crucial for preventing forgetting by ensuring representation of older experiences.
- **Sampling:** For each training minibatch, data is uniformly sampled from either $D_1$ or $D_2$.
- **Memory Efficiency:** The total capacity of WMAR's augmented buffer (approximately 2 * $2^{18}$ samples) is significantly smaller than DreamerV3's typical 1 million observations (about half the size).
- **Spliced Rollouts:** To manage memory and ensure granularity, especially for the LTDM buffer, rollouts are spliced into smaller chunks of 512 states instead of storing entire episodes. This prevents the buffer from being dominated by a few long episodes and ensures a more representative sample distribution. Remaining rollouts with fewer than 512 states are concatenated before the next episode, with a reset flag. Episodes are truncated after a fixed number of steps to standardize the number of environment steps per training iteration.

#### 3.4. Task-Agnostic Exploration

For tasks without shared structure, where environments and rewards can vary greatly, WMAR incorporates strategies to facilitate exploration without relying on task identifiers:
- **Fixed-Entropy Regularization:** Used during world model training to maintain sufficient randomness in the policy, aiding exploration in new environments.
- **Predetermined Reward Scales:** During actor-critic training, reward scales are individually set based on single-task baselines. This mitigates issues arising from highly varying reward magnitudes across diverse tasks, which could otherwise cause the agent to focus only on tasks with higher returns.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:** Evaluated on OpenAI Procgen (CoinRun variants for tasks with shared structure, involving visual perturbations) and Atari (4 selected games for tasks without shared structure, presented in arbitrary order).
- **Baselines:** Compared against a custom implementation of DreamerV3 (referred to as DV3') with an equal memory allowance for its single replay buffer as WMAR's total augmented buffer. Single-task WMAR and a random agent were also used for normalization.
- **Metrics:** Performance was assessed using normalized episodic reward, Forgetting (to evaluate backward transfer and stability, lower values are better, negative indicates backward transfer), and Forward Transfer (to evaluate plasticity, higher values are better, positive indicates accelerated learning).
- **Computational Efficiency:** All experiments were designed to be computationally feasible, running within one day of wall time on a single Nvidia A40 40GB GPU, making the work reproducible.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments compared WMAR with DV3' (the authors' implementation of DreamerV3) under equal memory allowance for replay buffers, across two main scenarios: tasks without shared structure (Atari) and tasks with shared structure (OpenAI Procgen CoinRun). Performance was evaluated using normalized episodic rewards, forgetting, and forward transfer metrics.

#### 4.1. Tasks without Shared Structure – Atari

- **Normalised Performance:** WMAR demonstrated consistent continual learning capabilities across the Atari tasks (Ms Pacman, Boxing, Crazy Climber, Frostbite). In contrast, DV3' struggled significantly, performing well only when actively training on a specific task and exhibiting a rapid decline in performance on previous tasks.
- **Forgetting (Backward Transfer):**
 - DV3' displayed substantial catastrophic forgetting, losing nearly all knowledge after learning each new task. Its average forgetting was 0.665 (0.636, 0.810), indicating significant performance degradation on past tasks.
 - WMAR, on the other hand, maintained much of its performance on previously learned tasks, showing significantly improved stability. Its average forgetting was 0.071 (0.026, 0.082). This highlights the effectiveness of the distribution-matching buffer in preserving knowledge across greatly differing tasks. An exception was "Crazy Climber," which still showed notable forgetting in WMAR.
- **Forward Transfer (Plasticity):**
 - WMAR generally maintained its ability to learn new tasks effectively, reaching performance levels close to single-task baselines for most tasks. However, it exhibited negative average forward transfer (-0.392 (-0.432, -0.357)), suggesting a barrier to learning newer tasks from previous ones in this dissimilar task setting. "Crazy Climber" was the weakest point in terms of learning effectiveness for WMAR.
 - DV3' showed a slight decrease in learning effectiveness for each subsequent task. Interestingly, it did not suffer as much on "Crazy Climber" and achieved performance well above single-task on "Frostbite," leading to a higher average forward transfer of 1.049 (0.245, 1.246). This indicates DV3' could sometimes leverage past experience for new tasks more effectively, despite its severe forgetting.

#### 4.2. Tasks with Shared Structure – CoinRun (Procgen)

- **Normalised Performance:** Both WMAR and DV3' were capable of continuous learning and exhibited forward and backward transfer of skills across the CoinRun environments (CoinRun, CoinRun+NB, CoinRun+NB+RT, CoinRun+NB+RT+MA), which share similar mechanics but have visual differences.
- **Forgetting (Backward Transfer):**
 - WMAR exhibited desirable forgetting characteristics, with an average forgetting score of -0.077 (-0.103, -0.062). The negative value implies that WMAR managed to gain performance on earlier tasks after training on new, similar tasks, demonstrating backward transfer.
 - DV3' showed very little backward transfer, with an average forgetting score of -0.019 (-0.050, 0.010). Performance on the first task, 'CoinRun', dropped after training on subsequent tasks.
 - While both achieved similar peak performance, WMAR was noticeably more consistent with fewer performance fluctuations during training.
- **Forward Transfer (Plasticity):**
 - Both WMAR (0.316 (0.286, 0.357)) and DV3' (0.300 (0.239, 0.335)) demonstrated good forward transfer properties. The augmented buffer in WMAR did not confer a noticeable additional benefit in this regard compared to DV3'.
 - Significant forward transfer was observed on all tasks, indicating that both agents effectively leveraged learned knowledge from previous environments to accelerate learning in new, related tasks.

**Summary of Key Findings:**

- **Forgetting Mitigation:** WMAR substantially outperformed DV3' in preventing catastrophic forgetting, especially on tasks without shared structure, due to its augmented distribution-matching replay buffer.
- **Consistency:** WMAR demonstrated more consistent performance across tasks and during training, particularly in scenarios with shared structure.
- **General Capability:** Model-based agents, when equipped with appropriate replay mechanisms, are capable of continual learning across both types of task structures.
- **Memory Efficiency:** WMAR achieved these results with memory requirements comparable to, or even less than, a standard DreamerV3 setup.
- **Task Agnostic:** WMAR could handle tasks with highly varying reward magnitudes without explicit task identifiers, provided approximate reward scaling was applied.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents WMAR as an effective approach to Continual Reinforcement Learning (CRL) using a model-based framework, demonstrating significant advancements in addressing key challenges within the field.

**Significance:**

- **Robust Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation:** WMAR demonstrated superior capability in preventing catastrophic forgetting compared to a standard DreamerV3 implementation (DV3'), particularly in challenging scenarios involving tasks without shared structure (Atari). This highlights the crucial role of the augmented replay buffer, specifically the long-term global distribution matching component, in preserving knowledge from past tasks.
- **Validation of Model-Based RL for CRL:** The findings reinforce the argument that model-based RL, centered around a world model, provides a strong foundation for CRL. Unlike many model-free approaches where replay primarily improves policy learning, WMAR's strategy of replaying experiences to the world model itself proves effective for continuous adaptation and knowledge retention.
- **Memory-Efficient Design:** By utilizing a smaller, augmented replay buffer (approximately half the size of a typical DreamerV3 buffer for a single task) while achieving improved CRL performance, WMAR addresses a critical scalability limitation of many replay-based methods. This makes the algorithm more accessible and computationally less demanding.
- **Task-Agnostic Learning:** WMAR's ability to learn multiple tasks with varying reward magnitudes without relying on explicit task identifiers is a desirable property for real-world applications where such information may be unavailable.
- **Comprehensive Evaluation:** The assessment across both tasks with shared structure (Procgen CoinRun) and tasks without shared structure (Atari) provides a robust evaluation of WMAR's generalizability, considering both backward transfer (forgetting/stability) and forward transfer (plasticity) metrics.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Broader Adoption of Model-Based CRL:** The results could encourage wider adoption and further research into model-based approaches for CRL, especially in domains where sample efficiency and robust learning are paramount.
- **Development of General-Purpose AI Agents:** WMAR's properties, such as reduced forgetting and ability to handle diverse tasks without explicit identifiers, contribute to the long-term goal of developing more general-purpose AI agents capable of lifelong learning in dynamic environments.
- **Applications in Robotics and Autonomous Systems:** The concept of a robot assistant learning new, related tasks within a home (tasks with shared structure) or adapting to drastically different operational environments (tasks without shared structure) directly aligns with WMAR's demonstrated capabilities.
- **Foundation for Future Hybrid Approaches:** The authors suggest that WMAR's methods are largely orthogonal to other state-of-the-art techniques (e.g., EWC, P&C for parameter consolidation, or CLEAR for behavior cloning). This implies a potential for combining WMAR with these techniques to achieve even more robust CRL performance, leading to new hybrid algorithms.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**

- **Finite Buffer Capacity:** Like all buffer-based methods, WMAR faces the fundamental limitation of finite capacity. With an increasing number of tasks and no revisits to older environments, samples will inevitably be lost, leading to eventual forgetting.
- **Scope of Experiments:** The experiments were limited in scope, including the length of task sequences, number of environment steps, diversity of environments, and comparison to a wider range of reference models.
- **Task Order Dependence:** The study used a fixed task order, acknowledging that task ordering can significantly impact CRL results. Randomizing task order over more seeds is suggested for future work.
- **Generalization to Abstract Changes:** While the world model's convolutional feature extractor benefited from visual commonality, it might struggle with more abstract changes between tasks (e.g., inverting colors, permuting controls), which could lead to decreased performance.
- **Reward Scaling:** WMAR showed robustness to somewhat different reward scales but required approximate reward scaling when magnitudes differed significantly (e.g., by a factor of 100). Automatic scaling of advantages was found to hurt learning on individual tasks.
- **Integration with Other Techniques:** Future work could explore combining WMAR with existing methods like behavior cloning (e.g., from CLEAR) to address potential shifts in the latent distribution during world model training.

Overall, this work provides a strong justification for continued research into model-based RL as a viable and effective paradigm for continual learning, offering practical advantages in memory efficiency and forgetting mitigation.
