# EarthSpatialBench: Benchmarking Spatial Reasoning Capabilities of Multimodal LLMs on Earth Imagery

> [!summary] Summary
> EarthSpatialBench introduces a comprehensive benchmark to evaluate the spatial reasoning capabilities of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) on Earth imagery. It provides 21,000 images and over 325,000 question–answer pairs, covering quantitative distance and direction, systematic topological relations, and diverse geometric entities like bounding boxes, polylines, and polygons, revealing MLLMs' limitations in precise geospatial reasoning.

## Problem

- Multimodal large language models (MLLMs), primarily trained on natural images, struggle with the unique characteristics of Earth imagery, such as noise, dense object populations, and diverse geometric representations.
- Existing MLLM benchmarks for Earth imagery lack support for quantitative direction (azimuth angle) and distance metrics, systematic topological relations, and complex object geometries beyond bounding boxes.
- Current spatial reasoning benchmarks generally do not extensively cover continuous metric reasoning, angular direction estimation, or formal topological relations with polygonal and polyline structures intrinsic to geospatial analysis.

## Method

- Developed ==EarthSpatialBench==, a comprehensive benchmark comprising ==21,000== Earth images and over ==325,000== question-answer pairs to evaluate MLLMs' spatial reasoning.
- The benchmark systematically assesses MLLMs across four dimensions: ==spatial relations== (distance, direction, topology), ==geometry types== (bounding boxes, polylines, polygons), object representations (text, visual overlays, coordinates), and question formats.
- Utilized a multi-stage quality assurance pipeline, combining automated checks with manual review, to ensure the validity and consistency of generated questions and ground-truth labels derived from exact geometry computations.

## Results

- Proprietary models (e.g., GPT-5, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) achieved higher classification accuracy, while open-source models (e.g., Qwen3-VL variants) demonstrated stronger performance in localization tasks.
- Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting generally improved classification and regression performance for most models, particularly in topological reasoning, but had limited or sometimes negative impact on localization tasks.
- Heterogeneous geometry pairs (e.g., bounding box-polyline, polygon-polyline) consistently yielded higher errors for distance estimation compared to homogeneous pairs, with polygon-polyline interactions proving most challenging, indicating difficulties in reasoning over irregular region-curve interactions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current MLLMs exhibit substantial performance gaps across numerical estimation, spatial grounding, and geometric reasoning tasks on Earth imagery, particularly for precise quantitative and topological queries.
> - Accurate object localization is a prerequisite for reliable spatial reasoning, yet models often show a disconnect between high-level spatial reasoning capabilities (e.g., classification accuracy) and precise low-level visual grounding.
> - Performance on spatial reasoning tasks varies significantly with different geometric representations and object reference modalities; heterogeneous geometry pairs and mixed reference types often lead to higher errors, highlighting the challenge of integrating diverse spatial information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and their application to complex tasks requiring spatial understanding has garnered increasing attention in computer vision. Benchmarking spatial reasoning capabilities in MLLMs is considered crucial for advanced AI systems such as embodied AI, which necessitate precise interaction with the physical world.

Existing research has produced numerous benchmarking datasets for spatial reasoning in MLLMs, primarily focusing on natural images. These benchmarks, including InternSpatial, SpatialVLM, SpatialRGPT-Bench, Spatial-MM, SpatialBench, Open3D-VQA, and CV-Bench, often evaluate object-centric relations like proximity, counting, or size comparison. However, these datasets generally do not extensively cover continuous metric reasoning, angular direction estimation, or formal topological relations, and they typically lack the polygonal and polyline geometric structures intrinsic to geospatial analysis.

Benchmarking spatial reasoning on Earth imagery, encompassing satellite, aerial, and drone observations, has progressed at a slower pace. Prior MLLM benchmarking datasets for Earth imagery, such as EarthVQA, GeoChatSet, VRSBench, SkyEyeGPT, XLRS-Bench, GeoBench-VLM, and DisasterM3, primarily concentrate on 2D spatial grounding, image captioning, and coarse spatial relations (e.g., simple directional or proximity cues). A common limitation across these existing Earth imagery benchmarks is the absence of support for:
1. Quantitative direction (azimuth angle) and distance metrics.
2. Systematic topological relations beyond basic intersections.
3. Complex object geometries, often limited to bounding boxes, omitting polylines and polygons.

Furthermore, Earth imagery presents unique challenges compared to natural images, including higher noise levels, denser object populations in a bird's-eye view (e.g., tiny visible roofs), and the necessity to refer to objects using both textual descriptions and precise vector geometric coordinates.

EarthSpatialBench is designed to address these identified gaps by introducing a comprehensive benchmark specifically tailored for spatial reasoning in MLLMs on Earth imagery. It extends the scope beyond current benchmarks by incorporating quantitative spatial reasoning, systematic topological relations, and diverse geometric object types and reference modalities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for developing EarthSpatialBench is to enhance the spatial reasoning capabilities of MLLMs on Earth imagery, which holds potential for addressing significant societal challenges. These challenges include, but are not limited to, natural disaster response, urban planning, precision agriculture, and ecological monitoring. For instance, in flood disasters, MLLMs could aid first responders by locating affected communities, estimating economic losses, and planning rescue operations through tasks like counting damaged houses within specific areas or identifying their proximity to major roads.

The current limitations of MLLMs, which are largely pretrained on natural images, in handling the unique characteristics of Earth imagery—such as noise, object density, and diverse geometric representations—underscore the need for a specialized benchmark. Existing benchmarks for Earth imagery primarily focus on qualitative spatial grounding and coarse relations, failing to evaluate the precise quantitative and topological reasoning capabilities essential for real-world geospatial applications.

To fill this gap, the authors established three core research questions (RQs) that guided the design of EarthSpatialBench:

- **RQ1: Spatial Relation Understanding:** To assess how effectively MLLMs can perform spatial reasoning based on various types of spatial relations in Earth imagery, specifically focusing on distance, direction, and topological relations.
- **RQ2: Reasoning with Different Geometric Representation:** To evaluate the proficiency of MLLMs in processing different forms of object reference expressions, including textual descriptions (semantic attributes, spatial context), visual overlays, and explicit geometric coordinates (bounding boxes, polylines, and polygons). This question also probes MLLM performance across these varied geometric types.
- **RQ3: Task Format and Reasoning Difficulty:** To investigate MLLM performance across diverse question formats, such as qualitative questions (discrete choices, Yes/No), quantitative questions (azimuth angles, Euclidean distance), single-object vs. composite group queries (e.g., aggregating objects based on spatial criteria), and counting vs. localization tasks.

The primary objective of this work is to introduce EarthSpatialBench as a comprehensive and systematic benchmark to evaluate MLLMs against these detailed spatial reasoning requirements, thereby providing insights into their strengths and limitations in geospatial contexts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EarthSpatialBench is structured around orthogonal evaluation dimensions: spatial relations, geometry types, object representations, and question formats. This design aims to comprehensively capture the breadth of spatial reasoning required for Earth imagery.

**Evaluation Dimensions:**

- **Spatial Relations:**
 - **Distance:** Tasks involve estimating distances between objects, determining if objects meet proximity thresholds, and identifying or counting objects within a defined buffer zone.
 - **Direction:** Queries require estimating angular measurements (azimuth angle), categorizing objects into coarse directional bins (e.g., 8 compass directions), and counting or locating objects within specific directional sectors.
 - **Topological Relations:** Focuses on fundamental relations such as "within" (containment) and "intersect," assessing whether one geometric entity lies entirely inside another or whether two entities share common points.
- **Geometry Types:**
 - **Bounding Boxes (BBoxes):** Utilized for small, visually distinct objects like buildings and facilities.
 - **Polygons:** Employed for representing areal regions such as parks, agricultural fields, and lakes.
 - **Polylines:** Captures linear features like roads, railways, and rivers.
 The benchmark systematically combines these types (e.g., polyline–polygon) to reflect diverse real-world spatial configurations.
- **Object Representations:**
 - **Textual Descriptions:** Objects are referenced via semantic attributes, functional roles, or spatial context (e.g., "the northernmost building," "the road at [[x,y], [x',y']]").
 - **Visual Overlays:** Target objects are highlighted directly on the image using visual markers or masks.
 - **Geometric Coordinates:** Objects are specified using precise 2D bounding boxes, polylines, or polygon coordinates.
- **Question Formats:**
 - **Choice-based Questions:** Require selecting from a discrete set of options (e.g., Yes/No, cardinal directions).
 - **Quantitative Questions:** Involve numerical reasoning, such as counting objects or estimating specific distances or angles.
 - **Localization Questions:** Task the model with identifying or outlining specific objects or regions that meet a given spatial constraint.

**Dataset Construction:**

- **Data Source:** The benchmark utilizes images and geometric object annotations from SatlasPretrain, a large-scale public remote sensing dataset. Images are optical RGB, captured at approximately 1m ground resolution, and standardized to 512 × 512 pixels.
- **Image Selection:** Only images containing geometric objects with discernible footprints were retained. To ensure meaningful spatial relationships, each image was required to feature at least two distinct semantic categories beyond generic buildings (e.g., river, road, park).
- **Geometry Processing and Spatial Relation Computation:**
 - Polygons from SatlasPretrain were preserved for medium- and large-scale regions. For smaller objects, polygons were converted into axis-aligned bounding boxes for consistency.
 - Spatial relationships between all geometry instances were computed using standard Geographic Information System (GIS) procedures:
 - Distance: Euclidean metrics were used, calculating the nearest distance between points of two objects. Buffer queries employed a consistent metric scale derived from georeferenced pixel resolution.
 - Direction: Computed using azimuth angles between object centroids, then discretized into 8-direction bins.
 - Topology: Determined using the DE-9IM model, a standard formalism for computational geometry.
- **Question Generation:** The computed spatial relations were combined with curated question templates for each reasoning category. Templates were designed to cover choice-based, quantitative, and localization tasks, including both positive and negative exemplars to mitigate answer biases. All ground-truth labels are derived from exact geometry computations.

**Quality Control:**

A multi-stage quality assurance pipeline was implemented, combining automated checks with targeted manual review.
- **Automated Checks:** Verified that all geometries were within image bounds and possessed non-zero area or length. Objects deemed too small, noisy, or visually ambiguous were removed. Geometry pairs with unstable or ambiguous spatial relations (e.g., nearly touching boundaries leading to inconsistent topological results) were excluded.
- **Manual Review:** A selected subset of complex samples underwent manual inspection, including images with very large geometric regions, high object density, objects intersecting image boundaries, or polygons with intricate shapes. Observations from this review informed refinements to question templates, removal of problematic examples, and adjustment of geometric thresholds.
- **Coverage Validation:** Verified that each spatial relation type included diverse examples across various geometry types and object categories.

**Dataset Statistics:**

EarthSpatialBench comprises 21,000 images and over 325,000 question–answer pairs. Each image contains, on average, 19.3 annotated geometric entities, consisting of 10.7 bounding-box objects, 6.7 polylines, and 1.9 polygonal regions. The dataset exhibits a wide distribution of geometry types, object counts per image, and question types (38.6% distance, 42.7% direction, 18.7% topology, with breakdowns by format).

**Experimental Setup:**

- **Evaluated MLLMs:** A range of open-source models (Qwen3-VL-30B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-30B-Thinking, InternVL3.5-38B) and proprietary models (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) were evaluated, including their Chain-of-Thought (CoT) variants.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Task-specific metrics were used:
 - Quantitative questions (counting, distance/angle estimation): Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).
 - Choice-based questions (Yes/No, directional classification): Overall accuracy and macro-averaged F1 score.
 - Localization questions (identifying 2D bounding boxes): Intersection-over-Union (IoU) and macro F1 score, with an IoU threshold of 0.1.
- **Computational Resources:** Open-source models were evaluated on a compute node with 8 NVIDIA B200 GPUs. Proprietary models were accessed via their respective APIs using default inference configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Experiments on EarthSpatialBench revealed performance variations across models and reasoning categories, highlighting limitations in current MLLM capabilities for Earth imagery spatial reasoning.

**Overall Performance:**
- **Performance Gaps:** Substantial gaps were observed across numerical estimation, spatial grounding, and geometric reasoning tasks.
- **Quantitative Tasks:** GPT-5 achieved the lowest MAE for distance estimation. Qwen3-VL-Thinking variants demonstrated competitive performance in angle regression tasks.
- **Localization Tasks:** Qwen3-VL variants generally exhibited stronger performance in localization. Proprietary models, in contrast, showed comparatively weaker performance on distance- and direction-related localization tasks.
- **Classification Tasks:** Large proprietary models attained the highest performance in classification tasks, suggesting capabilities in symbolic and relational reasoning.
- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting:** CoT prompting generally improved classification and regression performance for most models, particularly in topological reasoning. However, its impact on localization tasks was limited or sometimes negative, indicating that explicit reasoning steps do not consistently translate to enhanced spatial grounding.
- **Decoupling of Reasoning and Grounding:** Models such as Claude and Gemini achieved competitive classification accuracy but performed poorly on localization metrics. This suggests a disconnect between high-level spatial reasoning and precise low-level visual grounding.

**Grounding as a Prerequisite for Spatial Reasoning:**
- Accurate object localization was identified as a prerequisite for reliable spatial reasoning, given that geometric relations are computed using precise spatial inputs.
- Performance on grounding tasks varied significantly across models. Qwen3-VL variants showed strong and consistent performance. GPT-5 and Claude exhibited degradation in performance on exhaustive grounding tasks (e.g., identifying all buildings). Gemini 2.5 Pro performed well on unique object grounding but degraded on context-dependent and extreme cases.
- Extreme position object identification proved particularly challenging, indicating a bottleneck in grounding based on rare attributes. These findings suggest that robust spatial reasoning is tightly linked to effective low-level grounding, and combining both remains an open challenge.

**Comparisons on Different Geometric Representations:**
- **Visual Overlays vs. Coordinates:** Visual overlays generally improved localization performance. Their effect on counting and regression tasks was more varied.
 - Qwen3-VL models consistently leveraged visual overlays to improve localization and counting, indicating strong vision-language alignment.
 - GPT-5 performed competitively with coordinate-based descriptions but did not effectively utilize visual overlays, suggesting a preference for text-centric reasoning. This highlights divergent approaches in how MLLMs process spatial information, indicating a need for better integration of visual grounding and geometric reasoning.
- **Angle Estimation with Mixed References:** When explicit coordinates were provided for both objects (coordinate–coordinate), models achieved substantially lower MAE for angle estimation, reflecting reliable numerical reasoning. Performance significantly degraded when objects were specified using unique textual descriptions (textual–textual). The mixed setting (coordinate–textual) sometimes resulted in higher errors than textual–textual, suggesting that combining symbolic coordinates with ambiguous language descriptions can introduce additional grounding inconsistencies. This emphasizes the critical dependence of accurate spatial reasoning on reliable object grounding.

**Comparisons on Different Geometry Types:**
- **Distance Estimation MAE:** Significant performance variations were observed based purely on geometric representation.
 - Heterogeneous geometry pairs (e.g., BBox–Polyline and Polygon–Polyline) consistently yielded higher errors compared to homogeneous pairs.
 - Polyline–Polyline queries resulted in lower errors than BBox–BBox queries for most models, suggesting that linear structures might provide clearer geometric cues than coarse bounding boxes.
 - Polygon–Polyline settings proved among the most challenging, indicating difficulties in reasoning over irregular region-curve interactions.
- **Model-Specific Behavior:** Qwen3-VL variants generally demonstrated more stable performance across different geometry types, whereas GPT-5 and Claude showed pronounced degradation when handling polyline-based queries. These results highlight geometric representation as a critical factor influencing performance and suggest the need for incorporating explicit geometric reasoning mechanisms into future models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

EarthSpatialBench represents a significant contribution to the field of multimodal machine learning and geospatial intelligence by providing a comprehensive and systematic benchmark for evaluating the spatial reasoning capabilities of MLLMs on Earth imagery.

**Significance:**
- **Fills Critical Gaps:** The benchmark addresses previously identified limitations in existing MLLM evaluation datasets for Earth imagery, specifically by integrating quantitative distance and direction reasoning, systematic topological relations, and support for diverse geometric entities (bounding boxes, polylines, polygons) and object reference modalities.
- **Systematic Assessment:** By formalizing spatial relations into distance, direction, and topology, and by incorporating varied geometric entities and question formats, EarthSpatialBench enables a structured and thorough assessment of spatial intelligence in realistic geospatial contexts.
- **Reveals MLLM Limitations:** The experimental results identify specific weaknesses and performance disparities among state-of-the-art MLLMs across different reasoning categories, object representations, and geometry types. This highlights a measurable gap between current vision-language capabilities and the requirements of practical geospatial analysis applications. The findings underscore that advanced MLLMs often struggle with precise geometric reasoning, numerical estimation, and robust low-level spatial grounding in complex Earth imagery.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancing Methodological Research:** The benchmark is positioned to advance methodological research in multimodal machine learning by providing a rigorous tool for developing and evaluating MLLMs with improved spatial intelligence.
- **Supporting Socially Beneficial Applications:** By facilitating the development of MLLMs that can accurately interpret and reason about Earth imagery, this work has the potential to support a range of socially beneficial applications. These include enhancing disaster response efforts (e.g., rapid damage assessment, rescue planning), informing urban planning decisions, improving environmental monitoring (e.g., land use change detection), and optimizing infrastructure assessment.
- **Future Research Directions:** The authors identify extending the benchmark to include multi-temporal and multi-modal inputs (e.g., Digital Elevation Models (DEM), Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), and LiDAR data) as promising avenues for future research, further enhancing the complexity and real-world applicability of spatial reasoning tasks.

The authors anticipate that this work will primarily contribute to methodological advancements and do not foresee significant negative societal impacts when the benchmark is used responsibly within research and development contexts.
