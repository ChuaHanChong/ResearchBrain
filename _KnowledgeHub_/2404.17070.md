# Deep Reinforcement Learning for Bipedal Locomotion: A Brief Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at University College London systematically categorize and analyze Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) frameworks for robotic bipedal locomotion, identifying current limitations in generalization, sim-to-real transfer, and safety. The survey proposes a strategic roadmap for future research, including conceptual models like Bipedal Foundation Models and Multi-Layer Adaptive Models, to advance towards unified, versatile, and robust control systems.

## Problem

- The research landscape for DRL-based bipedal locomotion is fragmented, with inconsistencies in training pipelines, reward functions, observation spaces, and evaluation methods.
- Existing DRL approaches often lack sufficient generalization and robustness across diverse robot morphologies, terrains, and locomotion tasks, limiting real-world applicability.
- Traditional model-based control methods for bipedal robots struggle with adaptability in complex, dynamic, and uncertain environments.

## Method

- The authors conducted a systematic literature review (2018-2024) of DRL frameworks for bipedal robots, using specific criteria for paper selection across major academic databases and conferences.
- DRL frameworks were systematically categorized into two primary control schemes: End-to-End (reference-based and reference-free learning) and Hierarchical (deep planning hybrid, feedback DRL control hybrid, and learned hierarchy schemes).
- Each categorized framework was evaluated for its composition, strengths, limitations, and capabilities, forming the basis for identifying challenges and proposing future research directions.

## Results

- The survey provides a comprehensive categorization of DRL frameworks for bipedal locomotion, highlighting their specific advantages and disadvantages in areas like robustness, versatility, and computational efficiency.
- Key limitations were identified across all frameworks, centered on generalization versus precision, sim-to-real transfer, and safety-critical locomotion, underscoring fundamental challenges for real-world deployment.
- A strategic roadmap for future research was proposed, outlining directions such as multi-skill learning, efficient learning, bridging the sim-to-real gap, perception-conditioned locomotion, and constrained learning, alongside conceptual models (Bipedal Foundation Models and Multi-Layer Adaptive Models) for a unified DRL framework.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - End-to-end DRL frameworks offer high potential for novel gait exploration (reference-free) or accelerated learning via prior knowledge (reference-based), but often struggle with generalization, computational cost, or reliance on high-quality demonstrations.
> - Hierarchical DRL frameworks decompose control into layers, enhancing command tracking, generalizability across platforms, or stability, but introduce system complexity, communication challenges, and can suffer from inefficient sim-to-real transfer.
> - Overarching limitations include a persistent tension between achieving broad generalization and high precision, significant challenges in bridging the sim-to-real gap, and the lack of built-in safety guarantees for bipedal robots operating in real-world, human-centric environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bipedal robots are recognized for their potential in real-world applications, particularly in environments designed for humans and on challenging terrains where wheeled or tracked robots may be less effective. Historically, bipedal locomotion control has relied on traditional model-based methods, such as the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM), Model Predictive Control (MPC), and Trajectory Optimization (TO), since the 1980s. These methods offer advantages in convergence speed and predictability but often encounter limitations when faced with dynamically complex and uncertain environments, where adaptability is crucial.

In recent years, Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as a complementary and increasingly prominent paradigm. DRL-based approaches enable robots to autonomously discover control strategies through environmental interaction, enhancing adaptability and robustness. Hybrid methods, which integrate both model-based and learning-based techniques, have also gained traction, aiming to combine the strengths of both paradigms.

Despite the advancements brought by DRL, the research landscape for bipedal locomotion remains fragmented. The paper identifies inconsistencies in training pipelines, reward formulations, observation spaces, and evaluation setups, which collectively hinder systematic benchmarking and the development of generalizable locomotion capabilities. Many existing DRL methods are tailored to specific robot morphologies or tasks, limiting their transferability across different embodiments and environments.

While previous reviews have covered general reinforcement learning in robotics and model-based methods for bipedal robots, a focused survey specifically addressing DRL-based frameworks for bipedal locomotion was absent. This paper positions itself to fill this gap by systematically categorizing, comparing, and analyzing current DRL frameworks. The work aims to clarify the state of research regarding generalization and robustness across diverse conditions and to identify how existing DRL approaches can be organized and extended towards a unified framework capable of providing broad capabilities to bipedal robots. The authors conceptualize a unified framework not as a final product, but as a conceptual scaffold for consolidating methodologies, standardizing protocols, and driving progress toward adaptive robot-learning systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this survey stems from the growing potential of bipedal robots in various real-world applications and the significant advancements in their control through Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). Bipedal robots, especially humanoids, offer distinct advantages for operating in human-centric environments and traversing complex, uneven terrains, making them valuable in manufacturing, healthcare, and search-and-rescue operations.

However, the authors observe that despite notable progress, DRL-based research in bipedal locomotion is currently fragmented. This fragmentation manifests as inconsistencies in training methodologies, reward function designs, observation spaces, and evaluation criteria, which collectively impede systematic comparison, benchmarking, and the development of control policies that can generalize across different robot morphologies, terrains, and tasks. The existing approaches are often specialized, limiting their broad applicability and transferability.

Against this backdrop, the survey is driven by two central research questions:
1. **To what extent has current research achieved generalization and robustness across diverse morphologies, terrains, and locomotion tasks?** This question probes the current capabilities of DRL systems in handling varied operational conditions and robot designs.
2. **If full generalization has not yet been realized, how can existing DRL approaches be organized and extended towards a unified framework that enables such capability in bipedal robots?** This question guides the identification of pathways for consolidating disparate methods into a more cohesive and effective structure.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives and contributions of this survey are explicitly outlined:
- To provide a comprehensive summary and systematic cataloguing of existing DRL-based frameworks developed for bipedal locomotion. This involves organizing the diverse approaches into distinct categories based on their architectural designs.
- To offer a detailed comparison of each identified control scheme. This comparison highlights their respective strengths, intrinsic limitations, and distinguishing characteristics, thereby providing a clear understanding of their applicability and performance under different conditions.
- To identify the current challenges and limitations inherent in existing DRL pipelines for bipedal locomotion. This analysis serves as a foundation for understanding the gaps that need to be addressed.
- To propose insightful future research directions. These directions are aimed at overcoming identified limitations and fostering the development of a more integrated and efficient unified framework for bipedal locomotion, ultimately enhancing the applicability of bipedal robots in real-world environments.

In essence, the survey aims to consolidate the current state of DRL for bipedal locomotion, critically evaluate its shortcomings, and chart a strategic course toward developing more versatile, robust, and generalizable control systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors conducted a systematic literature review to categorize, compare, and analyze existing Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) frameworks for robotic bipedal locomotion. The methodology involved specific criteria for paper selection and a structured classification scheme for the identified frameworks.

**Selection Criteria:**
The literature search adhered to four main criteria:
1. Inclusion of studies specifically investigating DRL frameworks for bipedal robots.
2. Consideration of research involving both simulated and physical bipedal robots.
3. Prioritization of approaches that aim to improve policy transfer from simulation to real-world environments.
4. Focus on recent publications, specifically from the last five years (2018–April 2024).

**Data Sources and Search Strategy:**
The search for relevant literature was conducted across several reputable academic databases and conference proceedings, including Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, Web of Science, arXiv, and major robotics venues such as CoRL, RSS, ICRA, IROS, and Humanoids. Key terms used in the search included "deep reinforcement learning" or "reinforcement learning," combined with "bipedal locomotion," "bipedal walking," "biped robot," "humanoid robot," or "legged robot." The most relevant and impactful works identified through this search were then manually selected for detailed review.

**Categorization Scheme:**
The surveyed DRL frameworks were systematically organized into two primary categories based on their control schemes:
1. **End-to-End Frameworks**: These frameworks utilize a single neural network policy that directly maps sensory inputs (e.g., proprioceptive feedback, vision, LiDAR data, user commands) to joint-level control outputs. This approach minimizes the need for manual problem decomposition.
 - **Reference-based learning**: These methods leverage prior knowledge, such as pre-planned trajectories generated through trajectory optimization (TO) or motion capture data.
 - *Residual learning*: Policies modify motor commands by applying action offsets to predefined desired joint positions, thereby compensating for errors and enabling dynamic locomotion.
 - *Guided learning*: Policies are trained to directly output desired joint-level commands, aiming to imitate predefined reference trajectories.
 - **Reference-free learning**: These approaches train policies from scratch using carefully designed reward functions, without relying on any predefined motion trajectories, to autonomously discover control strategies.

2. **Hierarchical Frameworks**: These schemes decompose the overall locomotion control problem into multiple layers or stages of decision-making, each with specific objectives. Typically, they involve a High-Level (HL) planner for strategic goals (e.g., navigation, path planning) and a Low-Level (LL) controller for fundamental locomotion tasks (e.g., joint movements, balance). Communication between layers involves abstract goals from HL to LL and sensory data feedback from the robot to HL.
 - **Deep planning hybrid scheme**: This approach integrates a learned HL planner with a traditional model-based LL controller. The HL layer focuses on strategic goals in task space, while the LL layer handles basic locomotion skills using interpretable or feedback controllers.
 - **Feedback DRL control hybrid scheme**: In this configuration, DRL policies are employed as LL controllers, replacing traditional model-based feedback mechanisms, and operate in conjunction with non-learned, often model-based, HL planners that manage higher-level tasks like path planning or stability maintenance.
 - **Learned hierarchy scheme**: Both the HL planner and the LL controller are learned policies. The LL policy typically focuses on balance and basic locomotion, while the HL policy is trained for navigation and long-term task objectives.

**Analysis and Discussion:**
For each categorized framework, the authors provided a detailed evaluation covering its composition, identified strengths, specific limitations, and overall capabilities. This analysis forms the basis for identifying overarching challenges in DRL-based bipedal locomotion and for proposing future research directions aimed at achieving a more integrated and efficient unified framework suitable for real-world deployment. The paper also includes appendices on DRL algorithms and training simulation environments to provide foundational context for readers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey identifies Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) as a pivotal technology significantly advancing robotic bipedal locomotion, enabling adaptability and robustness beyond traditional model-based approaches. However, it concurrently highlights a fragmented research landscape where inconsistencies in training, reward formulations, and evaluation hinder the development of generalizable capabilities.

The main findings are presented through a systematic categorization and detailed analysis of DRL frameworks:

**A. End-to-End Frameworks:**
These frameworks map sensory inputs directly to joint-level control outputs via a single neural network.
- **Reference-based Learning:**
 - **Residual Learning:** This approach augments existing control policies by adding learned action offsets to predefined reference joint positions.
 - *Capabilities:* Demonstrated forward walking and unidirectional movements on bipedal robots like Cassie. It can achieve faster running speeds than some model-based methods.
 - *Advantages:* Fast convergence, leverages prior knowledge.
 - *Limitations:* Requires high-quality predefined references, restricts motions to those within the reference's scope, and shows limited robustness in complex or unforeseen terrains. Generalization to diverse motions (e.g., omnidirectional walking) is challenging.
 - **Guided Learning:** Policies directly output joint-level commands, aiming to imitate predefined trajectories.
 - *Capabilities:* Enabled versatile walking, turning, squatting, and even complex jumping maneuvers by tracking Hybrid Zero Dynamics (HZD) gait libraries or other motion references. Has shown sim-to-real transferability and adaptability to multiple targets.
 - *Advantages:* Accelerates the learning process by using expert knowledge, can achieve versatile and robust locomotion skills, and demonstrates robustness across different terrains.
 - *Limitations:* Constrained to predefined motions, limiting exploration of novel gaits. Exhibits reduced adaptability to novel environments or perturbations. Reliant on the availability of high-quality, task-relevant demonstrations, which are scarce and often require adaptation due to embodiment mismatch or computational expense of generation.
- **Reference-free Learning:** Policies learn locomotion skills from scratch without prior knowledge, relying solely on reward functions.
 - *Capabilities:* Achieved periodic motions (walking, hopping, galloping), blind walking on stairs, and navigation of stepping stones through terrain randomization and vision system integration.
 - *Advantages:* High potential for exploring novel gait patterns and highly robust to complex terrains.
 - *Limitations:* Requires intensive reward shaping, is computationally expensive, time-consuming, and potentially infeasible for certain complex tasks due to the absence of guidance.

**B. Hierarchical Frameworks:**
These frameworks decompose control into multiple layers, typically a High-Level (HL) planner and a Low-Level (LL) controller.
- **Deep Planning Hybrid Scheme:** Combines a learned HL planner with a model-based LL controller.
 - *Capabilities:* Achieved optimized task-space performance, improved velocity tracking, foot touchdown, and height control. Demonstrated robustness, training efficiency, and generalizability across platforms (Cassie, Digit, RABBIT). Some systems integrate visual perception for navigation and dynamic tasks like soccer.
 - *Advantages:* Enhanced command tracking, generalizability across different platforms, sampling-efficient, and robust.
 - *Limitations:* Introduces system complexity and communication challenges between layers. Relies on precise models for the LL controller, and lacks generalization across diverse tasks (e.g., complex dynamic motions like jumping). Navigation capabilities, while present, often need concurrent enhancement of overall locomotion.
- **Feedback DRL Control Hybrid Scheme:** Integrates DRL policies as LL controllers with non-learned HL planners (e.g., footstep planners).
 - *Capabilities:* Enhanced locomotion stability, accurate reference tracking, omnidirectional walking, and obstacle avoidance. Has achieved successful sim-to-real transfer. HL planners can provide abstract guidance (e.g., target foot locations) to expedite training.
 - *Advantages:* Short inference time, high robustness, navigation locomotion capabilities, and interpretability. Offers flexibility for on-the-fly adjustments compared to deep planning schemes.
 - *Limitations:* System complexity due to integration. Model-based HL planners can be computationally intensive, reliant on model assumptions, and require extensive training data, hindering rapid deployment. Challenges persist in sparse reward environments for LL DRL policies.
- **Learned Hierarchy Scheme:** Both the HL planner and LL controller are learned.
 - *Capabilities:* Validated in simulation for tasks like dribbling a soccer ball by decomposing locomotion and navigation. Enhanced stability and collision avoidance in wheeled bipeds. HumanPlus framework uses imitation learning and multimodal perception for loco-manipulation tasks (e.g., jumping, folding clothes).
 - *Advantages:* Provides layer flexibility, allowing independent retraining and reuse of policies. Alleviates challenges associated with training monolithic end-to-end policies.
 - *Limitations:* Inefficient sim-to-real transfer, complex interfaces between layers leading to suboptimal behaviors (e.g., stability issues), and expensive training processes. Often validated only in simulation, with practical bipedal robot application remaining largely unexplored. Challenges in coordination and communication between layers in general hierarchical frameworks.

**C. Overarching Limitations and Challenges (Sections IV):**
The survey identifies three fundamental categories of limitations:
1. **Generalization and Precision:** A key challenge is achieving both broad generalization across skills, terrains, and morphologies, and high precision for specific tasks. This tension arises from limited training diversity, conflicting reward signals, inefficient sampling of DRL algorithms (especially with sparse rewards), and scarcity of high-quality demonstration data. End-to-end approaches struggle with exploration and reconciling objectives, while hierarchical methods, though enforcing precision, can be brittle and limit generalization.
2. **Challenges in Transferring from Simulation to Reality (Sim-to-Real Gap):** This gap, driven by discrepancies between virtual and physical worlds, hinders deployment. Factors include inaccurate robot dynamics modeling (friction, inertia), difficulties in simulating contact and terrain properties, and the presence of noisy real-world sensor data versus perfect simulated states. Successful transfers often require extensive fine-tuning or result in degraded performance.
3. **Safety-Critical Locomotion:** The deployment of bipedal robots, particularly in human-centric environments, is fundamentally constrained by safety. Many existing frameworks prioritize performance over safety. Blind locomotion policies, relying solely on proprioception, pose risks by lacking environmental awareness. Furthermore, DRL frameworks often lack built-in mechanisms to guarantee physical constraint satisfaction (e.g., joint limits, torques, collision avoidance), making them prone to unsafe actions.

In summary, while DRL has demonstrated impressive capabilities in specific bipedal locomotion tasks, current frameworks are limited by fragmentation, a persistent sim-to-real gap, and significant challenges in simultaneously achieving broad generalization, high precision, and guaranteed safety.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey represents a significant contribution by systematically consolidating and analyzing the fragmented landscape of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for robotic bipedal locomotion. Its primary significance lies in providing a structured overview of existing methodologies, clarifying their respective strengths and limitations, and, crucially, outlining a strategic roadmap for future research.

**Consolidation and Clarification:**
By categorizing DRL frameworks into end-to-end and hierarchical schemes and further dissecting them into sub-types, the report offers a valuable organizational framework for researchers. This systematic approach clarifies the state of the art, making it easier for new entrants to the field to grasp complex concepts and for experienced researchers to identify trends and gaps. The detailed comparison of control schemes, including their characteristics, advantages, and disadvantages, serves as a practical guide for selecting or developing appropriate DRL approaches for specific bipedal locomotion challenges.

**Identification of Critical Research Gaps:**
The survey's detailed analysis of limitations, particularly concerning the tension between generalization and precision, the persistent sim-to-real gap, and critical safety considerations, highlights the major obstacles currently preventing the widespread deployment of DRL-driven bipedal robots. By clearly articulating these challenges, the paper directs future research efforts toward foundational problems that require resolution for robust real-world application. This focus on "blind spots" in current research is crucial for advancing the field beyond task-specific successes.

**Guidance for Future Research and Development:**
The report's most substantial impact is its comprehensive outline of future directions and opportunities. These are directly linked to overcoming the identified limitations and are divided into specific pathways:
- **Multi-skill learning**: Encourages the development of versatile robots capable of acquiring and deploying a broad repertoire of motor skills, crucial for unstructured environments.
- **Traversing challenging environments**: Emphasizes testing policies on diverse terrains to validate generalization and precision.
- **Efficient learning and reward shaping**: Aims to improve DRL algorithms' sample efficiency and robustness of reward designs, addressing computational cost and tuning effort.
- **Motion retargeting**: Proposes leveraging vast human motion data by developing methods to transfer physically feasible and stylistically accurate movements to robots.
- **Bridging the sim-to-real gap**: Suggests strategies like system identification, domain randomization, and adaptive policies to ensure reliable performance transfer from simulation to physical hardware.
- **Perception-conditioned locomotion**: Advocates for integrating exteroceptive sensors (cameras, LiDAR) to enable anticipatory control, moving from reactive to proactive navigation.
- **Constrained learning**: Calls for incorporating formal safety guarantees, through soft and hard constraints, to ensure the robot's integrity and safety in human-centric environments.

**Exploration of Emerging Opportunities:**
The survey also identifies synergistic opportunities from related fields that can significantly impact bipedal locomotion:
- **Leveraging Foundation Models (FMs)**: Proposes using large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) for high-level task planning and automated reward design, potentially revolutionizing how robots interpret commands and learn.
- **Loco-manipulation tasks**: Highlights the importance of integrating dynamic movement with object interaction, fostering the development of truly adaptable humanoid systems.
- **Insights from quadruped robots**: Suggests cross-pollination of successful DRL frameworks and techniques from quadrupedal robotics, where DRL is more mature, to accelerate bipedal advancements.

**Conceptual Models for Unified Frameworks:**
The proposal of two conceptual models—**Bipedal Foundation Models (BFMs)** and **Multi-Layer Adaptive Models (MLAMs)**—offers concrete blueprints for the next generation of bipedal controllers. BFMs aim for broad generalizability through large-scale pre-training and DRL fine-tuning, extending the end-to-end paradigm. MLAMs, conversely, represent an evolution of hierarchical approaches, focusing on modularity, adaptive layers, and interpretable outputs for flexibility. These models provide aspirational targets and distinct research trajectories toward achieving a unified, scalable, and generalizable control architecture.

In conclusion, this survey's significance lies in its capacity to serve as a foundational reference, guiding the DRL for bipedal locomotion community towards more integrated, efficient, and broadly applicable frameworks. By systematically dissecting the current state, pinpointing critical challenges, and charting forward-looking pathways, it aims to accelerate the development of robust, versatile, and safe bipedal robots capable of operating effectively in complex, unstructured real-world environments.
