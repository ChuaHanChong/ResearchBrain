# Phantom: Training Robots Without Robots Using Only Human Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> The Phantom framework enables training robot manipulation policies exclusively from human video demonstrations, eliminating the need for robot-specific data collection. It achieves this by converting human videos into robot-compatible observation-action pairs via hand pose estimation and visual data editing, leading to high success rates on real Franka and Kinova robots and generalization to novel environments.

## Problem

- Robot learning approaches are limited by the scarcity, cost, and lack of diversity in datasets collected using physical robots.
- Abundant human videos are difficult to leverage directly due to the absence of explicit robot action labels and a substantial visual/physical embodiment gap.
- Prior methods typically rely on co-training with robot data or struggle to bridge the human-to-robot embodiment gap, limiting scalability and zero-shot deployment capabilities.

## Method

- Employs hand pose estimation, refined with depth data and finger configuration constraints, to infer robot end-effector positions, orientations, and gripper openings from human hand movements.
- Implements a data-editing scheme ('==Hand Inpaint==') that segments and inpaints the human arm from video frames, then overlays a rendered virtual robot arm and gripper to bridge the visual embodiment gap.
- Trains a closed-loop imitation learning policy (Diffusion Policy) on these visually edited human video demonstrations for zero-shot deployment on real robotic hardware.

## Results

- Achieved high success rates, up to **92%** for Pick/Place Book and 72-**88%** for Tie Rope and Grasp Brush tasks, demonstrating effective policy transfer and zero-shot deployment on real Franka and Kinova robots.
- Demonstrated strong generalization to novel, unseen environments, achieving 72-**84%** success rates in out-of-distribution scenes for a sweeping task.
- The 'Hand Inpaint' data-editing strategy proved superior, offering better performance and efficiency (e.g., **73%** faster rollouts than 'Hand Mask') compared to other visual bridging methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Data-editing techniques can effectively bridge the challenging human-to-robot embodiment gap, allowing policies trained exclusively on human videos to deploy on physical robots.
> - Converting human videos to robot-compatible observation-action pairs by inferring actions and visually aligning embodiments eliminates the need for any robot-specific training data.
> - The presence of a virtual robot overlay during training is crucial for successful transfer, and high-quality inpainting of the human arm is optimal, though the method shows robustness to lower-quality inpainting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is continuously seeking to enhance the capabilities and generalization of robotic systems, particularly through learning-based approaches. A significant challenge in this domain is the scarcity of large, diverse datasets for training robot manipulation policies. Current state-of-the-art methods frequently rely on teleoperated robot demonstrations, which are costly, time-consuming, and difficult to scale across various environments and tasks. Consequently, existing robot datasets are orders of magnitude smaller than those used in other artificial intelligence fields like computer vision and natural language processing.

Human videos, in contrast, are abundant, diverse, and inherently rich in task information. However, their direct application to robot learning is hampered by two primary challenges: the absence of explicit action labels and the substantial visual and physical embodiment gap between human operators and robotic hardware.

Prior research has explored several strategies to leverage human videos for robot learning. One common approach involves co-training with robot data, where human videos supplement a baseline of robot demonstrations to improve generalization or refine policies. Examples include methods that pre-train visual representations, learn reward functions, build world models, or derive object motion priors from human videos. However, these methods typically still necessitate a significant amount of robot data to bridge the embodiment gap and extract sufficient learning signals, thereby limiting their scalability.

Another line of work focuses on object-centric approaches, which estimate and track target object poses from videos to inform robot policies. While effective for rigid objects, these methods often require object identification and rigid-body transformations, making them less suitable for deformable objects, granular materials, or multi-object manipulation scenarios. Flow-based methods attempt to overcome some of these limitations by tracking points rather than rigid bodies, but often still require robot data for motion refinement or are limited to open-loop execution.

More recently, data-editing methods have emerged as a promising direction, particularly for robot-to-robot transfer, where they mitigate visual distribution shifts by replacing the source embodiment with a rendered target embodiment during training. However, adapting these techniques to the more pronounced human-to-robot embodiment gap has typically still required co-training with robot data.

This work, "Phantom," addresses the limitations of prior approaches by proposing a framework designed to operate *exclusively* on human video demonstrations, thereby eliminating the need for any robot data. It specifically leverages data-editing techniques, previously primarily applied to robot-to-robot transfer, and adapts them for the more challenging human-to-robot setting. By demonstrating effective policy training and zero-shot deployment using only human videos, Phantom positions itself as a method that directly tackles the scalability bottleneck in robot learning, particularly in a future data regime where human videos are vastly more prevalent than robot demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable framework for training robot manipulation policies directly from human video demonstrations, without requiring any data collected using robotic hardware. This objective is motivated by several key challenges in advancing general-purpose robotics:

- **Data Scarcity and Diversity in Robotics:** Current robot learning approaches are constrained by the laborious and costly process of collecting data with physical robots. This results in robot datasets that are orders of magnitude smaller than those used in other AI fields. Furthermore, merely increasing data quantity is insufficient; diversity in data is critical for policies to generalize across varied environments and scenarios, which is also challenging to achieve with physical robots.
- **Leveraging Abundant Human Videos:** Human videos represent a vast, readily available, and diverse source of task-relevant information. Utilizing this data source could significantly overcome the data scarcity issue inherent in robot learning.
- **Overcoming Challenges of Human-to-Robot Transfer:** Despite their potential, human videos pose two fundamental challenges for robot learning:
 1. **Lack of Explicit Action Labels:** Human demonstrations do not inherently contain the precise, robot-compatible action commands (e.g., end-effector pose, gripper width) required for imitation learning.
 2. **Visual Embodiment Gap:** The visual appearance of human arms and hands differs substantially from that of robotic arms and grippers. A vision-based policy trained solely on human appearance would likely fail to generalize to a robot.
- **Limitations of Existing Approaches:** Prior methods attempting to leverage human videos typically rely on co-training with robot data or use reinforcement learning, which either introduces a fundamental scalability bottleneck due to continued reliance on robot data or struggles to extract sufficient learning signals from human data alone. Object-centric and flow-based methods also face limitations with deformable objects or require extensive manual setup or simulation environments.
- **Enabling Zero-Shot Deployment:** The motivation extends to enabling policies trained on human data to be deployed directly onto real robot hardware without any further fine-tuning or robot-specific data collection. This "zero-shot" capability is crucial for practical, scalable deployment.

The overarching motivation is to explore an emerging data regime where human videos become significantly more prevalent than robot demonstrations. The authors aim to remove the bottlenecks imposed by robot data collection by demonstrating that data-editing-based cross-embodiment learning techniques, previously successful in robot-to-robot transfer, can be effectively adapted to the more challenging human-to-robot transfer setting. This approach seeks to unlock the utility of human video demonstrations for scalable robot learning, making the collection of meaningful training data accessible to anyone with an RGBD camera.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Phantom framework operates by converting human video demonstrations into robot-compatible observation-action pairs through a pipeline involving hand pose estimation and visual data editing. This processed data is then used to train an imitation learning policy for zero-shot deployment on a physical robot.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup

The input consists of a dataset of $N$ human video demonstrations, each comprising a sequence of RGBD images captured from a third-person viewpoint, showing a manipulation task performed with a pinch grasp. The objective is to train a closed-loop policy using only this human video data that can be deployed directly on a target robot without any robot-specific training data. The system converts each human video frame $I_{h,t}$ into a corresponding robot observation-action pair $(I_{r,t}, a_{r,t})$. Robot actions $a_{r,t}$ are defined as the position ($p_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$) and orientation ($R_t \in \mathbb{R}^6$) of the end-effector, and the normalized gripper opening width ($g_t \in$). Camera extrinsics for the deployment setup are assumed to be known, and a similar viewpoint for data collection and testing is assumed.

#### 3.2. Action Labeling of Human Videos

Since human videos lack explicit robot action information, a method is employed to infer robot actions from human hand movements:
- **Hand Pose Estimation:** For each frame $I_{h,t}$, the HaMeR model is used to predict 21 3D keypoints ($\hat{X}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{21 \times 3}$) and a dense set of 778 vertices ($\hat{V}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{778 \times 3}$) representing the human hand mesh.
- **Depth-Enhanced Refinement:** To improve the absolute 3D pose, depth data is incorporated. The hand is segmented in the RGB image using SAM2 to create a mask $M_t$. This mask and the corresponding depth image $D_t$ are used to extract a partial hand point cloud $P_t$. Iterative Closest Point (ICP) registration aligns the HaMeR-predicted mesh $\hat{V}_t$ with $P_t$, yielding an optimal rigid transformation $T_t$. This transformation is then applied to the predicted keypoints: $X_t = T_t \hat{X}_t$.
- **Finger Configuration Constraint:** To address issues with occluded keypoints and anatomically unrealistic finger configurations from HaMeR's ball-joint model, the last two joints of the thumb and index fingers are constrained to a single degree of freedom, limiting their movement to physiologically plausible ranges.
- **Robot Action Definition:** The refined keypoints $X_t$ are used to define the robot action $a_{r,t}$:
 - **Position ($p_t$):** The midpoint between the keypoints at the tips of the thumb ($x^{thumb, tip}_t$) and index finger ($x^{index, tip}_t$).
 - **Orientation ($R_t$):** A plane is fitted through all keypoints of the thumb and index finger, and a principal axis is computed by fitting a vector through the thumb keypoints. $R_t$ is defined by the normal of this plane and the fitted vector.
 - **Gripper Opening ($g_t$):** The distance between the thumb and index fingertip keypoints. To prevent slippage during grasping, the lowest 20th percentile of predicted gripper distances within a trajectory are set to a fully closed state.
- **Coordinate Transformation:** The estimated hand poses (position and orientation) in the camera's reference frame are converted into the target robot's frame using known camera extrinsics.

#### 3.3. Bridging the Visual Observation Gap

To ensure visual consistency between training data (human videos) and test-time robot observations, a data-editing scheme is implemented:
- **Data-editing during training:**
 1. **Human Arm Segmentation and Inpainting:** The human arm is segmented from each video frame using SAM2. The segmented arm is then removed via inpainting using the E2FGVI method, filling the region with a realistic background.
 2. **Virtual Robot Overlay:** A virtual model of the target robot's arm and gripper is rendered in the pose $(p_t, R_t, g_t)$ derived from the action labeling step. This rendered image is then overlaid onto the inpainted background. Depth data is used to correctly handle occlusions by environmental objects, ensuring a realistic composite image.
- **Data-editing at inference time:**
 To minimize domain shift between the rendered robot in training images and the real robot at test time, a virtual robot arm is overlaid onto the image of the real robot arm in each observation frame during inference. This ensures that the policy always receives inputs with a consistent visual appearance.

#### 3.4. Policy Training Details

The imitation learning policy is trained using Diffusion Policy. Low-level control for the Franka robot is handled by an Operational Space Control (OSC) controller, while an Inverse Kinematics (IK) controller is used for the Kinova robot. Virtual robot renderings are generated using Mujoco models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the Phantom method across various tasks, focusing on its ability to train policies from human videos for zero-shot deployment on real robotic hardware, including both Franka and Kinova robots.

#### 4.1. Comparison of Data Editing Methods

The study compared four data-editing strategies:
- **Hand Inpaint (Phantom - Ours):** Segments out the human arm, inpaints the background, and overlays a rendered virtual robot. At inference, a virtual robot is overlaid on the real robot.
- **Hand Mask:** Masks out the human arm with black, overlays a virtual robot. At inference, a diffusion model generates a hand segmentation mask which is overlaid, followed by a virtual robot overlay.
- **Red Line:** Masks out the human arm, overlays a red line along its length. At inference, the robot arm is similarly masked and a red line is overlaid.
- **Vanilla:** No image modification at train or test time.

#### 4.2. In-distribution Scene Performance

Phantom's ability to transfer policies within the same scene as the human demonstrations was evaluated on five tasks: Pick/Place Book, Stack Cups, Tie Rope, Rotate Box, and Sweep Trash (with success metrics for grasping the brush and sweeping >0, >2, >4 pieces).
- **Success Rates:** The Hand Inpaint (Phantom) and Hand Mask strategies both achieved high success rates across all tasks. For example, Pick/Place Book achieved 92% success for both, while Hand Inpaint achieved 72% for Tie Rope and 88% for Grasp Brush. The Red Line and Vanilla strategies consistently failed on all tasks, achieving 0% success.
- **Efficiency:** Hand Mask required approximately 73% longer for rollouts due to the additional step of running a diffusion model at test time for hand mask generation.

#### 4.3. Out-of-distribution Scenes Generalization

The method's generalization capabilities were assessed on a sweeping task in three novel, unseen environments for the Kinova robot: Outdoor Lawn, Indoor Lounge, and Indoor Lounge with an Out-of-Distribution (OOD) surface.
- **Success Rates:** Hand Inpaint achieved notable success rates in OOD scenes, with 72% in Outdoor Lawn and 84% in Indoor Lounge. Performance slightly decreased to 64% when combined with an OOD surface, attributed to limited diversity in training surfaces. Hand Mask performed comparably but showed lower performance in the Outdoor Lawn setting (52%).
- **Overall Comparison:** Similar to in-distribution tests, Red Line and Vanilla baselines yielded 0% success in OOD scenes. Hand Inpaint consistently achieved higher or comparable success rates to Hand Mask, with the added benefit of faster rollout times.

#### 4.4. Evaluating the Necessity of High-quality Inpainting

The study investigated the impact of inpainting quality during training on the sweeping task in the Indoor Lounge scene. Three variations were compared: high-quality inpainting (E2FGVI), low-quality inpainting (OpenCV inpaint), and no inpainting (Mask Only).
- **Impact on Performance:** High-quality E2FGVI inpainting yielded the best performance (84% success). Low-quality OpenCV inpainting performed surprisingly well (76%), suggesting robustness to artifacts. Mask Only (no inpainting) resulted in a noticeable performance drop (60%).
- **Implication:** This indicates that while high-quality inpainting is optimal, the method can tolerate lower quality, and the presence of a robot overlay during training is crucial for success (as evidenced by Mask Only outperforming Red Line).

#### 4.5. Additional Findings (Appendix 6.3 and 6.4)

- **Benefits of Co-training with Diverse Human Data:** An experiment showed that a robot policy trained solely on 100 teleoperated robot demonstrations achieved 88% success in-distribution but 0% in an OOD scene. Co-training with a large, diverse human video dataset (950 demonstrations) improved OOD performance to 80%, indicating that human data significantly enhances generalization to novel environments.
- **Comparison of Human vs. Robot Data Precision:** When comparing policies trained on robot vs. human demonstrations in the same scene, a policy trained on 50 teleoperated demonstrations achieved 52% success, compared to 44% for 50 human demonstrations (not statistically significant). With 100 demonstrations, robot data yielded 88% success versus 64% for human data (also not statistically significant, p=0.095). Scaling to 300 human demonstrations achieved 84% success. These results suggest that while individual human demonstrations might be less precise, their ease of collection allows for scaling that can effectively reduce the performance gap.

In summary, the Phantom method successfully demonstrates that effective robot manipulation policies can be trained using only human video demonstrations, achieving high success rates and generalizing to novel environments and various task types (rigid, deformable, multi-object). The chosen data-editing strategy (Hand Inpaint) proved superior in terms of performance and efficiency compared to alternative visual bridging methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Phantom framework presents several significant contributions and has the potential for considerable impact on the field of robot learning:

- **Enabling Scalable Robot Learning without Robot Data:** The most prominent contribution is demonstrating that robot policies can be effectively trained *exclusively* from human video demonstrations, entirely removing the dependency on costly and slow robot data collection. This addresses a fundamental bottleneck in scaling robot learning datasets, which traditionally lag far behind those in other AI domains.
- **Broadening Access to Robot Training Data:** By enabling anyone with an RGBD camera to collect meaningful training data, the method dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for large-scale robot learning. This accessibility can lead to more diverse and extensive datasets, fostering broader research and development in robotics.
- **Generalization to Novel Environments and Tasks:** The system achieves zero-shot deployment on real hardware, demonstrating high success rates across a range of tasks including deformable object manipulation, multi-object sweeping, and insertion. Crucially, it generalizes to novel, unseen environments, indicating that training and deployment scenes do not need to match. This generalization capability is critical for deploying robots in varied real-world settings.
- **Validation of Data-Editing for Human-to-Robot Transfer:** The work successfully adapts data-editing techniques, previously primarily used for robot-to-robot transfer, to the more challenging human-to-robot embodiment gap. This highlights the effectiveness of visual domain alignment strategies in cross-embodiment learning.
- **Foundation for Generalist Robot Policies:** By generating robot-compatible observation-action pairs from human videos, Phantom produces data in a format amenable to integration into large-scale robotics datasets used to train autoregressive generalist policies. This offers a promising avenue for future work to combine the scalability of human videos with the power of foundation models in robotics.
- **Modularity and Future Improvement:** The modular nature of the approach, particularly its reliance on hand pose estimators, means that its performance can directly benefit from ongoing advancements in computer vision, allowing for continuous improvement without fundamental changes to the framework.

Despite current limitations such as reliance on hand pose estimator accuracy, applicability only when human and robot strategies align, and focus on quasi-static tasks, Phantom significantly advances the path to scalable and accessible robot learning. Its ability to leverage the vast reservoir of human videos represents a crucial step towards equipping robots with a broader range of manipulation skills for real-world deployment.
