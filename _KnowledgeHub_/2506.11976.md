# How Visual Representations Map to Language Feature Space in Multimodal LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Oxford, McGill University, and Meta, along with a mechanistic interpretability expert, employed Sparse Autoencoders to reveal that visual representations in multimodal LLMs gradually align with language features, converging in the middle-to-later layers around layer 18 of the Gemma-2-2b-it model.

## Problem

- The internal mechanisms by which multimodal LLMs achieve robust cross-modal understanding remain largely opaque, posing a 'black box' problem.
- A core challenge in multimodal AI is understanding how different sensory inputs (e.g., visual pixels, text tokens) are transformed into a shared, semantically rich representation space.
- Existing interpretability methods like 'logit lens' are limited, as they cannot capture meaningful representational alignment in earlier or intermediate layers of LLMs.

## Method

- Employed a constrained multimodal LLM architecture using the ==LiMBeR framework==, featuring a frozen vision transformer (CLIP ViT-L/14) and a frozen language model (Gemma-2-2b-it).
- A trainable ==linear adapter== was used to map visual features from the frozen ViT into the fixed input token space of the frozen LLM.
- Utilized pre-trained ==Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)==, specifically ==GemmaScope SAEs==, to conduct a layer-wise mechanistic analysis of visual-to-linguistic feature alignment across all 26 layers of the LLM.

## Results

- The constrained VLM achieved competitive performance on standard benchmarks (MME, LLaVA Bench, GQA, POPE), confirming its functional integration of modalities.
- SAE reconstruction error and feature sparsity for visual tokens significantly decreased, and semantic alignment increased, converging around **layers 18-20** of the Gemma-2-2b-it LLM.
- A fundamental misalignment was identified between ViT outputs and the early LLM layers, indicating that the linear adapter alone is insufficient to perfectly translate visual information into the early conceptual representations of the language model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cross-modal alignment is a gradual process within the LLM, not an instantaneous event, with convergence primarily occurring in the middle-to-later layers.
> - Visual information, even after adapter projection, initially remains out-of-distribution for the early language feature space of the LLM.
> - Sparse Autoencoders offer a robust tool for deep, layer-wise mechanistic interpretability in complex multimodal models, effectively probing internal representations beyond surface-level observations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving and critical areas within artificial intelligence: multimodal large language models (MLLMs or VLMs), mechanistic interpretability, and cross-modal representation learning.

**Multimodal LLMs (VLMs):** The past few years have seen an explosion in the development and capabilities of VLMs, such as LLaVA, InstructBLIP, CLIP, and various Gemma-based models. These models are capable of processing and reasoning across both visual and linguistic inputs, leading to impressive performance on tasks like image captioning, visual question answering, and multimodal dialogue. While their impressive capabilities are evident, the internal mechanisms by which these models achieve robust cross-modal understanding remain largely opaque. This paper directly addresses this "black box" problem.

**Mechanistic Interpretability:** This field aims to move beyond simply evaluating model performance or providing post-hoc explanations, instead seeking to understand the *algorithms* and *representations* learned by neural networks at a fundamental, neuron- or feature-level. Tools like Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are central to this endeavor, allowing researchers to decompose complex neural activations into more interpretable, disentangled features. By applying SAEs, this work pushes the boundaries of VLM interpretability beyond merely observing input-output relationships.

**Cross-Modal Representation Alignment:** A core challenge in multimodal AI is how different sensory inputs (e.g., pixels for vision, tokens for text) are transformed into a shared, semantically rich representation space that allows for coherent reasoning. Previous work has explored various architectural choices for achieving this, including joint training, adapter layers, and attention mechanisms. However, the *dynamic process* of alignment—how representations evolve and integrate across different layers of a VLM—is less understood. The paper specifically investigates this layer-wise alignment.

**Critique of Existing Interpretability Methods:** The authors explicitly highlight a limitation of "logit lens" methods, which are commonly used in LLM interpretability. While logit lens can detect alignment in the unembedding space (i.e., how hidden states map to vocabulary tokens), it cannot capture meaningful representational alignment in earlier or intermediate layers that do not operate directly in this space. This limitation creates a "blind spot" precisely where cross-modal integration might be initiating. By employing SAEs, the paper bypasses this limitation, offering a more granular and comprehensive view of feature alignment throughout the model's depth.

**Contribution to the Landscape:** This work advances the field by:
1. **Bridging Modalities with Interpretability:** It applies sophisticated mechanistic interpretability tools to the complex domain of VLMs, shedding light on their internal workings.
2. **Focusing on Layer-wise Dynamics:** It provides a novel, layer-by-layer analysis of visual-to-linguistic feature mapping, revealing a progression of alignment rather than just a static state.
3. **Informing Architectural Design:** By identifying bottlenecks (e.g., early layer misalignment), it offers actionable insights for designing more efficient and effective VLM architectures.
4. **Validating Adapter-Based Efficiency:** The paper uses the LiMBeR framework, which emphasizes efficient training with frozen backbones, and validates that this constrained setup still yields functional VLMs, making the interpretability findings directly relevant to practical, resource-efficient VLM development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **understand the specific mechanisms and layer-wise progression through which visual representations become aligned with language feature representations within multimodal large language models (LLMs).**

This overarching objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **Addressing the "Black Box" Problem in VLMs:** While VLMs exhibit impressive capabilities, the internal processes that enable them to effectively bridge visual and linguistic modalities remain poorly understood. The authors aim to demystify this critical aspect of VLM functionality.
- **Gaining Theoretical Insight into Cross-Modal Alignment:** A deeper theoretical understanding of how different modalities are integrated at a representational level is crucial for advancing the entire field of multimodal AI. The paper seeks to provide granular insights into this fundamental problem.
- **Informing Future VLM Architectural Design and Optimization:** By identifying *where* and *how* visual and linguistic features align (or misalign) across the layers of an LLM, the research aims to provide actionable insights for developing more efficient, robust, and effective VLM architectures. For instance, understanding early-layer misalignment could suggest improvements to adapter designs or training strategies.
- **Leveraging Advanced Interpretability Tools:** The authors are motivated to apply cutting-edge mechanistic interpretability tools, specifically Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), to a domain where traditional methods have limitations. This allows for a more fine-grained analysis of internal representations than previously possible.
- **Controlled Experimental Environment:** By adopting the LiMBeR framework (frozen vision transformer, frozen language model, trainable linear adapter), the researchers create a controlled environment. This setup ensures that the adapter is solely responsible for the cross-modal mapping, preventing the LLM from altering its intrinsic language representations. This design choice is motivated by the desire to use pre-trained LLM SAEs as perfectly aligned analytical probes, enabling a clear and unambiguous study of the mapping process.
- **Moving Beyond Behavioral Outputs:** The motivation extends beyond simply evaluating VLM performance to truly understanding the underlying representational changes that enable that performance, thereby shifting the focus from "what" the model does to "how" it does it.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a rigorous methodology centered around a constrained VLM architecture and the application of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) for detailed mechanistic interpretability.

#### 3.1. VLM Architecture and Training Setup (LiMBeR Framework)

The core of the experimental setup adheres to the LiMBeR framework, designed to isolate the cross-modal mapping function:
- **Frozen Backbones:** A **frozen vision transformer (ViT)**, specifically CLIP ViT-L/14, and a **frozen large language model (LLM)**, Gemma-2-2b-it, are used. Freezing both components is a critical design choice. It ensures that the LLM retains its original, pre-trained language representations without adapting to visual data, and the ViT maintains its visual encoding.
- **Linear Adapter:** A simple **trainable linear adapter** connects the frozen ViT and LLM. This adapter's sole responsibility is to map the visual features from the ViT into the fixed input token space of the LLM. This constrained design allows for a clear analysis of the adapter's role in alignment.
- **Feature Integration:** After processing an image with the CLIP ViT encoder, 256 visual representations (excluding the CLS token) are extracted. These are then projected by the linear adapter and concatenated after the beginning-of-sequence (BOS) token of the Gemma-2-2b-it model, followed by the remaining linguistic input tokens.

#### 3.1.1. Dataset and Training Stages

The VLM is trained using a LLaVA-style adapter training setup, involving two stages:
- **Pretraining:** Uses a subset of 595K filtered image-text pairs from Conceptual Captions 3M, formatted as instruction-response pairs where the instruction asks for an image description.
- **Finetuning:** Employs a 665K instruction-following dataset from LLaVA-1.5.

#### 3.1.2. Training Hyperparameters

Standard hyperparameters are applied:
- **Pretraining:** Batch size 128, 1 epoch, learning rate 1e-3, warmup ratio 0.03.
- **Finetuning:** Batch size 64, 3 epochs, learning rate 2e-5, no warmup.
Both ViT and LLM weights are stored in 16-bit precision.

#### 3.1.3. Benchmark Evaluation

The trained VLM's performance is benchmarked against established VLMs (LLaVA-v1-7B, LLaVA-v1.5-7B, InstructBLIP-7B) on standard vision-language benchmarks like MME, LLaVA Bench, GQA, and POPE. This evaluation serves to validate that the constrained experimental setup still yields a functional VLM, thus making the interpretability findings meaningful.

#### 3.2. Mechanistic Interpretability with Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)

The core analytical tool is Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs):
- **SAE Selection:** The researchers leverage **GemmaScope SAEs**, which are pre-trained for the Gemma-2-2b LLM. This is crucial because the LLM backbone remains frozen, ensuring that the SAEs are perfectly aligned with the LLM's unchanging feature space.
- **Rationale for SAEs over Logit Lens:** The paper explicitly argues that SAEs overcome the limitations of logit lens methods. While logit lens projects hidden states onto vocabulary space (mainly relevant for later layers), SAEs provide a "snapshot of the learned language feature-representations" at *every* layer, allowing for detection of alignment even in early layers.

#### 3.2.1. SAE-Based Analysis Framework

Three key metrics are used to assess cross-modal alignment layer-wise:
- **SAE Reconstruction Error (Equation 1):** Measures the L2 reconstruction error between the original visual representation (after adapter projection and entering the LLM) and its reconstruction by the SAE at each layer. A decrease in error indicates better integration into the LLM's feature space.
- **SAE Feature Sparsity (Equation 2):** Measures the fraction of non-zero activations in the SAE's dictionary for visual tokens at each layer. Comparing this to sparsity patterns for pure language inputs indicates whether visual information is activating sparse, precise subsets of the LLM's learned features.
- **SAE Feature Semantic Alignment:** This involves applying the SAE to visual token positions, identifying the top three strongest activating features, and using GPT-4o to assess whether their descriptions semantically match concepts present in the input image. This directly validates if the activated language features are truly encoding visual information. To ensure meaningful features, high-frequency features (activating on >5% of image data or >0.5% of original LLM dataset) are removed.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup for Analysis

- **Data for Analysis:** 5000 examples from the LLaVA-1.5 finetuning dataset are used for reconstruction and sparsity error calculations, and 1000 examples for feature description alignment.
- **Baselines:** Text-only examples (instruction + answer) are fed through the *frozen* Gemma-2-2b-it LLM to generate activations, serving as a baseline for language-only representation patterns.
- **Probing Positions:** Activations are collected from image token positions within the VLM, text token positions within the VLM, and all token positions for the text-only baseline (excluding BOS). For semantic alignment, VLM text token positions are excluded to avoid trivial matches from textual questions.
- **Layer-wise Analysis:** All experiments are conducted across all 26 layers of the Gemma-2-2b-it model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's meticulous methodology, particularly the use of frozen backbones and pre-trained SAEs, yielded several significant findings regarding the layer-wise alignment of visual and linguistic representations in multimodal LLMs.

#### 4.1. Validation of Experimental Setup

Despite the highly constrained architecture (frozen ViT, frozen LLM, linear adapter), the trained VLM (CLIP-Gemma-2-2B-it) demonstrated competitive performance on standard benchmarks (MME, LLaVA Bench, GQA, POPE) (Table 1). Its performance was comparable to LLaVA-v1-7B (which fine-tunes the LLM) and, while not reaching the upper bounds of LLaVA-v1.5-7B or InstructBLIP, it confirmed that the model was functional and effectively integrating modalities. This validation is crucial, as it ensures that the interpretability analysis is conducted on a genuinely capable multimodal system, not a trivial one.

#### 4.2. Layer-wise Convergence of Cross-Modal Mapping

The central finding, consistent across all three interpretability metrics, is that visual representations gradually align with language feature representations, with a clear convergence observed in the **middle-to-later layers** of the LLM, specifically around **layer 18** (out of 26 layers in Gemma-2-2b-it).

- **SAE Reconstruction Error (Figure 1, left):**
 - **Early Layers (0-~15):** Visual representations exhibit a **high reconstruction error** when probed by the LLM's SAEs. This indicates that the visual input, after being projected by the linear adapter, is initially largely "out-of-distribution" for the language model's early-layer features. The SAEs struggle to accurately reconstruct these visual activations using the learned language feature dictionary.
 - **Middle-to-Later Layers (~16-20+):** A **steep decline in reconstruction error** is observed for visual token positions. By layer 18-20, the error converges to levels comparable to or slightly above those for pure language prompt positions. This signifies that visual representations are progressively transformed and integrated into the LLM's inherent language feature space, becoming increasingly "interpretable" by the SAEs.

- **SAE Feature Sparsity (Figure 1, right):**
 - **Early Layers:** Visual token positions show a **higher sparsity** initially, which is somewhat counter-intuitive, but likely reflects that the early layers are not activating specific *relevant* language features.
 - **Middle-to-Later Layers:** Sparsity for visual token positions **decreases significantly** and converges with the sparsity levels observed for LLM prompt positions (pure language input). This suggests that as visual information progresses through the LLM, it begins to activate a more precise, focused, and *meaningful* subset of the language model's features. The visual information is no longer just broadly "activating" things but is being represented by specific, compact features relevant to the LLM's semantic space.

- **SAE Feature Semantic Alignment (Figure 2):**
 - **Early Layers:** The "match rate" between the descriptions of highly activating visual SAE features and the actual content of the input image is **low**. This corroborates the idea that visual features are not yet meaningfully encoded by the LLM's language features.
 - **Middle-to-Later Layers:** There is a **steep increase in the semantic alignment** score. By layers 18-20, the match rate for visual tokens reaches high levels (e.g., above 0.8 by layer 20), indicating that the activated language features in these layers genuinely correspond to semantically meaningful visual concepts present in the image. This provides direct evidence that the cross-modal mapping is successful in conveying visual semantics through the LLM's internal language features.

#### 4.3. Fundamental Early-Layer Misalignment

A critical outcome is the revelation of a **fundamental misalignment between ViT outputs and early LLM layers**. Visual information, even after passing through the linear adapter, does not immediately align with the native feature space of the LLM's initial layers. Instead, it takes several layers of processing within the LLM for this alignment to fully materialize. This strongly suggests that the linear adapter alone is insufficient to perfectly translate visual information into the early conceptual representations of the language model.

#### 4.4. Beyond Out-of-Distribution Detection

The findings underscore that the analysis goes beyond merely detecting that visual inputs are out-of-distribution for early LLM layers. By using SAEs and analyzing feature descriptions, the research identifies the *specific language model features* that the adapter utilizes to represent visual information in the middle-to-late layers. This offers a deeper, mechanistic explanation of cross-modal integration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the fields of multimodal AI and mechanistic interpretability, with substantial potential impact on future VLM development and our understanding of intelligence.

#### 5.1. Novel Mechanistic Understanding of Cross-Modal Integration

This study provides the first layer-wise, mechanistic understanding of *how* visual features map onto language representations within multimodal LLMs, particularly in an adapter-based architecture. By demonstrating that cross-modal alignment is not an instantaneous event but a gradual process converging in middle-to-late LLM layers, it sheds crucial light on the internal dynamics of VLMs. This moves beyond observing input-output behavior to explaining the underlying representational transformations.

#### 5.2. Critical Insights for VLM Architectural Design

The revelation of a **fundamental misalignment between ViT outputs and early LLM layers** is perhaps the most impactful finding for VLM architecture. It suggests that current linear adapter-based designs, even when effective behaviorally, might be suboptimal in how they initiate cross-modal representation learning.
- **Optimizing Adapter Placement and Complexity:** This insight could prompt research into more sophisticated adapters (ee.g., non-linear, multi-layered) that map visual features into *earlier* LLM layers more effectively. Alternatively, it might suggest that adapters should explicitly target mid-layers, or that early LLM layers need more direct visual exposure or specialized training to assimilate visual information more quickly.
- **Efficiency and Resource Allocation:** If visual information is truly integrated only in later layers, it raises questions about the computational efficiency of processing visual tokens through initial LLM layers where they are largely "misaligned." Future architectures might re-route or selectively activate paths to optimize this process.

#### 5.3. Advancing Mechanistic Interpretability

The work demonstrates the immense power of Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) as a robust tool for probing complex multimodal models. By successfully applying pre-trained SAEs to a frozen LLM backbone to understand visual information flow, the study validates SAEs' utility beyond purely language-centric analyses. It also highlights the limitations of other interpretability methods like the logit lens, advocating for deeper, layer-wise analysis.

#### 5.4. Foundation for Further Research

This research opens several avenues for future investigation:
- **End-to-End Fine-tuned Models:** How does cross-modal alignment differ in VLMs where the LLM backbone is also fine-tuned, allowing it to adapt its internal structure to visual data? Do these models achieve earlier or more robust alignment?
- **Impact of Adapter Design:** Exploring various adapter architectures (e.g., transformer-based projectors like in InstructBLIP, gated non-linear adapters) and their influence on the layer-wise alignment process.
- **Mitigating Misalignment:** Developing methods or training regimes specifically designed to encourage earlier and more efficient alignment of visual and linguistic features within VLMs.
- **Understanding Cross-Modal Attention:** While this paper focuses on feature mapping, future work could combine these insights with analyses of cross-modal attention mechanisms to understand how the LLM *selectively attends* to relevant visual features once they are integrated.

#### 5.5. Potential for Enhanced VLM Robustness and Safety

A deeper understanding of cross-modal feature alignment could contribute to building more robust and reliable VLMs. If the mechanism of integration is understood, it may be possible to:
- **Reduce Hallucinations:** By ensuring that visual features are accurately and precisely mapped to linguistic concepts, the likelihood of models generating incorrect or "hallucinated" descriptions could be reduced.
- **Improve Control:** A mechanistic understanding offers avenues for better control over VLM behavior, potentially allowing for more targeted interventions to correct biases or unwanted responses related to visual inputs.

In conclusion, this paper significantly advances our understanding of multimodal LLMs by peering into their internal representations. Its findings about the gradual, middle-to-late layer alignment of visual and linguistic features, coupled with the identification of early-layer misalignment, provide crucial theoretical insights and practical guidance for the next generation of VLM architectures.
