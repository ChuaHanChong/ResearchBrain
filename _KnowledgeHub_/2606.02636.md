# Too Much of a Good Thing: When sim2real Efforts Impede Policy Learning (And What to Do About It)

> [!summary] Summary
> A position paper arguing that high-fidelity sim2real tuning causes simulator lock-in and blocks exploration on large humanoids. It proposes sim2sim2real: train a teacher in an idealized, low-gain IsaacLab model while concurrently learning a kinematic forward model, then use that model's predicted next kinematic state as an imitation reference to retrain the policy in a hardware-validated MuJoCo model for zero-shot deployment on Apollo.

## Problem

- System identification results do not transfer between physics engines, so teams commit to one validated simulator and face insurmountable engineering cost to try alternatives.
- Excessive domain randomization is now known to make policies conservative, pushing the field toward rigid single-model system identification that narrows which research directions stay feasible.
- For heavy humanoids the stiffness needed for gravity compensation grows faster than motor torque, so Gaussian PD-target exploration saturates torque limits and stalls learning.

## Method

- **sim2sim2real** paradigm: decouple behavior generation from system identification by training in an idealized low-gain support simulator, then transferring into the hardware-validated validation simulator.
- Concurrent estimation and imitation: a ==forward model== trained alongside the ==PPO== teacher predicts next joint positions, velocities, body-link poses, and foot contact from the actor's observation.
- Kinematics as shared ground truth: the frozen forward model supplies references for ==state-based imitation rewards== training a student in a MuJoCo Playground-like Brax environment.

## Results

- Scaling analysis: stiffness grows as **c^4** with robot scale while peak motor torque grows as **c^3**, so torque available per unit gain falls as **1/c**.
- The forward model's lower-body joint position estimates track ground truth closely over the plotted rollout, confirming kinematic prediction is accurate enough to serve as an imitation reference.
- Case study on Apptronik Apollo: locomotion learned in a reduced-order IsaacLab model without joint friction, latency, or torque limits transfers into the validated full-order MuJoCo model for zero-shot deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Kinematics, not dynamics, is the portable interface between simulators; matching joint trajectories transfers behavior even when action spaces, gains, and actuation strategies differ.
> - Fidelity has an optimum: constraining exploration to dynamically feasible actions can make a task unlearnable, so learning and validation environments should be tuned separately.
> - Classic model-based practice, plan kinematically then refine for dynamic feasibility, still applies to RL pipelines and reduces lock-in to any single simulator.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research presented in this paper addresses a core challenge within the field of robotics, specifically in the deployment of reinforcement learning (RL) policies on physical hardware, commonly referred to as the `sim2real` problem. The `sim2real` paradigm aims to bridge the gap between simulation and real-world execution, allowing policies trained in simulated environments to transfer effectively to physical robots. This is a critical area, as training complex robotic behaviors directly on hardware is often resource-intensive, time-consuming, and potentially damaging to the robot.

The established practice in `sim2real` involves developing high-fidelity simulations that accurately model a robot's dynamics and kinematics. This typically entails extensive system identification, parameter tuning (e.g., rotor inertia, joint friction, PD gains, control latency), and state estimation efforts to ensure the simulation closely mirrors the real robot. Once a high-fidelity model is established, policies are trained in this "validation simulation" and then deployed zero-shot onto hardware. Subsequent hardware rollouts may then be used to refine the simulation model iteratively, thereby closing the `sim2real` gap. This approach has demonstrated success in enabling robots to operate near their mechanical and electrical limits.

However, this paper posits that while such rigorous `sim2real` efforts are necessary, they can inadvertently create adverse effects on policy learning itself. The authors argue that the current dominant paradigm for policy learning, which prioritizes a single high-fidelity simulation for zero-shot transfer, is "overfit to sim2real concerns." This overfitting leads to two primary issues: "simulator lock-in" and "poor policy exploration" due to the restrictive "unreasonable constraints imposed by the real world."

The paper positions itself as a diagnostic analysis of these problems and proposes an alternative paradigm, `sim2sim2real`, which aims to decouple the process of behavior generation (policy learning) from the stringent requirements of system identification and high-fidelity `sim2real` transfer. It contrasts its approach with existing policy transfer techniques like student-teacher distillation, latent-guidance, and motion imitation strategies. While these methods are effective for transferring policies across observation spaces or simulators, the paper argues they often fail when faced with significant differences in action spaces and underlying robot dynamics. By leveraging robot kinematics as a universal intermediate representation, the proposed method seeks to overcome these limitations. The work also draws inspiration from established model-based control architectures, which typically synthesize kinematically feasible solutions before refining them for dynamic feasibility, suggesting a convergence of ideas between model-free learning and classical control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation of this paper is to address the conflict that arises when applying reinforcement learning to complex robotic systems, particularly humanoids, within the current `sim2real` paradigm. While high-fidelity simulation is essential for reliable transfer to hardware, the authors contend that it often impedes the efficiency and effectiveness of policy learning itself. The key objectives and underlying motivations are detailed as follows:

#### 2.1. Diagnosing Impediments to Policy Learning within Current `sim2real`

The paper aims to diagnose and explain why existing `sim2real` practices, despite their necessity, create misaligned incentives with policy learning. Two primary scenarios are identified:

- **Simulator Lock-In:** The rigorous process of achieving reliable `sim2real` transfer requires extensive system identification and precise tuning of various simulation parameters. This includes properties like rotor inertia, joint friction, PD gains, control latency, and inertial characteristics. These identification results are often simulator-specific, meaning that re-implementing or validating them for a different simulator entails significant engineering effort. Consequently, teams become heavily invested in a single "validation sim" that has been proven to support `sim2real` transfer and integrate with hardware infrastructure. This "simulator lock-in" restricts the scope of possible research and development directions, as experimenting with alternative simulators or advanced learning environments becomes prohibitively costly. Past attempts to mitigate this rigidity through domain randomization were found to lead to overly conservative policies, reinforcing the reliance on highly tuned, single-simulator approaches.

- **Dynamic Feasibility as an Unreasonable Constraint:** Training policies directly in a high-fidelity simulator, while beneficial for zero-shot deployment, imposes dynamic feasibility as a strict constraint on policy exploration. This can hinder learning or even render tasks unsolvable, particularly for large, heavy humanoid robots. The paper illustrates this with the common RL exploration technique of sampling desired PD targets from a Gaussian distribution. For lighter robots, this leads to beneficial high-frequency "stuttering" that quickly evolves into effective locomotion. However, for larger humanoids, higher proportional-derivative (PD) gains are required to track joint positions against gravity. The authors explain that as a humanoid's size and weight increase, the required gains scale disproportionately faster than the available motor torque. This means that if gains are set appropriately for `sim2real` transfer, the exploration strategy quickly saturates the robot's torque limits, leading to poor sample efficiency and ineffective learning. Conversely, intentionally setting lower gains might facilitate better policy learning by allowing freer exploration, but it would compromise `sim2real` transfer quality. This presents a fundamental conflict between optimizing for policy learning and ensuring dynamic realism for hardware deployment.

#### 2.2. Decoupling Behavior Generation and System Identification

The overarching objective of the paper is to propose a framework that fundamentally decouples the process of generating effective robot behaviors (policy learning) from the demanding task of precisely modeling and validating robot dynamics for `sim2real` transfer (system identification). The authors state their aim to "decouple the relationship between behavior generation and system identification."

#### 2.3. Proposing a Scalable and Efficient Solution

Given the identified challenges, the paper's primary objective is to propose and demonstrate an alternative "recipe" for policy learning and transfer that:
- Scales effectively regardless of the robot's size, addressing the specific challenges faced by larger humanoids.
- Facilitates easier and more efficient policy learning by removing unnecessary dynamic constraints during early exploration.
- Significantly reduces the engineering overhead associated with evaluating and integrating new simulators and tools, thereby fostering greater flexibility and innovation.
- Ultimately, enables organizations to balance `sim2real` concerns with policy learning efforts more effectively for large humanoid robots.

The proposed "sim2sim2real" paradigm is presented as a means to achieve these objectives by treating robot kinematics as the sole shared design constraint for policy transfer, allowing for specialized simulation environments for different stages of development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed solution to the identified challenges is a `sim2sim2real` paradigm that separates the initial policy learning process from the high-fidelity `sim2real` validation. This paradigm introduces an intermediate transfer step between two distinct simulation environments before final deployment on hardware. The core idea is to leverage the robot’s kinematics as a common ground for policy transfer, enabling specialized environments for different tasks.

#### 3.1. Conceptual Framework: `sim2sim2real`

The methodology formally defines two types of simulation environments:
- **Support Simulation:** An idealized environment designed specifically to facilitate efficient policy learning. This simulation may simplify robot dynamics (e.g., lower gains, no system latency, idealized actuators, reduced order model) to allow for broader and more effective policy exploration, unconstrained by the strict dynamic feasibility requirements of the real world.
- **Validation Simulation:** A high-fidelity environment meticulously tuned through extensive system identification to accurately match the robot's forward dynamics. This simulation is critical for reliable zero-shot `sim2real` transfer and often integrates with hardware-in-the-loop deployment infrastructure.

The `sim2sim2real` paradigm proceeds in three main stages:

1. **Initial Policy Learning in Support Simulation:** A "teacher policy" is trained in the support simulation. This phase focuses purely on behavior generation, allowing the policy to learn desired motions in a simplified, learning-friendly setting.
2. **Concurrent Forward Model Training:** Simultaneously with the teacher policy, a "forward model" is trained within the support simulation. This model learns to predict the robot's *next kinematic state* (e.g., joint positions, joint velocities, body poses, contact states) based on the current observations and actions. The key aspect here is that kinematics, represented by the forward model, serve as the shared ground truth across different dynamic models.
3. **Kinematically-Guided Policy Transfer to Validation Simulation:** Once the teacher policy and forward model have converged in the support simulation, the learned behavior is transferred. The forward model, now "frozen," is used to generate kinematic reference trajectories. A "student policy" is then trained in the high-fidelity validation simulation. This student policy receives imitation rewards based on its ability to track the kinematic references provided by the forward model. This process implicitly embeds the kinematically feasible behaviors learned in the support simulation into the dynamically realistic context of the validation simulation, overcoming differences in action spaces and robot dynamics.
4. **Zero-Shot Hardware Deployment:** The student policy, having been refined in the high-fidelity validation simulation, is then deployed zero-shot onto the physical robot.

#### 3.2. Case Study Implementation with Apollo Humanoid Robot

To demonstrate this paradigm, a case study was conducted using the Apptronik Apollo humanoid robot.

- **Support Simulation (IsaacLab):**
 - **Robot Model:** A reduced-order, low-fidelity model of the Apollo humanoid was used. This model simplified the robot's closed-loop kinematics.
 - **Action Space:** The policy's actions were defined as offsets fed to a low-gain PD controller, a configuration noted as favorable for learning locomotion policies.
 - **Dynamic Simplifications:** To further facilitate learning, the simulation omitted modeling of joint friction, system latency, joint torque limits, and joint velocity limits. This created an idealized environment for behavior generation.
 - **Observation Space:** The policy's observations (`o_t`) included standard components like base velocity, angular velocity, estimated gravity vector, command velocities, command height, command gait frequency, joint positions and velocities, previous action, and the pose of each body link in the root frame.
 - **Policy Training:** The teacher policy was trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), specifically an implementation based on RSL-RL.
 - **Forward Model Training:** The forward model observed the same state as the actor and was trained concurrently to estimate the next kinematic state and contact state (`hat{o}_{t+1} = [hat{q}, hat{dot{q}}, hat{r}_{body}, hat{C}_{foot}]`). Training involved minimizing the expected sum of L1 and L2 losses, a common practice for penalizing both large and small estimation errors. The critic network for the policy received additional privileged information (e.g., ground reaction forces) not directly used for policy transfer.

- **Validation Simulation (MuJoCo):**
 - **Robot Model:** A full closed-loop kinematic model of the Apollo robot was used in MuJoCo. This model was extensively validated against real hardware to ensure high fidelity.
 - **Control:** The MuJoCo model translated reduced-order joint targets (from the learned policy) into full-order joint targets, which were then fed to a high-gain PD controller. These gains were identical to those used during actual hardware deployment.
 - **Simulator Selection Rationale:** MuJoCo was chosen as the validation simulator due to its established cross-platform compatibility, reduced computational requirements, ease of use, and straightforward integration with existing tools and infrastructure. The paper notes that while MuJoCo currently lacks some advanced RL features compared to IsaacLab, its robust `sim2real` validation history made it suitable for the deployment stage.
 - **Policy Transfer:** To transfer the learned behaviors from IsaacLab to MuJoCo, a new RL policy was trained in MuJoCo using state-based imitation rewards. The crucial aspect was that the reference state for this imitation was generated by evaluating the *frozen forward model* that was previously trained in IsaacLab. This effectively guided the MuJoCo policy to replicate the kinematic behaviors learned in the simplified environment, but within the constraints of the dynamically accurate model.
 - **Observation Space:** The student policy's observation space in MuJoCo was similar to the teacher policy but excluded base linear velocity and incorporated sinusoidal phase variables. The critic could access additional relevant information, such as feet air time or actuator output.

This methodology demonstrates how the `sim2sim2real` paradigm enables the advantages of a simplified learning environment (IsaacLab) to be leveraged for behavior generation, with the behaviors then adapted to a high-fidelity, hardware-validated environment (MuJoCo) via kinematic guidance, prior to zero-shot deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a position paper presenting a diagnosis and a proposed solution with an accompanying case study, the paper's "findings" primarily revolve around the feasibility and utility of the `sim2sim2real` paradigm rather than comparative empirical performance metrics against alternative methods. The main results demonstrated are the successful implementation and the implications for addressing the identified problems.

#### 4.1. Demonstrated Feasibility of the `sim2sim2real` Paradigm

The central finding is the successful application of the proposed `sim2sim2real` paradigm to the Apptronik Apollo humanoid robot.
- A locomotion policy was successfully trained in IsaacLab using a simplified, low-fidelity model of Apollo. This indicates that an idealized "support simulation" can indeed facilitate the learning of complex behaviors without the immediate constraints of hardware realism.
- The learned behavior from IsaacLab was subsequently transferred to an extensively validated, full-order MuJoCo model that incorporated different and more realistic dynamics. This transfer was achieved using the concurrently trained kinematic forward model as a reference signal via state-based imitation. This demonstrates the viability of using kinematics as an intermediate representation for transferring policies across simulators with different underlying dynamics and action spaces.
- The overall process suggests that the proposed paradigm provides a functional pipeline from idealized policy learning to a hardware-ready simulation.

#### 4.2. Efficacy of the Kinematic Forward Model

- Figure 3 illustrates the performance of the forward model, plotting estimated lower body joint positions against ground truth positions. The close alignment between the estimated and ground truth trajectories indicates that the forward model was capable of accurately learning and predicting the kinematic state of the robot within the support simulation. This accuracy is fundamental to the proposed transfer method, as the forward model serves as the kinematic reference for the student policy in the validation simulation. Its ability to capture the policy's kinematic behavior is crucial for effective transfer.

#### 4.3. Addressing Simulator Lock-In

The case study implicitly demonstrates how the `sim2sim2real` paradigm can mitigate simulator lock-in.
- By training the initial policy in IsaacLab, a simulator known for its expressiveness and ease of experimentation, the authors leveraged a tool optimized for policy learning.
- The final `sim2real` validation and deployment preparation still utilized MuJoCo, a simulator with a mature deployment infrastructure and proven `sim2real` transfer capabilities.
- This approach enables researchers to utilize the strengths of different simulators for distinct stages of development without requiring each learning environment to undergo extensive, costly `sim2real` validation. It allows for experimentation with cutting-edge learning platforms while retaining a reliable validation and deployment pipeline, thus reducing the dependency on a single "validation sim" for all development phases.

#### 4.4. Mitigating Dynamic Feasibility Constraints on Policy Learning

The methodology directly addresses the problem of dynamic feasibility hindering policy exploration, especially for large humanoids.
- Policy learning in IsaacLab was conducted under simplified dynamics, specifically using a low-gain PD controller and omitting real-world constraints like joint friction, latency, and torque limits. This allowed the policy to explore a broader range of motions and converge more efficiently without encountering torque saturation issues that would plague a large humanoid in a high-fidelity simulation.
- The dynamic feasibility concerns were then addressed during the `sim2sim` transfer to MuJoCo. Here, the student policy learned to track the kinematically feasible reference from the forward model *within* the realistic dynamic constraints of the MuJoCo environment (which used high gains and full-order kinematics corresponding to hardware). This separation allows for optimal conditions for both policy learning and dynamic realism.

#### 4.5. Overall Conclusion on Paradigm Viability

The paper concludes that the `sim2sim2real` paradigm represents a viable and effective alternative to the current restrictive `sim2real` approach. It demonstrates a method for synthesizing kinematic solutions in an idealized environment and then refining them to be dynamically feasible in a hardware-validated simulation, echoing established practices in model-based control. This suggests that the proposed framework offers a practical way to balance the conflicting demands of mechanical dynamics, learning dynamics, and infrastructure requirements in complex robotics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The proposed `sim2sim2real` paradigm carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of reinforcement learning for robotics, particularly for complex systems like humanoids.

**Decoupling of Development Concerns:** The primary significance lies in the decoupling of behavior generation (policy learning) from the rigorous demands of `sim2real` validation. This separation allows researchers and engineers to optimize each stage independently. Policy learning can be conducted in environments tailored for exploration and sample efficiency, free from real-world dynamic constraints, while `sim2real` validation can occur in highly accurate simulations focused solely on transfer fidelity. This modularity could lead to more efficient workflows and faster iteration cycles in robotic development.

**Enhanced Policy Learning Efficiency:** By removing restrictive dynamic feasibility constraints during the initial learning phase, the `sim2sim2real` approach can enable policies to explore a wider range of behaviors and learn more effectively. This is particularly impactful for large, heavy humanoid robots, where the conflict between exploration and torque limits in high-fidelity simulations has historically impeded progress. Improved learning efficiency could lead to the development of more complex, robust, and agile behaviors that were previously difficult to achieve.

**Reduction in Engineering Overhead and Simulator Lock-In:** The paradigm mitigates the issue of "simulator lock-in." It allows development teams to leverage various simulation tools, each optimized for a specific purpose (e.g., IsaacLab for rapid learning experimentation, MuJoCo for validated deployment), without the extensive cost of achieving `sim2real` validation for every single simulator. This flexibility reduces the engineering overhead associated with system identification and parameter tuning for every new policy or environment change, freeing up resources for more innovation.

**Scalability for Complex Robotic Systems:** The method directly addresses the scalability challenges associated with applying RL to larger and more dynamically complex robots. By using kinematics as a common denominator for transfer, it offers a principled way to manage the increased dynamic complexities and constraints that emerge with robot size, potentially accelerating the development of humanoids capable of more sophisticated tasks.

**Fostering Tool Interoperability and Innovation:** The `sim2sim2real` approach encourages the use of diverse simulation environments and learning frameworks. This promotes interoperability between different tools and allows the robotics community to adopt cutting-edge techniques from various domains (e.g., advanced physics engines, machine learning libraries) without being constrained by the `sim2real` capabilities of a single, monolithic simulation platform.

**Bridging Model-Free and Model-Based Control:** By explicitly drawing inspiration from model-based control architectures, which often first synthesize kinematically feasible solutions before refining them for dynamic feasibility, the paper highlights a potential convergence between model-free reinforcement learning and classical control. This integration of principles could lead to hybrid approaches that leverage the strengths of both paradigms for more robust and performant robot control.

In summary, the `sim2sim2real` paradigm proposed in this paper represents a significant conceptual shift in how `sim2real` challenges are approached in reinforcement learning for robotics. By strategically separating policy learning from dynamic validation through a kinematic intermediate, it offers a pathway to more efficient, scalable, and flexible development of advanced robotic capabilities, particularly for large humanoid platforms.
