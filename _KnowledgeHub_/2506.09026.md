# e3: Learning to Explore Enables Extrapolation of Test-Time Compute for LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> The e3 training recipe enables Large Language Models to truly extrapolate their reasoning performance by effectively utilizing increased test-time computation. A 1.7B parameter model trained with e3 outperforms much larger models in the extrapolation regime on mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Most existing Large Language Models (LLMs) trained for reasoning do not effectively extrapolate their performance, showing diminishing returns and plateauing even when given significantly more compute at test time.
- Current methods often fail to enable LLMs to perform genuine 'in-context exploration,' such as planning or self-correction, which is needed to leverage extended inference budgets for complex problem-solving.
- Achieving true extrapolation from LLMs is hindered by training regimes that either penalize longer, exploratory traces or lead to optimization instability with overly large training budgets.

## Method

- Introduces `e3` (in-context exploration enables extrapolation), a novel training recipe designed to train LLMs for effective ==in-context exploration==.
- Leverages the chaining of ==asymmetric capabilities== within the model, particularly the ==verification-generation gap==, to provide a natural pathway for iterative search and refinement.
- Employs ==negative gradients in Reinforcement Learning== to incentivize longer, more exploratory traces and uses a ==coupled curriculum== that dynamically adjusts both task difficulty and training token budget.

## Results

- Most tested open-source reasoning models fail to extrapolate effectively beyond their training token budgets, showing significant performance plateaus.
- The e3-1.7B model achieves state-of-the-art performance for models under **2B parameters** on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME'25, HMMT'25) and consistently outperforms larger models (**7B, 32B**) in the extrapolation regime (16k to 32k test tokens).
- Negative gradients promote diverse responses and increase the chaining of asymmetric skills within generated traces, while the coupled curriculum effectively structures exploration, balancing training efficiency with the ability to extrapolate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The presence of asymmetric competencies, such as a verification-generation gap, within a base model is crucial for enabling effective in-context exploration.
> - Negative gradients in Reinforcement Learning are essential for driving models to explore longer, diverse reasoning paths by redirecting probability mass from incorrect, short traces to longer, exploratory ones.
> - A coupled curriculum that coordinates increases in task difficulty with an adaptive training token budget is necessary to efficiently foster and maintain in-context exploration for extrapolation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical, yet often overlooked, challenge in the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning: the ability to *extrapolate* performance by utilizing more compute at inference time. While test-time scaling (e.g., via longer Chains of Thought, CoT) has shown promise in boosting LLM performance, the authors identify a "surprising" finding that most existing reasoning models do not truly extrapolate. This means they plateau quickly, failing to improve on harder problems even when given significantly more "thinking" budget than they were trained on.

The work directly situates itself within the paradigm of **long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning**, which involves LLMs generating detailed, step-by-step solutions. This contrasts with earlier methods like majority voting, best-of-n sampling, or simple beam search, by emphasizing the internal, free-form self-correction and iterative refinement within a single, extended generation process. Recent trends, such as DeepSeek-R1, OpenThinker, and OpenAI's O1 system, have pushed the boundaries of long CoT reasoning, often leveraging reinforcement learning (RL) or supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on expert-generated traces. This paper builds upon this foundation but critically distinguishes itself by focusing on the *mechanism* that enables *true extrapolation*, rather than just achieving high performance at a fixed, long inference budget.

The paper enters a nuanced debate within the RL for LLMs community: whether RL primarily "sharpens" (i.e., optimizes the likelihood of already good traces sampled from the base model, essentially acting as a form of implicit prompt tuning or distillation) or genuinely "explores" (i.e., discovers novel, longer reasoning paths not initially prevalent in the base model). The authors argue that while sharpening might be common in certain training regimes, their e3 recipe specifically pushes RL towards **structured exploration** by chaining capabilities, leading to the discovery of new, effective solutions. This stance challenges the notion that RL's benefits are limited to merely distilling or refining existing capabilities.

Furthermore, the paper intersects with research on **curriculum learning** in RL for LLMs. Prior works have explored curricula for problem difficulty or output length to improve training efficiency or performance. This paper, however, identifies the insufficiency of these isolated approaches and proposes a novel **coupled curriculum** over *both* data mixture and training token budget. This coordinated approach is presented as essential for fostering the specific type of in-context exploration required for extrapolation, addressing the pitfalls of training on static budgets or data mixtures (e.g., short budgets killing exploration, large budgets leading to optimization instability or over-exploratory behaviors).

Finally, the paper delves into the mechanics of RL, particularly the role of **negative gradients**. While exploration is a known benefit of RL, this paper mechanistically shows how negative gradients, derived from incorrect traces, drive the model to explore by extending responses and chaining asymmetric skills. This provides a deeper understanding of RL dynamics in the context of LLM reasoning, moving beyond high-level observations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable Large Language Models (LLMs) to **extrapolate test-time compute**, meaning their performance continues to improve on increasingly difficult problems as they are given longer "thinking" budgets during inference, even beyond the maximum token budget they were trained on. This represents the "true promise" of test-time scaling, where an LLM can genuinely "think" longer, akin to a human problem-solver, to discover more accurate and complex solutions.

The primary motivations underpinning this objective are:

- **Failure of Current Reasoning Models:** The authors observe that despite advancements in post-training techniques like Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for long output lengths, most existing reasoning models **do not extrapolate well**. Their performance gains diminish significantly when test-time compute scales beyond their training budget, indicating a fundamental limitation in their ability to leverage extended "thought" processes effectively. This is explicitly demonstrated in Figure 2, showing performance plateaus for various open-source models.
- **The Need for In-Context Exploration:** To achieve true extrapolation, LLMs must learn to implement "algorithmic procedures" internally, such as planning, self-reflection, backtracking, or generating and verifying multiple hypotheses. The authors posit that the key to unlocking this capability is training the LLM to perform **in-context exploration**. This involves the model learning to effectively spend its test-time budget by chaining relevant operations and systematically searching through reasoning paths.
- **Improving Generalization to Unseen Problems:** Beyond extrapolation, learning to explore in-context is expected to improve generalization to unseen or out-of-distribution problems, even under the original training compute budget. This highlights a broader benefit of fostering robust exploratory behaviors within the model.
- **Efficiency and Practicality:** If smaller LLMs can effectively extrapolate, it could reduce the need for extremely large models for complex reasoning tasks, offering a more efficient and practical path for deployment. The paper aims to demonstrate that a 1.7B parameter model, when trained with their recipe, can outperform significantly larger models (e.g., 7B/32B) in the extrapolation regime.

In essence, the paper seeks to fundamentally change how LLMs leverage extended inference time, shifting from mere length generation to intelligent, structured exploration that unlocks superior reasoning capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is the development of `e3` (in-context **e**xploration **e**nables **e**xtrapolation), a novel recipe designed to train Large Language Models (LLMs) to perform effective in-context exploration and, consequently, enable true extrapolation of test-time compute. The methodology revolves around three key ingredients:

1. **Chaining Asymmetric Capabilities:**
 - **Concept:** The authors hypothesize that LLMs can only learn to explore effectively when different "skills" within their output trace exhibit **asymmetric competence**. This means the model is much better at one skill (e.g., verifying an answer) than another (e.g., generating a correct answer from scratch). This difference, termed the "verification-generation gap" (VG Gap) in their key example, provides a natural pathway for implementing in-context search. If verification is easy but generation is hard, the model can iteratively generate a hypothesis, verify it, and then refine or regenerate if incorrect.
 - **Formalization & Validation:** They formalize this concept using a didactic "𝑝𝑘"-model and validate its importance through experiments on two tasks:
 - **Countdown (Cdown):** A mathematical game where LLMs are highly proficient at verifying equations but less so at generating correct ones, thus exhibiting a strong VG gap.
 - **N-digit Multiplication (Mult/Mult-V):** Tested with (Mult) and without (Mult-V) an induced VG gap. Mult-V specifically fine-tunes the base model on traces from capable models that include explicit intermediate verification steps.
 - **Approach:** Show that RL training preferentially learns solutions that chain these asymmetric skills, leading to longer, more successful reasoning traces.

2. **Leveraging "Negative" Gradients in Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
 - **Concept:** A critical distinction between RL and Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) is the presence of "negative gradients" in RL. While SFT only maximizes the likelihood of correct traces (positive gradients), RL updates also push down the likelihood of *incorrect* traces (negative gradients). The authors hypothesize that these negative gradients are crucial for driving exploration.
 - **Mechanism:** When an incorrect response is sampled, the negative gradient reduces the probability of all tokens in that trace, including the End-Of-Sequence (EOS) token. This "freed up" probability mass is then repurposed to increase the likelihood of alternative tokens, specifically those that lead to *longer traces* and *chain new asymmetries* (e.g., prompting "Wait," or initiating another verification step). This leads to increased response length, more verification attempts, and greater diversity in generated responses.
 - **Validation:** Compared standard GRPO (with negative gradients) against "GRPOMask" (masking out negative gradients) on Cdown and DMath. The didactic 𝑝𝑘-model further illustrates how negative gradients reduce `p(stop)` and increase `k` (number of attempts), thereby promoting longer traces until a successful outcome is found.

3. **Coupled Curriculum Training:**
 - **Problem Addressed:** Simply training with negative gradients on a fixed, arbitrarily chosen token budget (`B_tr`) or data mixture is insufficient.
 - Too small `B_tr` (e.g., 4k tokens) penalizes longer, exploratory traces, hindering in-context exploration.
 - Too large `B_tr` (e.g., 16k tokens) introduces significant optimization challenges (high gradient variance, typical of long-horizon RL) and can lead to over-exploratory behaviors on easy problems.
 - Static data mixtures (e.g., training on all difficulties at once) can also suppress exploration, especially for harder problems.
 - **Solution: Coupled Curriculum:** The authors propose a multi-stage curriculum that **coordinates changes in both task difficulty and training token budget**.
 - **Heuristic for `B_tr,i`:** At each stage *i*, for a given dataset `D_i`, select the smallest `B_tr,i` that still encourages in-context exploration. This is formalized as choosing `B_tr,i` such that the model's performance gain when doubling the budget (`J(pi_i; D_i, 2*B)`) is still significant (e.g., `kappa * J(pi_i; D_i, B)` where `kappa > 1`, e.g., 1.2). This ensures the model is incentivized to explore longer traces, but not so long as to cause training instability.
 - **Example Curriculum:** They fine-tune Qwen3-1.7B first on *easy* problems from the DeepScaleR dataset with a smaller `B_tr` (8k tokens), then transition to *medium and hard* problems with a larger `B_tr` (16k tokens). This sequential training helps the model first learn to explore effectively on simpler tasks within a manageable budget, then gradually extends this capability to more complex problems with increased compute.

**Base Model and Datasets:** The primary base model used is **Qwen3-1.7B**. Training is performed using the **DeepScaleR** dataset, which consists of mathematical reasoning problems. For RL, they employ the **GRPO algorithm** (Generalized Relative Policy Optimization) due to its stability in long-horizon RL, along with token-level normalization. Evaluation is conducted on standard benchmarks like AIME'25 and HMMT'25.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several key findings that collectively demonstrate the efficacy of the `e3` recipe in enabling LLM extrapolation:

1. **Extrapolation Deficiency in Current Models:** The paper first empirically confirms that most existing open-source reasoning models (both SFT- and RL-trained) **fail to extrapolate effectively** beyond their training token budgets. Performance gains become minuscule when test-time compute extends from 16k to 32k tokens, indicating a significant plateau in their reasoning capabilities (Figure 2, Appendix A).

2. **Asymmetries are Prerequisite for Exploration:**
 - **Verification-Generation Gap (VG Gap):** The presence of asymmetric competence in the base model, specifically a VG gap (where verification is easier than generation), is crucial. In tasks like Countdown (Cdown), where a strong VG gap exists, RL training successfully **amplifies response length and consistently improves performance** as the training budget (`B_tr`) increases. This is accompanied by an increase in the number of "chained asymmetries" (e.g., generate-verify loops) within the trace.
 - **Lack of Asymmetry Limits Gains:** In contrast, for the Multiplication (Mult) task without an inherent VG gap, performance plateaus, and test-time lengths oscillate, showing limited benefit from increased `B_tr`. However, when a VG gap is explicitly induced (Mult-V), RL training again leads to longer traces and stronger extrapolation, confirming the necessity of asymmetry (Figure 4, Figure 15).
 - **KL Divergence Trend:** Interestingly, with asymmetries present, higher `B_tr` leads to *lower* KL divergence from the base model, suggesting better generalization (Figure 13).

3. **Negative Gradients Incentivize Structured Exploration:**
 - **Diversity and Entropy:** Negative gradients from incorrect traces demonstrably **promote diverse responses** during RL training. This is evidenced by an increase in cumulative unique attempts (on Cdown) and token entropy, and a reduction in repetitive token streams, compared to masking out negative gradients (Figure 5(d), Figure 6).
 - **Chaining Asymmetries:** Critically, negative gradients "repurpose" probability mass from short, incorrect answers to **longer traces that chain more asymmetries** (e.g., "Wait..." signals for continued thinking and verification). This leads to significantly increased response length and more verification attempts over training (Figure 5(b,c)). Without negative gradients, these metrics plateau quickly.
 - **Enhanced Extrapolation:** LLMs trained with negative gradients **extrapolate better** because the reinforcement of longer, successful, exploratory traces leads to a "rich gets richer" effect, where more in-context exploration is incentivized over time (Figure 5(a)).

4. **Coupled Curriculum is Essential for Efficient Extrapolation:**
 - **Limitations of Static Approaches:**
 - **Fixed Low `B_tr` (e.g., 4k):** While good for in-distribution performance at that specific budget, it **kills in-context exploration** by penalizing longer, potentially successful traces, leading to poor extrapolation. Models learn to stop early (Figure 8(a,c)).
 - **Fixed High `B_tr` (e.g., 16k):** Introduces **significant optimization challenges** (high gradient variance), leading to suboptimal performance even at its own training budget compared to models trained at smaller `B_tr` and extrapolated.
 - **Static Data Mixtures:** Training on a static mixture of problem difficulties (e.g., all easy+medium+hard) leads to worse out-of-distribution (OOD) extrapolation performance compared to tailored curricula (Figure 8(b,d)).
 - **Efficacy of Coupled Curriculum:** The proposed coupled curriculum, which gradually increases task difficulty alongside the training budget (`B_tr`), effectively **structures exploration**. This approach balances the need for sufficient budget to encourage chaining asymmetries with mitigating optimization challenges. The heuristic `B_tr,i` selection ensures that the chosen budget positively rewards continued exploration (Figure 9(c)).

5. **State-of-the-Art Performance for Small Models:**
 - **e3-1.7B Achievements:** The Qwen3-1.7B model fine-tuned with the `e3` recipe achieves **state-of-the-art performance** for models under 2B parameters on both AIME'25 and HMMT'25 benchmarks (Table 1).
 - **Superior Extrapolation:** Crucially, e3-1.7B consistently **outperforms larger open-source models** (including 7B and 32B models like OpenThinker-7B and s1.1-32B) in the **extrapolation regime** (from 16k up to 32k test tokens), demonstrating its unique ability to leverage extended compute (Figure 10(a,b)).
 - **Beyond Sharpening:** e3 not only attains high pass@1 scores but also significantly **improves pass@k (up to pass@32)** over the base model, indicating that it genuinely learns to discover new solutions through structured exploration rather than merely "sharpening" existing good traces (Table 1).
 - **Outperforming Prompting:** `e3` achieves this superior extrapolation **without any explicit prompting or budget forcing** (e.g., appending "Wait"), unlike other methods (Figure 10(c)).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes significant contributions to the field of Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning, particularly in understanding and enabling the effective utilization of test-time compute. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Fundamental Mechanistic Understanding of Extrapolation:** The paper provides a deep, mechanistic understanding of *why* current LLMs often fail to truly extrapolate their reasoning capabilities and *how* this limitation can be overcome. By meticulously dissecting the roles of base model asymmetries, negative gradients in RL, and curriculum design, the authors illuminate the underlying processes that govern in-context exploration and its link to extrapolation. This moves beyond empirical observations to offer principled insights.

2. **Development of an Effective Training Recipe (e3):** The `e3` recipe offers a concrete, actionable framework for training LLMs to achieve genuine extrapolation. By integrating the three key components—leveraging asymmetries, utilizing negative gradients, and employing a coupled curriculum—e3 demonstrates a powerful synergistic effect that enables smaller models to punch above their weight class. This recipe provides a valuable blueprint for future LLM training strategies.

3. **Enhancing LLM Reasoning Efficiency:** The ability of a relatively small 1.7B parameter model (e3-1.7B) to outperform significantly larger models (7B, 32B) in the critical extrapolation regime is highly impactful. This suggests that computational efficiency in reasoning can be achieved not solely by scaling model parameters but also by intelligent training methodologies that optimize the *utilization* of available compute. This could lead to more accessible and deployable high-performance reasoning LLMs, reducing the immense computational and memory footprints often associated with frontier models.

4. **Challenging the "Sharpening" Narrative of RL for LLMs:** The paper directly engages with and provides empirical evidence against the prevalent view that RL primarily "sharpens" (i.e., refines existing good solutions) rather than truly "explores" in LLMs. By showing that `e3` fosters the chaining of new asymmetries and an increase in response length, the authors demonstrate a mode of RL training that facilitates structured exploration and the discovery of novel reasoning paths, leading to improvements in pass@k beyond simple distillation. This broadens our understanding of RL's potential in the LLM domain.

5. **Guiding Future Research Directions:** The paper explicitly outlines several compelling avenues for future work, including:
 - **Differentiating RL Regimes:** A deeper theoretical and empirical study into the conditions that lead RL training to either a "sharpening" or "structured exploration" regime.
 - **Connecting to Dense Rewards:** Exploring the relationship between coupled curricula and dense progress rewards, suggesting that the success of the former might foreshadow broader success for dense rewards in large-scale RL for LLMs.
 - **Identifying New Asymmetries:** Encouraging research into identifying and imbuing base models with other types of asymmetric competencies beyond the verification-generation gap.
 - **Necessity of Curricula for Larger Models:** Investigating if curricula remain fundamentally necessary as LLM scale increases, or if larger models intrinsically simplify these challenges.
 - **Explicit Exploration Bonuses:** Addressing current limitations like repetition bias in long traces by incorporating explicit exploration bonuses in RL to further enhance in-context search.

6. **Broader Societal Implications:** By improving the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, this work contributes to the development of more capable and intelligent AI systems. However, the authors also acknowledge the potential for unequal access to such advanced models, especially in resource-constrained environments, and emphasize the need for continued assessment of fairness, interpretability, and safety as these systems are deployed.
