# Self-Distilled Agentic Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Zhejiang University, Meituan, and Tsinghua University developed Self-Distilled Agentic Reinforcement Learning (SDAR), a method that robustly integrates on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) with reinforcement learning for multi-turn Large Language Model agents. This approach utilizes a token-level gating mechanism to selectively apply teacher guidance, achieving up to a 9.4% improvement in success rates on ALFWorld and outperforming other hybrid methods while effectively internalizing skills without needing them at inference.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) provides coarse, trajectory-level feedback, making it inefficient for long-horizon, multi-turn LLM agent tasks with sparse rewards.
- Naive on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) suffers from multi-turn instability due to compounding errors and an "asymmetric trust" issue where privileged teacher guidance can be unreliable (e.g., negative signals from imperfect skill retrieval can be detrimental).
- Existing hybrid RL-distillation methods often rely on rigid schedules or unstable updates, struggling to combine task-level optimization with dense token-level guidance robustly in multi-turn interactive settings.

## Method

- SDAR treats reinforcement learning (specifically GRPO) as the primary optimization backbone and integrates OPSD as a controlled, auxiliary objective, guided by a mixing coefficient.
- A token-level gating mechanism is introduced, which computes a trust weight `g_t` for each student-sampled token by composing the teacher-student log-probability gap `∆_t` (or student entropy `h_t`) with a sigmoid function, effectively filtering unreliable signals.
- The gate `g_t` is detached via a stop-gradient operator to ensure it acts solely as a confidence weight, modulating the OPSD signal without introducing self-referential instabilities in the gradients.

## Results

- SDAR consistently improved success rates across ALFWorld, WebShop, and Search-QA benchmarks for Qwen2.5 (3B, 7B) and Qwen3 (1.7B) models, with up to +9.4% on ALFWorld compared to pure GRPO.
- The method demonstrated effective skill internalization, often surpassing skill-augmented baselines during inference without requiring external skills, indicating robust knowledge transfer.
- SDAR exhibited strong generalization, performing particularly well with smaller models (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B on ALFWorld, 53.9%) and showed robustness to varying skill retrieval qualities, including random retrieval, due to its adaptive gating mechanism.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The privileged teacher's guidance in OPSD is often asymmetric; positive endorsements are valuable, but 'negative rejections' from lower-probability tokens can be misleading due to imperfect skill retrieval or multi-turn drift.
> - Dynamically gating token-level distillation based on the teacher-student log-probability gap or student uncertainty allows for adaptive knowledge transfer, prioritizing beneficial signals while suppressing potentially harmful ones.
> - Maintaining RL as the primary optimization objective, with OPSD providing a carefully calibrated, gated auxiliary signal, is crucial for stable and effective training of multi-turn LLM agents.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to their application in increasingly complex tasks, particularly as autonomous agents capable of interacting with environments over extended horizons. This paradigm, known as agentic post-training, involves sequential decision-making where each action influences subsequent observations.

Within this landscape, two primary training paradigms have emerged:
1. **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** This approach provides task-level optimization based on feedback from the environment or a verifier. While effective for overall task success, RL's trajectory-level reward signal offers coarse supervision, which can be inefficient for long-horizon interactions with sparse rewards.
2. **On-Policy Distillation (OPD) and On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD):** These methods provide denser, token-level guidance, often by leveraging a "teacher" model. OPSD specifically uses a teacher branch augmented with privileged, training-only context (e.g., retrieved skills) to provide this fine-grained supervision to a student policy operating on its own generated sequences.

The integration of these two complementary forces—RL for overall task optimization and OPSD for detailed token-level guidance—is a key challenge in the field. Previous attempts to combine OPSD with multi-turn agent training have encountered difficulties. The paper identifies two specific issues:
- **Multi-turn OPSD Instability:** As a student agent diverges from a teacher-supported trajectory, the compounding error can destabilize the token-level supervision, leading to unreliable signals and performance degradation. Existing approaches like Temporal Curriculum On-policy Distillation (TCOD) attempt to address this through rigid curriculum schedules, which may not be optimal.
- **Asymmetric Trust in Privileged Guidance:** The teacher in OPSD is often the same policy enhanced with privileged context, rather than an independently superior model. This means its guidance can be asymmetric; positive endorsements from skills are valuable, but "negative rejections" (where the teacher assigns lower probability to a student-sampled token) may stem from imperfect skill retrieval, utilization, or accumulating multi-turn drift, rather than a definitively incorrect student action. Directly applying such negative signals can be detrimental.

This research aims to reconcile these paradigms by addressing the identified instability and asymmetric trust issues. It positions itself as an advancement in hybrid methods that combine RL with knowledge distillation, seeking to create a more robust and effective training framework for multi-turn LLM agents compared to existing hybrid approaches like Skill-SD and RLSD, which can suffer from rigid scheduling or unstable updates.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a robust and effective method for training multi-turn LLM agents by harmoniously integrating Reinforcement Learning (RL) with On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD). The method aims to leverage the strengths of both paradigms—RL's task-level optimization and OPSD's dense token-level guidance—while mitigating their respective limitations, particularly within the challenging context of multi-turn interactions.

The motivation for this research stems from several detailed observations regarding the application of RL and OPSD to multi-turn LLM agents:

1. **Limitations of Pure RL in Long-Horizon Tasks:** While RL provides task-level optimization, its sparse, trajectory-level reward signals offer coarse supervision. For multi-turn interactions that involve long horizons and sequential decision-making, such coarse signals can make learning inefficient, particularly when a verifier provides feedback only at the end of a long sequence of actions. This necessitates more granular guidance.

2. **Challenges of Naive OPSD in Multi-Turn Settings:**
 - **Multi-turn OPSD Instability:** In multi-turn scenarios, agents generate sequences of actions that build upon each other. If a student agent deviates even slightly from the teacher-supported path, subsequent token-level supervision can become increasingly unreliable due to compounding errors. This can lead to a divergence between the student and teacher policies (surging KL divergence) and a catastrophic decline in task performance. The paper illustrates this with experimental evidence showing the degradation of "Multi-turn OPSD." Existing methods like TCOD attempt to address this with rigid curriculum learning, which may not be flexible enough for dynamic environments.
 - **Asymmetric Trust in Privileged Guidance:** OPSD often uses a teacher that is the same policy but augmented with privileged, training-only context (e.g., retrieved skills or reference answers). This creates an inherent asymmetry in the reliability of its token-level guidance.
 - **Positive Endorsement:** When the privileged teacher assigns a higher probability to a student-sampled token (a "positive gap"), it acts as an endorsement, suggesting the student can internalize a beneficial on-policy behavior. This is valuable for distillation.
 - **Negative Rejection:** Conversely, when the teacher assigns a lower probability to a student-sampled token (a "negative gap"), this signal should be interpreted with caution. A negative gap might correctly indicate a suboptimal token, but in skill-conditioned OPSD, it could also arise from several factors:
 - **Skill Quality:** Retrieved skills might be irrelevant, incomplete, or redundant.
 - **Skill Utilization:** The teacher model might fail to effectively ground even relevant skills into reliable token-level preferences.
 - **Multi-turn Drift:** The teacher-student gap tends to widen over turns, amplifying early mismatches.
 The paper's analysis shows that negative-gap tokens constitute over 50% of all tokens, highlighting the prevalence and importance of this issue. Naive distillation would apply equal weight to these potentially misleading negative signals, thus degrading performance.

3. **Limitations of Existing Hybrid RL-Distillation Methods:** While some methods (e.g., RLSD, Skill-SD, HDPO) combine RL with distillation, they often suffer from issues such as:
 - **Rigid Scheduling:** Relying on hand-crafted temporal schedules or thresholds for distillation.
 - **Unstable Updates:** Direct use of self-divergence or unbounded distillation gradients can lead to instability, especially when the teacher-student mismatch is large, potentially overwhelming the primary RL objective.

These observations motivate a paradigm shift: Reinforcement Learning should be the primary optimization backbone, with OPSD playing a carefully controlled auxiliary role. The core motivation is to devise a mechanism that allows OPSD to provide beneficial token-level supervision dynamically and selectively, filtering out unreliable or detrimental signals, thereby achieving stable and consistent performance gains without compromising the integrity of the RL objective.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Self-Distilled Agentic Reinforcement Learning (SDAR), is designed to integrate On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) as a controlled auxiliary objective within a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework, specifically Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

#### 3.1. Problem Setup

The agent interacts with an environment over multiple turns, receiving observation `o_k` at turn `k` and generating response `a_k`. Responses are flattened into a single token sequence `y = (y_1..., y_T) ∼ π_θ(· | x)`. The student policy `π_θ` generates tokens based on self-student context `s_t = (x, y_<t)`. For the teacher branch, a self-teacher context `s+_t = (x, c+, y_<t)` is used, where `c+` represents privileged training-only context (e.g., retrieved skills).

#### 3.2. Skills Retrieval

SDAR incorporates skill retrieval to provide `c+` to the teacher. Four strategies are implemented and evaluated for their impact on robustness:
- **UCB Retrieval:** Treats skill selection as a multi-armed bandit problem, choosing skills based on their average past reward `r(e)` and an Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) term for exploration.
- **Keyword Matching (KM):** Identifies task scenarios via keywords in the task description to retrieve a relevant skill file.
- **Full Retrieval:** All available skills are provided as context.
- **Random Retrieval:** Skills are selected randomly, representing the lowest quality of retrieval.

#### 3.3. Optimization Goals

SDAR defines its overall training objective as a combination of the GRPO loss and a self-distillation objective:
`L(θ) = L_GRPO(θ) + λ_SDAR · L_SDAR(θ)`
where `λ_SDAR` is a mixing coefficient. Masked token averaging `Agg(z_1:T)` is used for sequence-level losses.

##### 3.3.1. RL Optimization (GRPO)

For each input `x`, GRPO samples `G` responses. Environment rewards `R(x, y^(i))` are obtained for each trajectory `y^(i)`. A group-relative advantage `A^(i)` is computed by normalizing the rewards. The GRPO objective `L_GRPO(θ)` is based on a clipped surrogate loss, using importance sampling ratios `r^(i)_t` and a KL penalty toward a reference policy `π_ref`.

##### 3.3.2. OPSD Optimization

The OPSD objective leverages the difference between the teacher's and student's conditional token distributions.
- The per-token reverse KL divergence `D^(t)_RKL = D_KL(π_θ(· | s_t) ∥ π_T(· | s+_t))` is used.
- To avoid costly full-vocabulary summation, a single-sample estimate on the student-sampled token `y_t` is used, yielding the Teacher-Student log-probability gap `∆_t = log π_T(y_t | s+_t) − log π_θ(y_t | s_t)`. This `∆_t` is the basis for the token-level gating.

#### 3.4. Token-Level Gating (SDAR's Core Contribution)

The central idea of SDAR is to transform the privileged teacher guidance (`∆_t`) into a token-level trust weight `g_t ∈`. This gate `g_t` modulates the OPSD signal for each student-sampled token.
- `∆_t` is detached using a stop-gradient operator `sg(·)` to prevent gradients from flowing through the gate itself, ensuring it acts solely as a confidence weight.
- The raw `∆_t` (or student entropy `h_t`) is composed with the logistic sigmoid function `σ` and a sharpness parameter `β > 0` to ensure the gate is smooth, differentiable, and bounded between 0 and 1.

Three complementary gating strategies are proposed:
1. **Entropy gating:** `g_t = σ(β h_t)` – targets tokens where the student is most uncertain (high entropy).
2. **Gap gating:** `g_t = σ(β ∆_t)` – assigns larger weights to tokens where the privileged teacher strongly endorses the student's choice (positive `∆_t`) and softly attenuates negative `∆_t` signals.
3. **Soft-OR gating:** `g_t = σ(β [1 − (1 − h_t)(1 − ∆_t)])` – combines student uncertainty and the teacher-student gap.

The token-level OPSD loss `ℓ_SDAR_t` is then defined as `g_t · (log π+_θ(y_t | s+_t) − log π_θ(y_t | s_t))`. The `L_SDAR` objective is the averaged `ℓ_SDAR_t` over trajectories.

#### 3.5. Theoretical Properties (from Appendix A)

The paper provides theoretical justifications for its design:
- **Equivalent Weighted Likelihood Form:** `L_SDAR` is equivalent to maximizing a token-weighted log-likelihood objective where `g_t` is the weight, effectively making `C - Agg(g_t log π_θ(y_t | s_t))` the objective.
- **Gradient Form:** The gradient of `L_SDAR` is `−Agg(g_t∇_θ log π_θ(y_t | s_t))`, showing `g_t` directly modulates the gradient.
- **Monotonicity and Smoothness:** The gate `g_t` is strictly increasing in `∆_t`, implying larger discrepancies receive stronger weights, facilitating an online token-level curriculum. Its derivative is bounded `∈ (0, β/4]`, preventing gradient explosion.
- **Bounded Auxiliary Gradient:** The gate ensures that the auxiliary gradient cannot exceed the magnitude of the unweighted likelihood gradient, promoting stability.
- **Effect of Not Detaching the Gate:** Without `sg(·)`, a self-referential coupling term is introduced, `∇_θ ˜ℓ_t = −[g_t + β∆_t g_t(1 − g_t)]∇_θ log π_θ(y_t | s_t)`, which can lead to unstable updates.

This methodology positions SDAR to leverage the benefits of OPSD's dense supervision while preventing its inherent instabilities and effectively handling the asymmetric reliability of privileged teacher signals.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across diverse benchmarks and model scales demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of SDAR.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

SDAR exhibited consistent improvements across the Qwen2.5 (3B, 7B) and Qwen3 (1.7B) model families on ALFWorld, WebShop, and Search-QA.
- **Substantial Gains over GRPO:** For Qwen2.5-3B, SDAR improved success rates by +9.4% on ALFWorld (84.4% vs. 75.0%), +7.0% on Search-QA, and +4.7% on WebShop-Acc, with similar trends for the 7B model.
- **Stability:** Unlike naive GRPO+OPSD, which demonstrated catastrophic collapse (e.g., near-zero on Search-QA for OPSD alone, or severe degradation on Qwen3-1.7B for GRPO+OPSD), SDAR maintained stable optimization and consistent gains.
- **Outperformance of Hybrid Baselines:** SDAR consistently outperformed other RL–OPSD hybrid methods such as Skill-SD and RLSD across all tested model scales and benchmarks. For instance, on ALFWorld-3B, SDAR achieved 84.4%, surpassing Skill-SD (73.4%) and RLSD (79.7%).

#### 4.2. Skills Internalization

SDAR demonstrated effective internalization of privileged knowledge:
- **Inference Without Skills:** SDAR did not require external skills during inference. Nevertheless, it often surpassed skill-augmented baselines (e.g., Skill-GRPO*), which relied on skills during inference. For example, on ALFWorld-3B, SDAR achieved 84.4%, exceeding Skill-GRPO*'s 80.5%.
- **Avoiding Harmful Dependencies:** Baselines like Skill-GRPO showed a performance drop when skills were not provided at inference time (e.g., 60.2% vs. 80.5% on ALFWorld-3B with/without skills), sometimes even underperforming vanilla GRPO. SDAR avoided such detrimental distributional dependencies, confirming that the gated distillation genuinely transfers knowledge into the policy's parameters.

#### 4.3. Strong Generalization

SDAR exhibited enhanced generalization capabilities, particularly evident in settings where smaller models might struggle to effectively utilize retrieved skills:
- **Smaller Models:** On the Qwen3-1.7B model, where Skill-GRPO dropped to 21.1% on ALFWorld (compared to GRPO's 46.1%) and RLSD reached 42.2%, SDAR achieved the highest score of 53.9%. This suggests that SDAR's gating mechanism, by attenuating uncertain negative teacher guidance while preserving positive endorsements, provides a more robust way to incorporate privileged knowledge without sacrificing generalization.

#### 4.4. Training Dynamics

Analysis of training dynamics for Qwen2.5-7B on ALFWorld provided insights into SDAR's adaptive behavior:
- **Teacher-Student Log-Probability Gap (Figure 5, Left):** The mean Teacher-Student log-probability gap remained consistently negative throughout training, indicating that the privileged teacher often assigned lower probability to student-sampled tokens. This underscored the "asymmetric trust" issue, where naive distillation would degrade performance. Crucially, the gap steadily converged towards zero, suggesting SDAR successfully identified and emphasized beneficial teacher signals.
- **Gate Activation Ratio (Figure 5, Right):** For much of the early training, the gate activation ratio (fraction of tokens where `g_t > 0.5`) remained below 0.5. This indicated that SDAR effectively suppressed tokens carrying potentially negative or unreliable signals. As training progressed and the student policy evolved, the ratio gradually increased, reflecting a growing regime of constructive teacher guidance.

#### 4.5. Robust Analysis

SDAR's performance was evaluated across varying skill retrieval qualities (UCB, Keyword Matching, Full, Random retrieval) to assess its robustness (Table 2):
- **Consistent Outperformance:** All four retrieval strategies, including Random Retrieval, consistently outperformed the pure GRPO baseline (without OPSD). Random Retrieval still yielded gains (e.g., +1.9% on ALFWorld, +1.0% on WebShop-Acc).
- **Amplified Benefits:** Higher-quality retrieval strategies like Keyword Matching further amplified these benefits, achieving significant gains (e.g., +4.7% on ALFWorld, +10.2% on WebShop-Acc).
- **Gating Mechanism's Role:** These results suggest that SDAR's gains stem primarily from its gated distillation, which filters out noise from low-quality skills and prioritizes beneficial signals, rather than relying solely on the fidelity of skill retrieval.

#### 4.6. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies provided further validation for SDAR's design choices:
- **Token-Level Gating Strategy (Figure 6):** Teacher-Student Gap gating consistently outperformed entropy and soft-OR gating, achieving higher asymptotic success rates and steeper performance climbs. This was attributed to the directness of the gap as an importance signal.
- **Sharpness β (Figure 7):** An optimal `β` value (e.g., 5) effectively balanced the risks of indiscriminate distillation (low `β`, leading to instability) and overly strict binarization (high `β`, losing smooth modulation).
- **Distillation Coefficient λ (Figure 8):** A moderate `λ` (e.g., 0.01) was optimal, providing a steady complementary signal without dominating the primary RL objective. Overly large `λ` led to performance decline, while too small `λ` provided insufficient corrective pressure.
- **Distillation Objective (Figure 9):** Reverse KL divergence outperformed forward KL and Jensen–Shannon divergence. This was consistent with reverse KL's mode-seeking property, encouraging the student to focus on teacher-supported modes, which is beneficial when teacher signals are partial or "weak."

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents SDAR as a method that addresses known challenges in integrating token-level distillation with trajectory-level reinforcement learning for multi-turn LLM agents. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

1. **Stable Integration of RL and OPSD:** SDAR provides a framework that successfully reconciles the coarse, task-level supervision of RL with the dense, token-level guidance of OPSD. This integration is crucial for training agents that need to make sequential decisions over long horizons, where both overall task success and fine-grained action quality matter.
2. **Addressing Instability in Multi-Turn Settings:** The paper directly tackles the problem of multi-turn OPSD instability, which has been a barrier to effectively applying self-distillation in dynamic, interactive environments. By treating OPSD as a gated auxiliary objective and preserving RL as the primary backbone, SDAR avoids the catastrophic performance degradation observed in naive combinations.
3. **Adaptive and Robust Guidance:** The token-level gating mechanism, particularly gap gating, allows for an adaptive approach to privileged teacher guidance. This mechanism filters out potentially unreliable or negative teacher signals while amplifying beneficial endorsements. This robustness is critical when the "teacher" is derived from privileged context that may not always be perfect, as evidenced by SDAR's strong performance even with low-quality skill retrieval.
4. **Enhanced Skill Internalization and Generalization:** The ability of SDAR to surpass skill-augmented baselines without requiring external skills during inference suggests a deeper internalization of knowledge into the policy parameters. This is significant for developing agents that are self-sufficient and can generalize well beyond their training conditions, rather than superficially relying on external prompts.
5. **Practical Applicability:** Demonstrated improvements across diverse, challenging benchmarks (ALFWorld, WebShop, Search-QA) and various model scales (Qwen2.5-3B, 7B, Qwen3-1.7B) indicate the method's practical utility for a range of LLM agent applications. The careful calibration of hyperparameters (e.g., `λ`, `β`) and the choice of distillation objective contribute to its robust performance.

The potential impact of SDAR extends to advancing the capabilities of LLM-based agents in complex, interactive environments. By providing a more stable, adaptive, and effective method for incorporating expert knowledge and fine-grained supervision, SDAR could enable the development of agents with enhanced decision-making, reasoning, and task completion abilities across various domains, including embodied AI, web navigation, and advanced question answering.
