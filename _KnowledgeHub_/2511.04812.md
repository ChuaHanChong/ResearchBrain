# Multimodal Diffusion Forcing for Forceful Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Multimodal Diffusion Forcing (MDF) is a unified diffusion-based framework designed to learn the joint distribution of multimodal robot trajectories, encompassing vision, force, and actions. The method demonstrates robust performance in contact-rich manipulation tasks and enables flexible inference by conditioning on arbitrary subsets of modalities for diverse robotic functionalities.

## Problem

- Existing imitation learning approaches often model multimodal robot observations in isolation, overlooking the complex temporal and cross-modal dependencies critical for tasks like forceful manipulation.
- Current robotic learning models typically require a fixed set of input modalities, limiting adaptability to varying sensor availability or task requirements and lacking inherent robustness to noisy or partial sensor data.
- Many unified behavior learning frameworks, including diffusion models, do not fully generalize to rich multimodal sequences involving detailed geometric information (e.g., point clouds) or physical interactions (e.g., force), nor do they offer robustness to sensory noise or flexibility in configurable history lengths.

## Method

- **MDF** employs a generalized masked training scheme that uses a 2D Time-Modality Noise Level Matrix, treating Gaussian noise as a continuous mask to allow the model to learn to denoise and reason over partially corrupted multimodal inputs across time and specific modalities.
- The framework utilizes a bi-level diffusion architecture, combining pretrained diffusion-based ==point cloud== autoencoders for high-dimensional visual data with a latent diffusion ==transformer== to model temporal dependencies and ==cross-modal== interactions in a low-dimensional latent space.
- **MDF** provides flexible inference capabilities by configuring the 2D noise level matrix to define condition and target blocks, enabling a single model to act as a policy, world action model, inverse dynamics model, or fine-grained anomaly detector based on specific task requirements and available sensor inputs.

## Results

- MDF-Policy achieved performance on par with or superior to specialized architectures like DP3 (e.g., **100%** success rate on Nut Thread vs. DP3's **96%**) and consistently outperformed other unified baselines like UWM.
- The framework demonstrated superior robustness to simulated sensor noise (e.g., a **4%** performance drop in Nut Thread compared to DP3's **18%**) and achieved up to **70%** higher success rates than DP3 in real-world tasks with noisier point clouds.
- MDF's anomaly localization method, MDF-sweeping, achieved the highest accuracy in identifying both the timestep and modality of anomalous events (e.g., **73.8%** for wrench time and **66.0%** for wrench time-modality localization), outperforming baselines like ImDiffusion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling the joint distribution of multimodal robot trajectories via a continuous noise-as-masking diffusion process enhances robustness to sensor noise, partial observations, and enables effective reasoning over missing data.
> - A unified framework can achieve performance comparable to specialized task-specific models in challenging contact-rich manipulation domains while offering greater versatility in deployment, such as dynamic adjustment of history length and input modalities.
> - Incorporating 'privileged information' like full point clouds during training, even when unavailable at inference, can significantly improve the model's ability to infer object interactions from partial observations and increase overall robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotics research, particularly in manipulation, often deals with complex, contact-rich tasks that necessitate the integration of diverse sensory information. Humans naturally process visual, auditory, tactile, and proprioceptive cues to interact with their environment, adjusting motions based on perceived resistance or alignment. Similarly, robots require sophisticated reasoning over multimodal inputs to effectively perceive object states, predict task outcomes, and react appropriately in dynamic scenarios.

Traditional imitation learning approaches typically learn direct mappings from a limited set of observations, such as RGB images, to actions. These methods frequently do not account for the intricate interplay between different modalities, including sensory inputs (e.g., vision, force, proprioception), actions, and even rewards, which are integral to comprehensive robot behavior modeling. Furthermore, many existing systems assume a fixed set of input modalities and exhibit vulnerability to partial or corrupted sensor data during inference.

The field has seen growing interest in incorporating multimodal information into robotic systems, encompassing vision, language, audio, force, and tactile signals. While some works have explored fusing multiple sensor inputs directly into policies or jointly predicting future actions and observations, these often adhere to fixed input-output structures and rely on complete, consistent sets of modalities.

Diffusion models have emerged as a prominent generative modeling technique, finding applications in various robotics domains such as policy learning, forward dynamics modeling, subgoal prediction, and joint state-action modeling. While these models are effective, many are trained for single, specialized tasks. Recent advancements like Unified World Models (UWM) and Unified Video Action (UVA) models have proposed unified frameworks for behavior learning, primarily focusing on video and action sequences. However, these often do not fully generalize to rich multimodal sequences incorporating detailed geometric information like point clouds or physical interactions like force, and may lack robustness to sensory noise or flexibility in configurable history lengths.

Masked training strategies, where models are trained to predict missing parts of inputs, have also been explored in robotics to leverage multimodal joint learning and offer flexibility for downstream tasks. However, many current masked training methods either struggle with high-dimensional observations like point clouds or employ binary masking schemes. Binary masking limits the model's ability to reason about partially corrupted data, only considering fully clean or completely missing inputs. The concept of "noise-as-masking" in diffusion models offers a continuous masking approach, potentially enabling more robust reasoning over partially corrupted observations. This work positions itself within this context by extending diffusion forcing to a multimodal setting, aiming to overcome the aforementioned limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified framework, Multimodal Diffusion Forcing (MDF), capable of learning the joint distribution of multimodal robot trajectories. This framework is designed to move beyond simple action generation by capturing the complex interplay between various modalities over time. The motivations stem from several identified limitations in current robotic learning paradigms:

1. **Addressing the Oversight of Multimodal Interplay:** Existing imitation learning methods often treat modalities in isolation or fuse them without deeply modeling their temporal and cross-modal dependencies. However, successful robot operation in tasks like forceful manipulation necessitates understanding how actions affect force signals, how visual cues inform state estimations, and how proprioception correlates with tactile feedback. MDF aims to explicitly learn these intricate relationships.

2. **Enhancing Flexibility in Training and Inference:** Current models typically require a fixed set of input modalities, limiting their adaptability to varying sensor availability or task requirements. MDF is motivated by the need for a system that can condition on arbitrary subsets of modalities and predict the rest. This includes supporting privileged learning during training (e.g., using full point clouds in simulation even if unavailable in real-world deployment) to improve robustness by encouraging inference of unobserved information. At inference time, the goal is to enable a single model to serve diverse functionalities such as acting as a policy, a world action model, a dynamics model, or an anomaly detector.

3. **Improving Robustness to Noisy and Partial Inputs:** Robot perception systems are susceptible to noise, occlusions, and sensor failures, leading to partial or corrupted observations. Many existing methods lack inherent robustness to such imperfections. MDF seeks to build robustness by training with a continuous scale of corruption, where Gaussian noise acts as a continuous mask. This approach is intended to allow the model to reason effectively over a wide range of noisy or missing data, a capability often absent in models trained with binary masking.

4. **Achieving High Performance in Contact-Rich Forceful Manipulation:** The paper specifically targets contact-rich, forceful manipulation tasks, which demand high-precision prediction and multimodal reasoning due to the constant physical interaction and sensory feedback. The motivation is to demonstrate that a unified, flexible framework can not only offer diverse functionalities but also achieve performance comparable to, or even surpassing, specialized task-specific models in these challenging domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Multimodal Diffusion Forcing (MDF) is proposed as a unified framework to model multimodal robot trajectories using a diffusion-based approach. The core of MDF lies in its generalized masked training scheme, which utilizes a 2D Time-Modality Noise Level Matrix.

**1. Generalized Masked Training with Multi-modal Diffusion Forcing:**

- **Noise as Masking:** MDF conceptualizes diffusion as a continuous masking scheme. Unlike standard diffusion models that apply a scalar noise level uniformly, MDF introduces a **2D Time-Modality Noise Level Matrix K** (of size $T \times M$, where T is trajectory length, M is number of modalities). Each entry, $k_{t,m}$, specifies the noise level for modality *m* at timestep *t*.
- **Forward Diffusion Process:** During training, Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ and noise levels $k$ are sampled independently across modalities and timesteps. The noised version of a clean feature $x^0_{t,m}$ is generated by:
 $x^k_{t,m} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k_{t,m}}} x^0_{t,m} + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{k_{t,m}}} \epsilon_{t,m}$
 where $\epsilon_{t,m} \sim N(0, I)$.
- **Training Objective:** The model, parameterized as $\epsilon_\theta(\tau^K, K)$, takes the entire multimodal sequence (corrupted based on $K$) and the noise level matrix as input. It is trained with a standard DDPM objective to predict the noise:
 $L = E_{X, K, \epsilon} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \left\| \epsilon_{t,m} - \epsilon_\theta(\tau^K, K)_{t,m} \right\|^2 \right]$
- **Modalities:** The framework considers six modalities: partial point cloud, full point cloud (used only during training as privileged information), force, action, proprioception, and reward. Including the full point cloud during training encourages the model to infer object interactions from partial observations, analogous to occlusion reasoning or privileged learning.

**2. Architecture:**

MDF employs a bi-level diffusion framework:

- **Diffusion-based Point Cloud Autoencoders:** To handle high-dimensional, unordered point cloud data, MDF pretrains separate diffusion-based point cloud autoencoders for partial and full point clouds. These autoencoders consist of a lightweight PointNet encoder and an expressive diffusion decoder, compressing point clouds into low-dimensional latent embeddings and reconstructing them through iterative denoising. During MDF training, these tokenizers are frozen.
- **Latent Diffusion Transformer:** For sequence modeling, a latent diffusion transformer is used. At each timestep, encoded feature vectors from all modalities are concatenated with their corresponding noise-level embeddings, forming a fused multimodal feature vector. This sequence of fused vectors is then fed into the transformer, which learns bidirectional temporal dependencies and cross-modal interactions. The model operates entirely in the latent space, with point cloud decoding optional at test time for efficiency.

**3. Flexible Inference-time Capabilities:**

During inference, the 2D Noise Level Matrix $K$ is configured to define the desired conditional distribution, enabling diverse functionalities:

- **Condition Blocks:** Assigned near-zero noise levels throughout denoising to preserve their information.
- **Target Blocks:** Initialized with Gaussian noise and assigned high noise levels, then gradually denoised to zero according to a schedule.
- **Discard Blocks:** Assigned maximum noise levels, remaining noisy throughout, effectively masking them out.
- **Downstream Tasks:** This configuration allows MDF to act as:
 - **Policy:** Conditioning on past observations to predict future actions.
 - **World Action Model:** Additionally generating future states and observations.
 - **Inverse Dynamics Model:** Predicting actions from observations.
- **Practical Flexibility:** History length can be varied, and unavailable sensor modalities can be masked out while still producing coherent trajectories.
- **Fine-grained Anomaly Detection:** MDF can perform localized likelihood estimation. By selectively injecting noise into specific timesteps and modalities (Modality-Time Sweeping, Algorithm 1), it can not only detect anomalies but also precisely identify their source (timestep and modality) using the KL divergence between the true posterior and the learned reverse process.

**4. Training and Implementation Details:**

- Point cloud tokenizers are pretrained for 50k steps.
- Sequence length for MDF training is 10, with adaptable history and prediction horizons at test time.
- A 2D noise-level matrix is randomly sampled during training.
- Square Cosine Schedule with 1000 denoising steps for training; 200 DDIM steps for inference.
- CUDA Graphs are utilized to achieve 10 Hz inference speed.
- Training involves 1 million gradient steps with the Adam optimizer, batch size of 1024, and learning rate of 5e-4.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MDF was conducted on five contact-rich forceful manipulation tasks, including three simulated tasks (Nut Thread, Gear Mesh, Peg Insert) and two real-world car maintenance tasks (Oil Cap Installation, Oil Cap Removal).

**1. Performance in Action Generation:**
- **Competitive Performance:** MDF-Policy achieved performance on par with or superior to specialized architectures like DP3, despite being a unified model. For instance, MDF-Policy attained a 100% success rate on Nut Thread, surpassing DP3's 96%. It remained competitive on other benchmarks.
- **Superior to Unified Baselines:** Compared to UWM, another unified model, MDF consistently demonstrated higher success rates. This was attributed to MDF's capability for 3D geometric and force reasoning.
- **Privileged Information Benefit:** When able to leverage full point clouds (privileged information in simulation), MDF achieved 100% success on Peg Insert, demonstrating its ability to utilize richer inputs when available.
- **Role of Dynamics Modeling:** MDF-WA (World Action mode), which incorporates explicit dynamics modeling, generally performed worse than MDF-Policy. This suggests that for the evaluated short-horizon tasks, directly predicting actions without explicit future observation/state generation may be more effective.
- **Importance of Modalities:** Ablation studies showed that removing force signals (MDF-No Wrench) or restricting the model from estimating history states (MDF-Policy-No State Estimation) led to significant performance drops (6%–28%), indicating the importance of force reasoning and robust state estimation for contact-rich tasks.

**2. Robustness to Sensory Noise:**
- **Simulated Tasks:** MDF exhibited robustness to simulated sensor noise (random translations in point clouds). Under such perturbations, MDF's performance drop was minimal (e.g., 4% in Nut Thread), whereas DP3 experienced larger declines (e.g., 18%). This was attributed to MDF's training objective that involves denoising partially corrupted inputs.
- **Real-World Tasks:** In real-world Oil Cap Installation and Removal tasks, MDF achieved up to 26% higher success rates than DP3. When tested with noisier point clouds (from an alternative camera profile), MDF significantly outperformed DP3 by 23% and 70% respectively, demonstrating its superior robustness in challenging real-world conditions. An example rollout showed MDF successfully completing a task where DP3 failed due to mis-alignment with corrupted perception.

**3. Flexibility in Deployment:**
- **Variable History Length:** Unlike baselines with fixed history lengths, MDF demonstrated the ability to adapt its history length dynamically at test time, as illustrated by success rates across different history lengths (Fig. 5). This capability is presented as crucial for large-scale multi-task learning.

**4. Anomaly Localization:**
- **Fine-grained Localization:** MDF-sweeping, the proposed anomaly localization method, achieved the highest accuracy in identifying both the timestep and modality of anomalous events (e.g., 73.8% for wrench time and 66.0% for wrench time-modality localization), significantly outperforming baselines like MDF-global and ImDiffusion. ImDiffusion struggled to pinpoint the anomalous modality.
- **Real-time Detection:** Real-time experiments demonstrated MDF's ability to selectively increase anomaly scores based on the disturbance type. A visual distractor primarily increased the point cloud anomaly score, while an external physical push caused a pronounced spike in the wrench anomaly score, indicating effective modality-specific anomaly detection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Multimodal Diffusion Forcing (MDF) represents an advancement in learning robot behavior from multimodal trajectories by introducing a unified framework that extends beyond traditional action generation. Its significance lies in several key contributions:

1. **Unified and Versatile Modeling:** MDF consolidates the learning of diverse functionalities—serving as a policy, world action model, dynamics model, and fine-grained anomaly detector—within a single framework. This contrasts with specialized models, potentially streamlining development and deployment for complex robotic systems. The ability to model the joint distribution across various modalities (vision, force, action, proprioception, reward) captures a more comprehensive understanding of robot interactions and task outcomes.

2. **Enhanced Robustness:** By employing a continuous noise-as-masking training paradigm with a 2D Time-Modality Noise Level Matrix, MDF demonstrates increased robustness to noisy, partial, or missing sensory observations. This is a critical capability for real-world robot deployment, where sensor data is rarely pristine, contributing to more reliable and adaptable robotic systems.

3. **Flexibility in Deployment and Task Adaptation:** The ability to dynamically adjust input modalities, varying history lengths, and prediction horizons at inference time provides substantial flexibility. This allows the same learned model to be adapted to different sensor configurations or task requirements without retraining, which is valuable for multi-task learning and generalized robotic capabilities.

4. **Advanced Anomaly Detection:** The framework's capacity for fine-grained, modality- and timestep-specific anomaly localization offers a diagnostic tool for identifying the precise source of deviations in robot operation. This could lead to more intelligent error recovery mechanisms, improved safety, and better understanding of unexpected events in autonomous systems.

5. **Performance in Challenging Domains:** The empirical results on contact-rich, forceful manipulation tasks in both simulation and the real world suggest that MDF can achieve performance comparable to, or sometimes superior to, specialized state-of-the-art methods. This indicates that the proposed unified approach does not necessarily compromise task-specific performance while offering broader utility.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as improving training efficiency and scaling to heterogeneous datasets, suggests further avenues for enhancing the practical impact of MDF. Addressing these limitations could lead to even more broadly applicable and generalizable robotic learning systems.
