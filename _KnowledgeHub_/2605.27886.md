# Tabero: Learning Gentle Manipulation with Closed-Loop Force Feedback from Vision, Touch, and Language

> [!summary] Summary
> Nvidia and Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou) researchers introduced Tabero, a framework that provides a scalable vision-tactile-language dataset and a VTLA model suite for gentle, language-conditioned robotic manipulation. It integrates closed-loop force feedback to enable robots to modulate interaction forces based on linguistic cues.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models lack tactile feedback, preventing them from performing force-sensitive tasks requiring nuanced physical interaction.
- There is a scarcity of large-scale, high-quality datasets that align vision, tactile, and language modalities with corresponding robot actions, hindering VTLA model development.
- Current evaluation benchmarks for robotic manipulation primarily rely on task success rates, overlooking critical aspects of physical interaction quality like applied forces or gentleness.

## Method

- The **Tabero** benchmark provides a ==data-efficient pipeline== for generating diverse vision-tactile-language datasets by repurposing open-source robot trajectories through ==high-fidelity tactile simulation==.
- Tabero introduces a ==multidimensional evaluation protocol== that assesses task success alongside ==process-aware metrics== for physical interaction quality (e.g., Maximum Transient Grip Force, Average Applied Force).
- **Tabero-VTLA** proposes a novel architecture featuring a ==decoupled force-position command interface==, executed by a fixed ==hybrid controller==, to enable real-time, force-aware manipulation.

## Results

- Equipping the robot with a tactile sensor-integrated gripper led to a measurable drop in task success rates, validating the inherent challenges of gentle manipulation.
- Tabero-VTLA models with force-field tactile injection and explicit force supervision achieved firm task success rates of **0.86** and gentle success rates of **0.52**, significantly reducing average grip forces to **3.7 N** for gentle tasks.
- The model demonstrated an ability to adjust grip force based on linguistic adverbs (e.g., 'firmly' vs. 'gently') and generalized to some out-of-domain terms, showing zero-shot compositional understanding of force-related language.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating rich tactile representations (like marker motion fields) with explicit force supervision is crucial for enabling effective, gentleness-aware manipulation policies.
> - A decoupled hybrid controller, which independently manages grip force and applied force, is essential for accurate real-time force tracking and compliant physical interaction.
> - VTLA models can learn to semantically interpret linguistic adverbs related to force, allowing for intuitive and flexible control over interaction intensity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing towards enabling robots to operate autonomously in unstructured physical environments. A key aspect of this advancement involves endowing robots with the ability to perceive and interact with their surroundings through physical contact, similar to human tactile sensing.

Recent developments in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) foundation models have shown capabilities in robot control by leveraging internet-scale image-text-video data or large robot datasets of image-action pairs. However, these models predominantly lack a tactile modality. This absence limits their capacity to perform tasks requiring fine-grained contact force perception, such as gentle object handling, where excessive forces can lead to object damage or instability.

Prior attempts to integrate tactile sensing have faced challenges. Collecting real-world tactile data is often expensive, complex, and inefficient due to specialized hardware requirements. While simulation offers a scalable alternative, existing pipelines for synthetic data generation typically prioritize visual diversity and often do not provide mechanisms for generating and integrating high-fidelity tactile signals.

This work addresses the limitations of VLA models by extending them into Vision-Tactile-Language-Action (VTLA) models, specifically focusing on gentle manipulation. It aims to bridge the gap in tactile data availability and the lack of robust closed-loop force feedback mechanisms. The paper introduces a framework that not only generates scalable, multimodal datasets incorporating vision, touch, and language but also establishes a new evaluation standard that quantifies the quality of physical interaction beyond mere task success. This contributes to the development of more physically intelligent and adaptable robotic systems capable of nuanced interaction with their environment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to perform gentle, language-conditioned manipulation tasks by effectively utilizing closed-loop force feedback derived from multimodal sensory input, including vision, touch, and language. This overarching goal is motivated by several identified challenges within the current state of robotic manipulation and VLA models:

- **Absence of Tactile Feedback in VLA Models:** Existing VLA models, while advanced in visual and language understanding, generally lack tactile sensory input. This fundamental limitation prevents them from performing force-sensitive tasks that require nuanced physical interaction, such as grasping fragile objects without damage or navigating contact-rich environments with precision. The paper highlights that human manipulation heavily relies on touch for developing physical intuition, a capability currently missing in most robotic systems.
- **Scarcity of Aligned Multimodal Data:** A significant barrier to developing effective VTLA models is the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality datasets that comprehensively align vision, tactile, and language modalities with corresponding robot actions. Collecting such data in the real world is resource-intensive, requiring custom hardware and complex maintenance. Current simulation environments also fall short, focusing on visual fidelity but often neglecting the generation of realistic and diverse tactile signals.
- **Lack of Effective Closed-Loop Force Feedback Mechanisms:** Even when tactile data is available, existing VLA models often do not incorporate robust closed-loop force feedback mechanisms. This leads to learned policies that struggle to modulate interaction forces in real-time, frequently resulting in aggressive or damaging manipulation behaviors. The motivation is to move beyond open-loop control to a system where policies can dynamically adjust forces based on sensory feedback.
- **Inadequate Evaluation Protocols for Physical Interaction:** Current evaluation benchmarks for robotic manipulation primarily rely on task success rates. This outcome-oriented approach overlooks critical aspects of the manipulation process, such as the magnitude of applied forces, potential object damage, or the gentleness of interaction. There is a clear need for a standardized evaluation protocol that can quantify the quality of physical interaction, enabling a more nuanced assessment of a robot's physical intelligence.

To address these motivations, the paper introduces "Tabero," a benchmark and model suite designed to facilitate the learning and evaluation of gentle, language-conditioned robotic manipulation. The core objectives are to:
1. Develop a data-efficient pipeline to generate diverse vision-tactile-language datasets by repurposing open-source robot manipulation trajectories through high-fidelity tactile simulation.
2. Establish a multidimensional evaluation protocol that assesses task success alongside metrics for physical interaction quality (gentleness).
3. Propose "Tabero-VTLA," a novel architecture with a decoupled force-position command interface, executed by a fixed hybrid controller, to enable real-time, force-aware manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this work is divided into two main components: the Tabero benchmark and the Tabero-VTLA model suite.

#### 3.1. The Tabero Benchmark

The Tabero benchmark addresses data scarcity and evaluation limitations through a multi-pronged approach:

- **Cross-Platform Data Reutilization:** The pipeline reconstructs task environments from existing open-source datasets (e.g., those built on MuJoCo) within Isaac Lab, a high-fidelity simulator. This allows for enhanced visual rendering and the generation of tactile information. Challenges such as differing end-effector geometries (tactile gripper vs. original) and controller variations (OSC vs. PD joint control) are addressed by aligning the tool center point (TCP) and using high-gain PD controllers for trajectory replay, achieving an acceptable data retention rate.
- **Cross-Modal Data Acquisition:** A real-time, synchronized multimodal data acquisition system is built leveraging Isaac Lab's GPU-accelerated rendering. It captures:
 - **Visual information:** Synchronized RGB-D data from a wrist-mounted camera and a third-person view camera.
 - **Tactile observations:** Simulated GelSight sensors (320x240 resolution) providing RGB tactile images and an 11x9 marker grid. Tactile images are generated using the Taxim framework, simulating illumination changes via photometric stereo. Marker displacement fields model surface marker deformation on an elastic sensor, providing a raw signal for shear forces and torques, designed to be hardware-agnostic.
 - **Contact Force:** Ground-truth 6D contact forces (F_left, F_right) from simulated fingertips, allowing for computation of grip force (normal components) and applied force (sum of forces).
 - **Language instructions and executed actions:** All modalities are sampled synchronously at 20 Hz to ensure temporal alignment.
- **Enriching Tactile Force Diversity:** To overcome the limited force distributions in existing datasets (often binary gripper control), Tabero collects trajectories with varied force magnitudes. This is achieved by modulating low-level gripper impedance controller parameters, specifically the proportional (Kp) and damping (Kd) gains. Varying Kp scales steady-state forces, while adjusting Kd diversifies transient impact forces. Language instructions are augmented with adverbs like "gently" or "firmly" to semantically align with the simulated force levels. Continuous gripper aperture is logged for compatibility with force dynamics.
- **Metrics Beyond Success Rate:** To provide a comprehensive evaluation of physical interaction quality, Tabero introduces process-aware metrics:
 - **Maximum Transient Grip Force (MG):** Average of top 5% grip force values, indicating peak grasping effort.
 - **Average Grip Force (AG):** Mean grip force during contact.
 - **Maximum Transient Applied Force (MA):** Average of top 5% applied force magnitudes, characterizing impact events.
 - **Average Applied Force (AA):** Mean applied force magnitude during contact.

#### 3.2. The Tabero-VTLA Model Suite

Tabero-VTLA is an architecture designed to learn from the Tabero dataset and enable gentle manipulation:

- **Architecture:** It is built upon the Pi0 infrastructure and leverages flow matching for continuous prediction of both pose and force.
- **Tactile Modality Integration:** The model primarily uses the tactile marker motion field as the default tactile input, encoded via a specialized tokenizer. This field directly encodes normal force, shear force, and torque, offering generalization across sensor types. Other tactile injection strategies, such as tactile image adapters (processing composite historical frames) and force tokenizers (MLP for 6D force vectors), are explored for comparison.
- **Tactile Tokenizers:**
 - **Force-Field Tokenizer:** A lightweight Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) encodes the spatiotemporal sequence of 2D marker positions into conditional tokens for the transformer backbone.
 - **Tactile-Image Adapter:** A composite tactile image (historical frames from both fingers) is processed by the visual encoder, with its features interacting via cross-attention.
 - **Force Tokenizer:** A Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) encodes the 6D fingertip force vectors into a compact latent representation.
- **Force Supervision:** Flow matching supports continuous force prediction. A weighted loss function is employed where force dimensions in the action vector are upweighted by a factor λ_force to enhance the model's sensitivity to force prediction.
- **Decoupled Force–Position Hybrid Controller:** This low-level controller executes the commands from the high-level policy, providing physical compliance. It decouples grip force control from translational applied force control:
 - **Applied Force Control:** The policy outputs a desired end-effector pose (P_pred) and a target applied force (F_target_applied). An admittance-based position correction (p_cmd = p_pred + K_adm * (R_BC * F_target_applied - F_meas_applied)) is applied to the predicted position, generating a compliant target position. Differential inverse kinematics then computes joint velocity commands.
 - **Grip Force Control:** The controller implements a feedback loop for grip force. The target grip force (F_target_grip) is derived from the policy's predicted grip force (F_pred_grip) with a feedforward gain (k_ff). A correction (∆p) is applied to the gripper width (p_pred) based on the difference between target and exponentially smoothed measured grip force, using an admittance gain (k_adm_p) and a deadzone threshold. This allows the policy to specify desired grip force while the controller tracks it accurately.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the Tabero framework yielded several findings regarding data fidelity, force diversity, controller effectiveness, VTLA model performance, and semantic-force understanding.

- **Cross-Platform Data Validation:** Replication of LIBERO tasks in Isaac Lab demonstrated success rates comparable to the original MuJoCo datasets (e.g., average 0.76 in Isaac vs. 0.85 in MuJoCo). However, equipping the Franka arm with a tactile sensor-integrated gripper led to a measurable drop in success rates, particularly in tasks requiring delicate manipulation and lower grip forces (e.g., average 0.36 for T-10, 0.50 for T-25, 0.60 for T-100 compared to 0.76 for 'to Isaac'). This validated the challenge of gentle manipulation and the impact of hardware changes.

- **Tactile Data Diversity Analysis:** The data collection framework effectively expanded the distribution of interaction forces. By modulating the gripper controller's Kp and Kd parameters, distinct force magnitudes could be generated, ranging from "strong" (100% force) to "light" (25% or 10% force), contrasting with binary gripper control. Visualizations of tactile images, force fields, and contact force measurements showed clear multimodal variations aligned with the intended interaction intensity. Under the extreme 10% force setting, the data retention rate for most tasks was relatively low, indicating increased difficulty. Ablation studies on specific datasets showed that without tactile feedback, policies failed to modulate force. Furthermore, reducing force from 25% to 10% caused a substantial drop in success rate (e.g., from 0.79 to 0.52 for gentle SR), confirming the increased challenge of ultra-gentle constraints.

- **Effectiveness of Hybrid Controller:** Ablation studies on the gripper force control demonstrated that both the feedforward term (k_ff) and the admittance control component (k_adm_p) within the decoupled hybrid controller are essential for accurate force tracking. The absence of either component led to less precise force regulation or instability, highlighting the critical role of tactile-based grip force regulation for successful grasping.

- **Ablation and Comparison of VTLA Models:** A comparative analysis of different tactile injection methods and force control strategies on Dataset B revealed the following:
 - A baseline model without any tactile input ("NONE") exhibited complete failure (0.00 success rate), confirming the necessity of tactile sensing for gentle manipulation.
 - Models incorporating basic tactile tokens (e.g., "IMG" for tactile images or "FIELD" for marker fields, or "FORCE E" for encoded forces without force supervision) achieved some basic force modulation but low success rates (0.37-0.40 under firm conditions, 0.01 under gentle conditions), indicating insufficient gentleness.
 - The introduction of explicit force supervision ("FS" and combinations like "FORCE D+FS", "FORCE E+FS", "IMG+FS", "FIELD+FS") substantially improved performance, particularly under gentle conditions. For instance, "FIELD+FS" achieved a firm success rate of 0.86 and a gentle success rate of 0.52, with significantly reduced average grip forces (32.4 N for firm, 3.7 N for gentle). This demonstrated that combined rich tactile representations (like marker fields) with force supervision were most effective in achieving gentleness-aware manipulation.

- **Semantic Force Generalization:** The Tabero-VTLA model (using force-field tactile injection and force supervision) demonstrated an ability to adjust grip force based on linguistic adverbs. It applied significantly higher forces for "firmly" or "tightly" instructions compared to "gently" or "softly" instructions, while maintaining high task success. For out-of-domain adverbs like "lightly" and "forcefully," the model produced intermediate force magnitudes that aligned with their semantic meanings, though with some decrease in success rates. This indicated a degree of zero-shot compositional understanding of force-related language, with room for improvement in generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Tabero, a framework that addresses several fundamental challenges in robotic manipulation, particularly concerning gentle and force-aware interactions. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Advancing Gentle Manipulation:** The work provides a crucial step towards enabling robots to perform gentle, human-like manipulation. By integrating closed-loop force feedback from vision, touch, and language, Tabero allows robots to modulate interaction forces, which is essential for handling fragile objects, operating in human environments, and performing delicate tasks without causing damage. This capability can enhance the safety and versatility of robotic applications across various domains, including domestic assistance, healthcare, and sensitive manufacturing.

- **Addressing Multimodal Data Scarcity:** Tabero offers a scalable and data-efficient pipeline for generating diverse vision-tactile-language datasets. By repurposing existing open-source robot manipulation trajectories and leveraging high-fidelity tactile simulation, it bypasses the high cost and complexity associated with real-world tactile data collection. This approach can accelerate research and development in VTLA models by providing readily available, synchronized multimodal data, which has been a significant bottleneck.

- **Standardizing Evaluation of Interaction Quality:** The introduction of a multidimensional evaluation protocol that quantifies gentleness alongside task success is a notable contribution. Moving beyond binary success rates, these process-aware metrics (e.g., Maximum/Average Grip/Applied Force) allow for a more nuanced and physically grounded assessment of robot policies. This can foster the development of safer and more physically intelligent robots by providing clearer objectives for optimizing interaction quality.

- **Enhancing VTLA Model Capabilities:** The Tabero-VTLA model suite demonstrates effective strategies for integrating tactile feedback into existing VLA architectures. The decoupled force-position command interface, coupled with a compliant low-level controller and explicit force supervision, enables real-time, force-aware manipulation. This work provides a validated architectural approach for developing more capable foundation models that can reason about and control physical interactions.

- **Enabling Semantic-Force Understanding:** The model's ability to modulate forces based on linguistic adverbs, including some generalization to out-of-domain terms, indicates progress towards robots understanding semantic commands related to physical interaction. This can lead to more intuitive and flexible human-robot collaboration, where operators can naturally instruct robots on how gently or firmly to interact with objects.

In conclusion, Tabero offers a practical pathway towards building safer, more dexterous, and physically intelligent robots that can interact with the world in a nuanced and controlled manner. While the current framework does not jointly optimize for task success and minimal interaction force, highlighting an inherent trade-off in ultra-gentle regimes, and a move to real-world data collection is a future step, this work lays significant groundwork for future advancements in embodied AI and robot learning.
