# Guided Policy Optimization under Partial Observability

> [!summary] Summary
> The Guided Policy Optimization (GPO) framework enables efficient utilization of privileged information in partially observable reinforcement learning by co-training a guider with full state access and a learner with partial observations, ensuring the guider's policy remains imitable. This approach consistently achieved superior performance across didactic, noisy continuous control, and memory-based tasks compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning in Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) is difficult due to limited or noisy agent observations.
- Traditional Teacher-Student Learning (TSL) often faces an "imitation gap" where a privileged teacher's optimal policy is too complex or unlearnable for a partially observable student.
- Existing methods struggle to efficiently leverage privileged information or lack theoretical guarantees for learner optimality when using teacher supervision.

## Method

- Introduces Guided Policy Optimization (GPO), a framework co-training a "==guider==" with privileged information and a "learner" with partial observations.
- The guider's policy is continuously aligned with the learner's capabilities through a "backtracking" mechanism, preventing it from becoming "impossibly good."
- The learner primarily updates its policy by minimizing a distance metric to the guider's policy, effectively performing a supervised learning task.

## Results

- All GPO variants achieved optimal performance on didactic tasks like TigerDoor, empirically validating the theoretical guarantees of guided optimality.
- GPO-clip and GPO-penalty consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to various baselines across noisy continuous control tasks in Brax and memory-based tasks in POPGym.
- Incorporating an auxiliary Reinforcement Learning objective for the learner in GPO-penalty and GPO-clip generally accelerated learning and improved overall performance in complex tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The learner's policy updates can be viewed as a constrained policy mirror descent, providing theoretical guarantees for policy improvement towards optimality.
> - The framework effectively divides the learning challenge, with the guider handling complex RL gradient computations using privileged information and the learner performing a simpler supervised task.
> - Explicitly constraining the guider's policy (backtracking) is critical to prevent the "imitation gap" and ensure continuous, actionable supervision for the learner.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated efficacy in sequential decision-making problems, yet its application in complex and partially observable environments, known as Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs), remains challenging. These environments often involve agents operating with limited or noisy information, where obtaining a complete state representation is difficult.

A common scenario in RL training, particularly in areas like robotics and simulation, is the availability of "privileged information" – supplementary data that is not accessible during real-world execution but can significantly enhance learning. This privileged information might include full state observability in a simulator, noiseless sensor readings, or internal system variables.

Existing approaches to leverage such information generally fall into several categories:
- **Imitation Learning (IL) and Teacher-Student Learning (TSL):** Methods like Behavioral Cloning (BC) and policy distillation use a "teacher" (expert or policy with privileged information) to supervise a "student" (agent with partial observations). A significant challenge here is the "imitation gap" or "impossibly good" teacher problem, where the teacher's policy, optimized with full information, is too complex or unlearnable for the student, leading to suboptimal performance or hindering exploration (e.g., the teacher never performs necessary exploratory actions).
- **Hybrid RL-IL Approaches:** To address the imitation gap, some methods combine RL with IL, dynamically adjusting the weight of supervision or switching to pure RL when the teacher becomes inimitable. Other approaches modify environmental rewards based on the teacher's value function (reward shaping). These methods, however, can diminish the utility of privileged information and often lack theoretical guarantees regarding their benefits.
- **Representation Learning:** Some research aims to reconstruct privileged information from partial observations, typically by training encoders. This approach is limited by the "decodability" of the MDP, meaning it's only feasible if the privileged state can be reliably inferred from observations.
- **Asymmetric Learning:** This involves embedding supplementary information directly into the value function within an actor-critic framework, often seen in multi-agent settings where global information is used during training but local observations during execution.

This work, Guided Policy Optimization (GPO), positions itself within this landscape by drawing inspiration from Guided Policy Search (GPS) and addressing the core limitations of TSL and hybrid approaches. GPO aims to effectively utilize privileged information by co-training a "guider" (teacher) and a "learner" (student) while explicitly ensuring the guider remains "possibly good"—i.e., its policy is always aligned and imitable by the learner. This differentiates it from methods that use a static, pre-trained teacher or those that discard teacher supervision when it becomes too challenging. GPO seeks to provide theoretical guarantees for the learner's optimality under this co-training paradigm, bridging the gap between privileged information access during training and limited observation during execution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and theoretically grounded framework for effectively leveraging privileged information in Reinforcement Learning within partially observable environments. The authors aim to overcome the inherent challenges associated with learning under uncertainty while maximizing the utility of additional data often available during the training phase.

The key motivations and specific objectives driving this work are:

1. **Addressing the "Impossibly Good" Teacher Problem and Imitation Gap:** In traditional Teacher-Student Learning (TSL) or Imitation Learning (IL) setups, a teacher policy, especially one trained with privileged (full) information, can establish performance standards that are too high or behaviors that are unlearnable for a student agent operating under partial observability. This disparity, termed the "impossibly good" teacher or imitation gap, can hinder the student's learning, lead to suboptimal policies, and prevent the acquisition of necessary exploratory behaviors. The paper highlights this with didactic examples like TigerDoor, where the teacher's optimal policy (opening the correct door immediately) is unattainable for a partially observable student who first needs to perform an exploratory "listen" action. GPO seeks to train a "possibly good" teacher (guider) whose policy remains within the learner's imitable region.

2. **Improving the Utilization of Privileged Information:** Existing hybrid RL-IL methods often involve dynamically adjusting the influence of teacher supervision or incorporating it indirectly through reward shaping. While these approaches attempt to mitigate the imitation gap, they can lead to inefficient use of valuable privileged information by either discarding it or providing indirect guidance that requires additional learning. GPO aims to continuously and effectively leverage this information throughout the training process by maintaining alignment between the guider and the learner.

3. **Providing Theoretical Guarantees for Learner Optimality:** Many prior methods lack theoretical backing for the benefits of teacher supervision, especially in complex POMDP settings. A core motivation for GPO is to theoretically demonstrate that its learning scheme can enable the learner to achieve optimality comparable to direct RL. This addresses concerns about suboptimality and guarantees that the learner's policy improvement is aligned with standard RL objectives, even though it primarily learns through supervision.

4. **Simplifying Learning Under Partial Observability:** Policy gradients in partially observable environments often suffer from high variance, making direct RL training challenging. By introducing a guider that operates with privileged information, GPO aims to simplify the learning process for the partial observable learner. The guider can handle the complex RL gradient computations, while the learner is trained via an easier supervised learning task, potentially reducing variance and improving training stability.

5. **Enhancing Robustness to Noisy Observations and Memory-Based Challenges:** Real-world scenarios frequently involve noisy sensor data or require agents to synthesize information over time (memory-based tasks). GPO is motivated to provide a framework that can learn robust policies under these conditions by allowing the guider to operate on cleaner, more complete information while guiding a learner exposed to noisy or time-dependent observations.

In summary, the authors are motivated to develop a principled framework that not only harnesses the power of privileged information but also ensures this information is delivered in an actionable and beneficial way to a partially observable agent, complete with theoretical justification and empirical validation across diverse, challenging tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is Guided Policy Optimization (GPO), a framework that co-trains two agents: a **guider** ($\mu$) and a **learner** ($\pi$). The guider has access to privileged information (e.g., full state $s$), while the learner operates solely on partial observations ($o$). The central idea is to train the guider to leverage privileged information while ensuring its policy remains aligned with and imitable by the learner.

The GPO framework operates through an iterative four-step process:

1. **Data Collection:** Trajectories are collected by executing the guider's current policy, $\mu^{(k)}$. This ensures that the collected data benefits from the guider's privileged information, potentially leading to more optimal or efficient exploration.
2. **Guider Training:** The guider's policy, $\mu^{(k)}$, is updated to $\hat{\mu}^{(k)}$ by optimizing a standard Reinforcement Learning objective, $V_{\mu^{(k)}}$. This step allows the guider to learn and improve based on the environment's rewards, taking full advantage of its privileged information.
3. **Learner Training:** The learner's policy, $\pi^{(k+1)}$, is updated by minimizing a distance metric, $D(\pi, \hat{\mu}^{(k)})$, between the learner's policy and the updated guider's policy. This typically involves a form of Imitation Learning, where the learner attempts to mimic the guider's behavior. The authors specifically use KL-divergence as the distance measure.
4. **Guider Backtracking:** Before the next iteration, the guider's policy, $\mu^{(k+1)}$, is adjusted to align with the newly trained learner's policy, $\pi^{(k+1)}$. This critical step ensures that the guider does not become "impossibly good" and that its policy remains within the learner's imitable region, preventing the imitation gap.

**Theoretical Foundation:**
A key theoretical contribution is Proposition 1, which states that if the guider's policy is updated using policy mirror descent, then the learner's policy update can be viewed as a *constrained policy mirror descent*. This implies that despite the learner not directly interacting with the environment (only receiving supervision from the guider), its updates inherit properties of standard RL algorithms, such as policy improvement. This provides a theoretical basis for GPO's ability to achieve optimality comparable to direct RL training and mitigate suboptimality issues common in TSL.

**Advantages:** GPO strategically divides the learning challenge: the guider handles complex RL gradients with privileged information, while the learner performs an easier supervised learning task. This can reduce variance, simplify learning, and enhance robustness (e.g., by training the guider on clean inputs and supervising the learner with noisy ones).

The paper then introduces two practical variants of GPO:

1. **GPO-penalty:**
 - **Guider Objective:** The guider is trained using a PPO-like objective ($L_1(\mu)$) to maximize expected returns.
 - **Learner Imitation:** The learner is trained to minimize the KL-divergence ($L_2(\pi)$) between its policy and the guider's policy.
 - **Guider Backtracking (Penalty):** To ensure imitability, the guider's training objective incorporates a penalty term ($L_3(\mu)$), which is a KL-divergence between the guider's policy and the *learner's* policy. This penalty term is modulated by an adaptive coefficient $\alpha$, similar to the KL-penalty adjustment in PPO, to maintain a desired distance from the learner.
 - **Auxiliary RL for Learner:** An additional PPO-like objective ($L_4(\pi)$) is introduced for the learner, allowing it to also learn from environmental rewards directly. This is justified by Proposition 2, which suggests that under certain conditions, the behavioral policy (from the guider) closely matches the learner's policy, making sample reuse for the learner's RL objective valid. The $\alpha$ coefficient from the guider's penalty also scales this learner RL term, dynamically adjusting its influence.
 - **Shared Policy Network:** For efficiency, the guider and learner can share a single policy network. Inputs are designed to differentiate between privileged (guider: `[s, o, 1]`) and partial (learner: `[0, o, 0]`) observations.

2. **GPO-clip:**
 - **Modified Guider Objective:** Instead of an adaptive penalty, GPO-clip uses a "double-clip" function within the guider's PPO objective. This inner clipping mechanism (`clip(μ(a|s)/π(a|o), 1-δ, 1+δ)`) prevents the guider from moving too far ahead of the learner if the advantage is positive, or from falling too far behind if the advantage is negative.
 - **Masked Backtracking:** The backtracking loss for the guider is applied only when the guider's policy ratio relative to the learner's falls outside a specified `(1-δ, 1+δ)` region. This targeted application prevents unnecessary backtracking when policies are already sufficiently close.
 - **Fixed RL Coefficient:** Unlike GPO-penalty, GPO-clip uses a fixed $\alpha$ for the learner's auxiliary RL objective.
 - **Shared Policy Network:** Similar to GPO-penalty, a shared policy network with specific input formats is used.

The algorithms are implemented within a PPO framework, with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) for advantage estimation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of GPO was conducted across didactic examples, continuous control tasks with partial observability and noise, and memory-based tasks. The findings consistently indicate GPO's strong performance compared to various baselines.

#### 4.1. Didactic Tasks (TigerDoor and TigerDoor-alt)

- **Suboptimality of Direct Imitation:** As predicted, methods relying solely on direct behavioral cloning (BC) or PPO+BC converged to suboptimal solutions because the teacher's optimal policy was not imitable or failed to explore.
- **GPO Achieves Optimality:** All variants of GPO (GPO-penalty, GPO-clip, GPO-naive) successfully achieved optimal performance on both TigerDoor and TigerDoor-alt problems.
- **Validation of Theoretical Guarantees:** GPO-naive, which trains the learner purely through supervised learning (without the auxiliary RL loss), still reached optimality. This empirically validates Proposition 1, demonstrating that a guider constrained to an imitable region can effectively guide the learner to an optimal policy, even with asymmetric information.
- **Acceleration with RL:** The inclusion of direct RL training for the learner (in GPO-penalty and GPO-clip) was observed to accelerate the learning process compared to GPO-naive.

#### 4.2. Continuous Control Tasks in Brax (Partial Observability & Noise)

- **GPO Outperforms Baselines:** Across various Brax tasks (Ant, Halfcheetah, Humanoid, InvertedDoublePendulum, Swimmer, Walker2d) with varying levels of Gaussian noise added to partial observations, GPO-clip and GPO-penalty consistently demonstrated superior performance. The general hierarchy was GPO-clip > GPO-penalty > PPO-asym > GPO-naive > other baselines.
- **Limitations of Pre-trained Teachers:** Methods relying on separately pre-trained privileged teachers (e.g., DAgger, ADVISOR, ELF) performed less effectively, especially as noise levels increased. This is attributed to the "impossibly good" teacher problem: when the teacher's policy becomes too skilled or the observations too noisy for the student to imitate, the supervision becomes unhelpful or even detrimental.
- **Effectiveness of GPO Framework:** The superior performance of GPO-clip and GPO-penalty over baseline PPO indicates that the framework effectively leverages additional information to facilitate learner training.
- **Benefits of Learner RL:** The comparison between GPO-naive and GPO-penalty/clip confirmed that incorporating direct RL training for the learner generally improves performance in these complex continuous control tasks.
- **Importance of Backtracking:** The poor performance of PPO+BC compared to GPO-naive highlighted the necessity of constraining the guider's policy through backtracking. Without such constraints, unaligned guider supervision can negatively impact the learner.
- **Other Baselines:** ADVISOR, designed to adapt to inimitable teachers, often degenerated to pure PPO training in challenging partial observability scenarios. L2T-PPO also performed comparably to PPO+BC, suggesting similar limitations. Representation learning methods like RMA struggled in noisy environments where privileged information cannot be reliably reconstructed.

#### 4.3. Memory-Based Tasks in POPGym

- **Strong Performance in Memory Tasks:** GPO-penalty and GPO-clip again outperformed PPO-asym and standard PPO across 15 POPGym tasks requiring memory (e.g., Autoencode, Battleship, CountRecall, RepeatPrevious).
- **Guider Exploration Benefits:** The ability of the guider to explore more effectively without diverging too far from the learner proved valuable in memory-based tasks, where storing critical information is essential.
- **Asymmetric Value Function:** While PPO-asym improved upon PPO, its benefits were less pronounced in POPGym tasks compared to Brax, suggesting that an asymmetric value function might be less critical for memory challenges.
- **Potential for Hyperparameter Tuning:** Instances where GPO variants underperformed (e.g., BattleshipMedium, CountRecallHard) were attributed to fixed hyperparameters, suggesting that task-specific tuning could yield better results.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies and Discussions

- **Why pre-trained teachers fail:** Confirmed that pre-trained teachers often become "inimitable" in partially observable tasks, causing TSL methods (like ADVISOR and TGRL) to revert to base RL performance.
- **Sources of GPO's Superiority:**
 - **Effective RL training of the learner:** GPO-ablation (GPO-penalty without supervision) outperformed PPO-asym, indicating that the guider's better behavioral policy for data collection significantly improves learning efficiency.
 - **Effective and Constrained Supervision:** In memory-intensive tasks, GPO-clip (with RL disabled) outperformed GPO-ablation, PPO+BC, and PPO-asym, demonstrating the particular benefit of the guider's constrained supervision.
- **Failure Modes of GPO:** GPO can fail if the guider learns too slowly due to inadequate information, or if KL thresholds/clip parameters are poorly tuned. For very challenging memory tasks, large KL thresholds can push the guider beyond the learner's reachable region, creating an unrecoverable imitation gap when the memory model struggles.
- **Computational Cost:** GPO was found to be approximately 10-20% slower than PPO-asym but significantly faster than off-policy methods like TGRL, achieving its performance without additional networks.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that GPO provides a principled and empirically effective method for leveraging privileged information in challenging POMDPs, offering robust performance, theoretical guarantees, and solutions to issues prevalent in existing teacher-student learning paradigms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Guided Policy Optimization (GPO) framework represents a notable contribution to the field of Reinforcement Learning, particularly in addressing challenges within Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) where privileged information is available during training.

**Significance:**

- **Novel Framework for Leveraging Privileged Information:** GPO introduces a co-training framework that explicitly designs a "guider" (teacher) to operate with privileged information while ensuring its policy remains "possibly good"—i.e., imitable by a "learner" (student) operating under partial observability. This systematic approach differentiates it from traditional Imitation Learning (IL) or Teacher-Student Learning (TSL) methods that often struggle with the "impossibly good" teacher problem.
- **Theoretical Guarantees for Learner Optimality:** A key theoretical strength of GPO is the demonstration that the learner's policy updates can be viewed as a constrained policy mirror descent, inheriting properties of standard RL algorithms. This provides a principled basis for GPO's ability to achieve optimality comparable to direct RL training, which is often lacking in other teacher-supervision methods.
- **Robust Performance Across Diverse POMDPs:** Empirical results across didactic tasks, noisy continuous control problems (Brax), and memory-based challenges (POPGym) show that GPO consistently outperforms various contemporary baselines. This broad applicability suggests its versatility and robustness in complex environments.
- **Efficient Information Transfer:** GPO optimizes the transfer of knowledge from privileged information by allowing the guider to handle complex RL gradients, while the learner focuses on an easier supervised learning task. This partitioning of complexity can lead to more stable and efficient learning, as observed in the experimental results.
- **Practical Implementations:** The introduction of two variants, GPO-penalty and GPO-clip, provides practical and adaptable ways to implement the core GPO principles, offering choices for how to manage the balance between guider exploration and learner imitability. The shared policy network design further enhances its practical applicability by improving memory efficiency.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Advancement in Real-World RL Applications:** Many real-world applications of RL, such as robotics, autonomous driving, and industrial control, inherently involve partial observability and often benefit from simulation-based training where full state information is available. GPO offers a principled method to bridge the gap between simulation training and real-world deployment, potentially leading to more effective and robust policies.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency:** By effectively leveraging privileged information and guiding the learner, GPO has the potential to improve the sample efficiency of RL algorithms in POMDPs. This is crucial for tasks where data collection is expensive or time-consuming.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The GPO framework provides a strong foundation for future research. The authors specifically suggest extending GPO to multi-agent settings, where agents might train with global information but execute with local observations—a common and challenging problem in multi-agent reinforcement learning.
- **Mitigation of Common RL Difficulties:** By addressing the imitation gap and providing a structured way to handle noisy or incomplete observations, GPO can help overcome some of the persistent difficulties in deploying RL agents in complex, realistic environments.

In conclusion, GPO represents a significant step forward in integrating privileged information into partially observable reinforcement learning. Its theoretical grounding and strong empirical performance suggest it could become a standard methodology for training robust and efficient agents in scenarios where auxiliary information is available during the learning process.
