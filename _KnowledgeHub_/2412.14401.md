# The One RING: a Robotic Indoor Navigation Generalist

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Washington and the Allen Institute for AI developed The One RING, a generalist robotic policy for indoor navigation that achieves zero-shot transfer to diverse, unseen robot embodiments. Trained exclusively in simulation using large-scale embodiment randomization, the policy demonstrated an average 78.9% success rate on real-world robots and adapted navigation strategies based on inferred physical characteristics.

## Problem

- Most existing robotic navigation policies are designed for specific robot configurations, leading to significant performance degradation or failure when deployed on different hardware.
- Developing specialized policies for each new robot embodiment is costly and time-consuming, hindering rapid deployment and scalability.
- Previous cross-embodiment approaches often rely on limited real-world datasets or struggle with robust generalization to truly novel or out-of-distribution robot designs.

## Method

- ==Developed RING==, an embodiment-agnostic policy, by training it entirely in simulation across 1 million distinct, randomly sampled robot body and sensor configurations within the AI2-THOR environment.
- Utilized a deep learning architecture with a frozen pre-trained visual encoder, a goal encoder, and a transformer state encoder, combined with a causal transformer decoder for explicit memory modeling over time.
- Employed a two-stage training paradigm: initial large-scale imitation learning on expert trajectories followed by on-policy reinforcement learning finetuning using DD-PPO.

## Results

- Achieved an average zero-shot success rate of **72.1%** across five unseen simulated embodiments, an absolute improvement of **16.7%** over the CHORES-S benchmark.
- Demonstrated robust zero-shot transfer to four distinct real-world robotic platforms (Stretch RE-1, LoCoBot, Unitree Go1, RB-Y1) with an average success rate of **78.9%**, often outperforming specialized policies.
- Successfully generalized to human participants acting as navigation 'embodiments', achieving a **73.3%** average success rate and showcasing adaptive behavior such as navigating under furniture based on inferred robot size.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large-scale embodiment randomization in simulation is an effective method for achieving robust zero-shot generalization to diverse and unseen real-world robot platforms.
> - The policy can implicitly infer its own embodiment characteristics from visual inputs and environmental interactions, adapting navigation strategies without explicit body parameter knowledge.
> - Combining imitation learning with reinforcement learning provides a strong foundation for generalist navigation, enabling high zero-shot performance and efficient adaptation to specialized policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic navigation has seen significant advancements, particularly with the rise of deep learning and large-scale simulated environments. However, a prevalent challenge remains the lack of generalizability across diverse robot embodiments. Most existing navigation policies are designed for specific robot configurations, leading to performance degradation or complete failure when deployed on different hardware, even with minor variations in body size, camera placement, or field of view.

Prior research has addressed cross-embodiment training to some extent. Initiatives like Open-X-Embodiment (OXE) and methods such as CrossFormer and GET-zero demonstrate efforts to create policies that transfer across multiple robot platforms, primarily focusing on manipulation tasks or learning from a relatively limited set of real-world embodiments (e.g., 20-30 robots). While these approaches show some transfer capabilities, they often struggle with significant performance drops when faced with truly novel or out-of-distribution embodiments. The reliance on real-world data collection for these efforts also presents scalability challenges due to high costs.

Similarly, foundational navigation policies, including methods for point-goal navigation (e.g., DD-PPO) and object-goal navigation (e.g., SPOC, PoliFormer, FLaRe, NoMaD, ViNT, GNM), have achieved notable success. These policies often leverage powerful pre-trained vision models and large-scale procedurally generated virtual environments for efficient learning. However, even these general-purpose navigation policies are typically trained on a relatively small number of embodiments, limiting their robust generalization to an arbitrary spectrum of possible robot designs. Some approaches using Large Language Models (LLMs) or Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have explored embodiment-agnostic policies, but they are often constrained to short-horizon navigation and single-step predictions.

This work, "The One RING," directly addresses this generalization gap by introducing an embodiment-agnostic policy specifically designed for robust, zero-shot transfer to a wide array of unseen robot platforms. It distinguishes itself by eschewing real-world embodiment data during training, instead relying entirely on large-scale randomization of robot parameters within a simulator. This simulation-only training for real-world deployment represents a significant contribution, aiming to eliminate the need for re-training or adaptation for each new robot, a limitation that previous cross-embodiment and foundational navigation policies have not fully overcome.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a single, generalist robotic policy capable of effectively navigating indoor environments across a diverse and unconstrained range of robot embodiments, without requiring explicit knowledge of the robot's physical characteristics or any adaptation during deployment. This policy is named RING (Robotic Indoor Navigation Generalist).

The motivation for this work stems from several critical observations and needs in the robotics field:

- **Diversity and Evolution of Robot Embodiments:** Modern robots exhibit significant variations in shape, size, and sensor configurations (e.g., camera types, positions, fields of view). This diversity is continuously expanding as custom hardware is developed for specialized tasks and environments.
- **Embodiment-Specific Policies and Generalization Failure:** A fundamental limitation of current navigation policies is their embodiment-specificity. A policy trained on one robot typically performs poorly or fails entirely when deployed on another, even with minor changes to its physical or sensor parameters. This necessitates costly and time-consuming re-training or extensive adaptation for each new robot, hindering rapid deployment and scalability.
- **Need for a Generalist Solution:** The increasing prevalence of custom hardware creates an urgent demand for a generalist policy. Such a policy would eliminate the repetitive burden of re-training for every specific robot, streamlining the development and deployment of robotic systems.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap at Scale:** Previous attempts at cross-embodiment training often rely on limited real-world datasets, which are expensive to collect and insufficient to cover the vast space of possible embodiments. The authors are motivated to leverage the scalability of simulation for training, coupled with robust domain randomization techniques, to achieve effective zero-shot transfer to real-world robots. The goal is to demonstrate that large-scale simulation training, specifically on a vast distribution of randomized robot configurations, can effectively bridge the sim-to-real gap for diverse, unseen physical platforms.
- **Embodiment-Adaptive Behavior:** Beyond mere generalization, the research aims for a policy that can implicitly infer its own embodiment characteristics from visual inputs and environmental interactions, adapting its navigation strategies accordingly (e.g., navigating under furniture if small enough, or rerouting if a collision occurs due to a misjudgment of size).

In essence, the core motivation is to move beyond specialized robot control towards truly universal navigation capabilities, enabling any mobile robot to become an effective indoor semantic navigator out-of-the-box.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for developing RING integrates large-scale embodiment randomization in a simulated environment with a robust deep learning architecture and a combined imitation learning and reinforcement learning training paradigm.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is framed as navigating a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) where the agent's embodiment `e` is characterized by a configuration vector `c_e`. This vector influences both the observation space `O_e` (due to varying camera parameters) and the transition dynamics `T_e` (due to varying collider size and rotation center). The agents share a discrete, platform-agnostic action space: `{MoveBase(±20cm), RotateBase(±6°, ±30°), Done}`. These actions are executed via robot-specific low-level controllers in deployment.

#### 3.2. Embodiment Randomization at Scale

To foster generalization, the researchers employ embodiment randomization, a technique complementary to domain randomization. This involves training policies across a wide range of simulated robot body parameters.
The AI2-THOR simulator was augmented to support the generation of 1 million distinct, randomly sampled agent body configurations. Each agent is modeled as an invisible collider box and equipped with one or two RGB cameras. The randomized parameters include:
- **Collider Size:** Three scale factors (`αx, αy, αz`) are sampled from ranges `[0.2, 0.5]`, `[0.3, 1.5]`, and `[0.2, 0.5]` meters, respectively, to define the dimensions of the robot's bounding box.
- **Rotation Center:** Coordinates (`ox, oy, oz`) defining the agent's pivot point are sampled within ranges determined by the collider size (e.g., `[−αx/2, αx/2]`).
- **Camera Parameters:** For one or two RGB cameras, various parameters are randomized:
 - **Position (x, y, z):** Sampled within the collider box (e.g., `[0.3, αy]` for y-position).
 - **Rotation (Pitch, Yaw):** First camera faces forward; second camera's yaw can rotate up to 360 degrees, while pitch for both cameras is randomized.
 - **Field of View (FoV):** Vertical and horizontal FoV are sampled.
 - **RGB Dimensions (H, W):** Sampled from `` pixels.
Expert trajectories are also generated to account for these diverse embodiments, ensuring path safety by avoiding obstacles based on the agent's specific collider.

#### 3.3. Architecture

RING's architecture is inspired by PoliFormer and designed to handle rich visual observations and language instructions. It consists of:
- **Visual Encoder:** A frozen pre-trained Vision Transformer (SIGLIP-ViT-B/16 or VIT-L/14 for RING-LARGE). It takes padded and resized (256x256) RGB images from up to two cameras as input, producing patch-wise visual representations.
- **Goal Encoder:** A frozen pre-trained Text Transformer (SIGLIP-ViT-B/16) that encodes language instructions into goal embeddings.
- **Transformer State Encoder:** This module concatenates the projected visual representations (from one or two cameras), the goal embedding, and a special `STATE` token embedding. A multi-layer non-causal transformer processes these, with the output corresponding to the `STATE` token serving as a goal-conditioned visual state embedding.
- **Causal Transformer Decoder:** This module performs explicit memory modeling over time. It processes a sequence of state features, using a KV-Cache mechanism to incorporate past experiences and produce a current state belief. This enables both short- and long-horizon planning.
- **Linear Actor-Critic Head:** A linear layer that takes the state belief from the causal transformer decoder and predicts action logits over the discrete action space. During reinforcement learning (RL) finetuning, it also predicts a value estimate.

#### 3.4. Training Paradigm

The training of RING follows a two-stage approach:
1. **Large-scale Imitation Learning (IL):** The policy is initially pretrained on 1 million expert trajectories. These trajectories are collected from the 1 million randomized embodiments across 50,000 procedurally generated ProcTHOR houses, which contain over 40,000 annotated 3D objects from Objaverse. Training uses a cross-entropy loss between predicted action logits and expert actions, with a batch size of 240 trajectories (each with a 100-step temporal context).
2. **Large-scale RL Finetuning:** Following IL, the policy is finetuned using on-policy RL with the DD-PPO algorithm (from AllenAct). This phase allows the policy to learn through trial and error, enhancing robustness. It employs 64 parallel environments and 128 rollout steps, training for 40 million steps. A reward function adapted from previous work is used, rewarding proximity reduction to the target object, a success bonus for issuing `Done`, and a step penalty to encourage efficiency. The entropy term in the PPO loss is disabled to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Metrics

The performance of RING is assessed using several metrics:
- **Success Rate:** Percentage of episodes where the agent successfully reaches and identifies the target.
- **Success Weighted by Episode Length (SEL):** A metric that penalizes longer successful episodes.
- **Success Weighted by Collision (SC):** A novel metric proposed by the authors, which penalizes successful episodes based on the number of collisions, with heavier penalties for initial collisions.
- **Collision Rate (CR):** The number of collisions per step.
- **Safe Episodes:** The percentage of episodes completed without any collisions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates that RING, trained exclusively in simulation, exhibits robust zero-shot generalization to diverse, unseen robot embodiments in both simulated and real-world environments.

#### 4.1. Zero-shot Generalization in Simulation

RING was evaluated zero-shot on five simulated embodiments: Stretch RE-1 (two camera configurations), LoCoBot, and Unitree A1. These embodiments were not included in RING's training data.
- **Performance:** RING achieved an average success rate of 72.1% across these five embodiments, marking a 16.7% absolute improvement over the CHORES-S benchmark.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** RING matched or surpassed embodiment-specific policies (SPOC, PoliFormer, FLaRe) that were trained directly on the target embodiment or a single similar embodiment. For instance, RING outperformed PoliFormer when the latter was trained on LoCoBot (61.5% vs. 66.5% success rate for RING) and Unitree A1 (55.3% vs. 72.0% success rate for RING). This indicates that large-scale training across randomized embodiments enables more efficient learning, particularly for challenging configurations like those with lower FoV or camera placement.

#### 4.2. Zero-shot Real-world Transfer

Despite being trained purely in simulation, RING transferred directly to four distinct real-world robotic platforms without any finetuning:
- **Real Robots:** Stretch RE-1 (with two different camera setups), LoCoBot, Unitree Go1, and the RB-Y1 wheeled humanoid.
- **Performance:** RING achieved an average success rate of 78.9% across these real-world platforms. It consistently matched or outperformed specialized policies trained on specific real robots. For example, on Stretch RE-1 (factory config), RING achieved 72.2% success, compared to PoliFormer's 33.3%. On Unitree Go1, RING achieved 80.0% success, significantly higher than PoliFormer's 41.7%.
- **RB-Y1 Deployment:** RING was successfully deployed on the RB-Y1 wheeled humanoid in an unstructured kitchen environment, navigating to targets at different heights (standing/seated configurations) by streaming visual observations from an iPhone 16 Pro camera.

#### 4.3. Human as Navigation Assistant

To further assess its generalization to novel embodiments, RING was evaluated with five human participants serving as unique "embodiments."
- **Performance:** Humans followed RING's predicted actions on their phones to navigate a real-world kitchen. RING-assisted navigation achieved a 73.3% average success rate, outperforming FLaRe (a policy trained on Stretch RE-1) which yielded 40.0% success. This suggests RING's robustness to variations in human gait, height, and camera-holding posture.

#### 4.4. Efficient Adaptation to Specialized Policies

While demonstrating strong zero-shot performance, the study also showed that RING can be efficiently adapted to embodiment-specialized policies with minimal finetuning.
- **Performance:** With only 20 million additional RL steps of finetuning on a specific robot (e.g., LoCoBot, Unitree A1), RING achieved up to 10% absolute improvement in success rate, often surpassing FLaRe's performance under similar adaptation conditions. This indicates a strong foundation for rapid specialization.

#### 4.5. Embodiment-Adaptive Behavior

Qualitative analysis demonstrated that RING exhibits embodiment-adaptive behavior without explicit knowledge of its body parameters.
- **Adaptive Navigation:** The policy adapted its navigation strategy based on the robot's inferred physical configuration. For example, a low-profile Unitree A1 navigated directly under a bed, while the taller Stretch RE-1 navigated around it.
- **Adaptation after Collision:** In a scenario where an agent with a low-mounted camera but a tall body mistakenly attempted to go under a bed, it adapted its route to go around after an initial collision, showcasing learning from interaction.

#### 4.6. Collision Analysis

RING demonstrated safe navigation behavior comparable to or superior to embodiment-specific policies.
- **Safe Episodes:** RING achieved a higher percentage of "Safe Episodes" (episodes without collisions) compared to baseline policies.
- **Collision Penalty Impact:** Incorporating a small collision penalty into the RL reward function further reduced the collision rate by 50% and increased the percentage of safe episodes from 46.90% to 60.57%, making the policy even more conservative.

#### 4.7. Impact of Visual Encoder

An ablation study revealed that using a more powerful visual encoder (OpenAI's VIT-L/14 CLIP model) for RING-LARGE resulted in approximately a 9% improvement in average zero-shot success rate across the four evaluation embodiments, underscoring the importance of robust visual features for generalization.

#### 4.8. Out-of-Distribution Generalization

Policies trained on narrower ranges of embodiment parameters (excluding those of the real robots) still generalized to out-of-distribution values but exhibited significantly higher collision rates, emphasizing the necessity of broad embodiment randomization during training to ensure both high success and safety.

#### 4.9. Limitations and Ablations

Explicitly conditioning RING with a vector of embodiment parameters (RING-EMB-COND) did not show a clear benefit in performance metrics compared to the standard RING. This suggests that the visual observations might already contain sufficient information for the policy to infer relevant embodiment characteristics, or that alternative methods for integrating this information might be required.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "One RING" research presents a significant advancement towards the development of truly generalist robotic navigation policies, with several potential impacts on the field of robotics and embodied AI.

#### 5.1. Addressing a Core Robotics Challenge

The work directly tackles the fundamental challenge of generalization across diverse robot embodiments, a long-standing barrier to widespread robot deployment. By demonstrating robust zero-shot transfer to unseen real-world robots from simulation-only training, RING provides a foundational step towards overcoming embodiment-specific limitations.

#### 5.2. Streamlining Robot Development and Deployment

The ability of RING to operate effectively on a wide range of robots without retraining or extensive adaptation significantly reduces the development cycle and associated costs for new robotic platforms. This means that researchers and engineers can deploy navigation capabilities on novel hardware much faster, accelerating innovation in areas that rely on custom robotic solutions.

#### 5.3. Advancing Sim-to-Real Transfer

RING's success reinforces the efficacy of large-scale embodiment randomization in simulation as a powerful technique for achieving robust sim-to-real transfer. It showcases that comprehensive coverage of the embodiment space in a simulated environment can lead to general-purpose policies that are resilient to the complexities and variations of the real world. This approach reduces the reliance on costly and time-consuming real-world data collection for each new robot.

#### 5.4. Enhancing Robot Autonomy and Adaptability

The policy's demonstrated embodiment-adaptive behavior, where it implicitly infers its physical characteristics and adjusts navigation strategies, contributes to more intelligent and autonomous robotic systems. This adaptability is crucial for robots operating in dynamic and unstructured environments where precise, predefined knowledge of all potential interactions is not feasible.

#### 5.5. Broadening Accessibility and Applications

By providing a single, easily deployable policy, RING could democratize access to advanced navigation capabilities for a wider range of users and applications, including those with limited resources for specialized policy development. Its potential as a "navigation assistant" for humans, as demonstrated in the human evaluation, also opens avenues for novel human-robot interaction paradigms.

#### 5.6. Foundation for Future Research

The work provides a strong baseline and open-source resources (models, code, data to be released) for future research in generalist robotics. It encourages further exploration into how to better integrate embodiment information, enhance safety through more sophisticated collision avoidance, and extend generalization to even more complex tasks and environments, potentially informing the development of next-generation universal robot policies. The findings on the importance of robust visual encoders and the trade-offs of narrower training ranges also guide future architectural and data generation strategies.
