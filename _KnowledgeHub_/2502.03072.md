# RoboGrasp: A Universal Grasping Policy for Robust Robotic Control

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboGrasp introduces a framework that enhances diffusion-based visuomotor policies by integrating an auxiliary grasping-box detection model to provide explicit spatial guidance for robotic grasping. This approach achieved up to 34% higher grasp success rates in few-shot and prompt-based tasks compared to a standard Diffusion Policy baseline, improving generalization and adaptability.

## Problem

- Existing Behavior Cloning (BC) methods rely on raw sensor data (e.g., RGB images), leading to overfitting and poor generalization to unseen objects or cluttered environments.
- Current policies often learn implicit patterns without explicit task guidance, limiting their robustness and adaptability to novel scenarios.
- Large-scale BC models are computationally expensive, and point-based affordance representations lack actionable information for effective grasping.

## Method

- The **RoboGrasp** framework augments the ==Diffusion Policy (DP)== by integrating a Grasp Detection Module for explicit grasp-specific information.
- A fine-tuned YOLOv11-m model serves as the Grasp Detection Module, predicting the 2D coordinates (center, width, height) of grasping boxes for observed visual data.
- The policy's Observation Encoder combines visual data, robot arm state, and these explicit grasping box features to condition a diffusion-based action prediction head.

## Results

- RoboGrasp achieved up to **33.75%** higher Task Success Rate (TSR) in the PickBig task and **28.7%** higher TSR in the PickGoods prompt-based task compared to the Diffusion Policy baseline.
- In the PickCup task, RoboGrasp demonstrated **100%** TSR and Grasp Success Rate (GSR) for new cup instances with limited demonstrations, outperforming DP's lower success rates (e.g., **70%** TSR for green mug).
- The framework successfully performed prompt-based grasping for specific retail items (e.g., Meiji Chocolate Bar, Tissue) with **100%** TSR and high GSRs, using grasping boxes as explicit goal prompts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating explicit grasping box detections as structured visual guidance significantly improves the generalization, precision, and stability of diffusion-based robotic grasping policies.
> - Leveraging pretrained vision models to provide task-relevant information allows robotic policies to focus on pertinent objects and regions, mitigating overfitting to raw sensor data.
> - Grasping-based affordances facilitate robust few-shot learning for novel objects and enable goal-oriented, promptable grasping actions, reducing the need for extensive training data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

RoboGrasp operates within the evolving field of generalizable robotic learning, specifically addressing the challenges of robotic grasping. Current advancements in imitation learning and world models have shown promise, with Behavior Cloning (BC) and diffusion-based policies emerging as flexible and expressive solutions for complex, multi-modal action spaces. Diffusion Policy (DP), in particular, has demonstrated capabilities in generating diverse action trajectories and improving robustness in dynamic environments by mitigating issues like covariate shift, where policies struggle to generalize beyond their training data.

However, a recurring limitation in existing BC methods, including diffusion-based approaches, is their reliance on raw sensor data (e.g., robot arm state, RGB images) as conditional input. This often leads to overfitting to specific object shapes or positions, hindering generalization in cluttered, dynamic settings with unseen or distractor objects. These policies frequently depend on implicitly learned patterns without explicit task guidance, thereby limiting their robustness and adaptability to novel scenarios.

Recent efforts to scale BC architectures have been fueled by large-scale robotic expert demonstration datasets, with models like Octo and π0 demonstrating skill transfer and some level of zero-shot generalization. However, the computational expense associated with training these large-scale models restricts their accessibility. Concurrently, other research has explored point-based affordance representations, which use keypoints to identify task-relevant objects and provide structured information to guide policies. While these methods offer scalability, they primarily convey object locations without explicit actionable information on how to effectively grasp or manipulate them.

Grasping-based affordance representations are recognized as a more comprehensive solution, capable of encoding both spatial and actionable information by specifying feasible grasping strategies. Datasets such as Grasp Anything illustrate the potential for scalable data collection in this domain. Nevertheless, the integration of these explicit grasping affordances with advanced diffusion-based policies remains an area requiring further investigation, with existing works like GQCNN representing initial steps.

RoboGrasp positions itself by bridging this identified gap. It aims to enhance the efficiency and generalization capabilities of robot planning models, particularly in resource-constrained settings, by integrating grasping-based affordance representations as richer conditional inputs into diffusion-based policies. This approach seeks to overcome the limitations of raw sensor data reliance and implicit learning by providing explicit, structured visual guidance for grasping tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the development of RoboGrasp stems from the persistent challenges in achieving robust and generalizable robotic grasping. Despite advances in imitation learning and behavior cloning, existing methods often demonstrate limited adaptability, struggling to perform reliably on novel objects or in dynamic, cluttered environments. This limitation is largely attributed to their dependency on raw sensor data (RGB images and robot arm state) for policy conditioning, which can lead to models overfitting to specific object characteristics or environmental setups present in the training data. Without explicit task guidance, these policies learn implicit patterns, which may not translate effectively to unseen scenarios, impacting both precision and stability.

To address these limitations, the authors were motivated to leverage the capabilities of pretrained computer vision models for tasks such as object detection, segmentation, pose estimation, and depth estimation. The hypothesis was that providing structured, task-relevant information through these models could enable robotic policies to focus on pertinent objects and regions, thereby enhancing generalization to novel objects and tasks in complex environments.

Against this background, RoboGrasp was developed with the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a universal grasping policy framework:** The core objective was to design and implement a framework, RoboGrasp, that integrates an auxiliary grasping-box detection model. This model would be responsible for identifying precise grasp regions, providing explicit spatial guidance to the robot arm rather than relying solely on raw visual input.
2. **Enhance generalizability and adaptability:** By conditioning the robotic policy on these explicit grasping boxes, a key objective was to significantly improve the policy's ability to generalize across diverse objects and adapt to varying environmental conditions, including changes in object position and type.
3. **Evaluate few-shot learning capabilities:** The research aimed to investigate whether RoboGrasp could effectively leverage grasping-based affordances to facilitate robust few-shot learning for new or previously unseen objects. This objective sought to determine if minimal demonstration data could enable the robot to grasp novel items accurately.
4. **Assess promptable grasping actions:** A distinct objective was to ascertain if RoboGrasp could utilize grasping-based affordance prompts as explicit visual cues to define specific task objectives and, consequently, generate effective and goal-aligned robotic policies. This would demonstrate the policy's responsiveness to predefined outcomes rather than implicit pattern recognition.
5. **Improve grasp precision and stability:** Ultimately, the framework aimed to tangibly enhance the precision and stability of robotic grasps, leading to higher success rates in manipulation tasks across varied and complex scenarios.

Through these objectives, the authors sought to contribute to the deployment of robots in unstructured real-world settings, reduce the dependency on meticulously controlled laboratory data, and improve the adaptability of robotic systems for diverse and dynamic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RoboGrasp framework is designed as an augmentation of the Diffusion Policy (DP), a diffusion-based visuomotor policy. The core methodological innovation lies in integrating explicit grasp-specific information into the policy's observation stream to enhance its generalization and precision.

**Architectural Overview:**
RoboGrasp's architecture maintains the fundamental structure of Diffusion Policy but introduces a **Grasp Detection Module** and modifies the **Observation Encoder** to incorporate grasping-box features. The hyperparameters for the number of historical timesteps (2) and predicted actions (16) are consistent with the original DP framework.

**Grasp Detection Module:**
1. **Model Selection:** The module utilizes YOLOv11-m, a You Only Look Once (YOLO) variant, selected for its speed, simplicity, and generalization capabilities.
2. **Training:** YOLOv11-m was fine-tuned on a custom-labeled dataset. This dataset was meticulously annotated to predict the object class, the 2D spatial coordinates (x, y) of the grasping box's central point, and the box's width and height. The angle of rotation was excluded due to the specific robotic arm's rotational limitations in the experiments, and objects were intentionally kept unrotated.
3. **Operation:** During policy training, the module's generated labels were directly fed into the system. During inference, YOLOv11-m dynamically predicts grasping boxes for the observed visual data in real-time. For simplification, in tasks involving a single target object, the module outputs only the grasping box with the highest confidence score.

**Observation Encoder:**
1. **Input Integration:** The observation encoder is responsible for combining various data streams into a unified latent representation. This includes:
 - **Visual Data:** Multiview RGB data, processed separately for each camera view by a ResNet34-based feature pyramid encoder.
 - **Low-dimensional Data:** Robot arm's end pose and gripper sensor data, integrated as in the original DP design.
 - **Grasping Box Features (Novel Augmentation):** The class label and spatial information (coordinates, width, height) derived from the Grasp Detection Module are incorporated into the concatenated observation data.
2. **Temporal Encoding:** The concatenated data is projected into a fixed-dimensional latent space, forming a single token per timestep. An untrained, lightweight transformer then applies self-attention across designated historical timesteps to capture temporal dependencies.

**Diffusion Action Head:**
1. **Action Prediction:** A lightweight diffusion transformer, identical to that used in DP, predicts actions over 16 future timesteps.
2. **Denoising Schedule:** A DDIM scheduler with a Cosine Beta noise schedule is employed for the denoising process, ensuring efficient and smooth sampling of actions.
3. **Conditional Generation:** Cross-attention mechanisms are utilized to condition the noised actions on the observation tokens. This allows the policy to effectively integrate the visual and spatial context, including the explicit grasping box guidance, into its action generation. Actions are linearly projected into the latent space for transformer processing and then reprojected to their original dimensions.

**Experimental Setup and Data Collection:**
- **Hardware:** The system uses an industrial-grade robotic arm, RealSense cameras for visual input, and a Quest VR headset for collecting demonstration data.
- **Data Processing:** Datasets for each task were curated:
 - **PickBig:** 600 demonstration trials (8 placement positions × 75 demos each).
 - **PickCup:** 315 demonstration trials (3 cup types × 4 placement positions × 25 demos + 15 few-shot demos for mugs and paper cups).
 - **PickGoods:** 400 demonstration trials (100 demos per retail item).
- **Annotation Process:** Approximately 500 representative frames were manually labeled with grasping boxes. This manually labeled subset was used to fine-tune the Grasp Detection Module, achieving a mean Average Precision (mAP) exceeding 98%. The trained module then automatically generated grasping boxes for the entirety of the collected video data, ensuring consistent and scalable annotations.

**Experimental Design and Evaluation:**
- **Tasks:** Three distinct grasping tasks were designed to evaluate different aspects of generalization and adaptability:
 - **PickBig:** Distinguishing and grasping the larger of two similar blocks across 8 variable positions.
 - **PickCup:** Grasping different cup types using various strategies (handle, wall, diameter), including few-shot learning for novel cup instances.
 - **PickGoods:** Promptable grasping of diverse retail items, where the grasping box serves as an explicit goal prompt.
- **Ablation Study:** A comparative analysis was conducted between two model configurations:
 - **DP Model (Baseline):** The standard diffusion-based visuomotor policy.
 - **RoboGrasp Model:** The DP augmented with grasping box annotations from the Grasp Detection Module.
- **Standardization:** To ensure fair comparison, identical placement positions and objects were used for both models in each task during inference testing.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Task Success Rate (TSR) and Grasp Success Rate (GSR) were used. GSR was defined as the number of successful grasps divided by the total number of grasp attempts, assessing action accuracy, consistency, and stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of RoboGrasp consistently demonstrated performance advantages over the Diffusion Policy (DP) baseline across all tested tasks, particularly in scenarios requiring generalization and adaptability.

**Overall Performance Comparison:**
RoboGrasp showed superiority in both Task Success Rate (TSR) and Grasp Success Rate (GSR) across diverse objects, grasping strategies, and placement positions. The integration of grasping box detections was identified as a critical factor in these improvements.

**PickBig Task:**
- **RoboGrasp:** Achieved a TSR of 97.5% and a GSR of 96.25%.
- **DP:** Recorded a TSR of 67.5% and a GSR of 66.25%.
- **Outcome:** RoboGrasp outperformed DP by 33.75% in TSR. This improvement was attributed to RoboGrasp's ability to utilize grasping box detections to explicitly distinguish the larger block and dynamically adapt to eight varied placement positions. In contrast, DP frequently struggled with overfitting to low-dimensional robot state representations, leading to inconsistent performance and difficulty in adapting to positional changes or differentiating between similar objects.

**PickCup Task:**
- **RoboGrasp:** Consistently achieved high performance, with TSRs of 92.5-100% and GSRs of 92.5-100% across five distinct cup objects and various grasping strategies (handle, wall, diameter).
- **DP:** Exhibited inconsistent strategies, particularly for more challenging cases like blue plastic cups and green mugs, leading to lower success rates (e.g., Blue Plastic Cup: DP TSR 87.5%, GSR 85%; RoboGrasp TSR 92.5%, GSR 92.5%).
- **Few-shot Learning:** RoboGrasp demonstrated effective few-shot learning capabilities. For new cup instances introduced with limited demonstrations (e.g., green mug with 5 demos, blue plastic cup with 10 demos), RoboGrasp achieved 100% TSR and GSR, while DP's performance was lower (e.g., Green Mug: DP TSR 70%, GSR 60%). On average, RoboGrasp achieved a 23.33% higher GSR in this task. This indicated RoboGrasp’s ability to generalize grasping strategies with minimal data and consistently select appropriate grasps across geometric variations.

**PickGoods Task (Promptable Grasping):**
- **RoboGrasp:** Successfully performed prompt-based grasping for two out of four target objects (Meiji Chocolate Bar: TSR 100%, GSR 98%; Tissue: TSR 100%, GSR 100%).
- **DP:** Largely failed in this task, achieving 0% TSR for chocolate bars and biscuits, and only 4% for M&M, due to difficulties in object identification and inconsistent grasps.
- **Outcome:** RoboGrasp demonstrated a significant improvement of 28.7% in TSR over DP in this task. However, RoboGrasp's performance on some items (Lotus Biscuit, M&M) showed a relative decline compared to other tasks. This was analyzed to be due to the environment's complexity, specifically the presence of three closely adjacent candidate objects in a confined workspace. This created a multimodal action probability distribution, leading the policy to sometimes prioritize proximal targets rather than strictly adhering to the prompted object. Nevertheless, the grasping-based affordances still influenced directional choices, indicating their role in guiding the policy. The simpler PickBig environment, with only two candidate objects, resulted in a unimodal action distribution, leading to more deterministic policy execution.

**Data Compensation Analysis:**
- The study found that dataset size requirements are influenced by state space complexity and object similarity. For the PickBig task, doubling the dataset from 300 to 600 demonstrations resolved training instability and improved results, attributed to the large state space of eight distinct placement positions.
- Conversely, for the PickCups task, only 25 demonstrations per cup type at each position were sufficient for convergence, suggesting that distinct objects require less data for effective generalization. This analysis supports the conclusion that datasets need to be scaled proportionally to the state space size and when target objects are similar.

In summary, the results consistently highlight that incorporating grasping box detections as explicit guidance significantly improves a diffusion-based policy's ability to achieve higher task success rates, exhibit stronger generalization, and adapt effectively to varied grasping scenarios, including few-shot learning and prompt-based manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RoboGrasp framework introduces a significant advancement in robotic grasping by addressing key limitations in precision, stability, and generalization within robotic manipulation. By integrating pretrained grasp detection models to provide explicit visual guidance, the work moves beyond the conventional reliance on raw robot state data and RGB images, which often lead to overfitting and reduced adaptability.

**Significance:**
- **Enhanced Generalization and Precision:** RoboGrasp demonstrates substantial improvements in grasp success rates, achieving up to 34% higher success rates in few-shot learning and prompt-based tasks compared to a standard Diffusion Policy baseline. This indicates a more robust and adaptable policy capable of handling diverse objects and scenarios.
- **Effective Few-Shot Learning:** The framework exhibits strong capabilities in transferring grasping skills to novel objects with minimal demonstration data, reducing the need for extensive, task-specific training datasets.
- **Goal-Oriented Policy Generation:** RoboGrasp successfully utilizes grasping boxes as explicit visual prompts to define task objectives and generate effective, goal-aligned robotic policies. This represents a step towards more intuitive and controllable robot behavior.
- **Flexible and Scalable Architecture:** Built on diffusion-based methods, RoboGrasp offers a flexible and extensible framework that can potentially be adapted to a wider array of complex manipulation tasks and integrated into various robotic learning paradigms.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Real-World Deployment:** The improved robustness and generalizability of RoboGrasp facilitate the deployment of robotic systems in dynamic, unstructured real-world environments, reducing their dependence on highly controlled laboratory settings. This could accelerate the adoption of robotics in logistics, manufacturing, retail, and domestic applications where objects are diverse and unorganized.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** The framework's few-shot learning capabilities imply that robots could be rapidly trained for new grasping tasks with fewer human demonstrations, lowering the cost and effort associated with robot programming and data collection.
- **Advancement of Robotic Learning Research:** This work establishes a foundation for future research by highlighting the value of structured, task-relevant visual guidance in robotic policies. It suggests several promising future directions:
 - **Language Prompting Integration:** Combining grasp-guided approaches with language models (e.g., Grounding DINO, DINO-X) could enable more flexible and intuitive human-robot interaction, allowing users to define grasping tasks through natural language commands.
 - **Broader Framework Compatibility:** Applying grasp-guided methods to other leading robotic learning frameworks (e.g., ACT) and large foundation models (e.g., Robotics Diffusion Transformer) could unlock even greater scalability and generalizability across diverse manipulation skills.
 - **Enhanced World Models:** Incorporating grasping prompts into world models could improve their ability to understand and predict real-world interactions, leading to more intelligent planning in dynamic environments.
 - **Advanced Manipulation:** Extending grasping capabilities to include rotational parameters and utilizing rotatable robot arms could enable the handling of more complex and precise manipulation tasks, opening up new application domains.
- **Cost-Effective Development:** Evaluating these methods in simulation environments could provide a cost-effective means to assess robustness and scalability before extensive real-world deployment.

In conclusion, RoboGrasp represents a tangible step towards developing scalable, reliable, and generalizable robotic systems. By systematically addressing the challenge of robust grasping through explicit visual guidance, it contributes to the broader objective of achieving adaptable and autonomous robots capable of performing complex manipulation tasks in dynamic and unstructured settings.
