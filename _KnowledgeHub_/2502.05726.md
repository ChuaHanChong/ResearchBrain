# Improving Environment Novelty Quantification for Effective Unsupervised Environment Design

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced the Coverage-based Evaluation of Novelty In Environment (CENIE) framework, a scalable, domain-agnostic, and curriculum-aware method for quantifying environment novelty in Unsupervised Environment Design. Integrating CENIE into existing UED algorithms resulted in state-of-the-art zero-shot generalization performance across Minigrid, BipedalWalker, and CarRacing benchmarks, fostering broader state-action space exploration.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents struggle to generalize effectively to environments that differ even slightly from their training distribution.
- Existing Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) methods, primarily regret-driven, often fail to explicitly promote diverse or novel training experiences, potentially leading to 'regret stagnation' and limited generalizability.
- Prior novelty quantification techniques for UED suffered from limitations such as domain-specificity, high computational cost, or being curriculum-agnostic, hindering their practical application and scalability.

## Method

- The ==Coverage-based Evaluation of Novelty In Environment== (**CENIE**) framework was developed to quantify environment novelty by assessing the unfamiliarity of induced ==state-action space coverage==.
- **CENIE** models the student agent's accumulated state-action experiences using dynamically fitted ==Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)==; novelty is measured as the ==negative mean log-likelihood== of a new environment's trajectory under the GMM.
- The novelty objective is integrated into existing ==regret-based UED== algorithms, ==PLR⊥== and ==ACCEL==, by combining regret and novelty scores to prioritize level replay and guide level generation.

## Results

- CENIE-augmented algorithms achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot generalization performance across Minigrid, BipedalWalker, and CarRacing benchmark domains.
- In the BipedalWalker domain, CENIE led to significantly broader state-action space coverage (e.g., ACCEL-CENIE **47.6%** vs. ACCEL **42.5%**), demonstrating more diverse training experiences for the agent.
- The integration of novelty (PLR-CENIE in CarRacing) maintained comparable total regret while outperforming baselines, suggesting that novelty-driven exploration can indirectly facilitate the discovery of high-regret states, highlighting a synergistic relationship with regret-based methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Quantifying environment novelty based on an agent's state-action space coverage, relative to its past experiences, provides a scalable and domain-agnostic metric for effective curriculum design.
> - Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) are effective in modeling the distribution of an agent's cumulative state-action experiences, enabling a curriculum-aware assessment of novelty without relying on domain-specific encodings.
> - Explicitly prioritizing novelty in Unsupervised Environment Design complements regret-based objectives by driving agents into unexplored regions of the state-action space, thereby enhancing generalization and mitigating regret stagnation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated capabilities in specific domains, such as games and robotics. However, a persistent challenge in DRL research is the generalization of trained agents to environments that deviate even marginally from their training distribution. Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) addresses this challenge by formulating the autocurricula generation problem as a two-player game involving a teacher agent and a student agent. The teacher's role is to adaptively generate training environments that possess high learning potential, thereby constructing a curriculum designed to enhance the student's ability to operate in novel or unseen scenarios.

The majority of existing UED methods primarily rely on regret as a metric to guide curriculum design. Regret quantifies the difference between an agent's optimal performance and its actual performance in a given environment. While regret-driven approaches, such as PAIRED, PLR$_{\perp}$, and ACCEL, are effective in generating environments that incrementally increase in complexity to push the student's capabilities, they often do not explicitly promote diversity or novelty in the student's training experiences. This oversight can limit an agent's generalizability, particularly in large environment design spaces, and may lead to what is termed "regret stagnation," where learning plateaus even though opportunities for acquiring transferable skills might exist in less-than-maximal regret environments.

Previous attempts to incorporate novelty into UED include methods such as POET (Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer), which calculates novelty based on environment encodings, but is domain-specific and relies on human-designed parameters. EPOET (Enhanced POET) introduced a domain-agnostic approach by evaluating performance differences across a population of agents, yet it incurs significant computational costs and remains curriculum-agnostic in its assessment of whether environments induce rare state-action experiences. DIPLR (Diversity Induced Prioritized Level Replay) utilized Wasserstein distance between agent trajectory occupancy distributions for novelty, but this approach faces scalability and robustness limitations due to its computational intensity and pairwise comparison requirements.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by identifying the limitations of both regret-centric UED methods and existing novelty quantification techniques. It proposes CENIE as a framework designed to provide a scalable, domain-agnostic, and curriculum-aware method for quantifying environment novelty, specifically addressing the shortcomings of prior work and aiming to complement regret-based objectives to enhance generalizability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an improved method for quantifying environment novelty within the Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) paradigm to enhance the effectiveness of autocurricula for training generalized DRL agents.

This objective is motivated by several identified limitations and challenges within the broader UED research landscape:

1. **Limitations of Regret-Driven UED:** Current leading UED algorithms primarily optimize for regret, which measures the potential for student improvement in a given environment. While effective in increasing curriculum complexity, this singular focus can lead to environments that progressively challenge the student along a narrow dimension, neglecting the explicit introduction of diverse and novel experiences. This can result in "regret stagnation," where the agent's learning halts prematurely because it encounters environments where optimal performance is difficult due to partial observability, or where the regret, while not maximal, still offers significant learning opportunities if explored. Such stagnation can impede the development of agents capable of robust generalization to unseen scenarios.

2. **Insufficient Novelty in Curricula:** The lack of explicit novelty prioritization means that agents might repeatedly train on environments that, despite high regret, induce similar behaviors or visit already explored regions of the state-action space. To achieve broad generalizability, agents require exposure to a wide spectrum of scenarios, necessitating a mechanism that actively drives them into unfamiliar state-action combinations.

3. **Shortcomings of Existing Novelty Quantification Methods:** Prior attempts to quantify environment novelty in UED have faced significant limitations:
 - **Domain-Specificity:** Methods like POET rely on hand-designed environment encodings, limiting their scalability and transferability to complex or new domains where such encodings are difficult to define.
 - **Computational Cost and Population Dependence:** Approaches such as EPOET, while more domain-agnostic, require evaluating multiple student agents and converting their performance into rank-normalized vectors, leading to substantial computational overhead and dependence on a population-based training scheme.
 - **Scalability and Curriculum-Agnosticism:** DIPLR, which uses Wasserstein distance between trajectory distributions, is computationally expensive for pairwise comparisons across a growing buffer of levels. Furthermore, these methods often fail to assess novelty in a "curriculum-aware" manner, meaning they do not explicitly consider whether a new environment's induced experiences have already been adequately covered by the agent's aggregated past experiences within the existing curriculum.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to introduce a framework that quantifies environment novelty in a manner that is scalable, independent of specific environment parameters (domain-agnostic), and directly informed by the agent's cumulative learning experiences (curriculum-aware). This framework is intended to provide a complementary objective to regret, facilitating a more effective exploration of the state-action space and contributing to the development of DRL agents with enhanced generalization capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on the Coverage-based Evaluation of Novelty In Environment (CENIE) framework. CENIE quantifies environment novelty by assessing the extent to which a new environment induces state-action space coverage that is unfamiliar to the agent, based on its accumulated experiences from the existing curriculum.

**Core Principles of CENIE:**
CENIE is founded on the intuition that a novel environment should lead the student agent into previously unexplored regions of its state-action space, eliciting behaviors not commonly observed. This approach aims to be:
- **Domain-agnostic:** Novelty is quantified solely based on the student agent's state-action pairs, eliminating reliance on domain-specific environment encodings or parameters.
- **Curriculum-aware:** Novelty is evaluated in relation to the agent's aggregated past experiences throughout the curriculum. This prevents environments that elicit redundant experiences from being classified as novel, promoting sample-efficient exploration.
- **Scalable:** The framework is designed to avoid the computational burdens associated with exhaustive pairwise comparisons or computationally intensive distance metrics.

**Measuring Novelty using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs):**
To implement CENIE, the paper proposes modeling the student's past state-action space coverage distribution using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs). GMMs are chosen for their ability to represent high-dimensional continuous distributions as a weighted combination of multivariate Gaussian components.

The process involves the following steps:
1. **Data Collection:** State-action pairs collected from all previously encountered environments in the curriculum are stored in a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) buffer, denoted as $\Gamma$. The buffer has a fixed window length to ensure that the novelty metric remains relevant to recent experiences and mitigates the effects of catastrophic forgetting.
2. **GMM Fitting:**
 - For a given set of state-action pairs in $\Gamma$, a GMM is fitted to represent their underlying distribution.
 - The initialization of the GMM parameters (weights $\alpha_k$, mean vectors $\mu_k$, and covariance matrices $\Sigma_k$ for each of K Gaussian components) is performed using the k-means++ algorithm.
 - The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm is then used iteratively to refine these parameters, maximizing the likelihood of observing the data in $\Gamma$ given the GMM.
 - To dynamically adapt to the changing complexity of the state-action coverage, multiple GMMs with varying numbers of Gaussian components (within a predefined range $[K_{min}, K_{max}]$) are fitted at each time step. The GMM that achieves the highest silhouette score (a metric evaluating clustering quality based on both intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation) is selected to represent $\lambda_{\Gamma}$.
3. **Novelty Quantification:**
 - For a new candidate environment $l_{\theta}$ that generates a trajectory of state-action pairs $X_{\theta}$, its novelty is calculated by computing the negative mean log-likelihood of $X_{\theta}$ under the fitted GMM $\lambda_{\Gamma}$.
 - The formula used is: $NOVELTY_{l_{\theta}} = - \frac{1}{|X_{\theta}|} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(x_t | \lambda_{\Gamma})$, where $x_t$ is a sample state-action pair from $X_{\theta}$. A lower likelihood indicates state-action pairs that are less represented in the agent's past experiences, thus resulting in a higher novelty score.

**Integration with Existing UED Algorithms:**
CENIE's novelty objective is integrated into two state-of-the-art regret-based UED algorithms: PLR$_{\perp}$ and ACCEL.
- **Level Replay Prioritization:** Both regret and novelty scores are used to determine the probability of replaying a level from the buffer. A rank-based prioritization function $h(S_i) = 1/rank(S_i)$ is applied to convert scores into probabilities $P_N$ (novelty-prioritized) and $P_R$ (regret-prioritized).
- **Combined Objective:** The final level replay probability $P_{replay}$ is computed as a weighted sum: $P_{replay} = \alpha \cdot P_N + (1 - \alpha) \cdot P_R$, where $\alpha$ is a tunable coefficient (set to 0.5 in experiments) balancing the influence of novelty and regret.
- **ACCEL-CENIE Specifics:** For ACCEL, which uses a mutation-based level editor, the combined novelty and regret objective also guides the generation of new levels, beyond just replay prioritization.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology is evaluated across three distinct benchmark domains:
- **Minigrid:** A partially observable navigation task with discrete control and sparse rewards, testing exploration capabilities.
- **BipedalWalker:** A partially observable continuous control task with dense rewards, testing complex locomotion.
- **CarRacing:** A continuous control task with dense rewards, involving complex track generation using Bézier curves.

Student agents are trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Generalization performance is assessed using zero-shot transfer on held-out test environments. Evaluation metrics include aggregated Inter-Quartile Mean (IQM) and Optimality Gap, normalized for robust comparison. Ablation studies are conducted to analyze the isolated effect of the novelty metric.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluations demonstrate that the integration of CENIE's novelty quantification framework into existing UED algorithms consistently leads to enhanced performance in generalization capabilities.

**Overall Performance Enhancement:**
- CENIE-augmented algorithms (PLR-CENIE and ACCEL-CENIE) achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot generalization performance across the Minigrid, BipedalWalker, and CarRacing benchmark domains.
- The combination of regret and novelty in the curriculum design (using $\alpha = 0.5$) generally yielded stronger performance than using either metric in isolation, as indicated by ablation studies presented in the appendix.

**Minigrid Domain Results:**
- **Enhanced Generalization:** ACCEL-CENIE outperformed ACCEL in all eight human-designed test environments. Similarly, PLR-CENIE demonstrated significantly better performance than PLR$_{\perp}$ in seven out of eight environments.
- **Aggregate Metrics:** ACCEL-CENIE showed superiority over ACCEL in both Inter-Quartile Mean (IQM) and optimality gap metrics. PLR-CENIE also substantially outperformed PLR$_{\perp}$.
- **Performance Parity:** Notably, PLR-CENIE, despite utilizing a random environment generator, achieved performance comparable to ACCEL, which employs a more sophisticated editing mechanism for complexity introduction.
- **Out-of-Distribution Performance:** On the challenging PerfectMazeLarge (51x51 tiles, 5000 timesteps), an out-of-distribution environment, PLR-CENIE achieved nearly a 50% solved rate, a significant improvement over PLR$_{\perp}$ (less than 10%) and matching ACCEL's performance.

**BipedalWalker Domain Results:**
- **Leading Performance:** ACCEL-CENIE surpassed ACCEL in five out of six distinct test environments (BipedalWalker, Hardcore, Stair, PitGap, Stump, Roughness), achieving parity in the Roughness challenge. This established ACCEL-CENIE as a leading UED algorithm in this domain.
- **Consistent Improvement:** PLR-CENIE consistently outperformed PLR$_{\perp}$ across all testing instances except for the Stump challenge, where performance was similar.
- **Aggregate Metrics:** Both ACCEL-CENIE and PLR-CENIE showed improved IQM and optimality gap scores compared to their respective predecessors, with ACCEL-CENIE achieving approximately 55% of optimal performance.
- **Broader State-Action Space Coverage:** Quantitative analysis of 2D t-SNE projections of state-action pairs revealed that ACCEL-CENIE and PLR-CENIE achieved significantly broader state-action space coverage (e.g., 47.6% for ACCEL-CENIE vs. 42.5% for ACCEL; 55.3% for PLR-CENIE vs. 43.4% for PLR$_{\perp}$). This supports the hypothesis that CENIE's novelty objective promotes wider exploration.
- **Level Difficulty Composition:** Analysis of replayed levels showed that ACCEL-CENIE progressively incorporated more "Challenging" environments into its curriculum, while ACCEL primarily favored "Easy" to "Moderate" levels. This suggests that CENIE pushes the agent beyond its comfort zone, even when regret in simpler environments is high.

**CarRacing Domain Results:**
- **Superior Generalization:** PLR-CENIE achieved the best generalization performance in terms of both IQM and optimality gap on 20 held-out Formula One (F1) racing tracks, outperforming PLR$_{\perp}$, DIPLR, and other baselines.
- **Regret Dynamics:** Despite prioritizing novelty, PLR-CENIE maintained comparable or slightly higher total regret in its level replay buffer throughout training compared to PLR$_{\perp}$. This indicates that CENIE's novelty objective, by guiding exploration into less covered state-action regions, indirectly facilitates the discovery of high-regret states, even without direct optimization for regret. This outcome suggests that novelty-driven exploration can complement regret-based methods in uncovering diverse and challenging training scenarios.

**Ablation Studies (from Appendix):**
- Ablation models (PLR-CENIE$^{\dagger}$ and ACCEL-CENIE$^{\dagger}$), which used novelty alone for level prioritization, sometimes demonstrated comparable or superior zero-shot transfer performance compared to their regret-only counterparts (PLR$_{\perp}$ and ACCEL), particularly in Minigrid and CarRacing. This suggests that the novelty metric can be a potent curriculum shaper.
- However, the combined regret and novelty objective consistently yielded the strongest overall performance, supporting the synergy between the two metrics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of the Coverage-based Evaluation of Novelty In Environment (CENIE) framework holds several significant implications for the field of Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) and the broader development of generalizable DRL agents.

1. **Addressing Core UED Limitations:** CENIE provides a scalable, domain-agnostic, and curriculum-aware method for quantifying environment novelty, directly addressing critical shortcomings identified in previous UED approaches (e.g., domain-specificity of POET, computational cost of EPOET, and scalability issues of DIPLR). By grounding novelty in the agent's state-action space coverage, CENIE offers a robust and adaptable metric.

2. **Enhancing Generalization Capabilities:** The empirical results across diverse benchmark domains (Minigrid, BipedalWalker, CarRacing) consistently demonstrate that integrating CENIE's novelty objective significantly improves the zero-shot generalization performance of DRL agents. This indicates that explicit encouragement of novel experiences during training is crucial for an agent's ability to adapt to unseen environments.

3. **Complementary Objective to Regret:** The work highlights novelty as a powerful complement to the traditional regret-based objectives in UED. While regret drives complexity, novelty fosters exploration of diverse state-action spaces, mitigating issues like regret stagnation. The observation that novelty-driven exploration can indirectly lead to the discovery of high-regret states (as seen in CarRacing) suggests a synergistic relationship, where exploring unfamiliar territories reveals new learning opportunities. This unification of novelty-driven exploration and regret-driven exploitation offers a more holistic approach to curriculum design.

4. **Broader State-Action Space Exploration:** The quantitative analysis, particularly in the BipedalWalker domain, shows that CENIE-augmented algorithms lead to significantly broader coverage of the agent's state-action space. This expanded exploration is a fundamental requirement for learning robust and transferable policies that can generalize across varied scenarios.

5. **Impact on Autocurricula Design:** CENIE offers a principled mechanism for teachers in UED frameworks to make more informed decisions about which environments to include in a curriculum. By considering both the potential for improvement (regret) and the induction of new experiences (novelty), autocurricula can become more effective and sample-efficient in guiding agent learning. The framework's flexibility allows its integration with various UED algorithms, enhancing their capabilities.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The paper opens several avenues for future research. This includes exploring CENIE's potential for direct environment generation (rather than just prioritization), investigating dynamic weighting schemes for novelty and regret, addressing the curse of dimensionality in high-dimensional state-action spaces (e.g., through dimensionality reduction or latent representations), and experimenting with more advanced density models beyond GMMs to capture complex state-action distributions. Furthermore, the framework's generality positions it for potential crossover applications in Automatic Curriculum Learning (ACL), contributing to a deeper understanding of effective learning progression.

In summary, CENIE represents a step towards developing more intelligent and adaptive autocurricula, fostering the creation of DRL agents that are not only capable in familiar settings but also robust and generalizable to the unknown.
