# Stabilizing Humanoid Robot Trajectory Generation via Physics-Informed Learning and Control-Informed Steering

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Italian Institute of Technology developed a framework to stabilize humanoid robot trajectory generation, combining a physics-informed loss for training with a control-informed steering block during inference. This approach significantly reduced support foot sliding and trajectory drift, allowing for robust real-world deployment on the ergoCub robot for various walking behaviors.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots require smooth, safe, and human-like motion for collaborative environments, which is challenging due to their high degrees of freedom and nonlinear dynamics.
- Imitation Learning (IL) methods generate human-like trajectories but often produce physically unfeasible motions (e.g., foot sliding, trajectory drift) due to limitations in demonstration data.
- Existing Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) applications in humanoid control either use non-differentiable rewards, limiting their influence, or do not leverage IL, potentially resulting in less human-like policies.

## Method

- An end-to-end ==Mode-Adaptive Neural Network (MANN)== learns the full kinematic state of a floating-base robot from human locomotion data.
- A physics-informed loss component is incorporated during training to enforce kinematically feasible contacts, specifically encouraging the support foot to remain stationary.
- A control-informed steering block is applied at inference time to correct trajectory drift by blending user input with network predictions using proportional-integral control concepts.

## Results

- The proposed method, with the control-informed correction block, notably mitigated lateral and yaw drift in forward walking trajectories compared to a baseline MANN model.
- Models trained with the physics-informed loss exhibited reduced support foot sliding, yielding significantly lower RMSE values for both linear and angular velocities (e.g., down from **22.88** m/s and **17.74** rad/s respectively).
- The framework was successfully deployed on the ergoCub humanoid robot using both offline and online tracking controllers for mixed walking behaviors, demonstrating its modularity and real-world applicability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a differentiable physics-informed loss term directly into the supervised learning process significantly enhances the physical feasibility of generated trajectories, reducing support foot sliding.
> - A modular, control-informed correction mechanism at inference time effectively mitigates trajectory drift and steers the robot towards desired user inputs without requiring retraining of the learned model.
> - There is an optimal weighting for the physics-informed loss; excessive weighting can overpower data-fitting, leading to undesirable motion artifacts while satisfying physical constraints (e.g., tip-toe walking).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robot control, particularly in collaborative and human-centric environments, requires robots to exhibit smooth, safe, and human-like motions. Achieving human-likeness and stability is challenging due to the high number of degrees of freedom (DOFs) and complex nonlinear dynamics inherent in humanoid robots. Traditional approaches often employ a hierarchical control structure, typically involving trajectory generation, trajectory adjustment, and trajectory control.

Trajectory optimization methods are broadly categorized into model-based and model-free approaches. Model-based techniques, which can utilize full dynamics or simplified models (e.g., inverted pendulum or centroidal dynamics), aim for high-fidelity motions but are computationally intensive or may compromise human-likeness due to model simplifications. Model-free approaches, on the other hand, leverage optimization to approximate training data, offering computational efficiency. Imitation Learning (IL), a subset of model-free methods, has shown promise in generating human-like trajectories by mimicking demonstration data. Behavior Cloning (BC), a specific form of IL, has been applied to legged locomotion, both in simulation and on physical robots, and has also been integrated with Reinforcement Learning (RL) for robust control.

While IL offers advantages in sample efficiency, problem simplicity, and computational requirements compared to RL, it is inherently limited by the quantity and quality of available demonstration data. This can lead to generated trajectories that violate physical laws, exhibit asymmetries, or display biases, thereby compromising real-world stability through issues such as divergent trajectories or sliding contacts.

Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) has emerged as a strategy to address these limitations by integrating prior knowledge of physical system dynamics into the learning process. PIML combines the strengths of both model-based and learning-based methods, capable of encoding differentiable priors directly into the loss function, which can accelerate convergence and reduce data requirements. Previous applications of PIML in humanoid control have included incorporating physical laws via reward functions or Lipschitz constraints; however, these approaches have either used non-differentiable rewards (limiting their influence on backpropagation) or have not leveraged IL, potentially resulting in less human-like policies.

This work positions itself at the intersection of IL (specifically BC) and PIML, aiming to address the instability and physical constraint violations observed in IL-generated humanoid trajectories. By integrating physics-informed loss components and control-informed steering mechanisms into a BC framework, the authors seek to enhance the physical fidelity and stability of generated trajectories while preserving the human-likeness learned from demonstration data. The research thus contributes to improving the robustness and real-world applicability of learned humanoid locomotion policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to stabilize humanoid robot trajectory generation by improving its physical fidelity and adherence to desired user inputs. This is achieved by addressing specific limitations observed in current imitation learning (IL) methodologies.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in humanoid robot control:

1. **Ensuring Smooth, Safe, and Human-like Motion:** Humanoid robots operating in collaborative environments require motions that are not only smooth and safe but also human-like to foster trust, understanding, and predictability in human-robot interaction. However, achieving "human-likeness" is difficult to define and computationally expensive to enforce via explicit modeling due to the robot's highly nonlinear dynamics and many degrees of freedom.
2. **Limitations of Imitation Learning (IL):** While IL methods, particularly Behavior Cloning (BC), have been successful in generating smooth, human-like trajectories from demonstration data, they are constrained by the amount and quality of available motion data. This dependency often leads to generated trajectories that violate fundamental physical laws, exhibit undesired asymmetries, or display biases. These violations can manifest as divergent trajectories or sliding contacts, which severely limit the real-world stability and deployability of humanoid robots.
3. **Gaps in Existing Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) for Humanoids:** PIML offers a solution by embedding physical priors into the learning process, thereby compensating for data scarcity and promoting physically consistent solutions. However, prior PIML applications in humanoid control have not fully integrated these advantages with IL for human-like motion generation. Some approaches incorporate physical laws through non-differentiable reward terms, which limits their direct influence on the learning gradient, while others do not use IL, potentially resulting in less human-like policies.

To overcome these limitations, the authors propose a two-pronged strategy:

- **Encoding Physics Priors:** Integrate knowledge about the physical laws governing the humanoid robot system directly into the supervised imitation learning process. This aims to promote the generation of kinematically feasible trajectories from the outset.
- **Minimizing Drift with Control Principles:** Apply fundamental control principles at inference time to directly steer the generated output states, thereby counteracting accumulated drift and ensuring closer adherence to desired user inputs without modifying the core learning architecture.

Specifically, the contributions of this paper are detailed as follows:

- **Development of a Physics-informed Loss Component:** Propose a framework that learns the full kinematic state of a floating-base robot. This framework incorporates a physics-informed loss term, in addition to a standard data-fitting loss, to enforce kinematically feasible contacts, specifically encouraging the support foot to remain stationary during contact.
- **Implementation of a Control-informed Correction Scheme:** Introduce a corrective mechanism based on proportional-integral control concepts. This mechanism is applied to the network's output to blend user input with network predictions, effectively reducing trajectory drift and steering the robot towards desired positions and orientations. This block is designed to be modular, operating without requiring retraining or architectural modification of the learned model.
- **Empirical Validation and Ablation Studies:** Validate the proposed approach through ablation studies to isolate the effects of each contribution and perform real-world experiments on the ergoCub humanoid robot using two distinct control architectures (offline and online). This validation aims to demonstrate the efficacy, modularity, and practical applicability of the developed methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper introduces a modular framework designed to enhance the physical fidelity and input-following capabilities of imitation learning-based humanoid trajectory generation. This framework integrates a physics-informed (PI) loss component during training and a control-informed steering block during inference. The overall architecture is summarized in Figure 2 of the paper.

**A. Learning Architecture and Features**
The core learning model adopted is a Mode-Adaptive Neural Network (MANN), which is a supervised learning architecture suitable for generating various locomotion modes.
- **Input Features (x_i):** The input vector at time step *i* comprises a comprehensive set of kinematic state information. This includes vectorized base linear and angular velocities (expressed in the body-fixed frame) sampled over a 2-second window centered at *i-1*, as well as the base position and extrinsic Tait-Bryan angles (orientation) measured in the inertial frame at *i-1*. The full input vector has a dimension of R^(2n+78), where *n* is the number of joints.
- **Output Features (y_i):** The network predicts the desired kinematic state at the next time step *i*. The output vector includes flattened future body-fixed base velocities over a 1-second window starting at the current timestep, along with the base position and orientation at timestep *i*. The full output vector has a dimension of R^(2n+48).
- **MANN Components:** The MANN architecture consists of two neural network components: a gating network and a motion prediction network. The gating network employs a Mixture of Experts (MoE) paradigm to compute blending coefficients for *K* experts. These coefficients are then used to weight the *K* expert weights, which are then applied by the motion prediction network to predict the next state. Unlike prior MANN implementations for locomotion, this work provides the full, 3D kinematic state to the model.

**B. Physics-informed Loss Component**
To address foot sliding and promote physical feasibility, a PI loss component is introduced into the training process.
- **Principle:** The loss encourages the support foot (SF) of the humanoid to remain fixed when in contact with the ground, mathematically expressed as a zero velocity constraint: v_SF = J_SF * ν = 0.
- **Formulation:** This constraint is reformulated to define the base velocity (v_B) as a function of joint velocities (s_dot): v_B = -(J_B_SF)^-1 * J_s_SF * s_dot. This formulation leverages the invertibility of J_B_SF (the Jacobian component mapping to SF velocities from base velocities).
- **Loss Term (L_B):** The PI loss component, L_B, penalizes deviations from this condition by calculating the squared L2-norm of the difference between the predicted base velocity and the base velocity derived from the zero support foot velocity constraint. A binary variable, *α*, indicates whether the left or right foot is the support foot.
- **Overall Loss Function:** The total loss function, L(x; θ), combines the standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) data-fitting loss (L_D) with the PI loss component (L_B): L(x; θ) = L_D(x; θ) + w * L_B(x; θ), where *w* is a positive scalar weight for the PI loss, and w_D is implicitly set to 1. This enables the physics prior to directly influence backpropagation through automatic differentiation.

**C. Control-informed Steering for Network Drift Correction**
To mitigate trajectory drift, a common issue in IL due to data biases, a control-informed correction scheme is applied at inference time. This method operates independently of the training process and the learned model's architecture.
- **Mechanism:** The scheme closes the loop on the robot's velocity vector (ν) by incorporating user-provided desired base pose information (q_B,d).
- **Linear Velocity Controller:** The corrected linear base velocity (B_v_c_I,B) is computed by subtracting a proportional term of the positional error from the network's predicted linear base velocity (B_v_hat_I,B). The error is calculated between the current (I_p_B) and desired (I_p_d_B) base positions, transformed into the body-fixed frame by the current base orientation (I_R_B). A gain *k_0* scales the error.
- **Rotational Velocity Controller:** Similarly, the corrected angular base velocity (B_ω_c_I,B) is derived by subtracting a proportional term of the orientation error from the predicted angular velocity (B_ω_hat_I,B). The orientation error is represented by the vectorized skew-symmetric part of the relative rotation matrix between the desired (I_R_d_B) and current (I_R_B) base orientations, scaled by gain *k_1*.
- **Function:** These corrected velocity outputs serve as the input for the next time step's network prediction, effectively steering the trajectory towards the desired values and introducing dynamic stability features (e.g., slowing down and stopping at a goal).

**D. Data and Training Details**
- **Dataset:** 30 minutes of multi-directional human locomotion data, captured using an XSens sensor suit. The dataset size was doubled by mirroring the base pose across the world xz plane and joint positions across the sagittal plane, resulting in approximately 200,000 data points sampled at 50 Hz.
- **Retargeting:** The human motion data was retargeted onto the ergoCub humanoid robot (160 cm height, 56 kg mass, 26 joints).
- **MANN Training:** The MANN model was trained using PyTorch over 150 epochs. The data was downsampled to 50 Hz, and vectorized batch computations were used. The gating network had one hidden layer with 32 neurons, and the prediction network had one hidden layer with 512 neurons. The AdamW optimizer was employed for training, which required approximately 24 hours on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Ti GPU.

**E. Real-world Control Architectures**
To validate the modularity and practical application of the trajectory generator, two distinct control strategies were used for deployment on the ergoCub robot:
- **Offline Controller:** This controller processes the entire generated trajectory. An offline trajectory adjustment layer stabilizes the Zero Moment Point (ZMP), Center of Mass (CoM) position, and CoM velocity. A whole-body Quadratic Programming (QP) control layer then computes optimal robot velocities based on specified tasks.
- **Online Controller:** This controller performs trajectory generation and adjustment in real-time during deployment. The trajectory adjustment is formulated as a Model Predictive Control (MPC) optimization problem, stabilizing the generated trajectory over a future horizon. The MPC adjusts centroidal momentum and contacts based on predictions and stability constraints. Finally, an online trajectory control layer uses robot feedback to track desired ZMP/CoM, generate swing foot trajectories, and compute joint values via a QP optimization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of the proposed approach involved three main sets of experiments: a comparison of trajectories with and without the proposed components, real-world deployment on the ergoCub robot with different controllers, and ablation studies.

**A. Performance of the Integrated Approach**
The integrated approach, combining the physics-informed loss and the control-informed correction block, demonstrated improvements in trajectory physical feasibility and adherence to user input when compared to a baseline MANN model.
- **Reduced Trajectory Drift:** The proposed method, with the correction block enabled, significantly reduced the deviation from the desired walking direction. For forward walking, lateral (y-axis linear) and yaw (z-axis angular) drift were notably mitigated (Figure 3c) compared to the baseline (Figure 3a) and the model without the correction block (Figure 3b). The x-displacement was expected as it corresponds to the walking direction.
- **Improved Physical Feasibility (Foot Sliding):** Models trained with the physics-informed loss exhibited reduced support foot sliding. The baseline model showed average support foot RMSE values of 22.88 m/s for linear velocities and 17.74 rad/s for angular velocities. In contrast, the proposed approach, across various PI loss weights (w ∈ {0, 1, 10, 20}), yielded significantly lower RMSE values, particularly for angular velocities (Figure 4), indicating improved stability during locomotion.

**B. Real-world Experiments on the ergoCub Robot**
The approach's modularity and real-world applicability were demonstrated by deploying the trajectory generator with two different tracking controllers on the ergoCub humanoid robot. Both offline and online controllers successfully tracked the generated trajectories for mixed walking behaviors (standing, forward, side, backward).
- **Controller Performance:** The offline DCM controller achieved slightly faster walking speeds and larger step sizes (0.33x speed, 0.4x step size) compared to the online MPC controller (0.25x speed, 0.3x step size). The offline controller also produced noticeably smoother joint trajectory tracking (Figure 5a) than the online controller (Figure 5b), which was attributed to the online controller's lower operating frequency and slower reaction speed to rapid motions.
- **Emergent Behavior and Controller Interaction:** An emergent human-like behavior observed was the MANN's generation of backward-facing neck configurations during backward walking. The controllers modified MANN outputs that exceeded joint limits (e.g., neck yaw limits for backward walking, producing small torso yaw displacements for side walking).

**C. Ablation Studies**
Ablation studies were conducted to quantify the isolated effects of the drift correction block and the physics-informed loss component.

1. **Drift Correction Block:**
 - **Drift Mitigation:** The correction block effectively mitigated trajectory drift. When disabled, the model (even with physics-informed training) exhibited greater lateral (y) and yaw drift than the baseline (Figure 3b vs. 3a). This increased drift without correction was attributed to the new model's greater versatility, allowing variations in base roll and pitch.
 - **Trajectory Adherence:** With the correction block enabled, the ground-projected base displacement for forward walking significantly adhered to the desired direction (Figure 6).

2. **Physics-informed Loss Component:**
 - **Optimal Weight 'w':** A statistical analysis varying the PI loss weight (w ∈ {0, 1, 10, 20, 100}) showed that increasing *w* up to 10 resulted in a decrease in support foot sliding (Figure 4).
 - **Over-weighting Effects:** When *w* was excessively large (e.g., w=100), the PI loss component overpowered the data-fitting loss. This led to undesirable artifacts, such as excessive foot rotation (Figure 7), which compromised the smoothness and human-likeness of trajectories. This was interpreted as the learned model "cheating" by keeping only the heel on the ground to satisfy the zero-velocity constraint, rather than maintaining a parallel foot. Consequently, models trained with w=100 could not walk without the drift correction block, but could walk with it enabled, suggesting the correction block can compensate for such learned artifacts.
 - **Foot Height Consistency:** The PI loss significantly improved the consistency of foot height during contact phases. Compared to a model trained with only the data-fitting loss, the model with w=10 PI loss showed substantially decreased support foot displacement and more consistent step heights during forward locomotion (Figure 8).
 - **Compromise:** Training with w=10 was determined to provide the optimal balance between minimizing contact foot sliding and maintaining stable walking behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a methodological advance in the field of humanoid robot control, addressing critical limitations in trajectory generation using imitation learning (IL). The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Stability and Physical Fidelity:** The primary contribution lies in demonstrably improving the physical fidelity and stability of humanoid robot trajectories. By integrating physics-informed learning and control-informed steering, the method reduces issues such as divergent trajectories and sliding contacts, which are common shortcomings of pure IL approaches that rely solely on demonstration data. This enhancement contributes directly to safer and more reliable robot operation in real-world environments.
- **Addressing Data Limitations in IL:** The physics-informed loss component allows the learning process to incorporate prior knowledge of physical laws. This helps to compensate for the limitations of finite or imperfect demonstration data, a pervasive challenge in IL. By encoding differentiable priors, the approach can potentially speed up convergence and reduce the overall data required to infer underlying physical behaviors.
- **Mitigation of Trajectory Drift:** The control-informed correction block effectively mitigates trajectory drift, which can arise from data biases or inherent asymmetries in learned policies. By actively steering the network's output towards desired positions and orientations, the robot can follow user inputs more accurately, improving predictability and control. This "closed-loop" aspect, applied at inference time without retraining the network, offers a practical solution to maintain trajectory integrity.
- **Preservation of Human-likeness:** Unlike some model-based or physics-only approaches that may compromise the naturalness of motion, this work integrates physics priors within an IL framework. This allows the system to retain the human-like qualities learned from demonstration data while simultaneously ensuring physical plausibility, a crucial aspect for human-robot interaction and tasks designed for human capabilities.
- **Modularity and Generalizability:** Both the physics-informed architecture and the control-informed correction block are designed to be modular. This characteristic implies that these components are not restricted to the specific MANN architecture or the ergoCub platform and could potentially be applied to other deep learning models or robotic systems to enhance their adherence to physical laws and desired behaviors. This generalizability expands the potential impact beyond humanoid locomotion to other robotics and machine learning applications.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The findings lay a foundation for further exploration in physics-informed machine learning for robotics. Future work can investigate the integration of other physics priors (e.g., ground reaction forces, dynamic stability constraints) into the loss function, and explore the application of these concepts with more recent and advanced IL methods, such as diffusion models, to further refine and robustify robot motion generation.

In conclusion, this research provides a practical and effective framework for generating stable, physically consistent, and human-like trajectories for humanoid robots. Its modular design and empirical validation underscore its potential to advance the capabilities and safety of humanoid systems in complex, real-world scenarios.
