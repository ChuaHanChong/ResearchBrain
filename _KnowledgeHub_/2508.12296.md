# A robust and compliant robotic assembly control strategy for batch precision assembly task with uncertain fit types and fit amounts

> [!summary] Summary
> Wang et al. developed FVFC-MTRL-PD, a robust and compliant robotic control strategy for batch precision assembly with uncertain fit types and amounts. This method achieved a 98.5% average success rate across diverse fit conditions and demonstrated superior force compliance, significantly reducing contact forces while maintaining robustness to unseen components and minor manufacturing defects.

## Problem

- Robotic precision assembly tasks face challenges with uncertain fit types (clearance or interference) and varying fit amounts due to unavoidable machining errors in batch manufacturing.
- Existing control methods, both model-based and learning-based, often lack robustness to continuous variations in fit, requiring tedious re-parameterization or retraining for different components.
- High-precision assembly, particularly with delicate parts and interference fits, demands low contact forces to prevent damage, a critical aspect often not adequately addressed by prior robust reinforcement learning strategies.

## Method

- The FVFC-MTRL-PD framework decomposes the overall batch assembly task, characterized by a continuous range of uncertain fit amounts, into multiple deterministic subtasks.
- A Multi-Task ==Reinforcement Learning== (MTRL) approach, driven by a Force-Vision Fusion Controller (FVFC), is used to efficiently learn compliant control strategies for each subtask, leveraging shared networks and gradient projection.
- Policy distillation is then applied to integrate these subtask-specific policies into a single, robust student policy capable of generalizing to unseen and continuously varying fit conditions without requiring explicit task encoding.

## Results

- FVFC-MTRL-PD achieved a **98.5%** average success rate across four distinct subtasks and maintained consistent high performance (96-**100%** success rate) on eight groups of randomly selected, unseen batch-manufactured components with varying fit amounts.
- The method demonstrated superior force compliance, achieving the lowest maximum contact forces and moments (e.g., maximum Fx of 0.207N in small interference fit) and higher assembly efficiency compared to baseline methods like FBCC and MDRL.
- It successfully generalized beyond the training range, handling interference forces higher than those observed during training, and showed robustness to manufacturing defects such as non-chamfered edges or peg deformations, achieving approximately **92%** and **88%** success rates, respectively, in scenarios where baselines completely failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing the continuous uncertainty of batch precision assembly into discrete subtasks for multi-task reinforcement learning, followed by policy distillation, effectively constructs a robust policy that generalizes across continuous fit variations.
> - The Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning framework, utilizing shared networks and PCGrad, significantly improves training efficiency and stability, achieving over 50% sample efficiency gain compared to single-task learning.
> - Integrating a Force-Vision Fusion Controller within the reinforcement learning loop provides enhanced assembly capability and force compliance, proving superior to purely force-based or vision-only approaches, which is critical for preventing damage in high-precision tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of robots into industrial manufacturing, particularly for peg-in-hole assembly tasks, has demonstrated benefits in production efficiency and product quality. This research operates within the domain of robotic precision assembly, specifically addressing the challenges posed by batch manufacturing where components exhibit uncertain fit types and fit amounts due to unavoidable machining errors. Such uncertainties are prevalent in high-precision industrial applications, including the assembly of 3C (computers, communication, and consumer electronics) products that often involve tight clearance or small interference fits (e.g., 0.01 mm).

Existing precision assembly control methods generally fall into two categories: model-based and learning-based approaches.
Model-based methods attempt to mathematically model the relationship between peg-in-hole pose errors and sensor signals (e.g., force, vision, tactile). Visual servo control offers precise alignment but can be time-consuming and lacks direct force control. Force-based control methods, relying on jamming analysis, Gaussian models, or elastic mechanics, achieve compliance but often suffer from oversimplified models, limiting their performance and robustness, especially for complex geometries or varying contact dynamics induced by different fit types. Force-vision fusion controllers (FVFC) combine these modalities to enhance both speed and compliance, but their parameters are typically manually designed and require re-design for changes in component fit. A significant limitation of model-based approaches is their lack of robustness to variations in fit types and amounts, necessitating manual re-parameterization.

Learning-based methods, in contrast, utilize data rather than explicit theoretical models. Demonstration learning and supervised learning rely on expert trajectories or pre-labeled datasets, which are challenging to acquire reliably for precision assembly tasks. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers an advantage by continuously learning through online interaction, often by adjusting pre-defined controller parameters or outputting residual actions. However, most existing RL-based strategies for assembly are tailored to specific components used during training and exhibit limited robustness when confronted with variations in shape, size, or fit type, often requiring retraining or transfer learning.

While some research has explored robust RL strategies for low-precision assembly (0.5mm-2.0mm clearance fit), these studies often rely on domain randomization (which faces significant sim-to-real gaps for contact force dynamics) or feature encoders that are not generalizable to new components. Crucially, these approaches often prioritize success rate without adequately evaluating contact force compliance, a critical requirement for high-precision tasks to prevent damage to parts, particularly in interference fits.

The current paper identifies a research gap: the absence of robust RL control strategies specifically designed for batch precision assembly with continuously varying and unknown fit amounts and types, while simultaneously ensuring force compliance. It notes that prior work on robustness typically addresses discrete, known categories of shapes, rather than a continuous range of uncertain fit amounts. The authors draw inspiration from multi-task reinforcement learning (MTRL) and policy distillation techniques from general artificial intelligence, which have shown potential for improving robustness and integrating diverse knowledge, to address this specific challenge in robotic assembly.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the demands of high-precision industrial manufacturing, particularly in the mass production of components for products like 3C electronics. In such scenarios, robots are increasingly deployed for precision assembly tasks. A core challenge arises from manufacturing variability: even when components are designed with transition fits (where clearances or interferences can occur), machining errors inevitably lead to a range of uncertain fit types (clearance or interference) and varying fit amounts for individual peg-hole pairs within a batch. Due to the high volume of components, individual measurement of each part prior to assembly is typically not feasible or cost-effective, meaning the exact fit type and amount for any given assembly operation are unknown to the robotic system.

This uncertainty in fit states poses a significant obstacle to the reliable deployment of robotic assembly solutions. Different fit types and amounts result in substantially different mechanical properties and contact dynamics during insertion. Consequently, a control strategy designed for a specific fit condition may fail or cause damage when applied to another. Current robotic assembly controllers often lack the robustness required to effectively manage this inherent variability, leading to reduced success rates or excessive contact forces that can damage delicate parts. Ensuring low contact forces is especially critical for high-precision components, where small interference fits are common and jamming or wedging can easily occur, leading to operational failures or material deformation.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this research are detailed as follows:
1. **Develop a robust robotic assembly control strategy:** The primary objective is to create a control strategy that can consistently perform high-precision peg-in-hole assembly tasks on batches of components, effectively accommodating uncertain fit types and fit amounts that vary continuously within a predefined range.
2. **Ensure high compliance:** The strategy must maintain low contact forces and moments throughout the assembly process, thereby preventing damage to delicate components, particularly during insertion with tight clearances or interference fits.
3. **Enhance training efficiency:** To make the development of such robust strategies practical for real-world deployment, the research aims to establish an efficient training methodology for the underlying learning algorithms, minimizing the time and hardware resources required.
4. **Integrate multi-modal sensory information:** The control strategy should effectively fuse information from both force/moment sensors and vision systems to gain a comprehensive understanding of the contact state and relative pose between the peg and hole, even under conditions like suction cup deformation.
5. **Generalize capabilities through policy integration:** The approach aims to leverage knowledge from multiple specific assembly scenarios (subtasks) to construct a unified policy that generalizes effectively to unknown fit amounts, moving beyond task-specific policies.

In summary, the research is motivated by the industrial need for automated precision assembly that can handle real-world manufacturing variability with reliability and delicacy, and it aims to achieve this through a novel integration of force-vision fusion, multi-task reinforcement learning, and policy distillation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed FVFC-MTRL-PD (Force-Vision Fusion Controller-driven Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning with Policy Distillation), is an efficient framework designed to construct a robust and compliant control strategy for robotic batch precision assembly tasks. It comprises three main stages: task decomposition, subtask strategies learning, and strategies integration.

#### 3.1. Task Decomposition

The batch precision assembly task, characterized by a continuous range of uncertain fit amounts, is initially decomposed into multiple deterministic subtasks. This is achieved by dividing the overall fit amount interval (e.g., from minimum peg dimension minus maximum hole dimension to maximum peg dimension minus minimum hole dimension) into several smaller sub-intervals. For each sub-interval, a representative set of assembly components with a predetermined fit amount is generated, forming distinct subtasks (T1, T2..., Tn). A linear subdivision scheme is employed, based on the principle that contact forces vary approximately linearly with fit amounts, to uniformly cover the possible force state space. In the experimental setup, `n` is set to 4, defining subtasks with large interference, small interference, small clearance, and large clearance fits, respectively. This decomposition allows for local robustness learning within each sub-interval by randomizing only initial alignment errors, rather than directly randomizing the fit amount itself, which would complicate RL training due to significant changes in contact dynamics. The framework is also noted to be adaptable for different geometric shapes by altering the decomposition scheme.

#### 3.2. Force-Vision Fusion Controller-Driven Reinforcement Learning (FVFC-MTRL)

For each defined subtask, a control strategy is learned using a combination of a Force-Vision Fusion Controller (FVFC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL).

- **Assembly System Definition:** A six-axis serial robot (UR10) equipped with an F/M sensor (SRI M3815B) and a flexible suction cup gripper (SMC) is used. The peg is affixed to the suction cup. Two orthogonal monocular cameras (Hik MV-CH120-10UC) provide visual feedback. Coordinate systems are defined for the robot (O_R), F/M sensor (O_S), peg (O_P), and cameras (O_C1, O_C2) to describe movements and sensor data. The peg coordinate system (O_P) is dynamically adjusted to the insertion region of the peg, which enhances the accuracy of estimated contact moments.

- **Force-Vision Fusion Controller Design:** The FVFC aims to maintain small contact forces and pose errors. It computes Cartesian pose commands `p_R(t)` for the robot.
 - **Force Control:** The 6-D force and moment signal `F_S(t)` from the F/M sensor is filtered, gravity-compensated, and transformed into the peg coordinate system `F_P(t)`. This transformation accounts for the insertion depth `l_hat` to approximate moment arms. A proportional controller then generates translational and rotational adjustments `p_delta_F` based on `F_P(t)` and a reference force `F_ref`, using a diagonal stiffness matrix `K_F`.
 - **Vision Control:** Two monocular cameras capture images (`I_C1(t), I_C2(t)`). A feature extraction module (using ROI extraction, adaptive binary segmentation, Ed-Lines detection) identifies corner points and edge lines of the peg. These visual features `V_P(t)` accurately represent the translation and rotation error between the peg and hole, implicitly indicating jamming. A vision controller generates additional translational and rotational adjustments `p_delta_V` along the X_P and Y_P directions using parameters `k_V`.
 - **Fusion and Robot Command:** The total relative motion of the peg `p_delta_P` is the sum of `p_delta_F` and `p_delta_V`. This is then transformed into the robot end-effector motion command `p_delta_R` and integrated to obtain the absolute pose command `p_R(t)`.

- **Combining RL with FVFC:** An RL agent adaptively adjusts the parameters of the FVFC to optimize comprehensive control performance.
 - **State (`s_t`):** Comprises the robot’s relative pose (difference from initial pose), transformed force features (`F_P(t)`), and vision features (`V_P(t)`). A multi-step stack (`m=5`) of `s_t` forms the temporal state for an LSTM network.
 - **Action (`a_t`):** The RL agent outputs adaptive adjustments (`delta_k`) to the FVFC controller parameters (`K_F`, `k_V`). The actual parameters used by FVFC are `k_hat + a_t`, where `k_hat` are manually pre-set baseline parameters.
 - **Reward Function:** A composite reward function is designed, penalizing high forces/moments (`r_FM`), high control steps (`r_stp`), and rewarding insertion progress (`r_i`). A sparse reward (`r_S`) is given for success or failure. The penalty for axial force (`F_z,t`) is omitted in interference-fit subtasks where large axial forces are unavoidable.
 - **Failure Conditions:** Defined by thresholds on `F_S,max'`, `V_P,max`, or maximum control steps `T_max`. Success is defined by reaching a target insertion depth `H`.

- **Efficient MTRL Training Method:** To improve training efficiency and leverage correlations among subtasks, a Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning (MTRL) framework is adopted.
 - **Algorithm:** Multi-task Soft Actor-Critic (MTSAC) is used.
 - **Shared Networks:** Both the actor and critic networks are shared across all subtasks.
 - **Subtask Encoding:** To differentiate tasks, a one-hot encoding `c_Tj` for task `Tj` is appended to the temporal state `s_t`, forming an augmented state `s_t_tilde` as the input to the networks.
 - **Replay Buffers:** Separate replay buffers (RB1 to RBn) are maintained for each subtask.
 - **Task Selection:** A dynamic task selector chooses the next subtask to execute based on the inverse proportion of transitions collected in each replay buffer, promoting balanced sampling.
 - **Gradient Handling:** Projecting Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad) is employed to mitigate gradient conflicts between different subtasks, thereby stabilizing the training process.
 - **Entropy Coefficients:** Task-specific disentangled entropy coefficients (`alpha_j`) are used and automatically adjusted to support task-specific exploration.
 - **Update Frequency:** Network parameters are updated using mini-batches sampled from all replay buffers whenever a new transition is collected from any subtask.
 The MTRL process trains a single policy network that, when given a specific subtask encoding, generates a task-specific compliant control strategy (`pi_j`).

#### 3.3. Policy Distillation for Robustness Strategy (FVFC-MTRL-PD)

The final stage constructs a single, robust control strategy (`pi_rho`) that generalizes across uncertain fit amounts, independent of subtask encodings.
- **Teacher Models:** The `n` task-specific policies (`pi_j`) learned during MTRL training serve as multiple teacher models.
- **Knowledge Transfer:** Policy distillation is performed via supervised learning. The experience data (transitions) collected and stored in the replay buffers during MTRL training is reused.
- **Dataset Generation:** For each transition `(s_t_tilde, a_t, r_t, s_t+1_tilde)` in the replay buffers, the trained MTRL actor network generates an optimal action `pi_phi(s_t_tilde)`. The subtask encoding is then removed from `s_t_tilde` to recover the original state `s_t`. This forms a labeled dataset of `(s_t, pi_phi(s_t_tilde))` pairs.
- **Student Network:** A new policy network (`pi_rho`), implemented with a deeper and wider LSTM module, is trained using this labeled dataset. The objective is for `pi_rho` to implicitly infer the fit amount from the assembly trajectory (vision and force) without explicit task encoding.
- **Loss Function:** The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence is used to minimize the difference between the student network's output distribution and the teacher network's output distribution.
The resulting distillation network (`pi_rho`) is then directly deployed for batch precision assembly, capable of handling varying fit types and amounts within the specified range, and demonstrating generalization to unseen fit amounts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of the proposed FVFC-MTRL-PD method was validated through real-world experiments involving the batch precision assembly of irregular hexagonal components with uncertain fit types and fit amounts. Comparisons were made against several baseline methods: FBCC (Feature-Based Compliance Control, a constant-parameter force control), MDRL (Model-Driven Reinforcement Learning, combining STRL with a force controller), and FVFC-CP (FVFC with constant parameters).

#### 4.1. Training Process Efficiency

- **MTRL vs. STRL:** The training process of FVFC-MTRL demonstrated significantly faster convergence and greater stability compared to FVFC-STRL (which trains subtasks individually). FVFC-MTRL's average reward across four subtasks converged around 50k steps, while FVFC-STRL fluctuated and converged slower. This indicates that the MTRL framework effectively leveraged similarities among subtasks, improving sample efficiency by over 50%.
- **FVFC vs. Force-Based Control:** The final reward of MDRL was notably lower than FVFC-based methods, indicating the superior assembly capability of the Force-Vision Fusion Controller over purely force-based controllers.
- **Policy Distillation:** During the policy distillation process, the Mean Square Error (MSE) for both the predicted mean and standard deviation of the action Gaussian distribution consistently decreased on the testing dataset. This reduction in MSE suggests that the student network successfully learned to mimic the decision-making behaviors of the multiple teacher strategies.

#### 4.2. Compliance and Robustness Verification on Subtasks

- **Overall Performance:** FVFC-MTRL-PD achieved the highest average reward (22.1) and success rate (98.5%) across the four distinct subtasks (T1-T4, covering large interference to large clearance fits). This demonstrated its ability to maintain high performance while exhibiting robustness across varying fit conditions.
- **Comparative Performance:** FVFC-MTRL-PD outperformed all baseline methods: FBCC (1.7 reward / 42.0% success rate), MDRL (5.8 reward / 54.0% success rate), and FVFC-CP (8.6 reward / 61.0% success rate). While FVFC-MTRL (non-distilled, task-specific policies) showed strong performance (21.6 reward / 97.0% success rate) in its respective subtasks, it lacked generalization, with performance degrading (e.g., 82% success rate) when policies were applied to different subtasks. FVFC-MTRL-PD successfully preserved the high performance of FVFC-MTRL while gaining robustness.
- **Force Compliance and Efficiency:** Quantitative evaluation of successful assembly trials in subtasks T2 (small interference) and T4 (large clearance) revealed that FVFC-MTRL-PD achieved the lowest maximum contact forces (Fx, Fy, Fz) and moments (Mx, My, Mz), as well as the highest assembly efficiency (fewer control steps), compared to all other methods. For instance, in T2, FVFC-MTRL-PD's maximum Fx was 0.207N, significantly lower than FBCC's 0.656N. Similarly, in T4, its maximum Fz was 0.202N, compared to FBCC's 1.818N. Force trajectory plots further illustrated that FVFC-MTRL-PD minimized jamming and maintained substantially reduced contact forces throughout the insertion process in both interference and clearance fits.

#### 4.3. Batch Precision Assembly Verification

- **Generalization to Unseen Components:** FVFC-MTRL-PD was tested on eight groups (G1-G8) of randomly selected, batch-manufactured components, each with uncertain and potentially un-encountered fit amounts within the range of -0.04 mm to 0.04 mm.
- **Consistent Superiority:** The proposed method consistently demonstrated superior performance, achieving high rewards and success rates across all eight groups. For clearance-fit groups (G1, G2), it reached 100% success rate. For interference-fit groups (G3-G8), success rates ranged from 96% to 100%. In contrast, MDRL, even when using the policy trained for the most similar subtask, exhibited lower and less stable performance (e.g., 22% success rate for G3, 36% for G4).
- **Beyond Training Range:** FVFC-MTRL-PD successfully generalized to Group G6, where the interference force (5.18 N) exceeded the range of interference forces observed in the four training subtasks (approx. 1N-3N), demonstrating an ability to handle extrapolation to some extent.
- **Robustness to Defects:** For intentionally challenging scenarios (G7 with non-chamfered edges, G8 with manufacturing-induced peg deformations), MDRL failed completely (0% success rate), while FVFC-MTRL-PD still achieved success rates of approximately 92% and 88%, respectively. This indicates a notable robustness beyond just fit amount uncertainty, extending to certain types of manufacturing defects.
- **Force Heatmaps:** Force heatmaps, showing contact forces over 40 trials (5 for each group), confirmed that FVFC-MTRL-PD maintained stable and low contact forces (Fx, Fy peak below 0.7 N) across all component groups, further validating its superior force compliance and robustness in real-world batch assembly scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic precision assembly, particularly for industrial applications involving high-volume manufacturing with inherent component variability. The primary significance of this work lies in its direct addressing of a critical, under-researched challenge: developing robotic assembly strategies that are robust and compliant in the face of uncertain and continuously varying fit types and fit amounts within a batch of components.

The potential impact is multi-faceted:

- **Enabling Reliable Automation in High-Precision Manufacturing:** Current limitations in handling uncertain fit types and amounts hinder the widespread and reliable deployment of robots in high-precision industries (e.g., 3C electronics). This method offers a robust control strategy that can accommodate such real-world manufacturing variabilities, making automated assembly more dependable and reducing the need for costly manual adjustments or individual part pre-measurement.
- **Improved Product Quality and Reduced Damage:** By ensuring high force compliance and minimizing peak contact forces during assembly, the proposed method reduces the risk of part damage, especially with delicate or tightly fitted components. This directly contributes to higher product quality, lower scrap rates, and reduced manufacturing costs.
- **Enhanced Training Efficiency for Complex Tasks:** The introduction of the Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning (MTRL) framework significantly improves the efficiency of training assembly strategies. By leveraging inherent correlations across different subtasks, it reduces the sample complexity and training time by over 50% compared to single-task learning. This makes the development of complex, robust policies more practical for industrial implementation, where time and resource constraints are significant.
- **Generalizable Framework:** The modular nature of the proposed framework, particularly the task decomposition and policy distillation stages, suggests broader applicability. The authors indicate that by modifying the task decomposition scheme, the method could also be used to construct robust assembly strategies for components with varying geometric shapes, extending its utility beyond just fit amount variations. This generalizability enhances its long-term impact on flexible manufacturing systems.
- **Advancing Reinforcement Learning for Robotics:** The work demonstrates an effective integration of deep reinforcement learning techniques (MTRL, policy distillation) with classical control (Force-Vision Fusion Controller) to solve a complex real-world robotics problem. This hybrid approach capitalizes on the strengths of both, providing a blueprint for developing intelligent, adaptive, and robust robotic skills.

In the near future, this research is poised to facilitate the deployment of more autonomous and adaptive robotic systems in assembly lines where component variability is a given. It also highlights important future research directions, such as developing more accurate simulation environments for precision assembly and improving sim-to-real transfer techniques, which are crucial for further advancements in this domain.
