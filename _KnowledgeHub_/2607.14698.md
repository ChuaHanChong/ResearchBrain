# Lights, Camera, Malfunction: When Illumination Robustness Leaves VLA Models Blind to Color

> [!summary] Summary
> Keio University researchers identified that Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models exhibit fundamental generalization vulnerabilities to subtle physical lighting changes and that naive adversarial training can inadvertently degrade their color perception. They developed ChromaGuard, a hue-preserving adversarial training method, which successfully defends against severe lighting attacks while maintaining the model's ability to accurately perceive and utilize color information, achieving high success rates in color-dependent tasks.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art VLA models struggle with generalizability, failing under minor, non-adversarial environmental perturbations like localized lighting changes.
- Standard adversarial training methods, particularly those using aggressive visual data augmentation, can inadvertently condition VLA models to disregard essential visual features like color.
- The challenge lies in developing VLA models that are robust to environmental variations without sacrificing semantic understanding, especially for safety-critical real-world applications.

## Method

- Proposed **FLARE** (Framework for Lighting Adversarial Robustness Evaluation), an optimized ==black-box physical spotlight attack== to generate worst-case illumination scenarios against VLA models.
- Introduced **ChromaGuard**, a ==chroma-preserving adversarial training== methodology that restricts ==hue perturbations== while allowing other bounded augmentations, aiming to maintain color perception.
- Conducted extensive experiments in both simulated (LeRobot, ==LIBERO benchmark==) and real-world environments using SmolVLA and "0.5 architectures to evaluate vulnerabilities and defense strategies.

## Results

- The FLARE framework reduced baseline VLA success rates to **0.0%** across all simulation task suites, inducing physical trajectory errors up to **115.5 cm**.
- Naive-Aug models showed significant degradation in real-world color-dependent tasks, with SmolVLA's success rate dropping to **47.5%** (from **77.5%** benign) and **85.7%** of failures due to incorrect color grasping.
- ChromaGuard effectively defended against spotlight attacks in both color-invariant and color-dependent tasks, achieving high success rates (e.g., SmolVLA **92.5%** SR under attack in color-dependent tasks) while preserving accurate color perception.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA models possess a fundamental generalization vulnerability to subtle physical environmental perturbations, leading to catastrophic task failures and significant trajectory deviations (e.g., 0.0% success rate under FLARE attacks).
> - Naive adversarial training with indiscriminate color augmentation inadvertently causes VLA models to treat color information as noise, effectively turning them into shape-biased, grayscale processors.
> - Achieving true robustness for VLA models requires not only defending against attacks but also preserving perceptual fidelity, especially for semantic cues like color.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an emerging paradigm in robotic control, extending large-scale vision-language learning to enable general-purpose robot manipulation. These models have demonstrated capabilities in diverse applications, including household manipulation, autonomous driving, and industrial automation, by allowing robotic agents to generalize across a wide array of dynamic tasks.

However, the transition of VLA models from controlled laboratory environments to real-world, safety-critical deployments has exposed vulnerabilities arising from their tightly coupled multimodal architectures. Prior research has identified various threats to VLA models, such as physical sensor attacks, adversarial attacks, and training-time backdoor injections. The present work contributes to this landscape by suggesting that a fundamental underlying issue is not solely advanced security vulnerabilities but rather a broader lack of generalizability to minor environmental perturbations.

Specifically, the paper positions itself within a context where state-of-the-art VLA models are reported to perform poorly under small task and position perturbations, as highlighted by benchmarks like LIBERO-PRO. This indicates that current VLA models may be fundamentally susceptible to even subtle environmental changes. Adversarial training is a recognized countermeasure for improving model robustness and generalizability across various deep neural network applications, including VLAs. However, this paper identifies and investigates a critical limitation in the naive application of adversarial training, particularly concerning visual data augmentation, which can inadvertently degrade a model's semantic understanding of visual cues, such as color. The research therefore contributes to the ongoing effort to develop robust and reliable VLA systems by focusing on an overlooked aspect of environmental robustness and its implications for semantic perception.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research outlines several key objectives driven by the motivation to enhance the robustness and reliability of VLA models for real-world deployment.

The **primary objective** is to systematically expose a fundamental generalization vulnerability in state-of-the-art VLA models. The authors specifically aim to demonstrate that these models can fail on standard benchmarks when subjected to minor physical environmental variations, such as localized changes in lighting conditions caused by a targeted physical spotlight. This objective is motivated by the observation that despite their advanced capabilities, VLA models exhibit poor generalizability, rendering them susceptible to relatively simple, non-adversarial perturbations that could occur in benign real-world scenarios.

A **secondary objective** is to identify and diagnose a critical pitfall in standard adversarial training methods, particularly those employing naive data augmentations. The paper aims to demonstrate that while such augmentations might appear to improve robustness to lighting changes, they can inadvertently condition VLA models to disregard or incorrectly process essential visual features, specifically color information, treating it as noise. This objective is motivated by the hypothesis that a simple countermeasure like broad HSV-space data augmentation, intended to improve generalizability, might degrade the model's capacity to perform tasks where fine-grained color information is crucial.

The **tertiary objective** is to propose and empirically validate a novel adversarial training methodology designed to mitigate the identified pitfall. This method, termed ChromaGuard, aims to provide robust defensive capabilities against severe lighting attacks while simultaneously preserving the model's ability to accurately perceive and utilize color information in semantic tasks. The motivation here is to develop a defense strategy that resolves the trade-off between robustness to illumination variations and the maintenance of perceptual fidelity, addressing the "defense dilemma" where traditional augmentations lead to semantic degradation.

Overall, the underlying motivation stems from the increasing deployment of VLA models in safety-critical, real-world applications. Ensuring that these models are robust not only against advanced cyber threats but also against common environmental fluctuations is essential for their practical viability. The research highlights the necessity for VLA models to develop genuinely robust visual representations rather than simply learning to ignore perturbed visual cues, emphasizing a critical lesson for embodied AI development regarding the potential for handcrafted countermeasures to introduce detrimental biases.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted methodology encompassing a defined threat model, a novel attack framework, a proposed defense strategy, and extensive experimental evaluations in both simulated and real-world environments.

#### 3.1. Threat Model

The study assumes a strict black-box threat model, where the adversary has no access to the target VLA model’s internal architecture, weights, gradient information, or training datasets. The adversary can deploy physical lighting fixtures within the robot’s workspace and control their spatial placement, illumination footprint, intensity, and specific chromatic properties (hue, saturation, value). Once deployed, these parameters remain fixed throughout an evaluation episode, meaning the adversary cannot dynamically change the light.

#### 3.2. FLARE Framework: Optimized Physical Spotlight Attack

The Framework for Lighting Adversarial Robustness Evaluation (FLARE) is proposed to generate worst-case lighting scenarios. It simulates a physically plausible spotlight attack that does not modify the robot, camera, or target objects, operating solely through illumination.
- **Parameterization:** The spotlight is parameterized by `θ = [h_light, s_light, v_light, I, z_light, α_cutoff]`, representing hue, saturation, value, intensity, spatial height, and cutoff angle, respectively.
- **Attack Objective:** The goal is to maximize a combined objective score `J(θ)` over multiple episodes. For each episode, a loss function `L_i(θ)` is calculated, which combines the average trajectory deviation (`||P_t - P'_t||^2`), the final trajectory deviation (`||P_T - P'_T||^2`), and a binary indicator of task failure (`I_f`). `P_t` and `P'_t` represent clean and attacked end-effector positions. Weighting hyperparameters `λ_1` and `λ_2` balance these components.
- **Optimization:** Bayesian Optimization with a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler (using Optuna) is employed to efficiently search the continuous spotlight parameter space. TPE is chosen for its computational efficiency in high-dimensional search spaces under budget constraints, especially given the cost of robot rollouts. To prevent overfitting and manage computational load, tasks are alternately evaluated in subsets during optimization.

#### 3.3. ChromaGuard: Hue-Preserving Adversarial Training

To address the defense dilemma, ChromaGuard is introduced as a chroma-preserving adversarial training method.
- **Naive Data Augmentation Baseline (Naive-Aug):** A standard approach, this involves aggressive perturbations to hue, saturation, brightness, contrast, and sharpness, randomly sampled from uniform distributions. The paper defines these as `ϕ = [h, s, v, c, γ]`.
- **ChromaGuard Approach:** In contrast, ChromaGuard restricts the augmentation parameter space. It fixes hue perturbation to zero (`h = 0`) while allowing bounded perturbations to saturation, brightness, contrast, and sharpness. This constrained parameter space (Φ_CG) is then used within a standard adversarial objective: `min_w E_x,y [max_ϕ∈Φ_CG L(f_w(T(x; ϕ)), y)]`. This approach aims to simulate challenging lighting conditions without altering the intrinsic colors of objects, thus preserving essential hue information.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Simulation Experiments:**
 - **Framework:** LeRobot framework, using the LIBERO multi-task manipulation benchmark.
 - **VLA Architecture:** SmolVLA architecture, trained for 200,000 steps with pre-trained vision/language backbones and a scratch-trained action expert.
 - **Models:** Baseline (no augmentation) and Naive-Aug (broad HSV-space augmentation).
 - **Evaluation:** 500 episodes per task suite (LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-10). Attacks included random spot lighting and FLARE-optimized attacks.
 - **Metrics:** Task Success Rate (SR), Average Trajectory Error (TE-Avg), and Maximum Trajectory Error (TE-Max). TE is computed specifically for episodes where the baseline succeeds but fails under attack to isolate adversarial influence.
- **Real-World Experiments:**
 - **Hardware:** 6-DoF SO-101 Arm Pro manipulator, dual cameras (wrist-mounted and static third-person), and an external programmable RGB spotlight.
 - **Task Configurations:** Two types: color-invariant (e.g., picking a red ball to a black bin) and color-dependent (e.g., selecting between identical red and blue balls based on language commands).
 - **VLA Architectures:** SmolVLA and π0.5 architectures, fine-tuned on collected real-world demonstrations.
 - **Training:** Baseline, Naive-Aug, and ChromaGuard models.
 - **Attack Generation:** Attack candidates generated in simulation with FLARE, then the most effective selected for real-world deployment.
 - **Evaluation:** 20 trials for color-invariant tasks, 40 trials for color-dependent tasks.
 - **Diagnostic Evaluation (Grayscale Inference):** Input images are converted to grayscale during inference to quantify models' reliance on color information.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings concerning VLA model vulnerability, the limitations of naive adversarial training, and the efficacy of the proposed ChromaGuard defense.

#### 4.1. Simulator Experiments: Vulnerability to Spotlight Attack

- **Baseline Vulnerability:** The FLARE optimized spotlight attack demonstrated significant degradation of the baseline SmolVLA model. Its task success rate (SR) dropped to 0.0% across all evaluated LIBERO task suites (Spatial, Object, and LIBERO-10). Even random spot lighting reduced the SR by more than half compared to benign conditions (e.g., 83.0% to 15.4% for LIBERO-Spatial).
- **Physical Hazards:** The attack induced substantial physical deviations. The maximum trajectory error (TE-Max) reached up to 115.5 cm, indicating the robot arm was forced far outside its intended operational path.
- **Naive-Aug Apparent Robustness:** In simulation, the Naive-Aug model appeared robust, maintaining high success rates even under optimized spotlight attacks (e.g., 78.8% SR for LIBERO-Spatial, compared to 79.4% benign). This initial observation suggested that naive data augmentation might be sufficient.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments: Semantic Degradation and ChromaGuard Efficacy

- **Grayscale Diagnostic Evaluation:** This diagnostic test revealed a critical flaw in naive data augmentation.
 - **Baseline Performance:** The baseline model, trained without color augmentation, showed a significant drop in SR (near zero) when presented with grayscale images, confirming its reliance on color.
 - **Naive-Aug Performance:** The Naive-Aug model maintained almost identical performance on grayscale inputs as on standard RGB inputs (e.g., 90.5% vs. 89.8% SR on LIBERO-Object). This indicated that the naive augmentation inadvertently conditioned the model to discard color channels as noisy variables, making it effectively a shape-biased, grayscale processor.
- **Evaluation of Color-Invariant Tasks (Real-World):**
 - **Baseline Vulnerability:** Both SmolVLA and π0.5 baseline models showed significant drops in SR under attack (e.g., SmolVLA from 70.0% to 0.0%, π0.5 from 75.0% to 30.0%).
 - **Defense Efficacy:** Both Naive-Aug and ChromaGuard models demonstrated improved robustness against the spotlight attack in color-invariant tasks, achieving comparable success rates (e.g., SmolVLA: 70.0% SR for both under attack; π0.5: 75.0% for both under attack).
- **Evaluation of Color-Dependent Tasks (Real-World):**
 - **Naive-Aug Degradation:** In tasks requiring color differentiation, the Naive-Aug model generalized poorly. Even under benign lighting, its SR dropped significantly (SmolVLA: 47.5% vs. Baseline's 77.5%). A substantial proportion of these failures (85.7% for SmolVLA) were attributed to grasping incorrectly colored objects. This confirmed that indiscriminate color perturbation during training degraded the model's semantic understanding of color.
 - **ChromaGuard Performance:** ChromaGuard effectively addressed this trade-off. For SmolVLA, it achieved high success rates in color-dependent tasks, both under benign conditions (97.5% SR) and under attack (92.5% SR). For π0.5, ChromaGuard also improved performance over Naive-Aug (55.0% benign, 70.0% attacked), even considering π0.5's inherent color discrimination limitations (66.7% of benign failures for baseline π0.5 were due to incorrect color grasping). This demonstrated ChromaGuard's ability to maintain high perceptual fidelity while ensuring robustness to lighting attacks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides significant insights into the robustness of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and offers practical implications for their development and deployment.

**Exposure of Fundamental Vulnerability:** The FLARE framework systematically exposes a critical generalization vulnerability in state-of-the-art VLA models, demonstrating that even subtle physical environmental perturbations, such as targeted lighting changes, can lead to catastrophic task failures and significant trajectory deviations. This finding suggests that current VLA models possess a fundamental weakness in generalizability, challenging the notion that only sophisticated, advanced attacks pose a significant threat. This re-prioritizes the focus from complex security flaws to ensuring basic environmental robustness for embodied AI.

**Identification of a Critical Defensive Pitfall:** The study identifies and quantifies a previously underestimated pitfall in standard adversarial training methods, specifically naive data augmentation techniques that indiscriminately perturb color channels. The diagnostic grayscale evaluation provides empirical evidence that such augmentations inadvertently condition VLA models to disregard essential color cues, effectively reducing their visual perception to a shape-biased, grayscale processor. This insight is crucial because it reveals that seemingly beneficial robustness-enhancing techniques can inadvertently degrade a model's semantic understanding, leading to failures in real-world tasks where color is a critical distinguishing feature.

**Development of a Chroma-Preserving Defense:** The introduction of ChromaGuard addresses this defensive dilemma by offering a hue-preserving adversarial training methodology. By restricting color augmentation to exclude hue perturbations, ChromaGuard effectively achieves robustness against dynamic illumination while strictly maintaining the perceptual capabilities necessary for distinguishing objects by color. Its demonstrated performance in both color-invariant and color-dependent tasks under benign and attacked conditions highlights a viable path toward developing VLA models that are both robust and perceptually accurate.

**Broader Impact on Embodied AI Development:** The findings underscore a critical lesson for the field of embodied AI: handcrafted countermeasures aimed at improving robustness can introduce biases that override fundamental semantic signals, particularly in multimodal systems where visual cues directly dictate safety-critical actions. This research emphasizes the urgent necessity for VLA models to achieve a foundational level of generalizability and develop genuinely robust representations of visual features before widespread deployment in safety-critical applications. The FLARE framework and ChromaGuard serve as analytical tools to facilitate this ongoing research and development, prompting a re-evaluation of current robustness strategies for VLA systems. The work's limitations suggest future avenues for exploring more dynamic and adaptive adversarial scenarios, further solidifying the robustness of VLA models.
