# A Unified and General Humanoid Whole-Body Controller for Versatile Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> HUGWBC, developed by researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Shanghai AI Lab, provides a unified whole-body controller for humanoid robots that enables versatile locomotion across various gaits and robust loco-manipulation. The system leverages an extended command space and a unique external upper-body intervention training strategy to achieve stable performance on a physical Unitree H1 robot, even when subjected to diverse upper-body movements.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid locomotion systems are often limited to single, pre-defined, or tedious movements, lacking the versatility (e.g., walking, jumping, hopping) seen in humans.
- Learning-based humanoid controllers typically struggle to achieve comprehensive gait versatility and robustly integrate with real-time manipulation tasks.
- Maintaining stable whole-body locomotion while simultaneously allowing for arbitrary or precise upper-body movements (loco-manipulation) remains a significant challenge for humanoid robots.

## Method

- HUGWBC is a whole-body controller trained via reinforcement learning within an asymmetric actor-critic framework, designed for direct sim-to-real transfer.
- It employs an extended command space that includes task-level commands (e.g., velocities) and detailed behavior commands (e.g., gait frequency, foot swing height, body posture) to control versatile gaits.
- A unique "external upper-body intervention training" strategy, using noise and human motion data, is introduced to make the locomotion policy robust to arbitrary, externally controlled upper-body movements.

## Results

- HUGWBC successfully enables a Unitree H1 robot to perform versatile gaits including walking, jumping, standing, and hopping, with customizable parameters for speed, frequency, and posture.
- The external upper-body intervention training strategy consistently yields superior robustness, showing minimal degradation in tracking accuracy under various upper-body movements, outperforming policies trained without this intervention.
- Real-world experiments demonstrate HUGWBC's ability to seamlessly integrate locomotion with real-time upper-body manipulation, validating its utility for complex loco-manipulation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A rich command space combined with periodic phase variables can effectively enable a single reinforcement learning policy to generate diverse and customizable gaits.
> - Explicitly training with "intervention" (random upper-body disturbances) significantly enhances the policy's robustness to external upper-body control and general disturbances, which is crucial for robust loco-manipulation.
> - Curriculum learning, including terrain, speed, and noise curricula, is essential for training complex, robust humanoid behaviors in simulation for effective sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robotics has made impressive strides in recent years, demonstrating complex task execution and showing immense potential as a general robot platform. A fundamental challenge in this field is achieving agile and adaptable locomotion that mimics human-like versatility. Historically, humanoid locomotion systems have often been limited to single, pre-defined, or tedious movements, lacking the dynamic adaptability seen in humans who can seamlessly transition between walking, running, jumping, hopping, and fine-tuning gait parameters like frequency or stride.

The broader research landscape for humanoid control is broadly divided into two main paradigms:

- **Model-Based Approaches:** These methods typically employ optimal control and trajectory optimization, relying on precise dynamic models (e.g., linear inverted pendulum, centroidal dynamics, full-body dynamics) to plan motions. While they have achieved impressive results in structured environments, they face significant challenges. These include high computational demands for real-time performance, reliance on perfect state estimation and flawless execution, and poor robustness to real-world uncertainties (e.g., imperfect models, noisy sensors, unexpected disturbances). Decoupling control into contact planning and motion optimization has simplified some problems, but inherent assumptions still limit their real-world applicability.
- **Learning-Based Approaches:** Driven by advancements in reinforcement learning (RL), these methods have shown remarkable success in enabling legged robots to navigate rough terrains, achieve smooth motions, and even perform complex parkour. They learn a control policy directly from data, often in simulation, and leverage sim-to-real techniques to transfer policies to physical robots. However, challenges remain in robustly handling partial observability and adapting to unforeseen circumstances.

This paper positions **HUGWBC** directly at the intersection of these challenges by addressing the limitations of existing learning-based controllers in achieving comprehensive gait versatility and seamless integration with manipulation tasks. While other learning-based works have explored motion tracking (e.g., tracking motion capture keypoints or retargeted joint positions for whole-body control) or decoupled control (e.g., IK-based upper-body control combined with learning-based lower-body control), HUGWBC differentiates itself significantly:

1. **Extended Command Space:** Unlike prior works that focus on basic locomotion or motion imitation, HUGWBC introduces a highly extended and general command space. This includes not only task-level commands (like velocity) but also detailed "behavior commands" that finely control foot behavior (frequency, swing height) and posture (body height, pitch, waist rotation), and explicitly incorporate distinct gaits (walking, jumping, standing, hopping). This allows for unprecedented versatility in controlling the robot's movement style.
2. **Unified Whole-Body Control with External Intervention Support:** Most existing learning-based controllers either control only the lower body or integrate upper-body commands directly into the policy. HUGWBC, however, trains a single whole-body policy (for walking, standing, jumping) that inherently controls both upper and lower body, *but also* robustly supports real-time *external* upper-body control (e.g., teleoperation). This "intervention training" is a crucial distinction, as it aims to make the locomotion robust to arbitrary upper-body movements without needing to re-train the policy for specific manipulation tasks. This is essential for loco-manipulation, where precise upper-body control might be provided by a separate system while the robot maintains stable locomotion.

By combining an extensive command space with a unique intervention training strategy, HUGWBC aims to push the boundaries of what is possible with unified whole-body control for humanoid robots, enabling them to perform a wider range of dynamic and interactive tasks in diverse environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop **HUGWBC: a Unified and General Humanoid Whole-Body Controller for Versatile Locomotion**. The authors are motivated by the fundamental limitations of existing humanoid locomotion systems and the aspiration to imbue robots with human-like versatility and adaptability.

The key motivations and specific objectives can be summarized as follows:

- **Overcoming Limited Kinematic Capabilities:** Current humanoid locomotion often results in "single, tedious, unextendable, and unconstrained movements." The authors seek to break away from this rigidity, inspired by the vast and versatile athletic abilities of humans (running, jumping, hopping, fine-tuning gait parameters).
- **Enabling Versatile Gait Generation:** A primary objective is to allow humanoid robots to naturally produce a variety of gaits, including walking, jumping, standing, and hopping. Furthermore, the controller should support customization of these gaits through adjustable parameters such as gait frequency, foot swing height, body height, waist rotation, and body pitch. The aim is to achieve this versatility within a single, unified policy (for walking, jumping, and standing, with hopping handled by a separate policy due to its unique dynamics).
- **Supporting Real-time Loco-Manipulation:** A critical objective is to enable "loco-manipulation" – the ability to perform precise manipulation tasks while simultaneously moving. This requires the whole-body controller to seamlessly integrate with and remain robust to real-time interventions from external upper-body controllers (e.g., teleoperation, retargeted motions). The motivation here is to establish HUGWBC as a fundamental building block for higher-level, complex humanoid tasks in diverse real-world scenarios.
- **Improving Robustness and Flexibility:** The authors aim for a highly robust and flexible controller that can maintain high tracking accuracy across various commands and under different gaits, even when subjected to unexpected upper-body disturbances or highly dynamic movements. This includes enhancing stability and resistance to external forces.
- **Developing an Extended Command Space and Advanced Training Techniques:** To achieve the above, a key objective is the design of a novel and comprehensive command space that effectively captures diverse locomotive and postural behaviors. This is coupled with advanced reinforcement learning techniques in simulation, including specific reward designs for complex gaits, a mirror function for symmetric motion, and an innovative intervention training strategy for robust upper-body control.

In essence, the paper strives to create a foundational, adaptable, and robust control system that pushes humanoid robots closer to the agile and versatile physical capabilities observed in humans, thereby broadening their potential applications significantly.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HUGWBC leverages **reinforcement learning (RL)** within an **asymmetric actor-critic (AAC) framework** to learn a unified and general whole-body control policy in simulation for direct transfer to real robots. The core of their approach lies in a meticulously designed command space, a comprehensive reward structure, and novel training techniques for robustness.

**A. General Command Space (C = K × B):**
The authors define a rich command space composed of **task commands (K)** and **behavior commands (B)**.
- **Task Commands (K):** Primarily target velocities (longitudinal linear velocity $v_x$, horizontal linear velocity $v_y$, and angular velocity $\omega_z$). These define the robot's intended movement goal.
- **Behavior Commands (B):** A vector encompassing:
 - **Foot Behaviors:** Gait frequency ($f_t$) and maximum foot swing height ($l_t$).
 - **Posture Behaviors:** Body height ($h_t$), body pitch angle ($p_t$), and waist yaw rotation ($w_t$).
 - **Gait-Specific Parameters:** Two time-varying periodic phase variables ($\phi_{i=1,2}$ for each leg) and a duty cycle ($\phi_{stance}$). A smooth **expected contact probability function** $C(\phi_{t,i})$ is introduced using the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of a standard normal distribution, ensuring smooth transitions between stance and swing phases.
 - **Distinct Gaits:** The policy supports walking ($\psi=0.5$ phase offset between legs), jumping ($\psi=0$, both feet in phase), and standing ($\phi_i=0.25$, constant contact). For hopping, an independent policy is trained due to its asymmetric nature ($\phi_i=0.75$ for flying leg, other steps with frequency).

**B. Observations:**
The AAC framework uses different observations for the actor and critic networks:
- **Critic ($o_V$):** Has access to comprehensive information including proprioceptive observations (angular velocity, gravity projection, joint positions/velocities, previous action), privileged observations (linear velocity, base height error, foot clearance, friction, contact forces, collision detection), terrain observations (elevation map samples), commands, and an **intervention indicator signal I(t)**.
- **Actor ($o_\pi$):** Receives a history of proprioceptive observations (to infer unobserved states), commands, and the intervention indicator. A separate encoder estimates key privileged information (linear velocity, body height, foot swing height) for the actor.
- The phase variables ($\phi_i$) are extended into **clock functions** (sine of homogenized phase) in the command input to the network, providing a smoother cyclical representation for gait control.

**C. Reward Design:**
The policy is trained via RL to maximize a reward function comprising three main parts:
- **Task Rewards:** Primarily based on L2-norm (or exponential of negative L2-norm) to track target linear and angular velocities.
- **Behavior Rewards:**
 - MSE for tracking body height, body pitch, and waist yaw commands.
 - **Periodic Contact-Swing Reward ($r_{contact}$):** Penalizes foot contact force during swing phase and foot velocity during stance phase, guiding the robot to learn desired contact patterns based on $C(\phi_{t,i})$.
 - **Foot Trajectory Reward ($r_{traj}$):** Encourages the feet to follow a desired swing height trajectory (specifically, a **quintic polynomial trajectory** for smooth movement from start, through peak height $l_t$, to end of swing phase, with zero velocity and acceleration at boundaries).
- **Regularization Rewards:** Standard penalties for angular velocity, vertical body movement, feet slip, action rate/smoothness, joint torque, joint acceleration, and joint deviations (upper body, hip). A "Feet Symmetry" reward is added for symmetric gaits.

**D. Mirror Function and Symmetry Loss ($L_{sym}$):**
To encourage natural and energy-efficient symmetric motion, a symmetry loss is introduced. This loss penalizes deviations from expected symmetric actions given mirrored observations and commands. It's added to the overall RL objective: $L = L_{AAC} + \beta L_{sym}$. This helps the policy explore symmetrical gaits more efficiently.

**E. External Upper-Body Intervention Training:**
This is a critical component for achieving loco-manipulation capabilities. The goal is to make the lower-body locomotion robust to arbitrary upper-body movements not generated by the policy itself.
- **Binary Indicator I(t):** A signal indicates whether an external controller is taking over the upper body.
- **Intervention Sampling:** During training, the upper-body actions produced by the policy are randomly replaced with alternative actions, sampled from uniform noise (simulating erroneous teleoperation) or from filtered AMASS human motion data (for comparison). A small probability controls the switching between policy control and external intervention.
- **Noise Intervention Interpolation:** To prevent jerky motions, sampled noisy actions are smoothly interpolated over an interval.
- **Reward Mask:** When intervention occurs, regularization rewards for upper-body joints are masked to avoid conflict with the external control signal.
- **Noise Curriculum:** The degree of noise in the intervention action is gradually increased (smoothed by factor $\alpha$) as the policy improves in velocity tracking, forcing it to adapt to increasingly aggressive upper-body movements.

**F. Curriculum Learning:**
To manage the complexity of learning diverse behaviors, the authors employ a curriculum learning approach with two parallel training groups:
- **"Agile Group":** Focuses on high-speed, agile locomotion.
- **"Intervention Group":** Focuses on robustness to upper-body interventions.
Both groups start with random gait selection, uniform command sampling, and a **terrain curriculum** (progressively rougher terrains). Once challenging terrains are mastered, the "agile group" enters a **speed curriculum** (gradually increasing velocity commands), while the "intervention group" begins the **noise curriculum** for upper-body intervention.

**G. Robot and Simulator:**
All training is conducted on the **NVIDIA IsaacGym simulator** using the **Unitree H1 robot model** (19 Degrees of Freedom). The training takes 16 hours on a single RTX 4090 GPU. Real-world validation is performed on the physical Unitree H1 robot.

This comprehensive methodology, particularly the innovative command space and intervention training strategy, allows HUGWBC to learn highly versatile and robust whole-body control policies for humanoid robots.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments in both simulation and on a real-world Unitree H1 robot to validate HUGWBC's performance across various commands and gaits, as well as the efficacy of their intervention training strategy.

**A. Single Command Tracking (Simulation - Q1):**
- **Gait-Specific Performance:** Walking and standing gaits exhibit significantly lower tracking errors compared to jumping and hopping. Hopping shows the largest errors, attributed to its inherent instability and the policy prioritizing balance over precise command tracking.
- **Velocity Dependence:** Tracking accuracy is better at low velocities than high velocities, as high-speed dynamic stability is more challenging for the robot.
- **Directional Differences:** Longitudinal velocity ($v_x$) tracking outperforms horizontal velocity ($v_y$), which is attributed to the Unitree H1's hardware configuration.
- **Command Prioritization:** Foot swing height ($l$) is the least accurately tracked command. Foot placement tracking generally outperforms posture control (body height, pitch, yaw), as adjusting posture introduces greater stability challenges, leading the policy to adopt more conservative actions.

**B. Command Combination Analysis (Simulation - Q2):**
The analysis of command orthogonality (how much one command interferes with another's tracking) through heatmaps revealed insights into practical command combinations:
- **Walking:** Generally exhibits good orthogonality between linear velocity ($v_x$), angular velocity yaw ($\omega$), body height ($h$), and waist yaw ($w$). However, at high speeds ($v_x > 1.5$ m/s), orthogonality decreases due to stability demands. Gait frequency ($f$) shows discrete optimal performance at 1.5 or 2 Hz, with higher frequencies reducing accuracy. Linear velocity $v_y$, foot swing height ($l$), and body pitch ($p$) are orthogonal only within narrow ranges.
- **Jumping:** Command orthogonality is similar to walking but with a smaller overall orthogonal range. This is due to the high dynamic challenge of jumping, which requires precise posture and significant force generation, limiting the robot's ability to simultaneously execute other commands broadly.
- **Hopping:** Shows a lack of clear orthogonal relationships, indicating its extreme instability. Effective tracking is mostly limited to $v_x$, $v_y$, $\omega$, and $h$. Lower body height ($h$) improves dynamic stability during hopping.
- **Standing:** Waist yaw ($w$) is almost orthogonal to body height ($h$) and body pitch ($p$). However, orthogonality between $h$ and $p$ decreases with increasing command range, constrained by the H1 robot's single DOF at the waist and limited hip pitch range.
- **General Conclusions:** Gait frequency significantly impacts movement tracking at extreme values. Posture commands near their limits can profoundly affect other command tracking errors. The walking gait provides the largest orthogonality range, while hopping provides the smallest.

**C. Ablation Study on Intervention Training Strategy (Simulation - Q3):**
Comparing HUGWBC (noise curriculum) with policies trained using AMASS data or no intervention:
- **Superior Robustness:** HUGWBC consistently achieves the best performance, showing minimal degradation in tracking accuracy under various upper-body interventions (uniform noise or AMASS data). This validates that the noise curriculum effectively trains the policy to handle a wide range of arm movements and supports loco-manipulation.
- **Limited Data Issues:** The AMASS-trained policy shows a significant drop in accuracy when exposed to uniform noise intervention, highlighting its dependence on the limited diversity of its training data.
- **Vulnerability Without Training:** The "no intervention" policy performs poorly and often fails when external upper-body control is introduced, demonstrating the necessity of intervention training for robust loco-manipulation.
- **Overall Policy Robustness:** Surprisingly, policies trained with intervention (especially HUGWBC) also show improved movement and foot tracking accuracy compared to the "no intervention" baseline, suggesting that exposure to upper-body disturbances during training enhances overall policy robustness and dynamic leg strength adjustments.
- **Standing Stability:** HUGWBC exhibits minimal foot displacement during standing tasks with posture command changes under intervention, indicating that it can maintain a stationary stance crucial for manipulation. In contrast, other policies require frequent small steps or tip over.
- **External Disturbance Tolerance:** HUGWBC demonstrates greater tolerance to external pushing and loading forces across various directions, directly benefiting from the "disturbance" introduced by upper-body intervention training.

**D. Real-World Experiments (Q4):**
HUGWBC was successfully deployed on a physical Unitree H1 robot.
- **Versatile Capabilities:** Demonstrations (Fig. 1) show the robot performing walking, jumping, standing, hopping, and loco-manipulation with customizable parameters and dynamic command combinations.
- **Tracking Accuracy (Qualitative & Quantitative):** Real-world tracking errors for body pitch ($p$) and waist yaw ($w$) are slightly higher than in simulation, mainly due to sensor noise and hardware wear. Waist yaw tracking is more accurate than body pitch, as waist control has less impact on overall stability. Consistent with simulation, jumping shows the smallest posture tracking errors, while walking shows slightly higher errors.

In summary, the results strongly validate HUGWBC's ability to achieve versatile and robust whole-body locomotion for humanoids. The command space design and, crucially, the intervention training strategy are shown to be highly effective in enabling both diverse gaits and seamless loco-manipulation, addressing critical gaps in current humanoid control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HUGWBC represents a significant advancement in humanoid whole-body control, offering capabilities that push the boundaries of what is currently achievable with versatile and robust locomotion. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

- **Enabling Truly Versatile Humanoid Locomotion:** By unifying multiple distinct gaits (walking, jumping, standing) under a single policy, and incorporating detailed, customizable parameters for foot behavior and posture, HUGWBC moves beyond the "single, tedious, unextendable" movements often seen in humanoid robots. This allows for more dynamic, natural, and adaptable motion, bringing robots closer to human-level athletic versatility. The ability to smoothly adjust gait frequency, foot swing height, body height, pitch, and yaw on the fly is a crucial step towards adaptable navigation in complex, unstructured environments.

- **Foundation for Advanced Loco-Manipulation:** The most profound impact of HUGWBC lies in its robust support for real-time external upper-body intervention. This is a critical paradigm shift, as it decouples the highly dynamic and stability-critical lower-body locomotion from precise upper-body manipulation. By training the policy to be inherently robust to arbitrary upper-body movements (simulated through noise and curriculum learning), HUGWBC provides a solid and stable base for complex loco-manipulation tasks. This means that a separate, specialized controller or human teleoperator can control the arms and hands for delicate tasks (e.g., assembly, object interaction) without compromising the robot's balance or locomotion. This capability is vital for humanoid robots operating in human-centric environments where mobility and dexterous interaction are simultaneously required.

- **Enhanced Robustness to Disturbances:** The intervention training strategy, by exposing the robot to diverse upper-body disturbances during training, significantly enhances the policy's overall robustness. This is not limited to upper-body control but also translates to increased tolerance for external forces and better stability in dynamic scenarios. Such robustness is paramount for real-world deployment, where robots constantly encounter unexpected pushes, uneven terrains, or varying loads.

- **Simplification of Robot Control Architectures:** By providing a unified, low-level whole-body controller for versatile locomotion and robust loco-manipulation, HUGWBC can serve as a fundamental building block. This could simplify the development of higher-level planners and task-specific controllers, as they can rely on HUGWBC to handle the complex dynamics and balance control, allowing researchers to focus on perception, reasoning, and task execution. This modularity fosters more efficient and scalable robotic system design.

- **Contribution to Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful deployment of HUGWBC from simulation to a real Unitree H1 robot further validates the efficacy of the adopted asymmetric actor-critic framework and the specific sim-to-real techniques employed. This provides valuable insights and benchmarks for future research aiming to bridge the simulation-reality gap for complex humanoid behaviors.

- **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated on the Unitree H1, the principles behind HUGWBC – particularly the general command space design and the intervention training – are likely transferable to other humanoid platforms. This suggests a potential for HUGWBC to become a standard control paradigm across a variety of humanoid robot hardware.

In conclusion, HUGWBC addresses a critical need in humanoid robotics for controllers that are both versatile in locomotion and robust during manipulation. Its focus on broad behavioral capabilities and seamless external control integration marks a significant step towards enabling humanoids to operate more effectively and naturally in a wider array of real-world applications, from industrial settings to service robotics.
