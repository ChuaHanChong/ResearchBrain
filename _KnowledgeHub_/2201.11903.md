# Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting enables large language models to perform complex multi-step reasoning by including intermediate natural language steps in few-shot exemplars. This method led to substantial performance improvements on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning benchmarks, particularly for models with over 100 billion parameters like PaLM 540B developed by Google Research, Brain Team.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) often struggled with complex, multi-step reasoning tasks such as arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic problems, despite their general language capabilities.
- Prior approaches involving rationale-augmented training required expensive and difficult-to-create datasets of high-quality rationales.
- Standard in-context few-shot prompting, effective for many tasks, performed poorly and did not scale for complex reasoning problems.

## Method

- Introduces **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting**, an extension of ==few-shot prompting== where exemplars include intermediate natural language ==reasoning steps== leading to the final answer.
- Evaluates ==CoT prompting== across various large language models (e.g., GPT-3, LaMDA, PaLM) and their scales, without requiring any model finetuning.
- Applies the method to a diverse set of tasks spanning arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning.

## Results

- Chain-of-Thought prompting led to substantial performance gains across various reasoning benchmarks; for instance, **PaLM 540B** achieved **56.9% accuracy on GSM8K**, outperforming standard prompting (**17.9%**) and prior finetuned models.
- Significant improvements were observed in commonsense reasoning, with PaLM 540B achieving new state-of-the-art results on StrategyQA (**77.8%**) and SportsUnderstanding (**95.4%**).
- The method demonstrated robustness to different annotators, exemplar sets, and exemplar orders, and scaling from PaLM 62B to 540B addressed a portion of semantic understanding and 'one step missing' errors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The benefits of Chain-of-Thought prompting are an emergent ability of model scale, appearing significantly only in models with over approximately 100 billion parameters.
> - The natural language reasoning steps themselves are crucial for eliciting reasoning, as evidenced by ablation studies showing that mere token generation or equation-only prompts do not yield comparable improvements.
> - CoT prompting enables length generalization in symbolic reasoning tasks, allowing large models to solve problems with more steps than present in the few-shot exemplars.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Prior to this work, the landscape of large language models was characterized by a paradoxical situation: despite their unprecedented scale and impressive performance on many language understanding and generation tasks, LLMs often struggled with complex, multi-step reasoning problems. While they could perform well on simple question-answering, tasks requiring arithmetic, commonsense, or symbolic manipulation often remained challenging, showing flat scaling curves even with increasing model size (Rae et al., 2021).

Two primary approaches had emerged to tackle reasoning in LLMs:
1. **Rationale-augmented training or finetuning:** This involved training models to generate intermediate natural language rationales (or formal language steps) that lead to a final answer. Pioneers like Ling et al. (2017) demonstrated the benefit of generating natural language intermediate steps for math word problems, a concept extended by Cobbe et al. (2021) who finetuned pretrained models on such data. However, this approach faced a significant limitation: the high cost and difficulty of creating large datasets of high-quality rationales, which are far more complex than simple input-output pairs.
2. **In-context few-shot learning via prompting:** Popularized by Brown et al. (2020) with GPT-3, this paradigm allowed LLMs to learn new tasks by simply being "prompted" with a few input-output exemplars, without any finetuning. This was revolutionary for many tasks, but critically, it performed poorly on tasks requiring complex reasoning, and its effectiveness did not scale substantially with model size for these challenging domains (Rae et al., 2021).

"Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models" directly addresses the limitations of both these preceding lines of work. It innovatively combines the strength of *generating intermediate steps* (from rationale-augmented approaches) with the *simplicity and flexibility of few-shot prompting* (from in-context learning). By showing that large, *off-the-shelf* language models can naturally *emerge* reasoning abilities simply by being provided with a few demonstrations that include intermediate reasoning steps (chains of thought), this paper opened up a new avenue for enhancing LLM capabilities. It demonstrated that LLMs possess latent reasoning abilities that are not readily apparent with standard prompting but can be "unlocked" through a specific input format. This work became a foundational paper, inspiring a vast body of subsequent research into more advanced prompting techniques, self-consistency methods (Wang et al., 2022a), and strategies to improve LLM reasoning without expensive model architecture changes or large-scale finetuning. It profoundly shifted the perspective on how to elicit complex behaviors from LLMs, moving beyond mere input-output mapping to structured internal thought processes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **significantly improve the ability of large language models to perform complex reasoning tasks by eliciting a "chain of thought"—a series of intermediate natural language reasoning steps.**

The authors are motivated by several key observations and challenges in the field of large language models:

- **Addressing LLM Limitations in Reasoning:** Despite their impressive general language capabilities, existing LLMs struggled with tasks requiring multi-step arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning. Scaling model size alone had not sufficiently overcome this hurdle.
- **Cost of Rationale-Augmented Training:** While prior work showed that models could benefit from generating natural language rationales, training or finetuning models to do so required large, expensively annotated datasets of rationales. This high data cost limited widespread applicability.
- **Ineffectiveness of Standard Few-Shot Prompting for Reasoning:** Traditional few-shot prompting (providing only input-output examples) proved ineffective for complex reasoning tasks, failing to show substantial improvements even with larger models. This indicated a gap in how information was presented to the model.
- **Unlocking Latent Abilities:** The researchers hypothesized that LLMs, especially very large ones, might possess latent reasoning capabilities that are not activated by simple input-output prompts. By mimicking a human-like step-by-step thought process, they aimed to "unlock" these abilities.
- **Simplicity and Generalizability:** The motivation was to find a simple, broadly applicable method that did not require finetuning specific model checkpoints for each task. A prompting-only approach would allow a single, general-purpose LLM to perform a wide range of reasoning tasks.
- **Interpretability:** An additional motivation was that generating intermediate steps would provide an "interpretable window" into the model's behavior, allowing for a better understanding of its reasoning process and aiding in debugging errors.

In essence, the paper sought to bridge the gap between the theoretical potential of LLMs for complex reasoning and their practical limitations, leveraging the in-context learning paradigm to enable a more sophisticated problem-solving approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology introduced is **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting**. This approach is a straightforward extension of standard few-shot prompting but with a critical modification: the exemplars provided in the prompt include intermediate reasoning steps in natural language that lead to the final answer.

**Detailed Approach:**

1. **Chain-of-Thought Prompting:** Instead of providing simple input-output pairs (Q: What's 2+2? A: 4.), CoT prompting uses triples of the form: `〈input, chain of thought, output〉`. The "chain of thought" is a series of natural language sentences detailing the intermediate steps a human might take to solve the problem. For example, for "Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls. Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have now?", a CoT exemplar would include: "Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11."

2. **Baselines:** The primary baseline for comparison is **Standard Few-Shot Prompting**, where only input-output pairs are provided without the intermediate reasoning steps.

3. **Language Models Evaluated:** The study rigorously tested CoT prompting across a range of large language models and their various scales, including:
 - **GPT-3:** Models from OpenAI (350M, 1.3B, 6.7B, and 175B parameters, specifically InstructGPT variants like `text-davinci-002`).
 - **LaMDA:** Google's Language Model for Dialogue Applications (422M, 2B, 8B, 68B, and 137B parameters).
 - **PaLM:** Google's Pathways Language Model (8B, 62B, and 540B parameters).
 - **UL2 20B** and **Codex** (OpenAI's code-davinci-002).
 Models were used off-the-shelf, without any finetuning. Greedy decoding was primarily used for sampling outputs.

4. **Tasks and Benchmarks:** The effectiveness of CoT prompting was evaluated on a diverse set of tasks requiring different types of reasoning:
 - **Arithmetic Reasoning:**
 - GSM8K: Grade School Math 8K word problems (complex).
 - SVAMP: Math word problems with varying structures.
 - ASDiv: Diverse math word problems.
 - AQuA: Algebraic word problems (multiple choice).
 - MAWPS: A math word problem repository, including subsets like SingleOp (single-step) and MultiArith (multi-step).
 - **Commonsense Reasoning:**
 - CSQA: CommonsenseQA.
 - StrategyQA: Questions requiring multi-hop strategy inference.
 - DateUnderstanding & SportsUnderstanding (from BIG-bench): Specialized reasoning tasks.
 - SayCan: Mapping natural language instructions to robot actions.
 - **Symbolic Reasoning (Toy Tasks for Generalization):**
 - Last Letter Concatenation: Concatenating last letters of words in a name.
 - Coin Flip: Tracking coin state after a series of flips.
 - These symbolic tasks were designed with "in-domain" (same number of steps as exemplars) and "out-of-domain" (more steps) test sets to evaluate length generalization.

5. **Exemplar Creation:** For most benchmarks, a small set of eight few-shot exemplars (or four for AQuA due to multiple-choice format) with manually composed chains of thought were used. For some datasets (e.g., GSM8K), additional exemplars with crowd-sourced rationales were tested for robustness.

6. **Ablation Studies:** To understand *why* CoT prompting works, the authors performed ablations:
 - **Equation Only:** Prompting models to output only a mathematical equation before the answer.
 - **Variable Compute Only:** Prompting models to output a sequence of dots (mimicking token generation but without meaningful content) before the answer, to isolate the effect of "more computation."
 - **Reasoning After Answer:** Providing the chain of thought *after* the final answer, to see if the sequential reasoning process itself is critical or just the presence of knowledge.

7. **Robustness Analysis:** The study assessed the sensitivity of CoT prompting to various factors:
 - Different human annotators writing the chains of thought.
 - Using different sets of exemplars (e.g., randomly sampled from training data).
 - Different orders of few-shot exemplars within the prompt.
 - Varying the number of exemplars.
 - Performance consistency across different language models.

8. **Error Analysis:** Manual examination of incorrect model outputs (LaMDA 137B on GSM8K) to categorize common failure modes (calculator errors, symbol mapping errors, one step missing errors, semantic understanding errors, incoherent chains of thought). They also analyzed how scaling from PaLM 62B to 540B affected error correction.

9. **External Calculator Integration:** For arithmetic tasks, an optional post-hoc step involved using a Python `eval` function to execute mathematical equations produced in the chain of thought, isolating errors due to arithmetic miscalculation from reasoning flaws.

This comprehensive methodological approach allowed the researchers to not only demonstrate the effectiveness of CoT prompting but also to gain insights into its underlying mechanisms and robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded several striking and significant findings:

1. **Emergent Ability of Model Scale:** A pivotal finding is that the benefits of chain-of-thought prompting are an **emergent ability of model scale**. CoT prompting did not positively impact performance for smaller models (typically below ~100 billion parameters); in some cases, it even hurt performance because smaller models generated fluent but illogical chains of thought. Significant gains only appeared with sufficiently large models (e.g., LaMDA 137B, GPT-3 175B, PaLM 540B). This suggests that the capacity for coherent, multi-step reasoning in natural language is a property that arises above a certain threshold of model size.

2. **Dramatic Performance Gains Across Tasks:**
 - **Arithmetic Reasoning:** CoT prompting led to substantial improvements. For instance, PaLM 540B with just eight CoT exemplars achieved new state-of-the-art accuracy on the GSM8K benchmark of math word problems (56.9% vs. 17.9% for standard prompting, and surpassing prior finetuned models). Similar significant gains were observed on SVAMP and MAWPS. The gains were particularly pronounced for more complex, multi-step problems (e.g., GSM8K, MultiArith), while simpler one-step problems saw minimal or negative improvements, as LLMs often already perform well on them. The use of an external calculator further boosted performance by addressing arithmetic miscalculations, highlighting that the LLMs' primary struggle was often with reasoning structure rather than basic math.
 - **Commonsense Reasoning:** CoT prompting also improved performance on commonsense tasks. PaLM 540B with CoT prompting achieved new state-of-the-art on StrategyQA (77.8% vs. 68.6% standard prompting) and outperformed an unaided human baseline on SportsUnderstanding (95.4% vs. 80.5% standard prompting; human benchmark was 84%). This demonstrated the broader applicability of CoT beyond numerical problems.
 - **Symbolic Reasoning:** CoT prompting not only enabled LLMs to perform challenging symbolic tasks but also facilitated **length generalization** (out-of-domain performance). For "Last Letter Concatenation" and "Coin Flip" tasks, CoT prompting allowed large models to solve instances with more steps than seen in the exemplars, whereas standard prompting failed.

3. **Insights from Ablation Studies:**
 - **"Equation only" prompting:** This variant helped for simpler arithmetic problems (where equations could be easily derived) but not for complex ones like GSM8K. This indicated that the *natural language reasoning steps* in a chain of thought are crucial for semantically challenging problems, not just the eventual mathematical formula.
 - **"Variable compute only" prompting:** This approach, where models generated arbitrary non-semantic tokens (dots) to mimic increased computation, showed no significant improvement over standard prompting. This strongly suggested that simply allocating more "computation" (in terms of token generation) is insufficient; the *meaningful sequence of reasoning steps* is what matters.
 - **"Reasoning after answer" prompting:** This variant performed about the same as the baseline. This confirmed that the sequential reasoning steps must precede the final answer for the model to leverage them effectively in deriving the solution.

4. **Robustness of CoT Prompting:** The benefits of CoT prompting were found to be largely robust across various conditions:
 - **Different Annotators:** While there was some variance, chains of thought written by different annotators consistently outperformed standard prompting by a significant margin. This implies that successful CoT prompting does not critically depend on a specific linguistic style.
 - **Different Exemplars:** Using exemplars from different sources (e.g., randomly sampled from the GSM8K training set) also yielded comparable and substantial improvements.
 - **Different Order of Exemplars and Number of Exemplars:** The performance was relatively stable across different exemplar orders and varying numbers of exemplars, demonstrating its general applicability.

5. **Error Analysis and Scaling:** Manual error analysis on LaMDA 137B for GSM8K showed that common errors in incorrect outputs included calculator errors, symbol mapping errors, and missing one reasoning step. Crucially, scaling from PaLM 62B to 540B fixed a significant portion of semantic understanding and "one step missing" errors, providing qualitative evidence that increased model scale genuinely improves reasoning capabilities.

In summary, the main findings firmly established Chain-of-Thought prompting as a simple yet extremely powerful technique for unlocking latent reasoning abilities in large language models, particularly as an emergent property of model scale.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance and potential impact of "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models" are profound and multi-faceted:

1. **Paradigm Shift in LLM Reasoning:** This paper fundamentally shifted the approach to enhancing LLM reasoning. Before CoT, the prevailing wisdom often involved complex architectures, specialized training, or large-scale finetuning on reasoning datasets. CoT demonstrated that a simple, few-shot *prompting technique* could achieve state-of-the-art results on challenging reasoning benchmarks without any model retraining or finetuning. This makes powerful reasoning capabilities accessible "off-the-shelf" with existing large models.

2. **Unlocking Latent Capabilities:** The discovery that reasoning ability is an *emergent property* of model scale, only becoming apparent with CoT prompting in very large models, is highly significant. It suggests that LLMs possess latent reasoning capabilities that are not obvious through standard prompting methods, implying that current evaluations only provide a "lower bound" on what these models can do. This opens new research avenues into what other latent abilities might exist and how to elicit them.

3. **Enhanced Interpretability:** By compelling the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps, CoT prompting provides an "interpretable window" into the model's decision-making process. While not a definitive proof of "true reasoning," these natural language steps help users understand *how* the model arrived at an answer, identify errors in its logic, and potentially debug its behavior. This is crucial for building more transparent and trustworthy AI systems.

4. **Broad Applicability and Generalizability:** The demonstrated effectiveness of CoT prompting across diverse domains—arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning—underscores its general applicability. The fact that it works for tasks where humans naturally decompose problems into steps suggests its potential for even broader use cases, simplifying the development of LLM-powered applications that require complex problem-solving. Its ability to facilitate length generalization in symbolic tasks hints at more robust and flexible problem-solving.

5. **Reduced Annotation Costs:** While creating the initial few-shot CoT exemplars requires manual effort, this is minimal compared to the prohibitive costs of annotating large datasets for finetuning. This makes advanced reasoning capabilities more economically viable for researchers and developers.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated AI Development:** CoT prompting offers a practical and immediate method for improving LLM performance on reasoning tasks, accelerating the deployment of more capable AI systems in various fields, from education (e.g., intelligent tutors that show step-by-step solutions) to complex data analysis.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work has spawned extensive follow-up research, including:
 - **Self-consistency:** Methods that sample multiple CoTs and take a majority vote (Wang et al., 2022a), further boosting accuracy.
 - **Automatic CoT generation:** Techniques to automatically generate CoT examples (e.g., zero-shot CoT by Kojima et al., 2022) or refine them, reducing manual annotation.
 - **Modular reasoning:** Breaking down complex problems into smaller, solvable sub-problems, inspired by the step-by-step nature of CoT.
 - **Improved Safety and Robustness:** A better understanding of model "reasoning" pathways can aid in developing more robust and reliable AI systems, where logical flaws are more easily detectable.

**Limitations and Future Directions highlighted by the authors:**

- **Nature of "Reasoning":** The paper explicitly states that while CoT emulates human thought processes, it doesn't definitively answer whether the neural network is truly "reasoning" in a human-like cognitive sense. This remains an open philosophical and scientific question.
- **Factuality and Coherence:** There's no guarantee that the generated chains of thought are factually correct or perfectly coherent, even if they lead to the correct answer. Improving the faithfulness and factual correctness of LLM generations remains an important area for future work.
- **Cost of Large Models:** The emergence of CoT reasoning only at large model scales means it is computationally expensive to serve in real-world applications. Future research needs to explore how to induce similar reasoning abilities in smaller, more efficient models.
- **Prompt Engineering:** While relatively robust, CoT prompting is still susceptible to prompt engineering, and optimal performance may still require some tuning of exemplars. Automatically generating robust CoT annotations is a promising direction.

In conclusion, "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models" represents a landmark contribution, demonstrating a simple yet powerful technique that significantly elevates the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, fundamentally impacting how researchers and practitioners approach complex problem-solving with these models.
