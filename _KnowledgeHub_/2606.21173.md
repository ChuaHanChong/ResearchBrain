# Inverting the Bellman Equation: From $Q$-Values to World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper introduces P-learning, an algorithm that inverts the Bellman equation to extract world models from model-free agents' goal-conditioned Q-values, and theoretically proves that training with diverse goals resolves the value equivalence problem. The method extracts highly accurate models that enable generalization to out-of-distribution tasks, revealing implicit capabilities within goal-conditioned reinforcement learning.

## Problem

- Model-free agents often learn task-specific behaviors and are considered "black boxes," limiting their reusability and interpretability.
- The "value equivalence problem" states that distinct world models can produce identical value functions, making it difficult to uniquely identify the true environment dynamics from an agent's learned values.
- It was unclear under what conditions model-free agents implicitly encode an accurate and unique model of their environment, especially when trained with multiple goals.

## Method

- Proposed **P-learning**, an algorithm that minimizes the ==Bellman residual== to iteratively update a candidate world model (Pφ) to be consistent with fixed Q-functions, policy, and reward function.
- Developed theoretical conditions for unique identifiability of the world model from Q-values, policies, and rewards across finite deterministic, finite stochastic, and continuous deterministic MDPs.
- Empirically validated the methodology using environments like FourRooms, Reacher (MuJoCo), and MountainCar, training agents with goal-conditioned RL and extracting world models.

## Results

- P-learning successfully extracted world models with extremely high fidelity (e.g., NMSE = **1.2** × 10⁻⁴ for Reacher), even from imperfect Q-values, across various finite and continuous environments.
- Policies derived from the extracted implicit world models achieved quasi-optimal performance on out-of-distribution goals, including those defined on state variables (like angular velocity) for which the agent was not explicitly trained.
- Theoretically, a single 'generic' goal can suffice for unique world model identifiability in deterministic finite MDPs, while stochastic finite MDPs typically require at least |S| goals.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training model-free agents on a sufficiently rich set of goal-conditioned rewards can resolve the value equivalence problem, enabling unique identification of the underlying world model.
> - Model-free agents implicitly learn highly accurate world models, even for out-of-distribution state variables not directly present in training rewards (e.g., velocity from position-only goals).
> - There is a strong correlation between an agent's performance on training goals and the accuracy of its internally learned world model, suggesting goal-conditioned RL acts as an implicitly hybrid method.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) research traditionally categorizes agents into model-free and model-based paradigms. Model-free agents, such as Q-learning or policy gradient methods, directly learn policies or value functions that map states to actions or predict future rewards, bypassing the explicit modeling of environment dynamics. This approach has yielded successful agents across complex domains but often results in policies or value functions that are highly specific to the trained reward function, limiting their reusability and interpretability.

Conversely, model-based RL agents explicitly learn a world model, which describes the environment's transition dynamics and reward function. This model can then be used for planning, prediction, and potentially generalization to new tasks. However, accurately modeling complex world dynamics can be computationally intensive and challenging.

A significant challenge in model-free RL is the "value equivalence problem," which posits that distinct world models can produce identical value functions and optimal policies. This underdetermination suggests a fundamental limitation in extracting a unique underlying environment model solely from an agent's learned value function or policy.

Recent advancements in RL, particularly in goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL), involve training agents on diverse sets of goals or reward functions. This practice could potentially introduce additional constraints on the agent's internal representation, offering a pathway to overcome the value equivalence problem. This paper investigates whether such multi-goal training enables model-free agents to implicitly encode an accurate and unique model of their environment, thereby bridging the conceptual gap between model-free, model-based, and goal-conditioned RL. The work differentiates itself from prior efforts in value equivalence by focusing on extracting an agent's internal world model from its value functions and policies, rather than learning surrogate models for planning or merely demonstrating implicit forward models. It also extends beyond successor features by not requiring linear reward decompositions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to ascertain whether model-free agents, particularly those trained with a diverse set of goals, implicitly learn an accurate and unique model of their environment. This is driven by the core question: "When do model-free agents implicitly encode an accurate model of their environment?"

The motivation for this investigation stems from several points:
- **Limitations of Model-Free RL:** While effective, model-free agents often learn task-specific behaviors. Their learned value functions or policies are tied to particular reward functions, making adaptation to new tasks difficult without extensive retraining.
- **Interpretability and Understanding:** Model-free agents are often viewed as "black boxes," making it challenging to understand what they have learned about the world's dynamics versus merely what they value for a specific task. Extracting an explicit world model could provide a more interpretable representation of their understanding.
- **The Value Equivalence Problem:** The existence of multiple world models that can generate the same optimal value functions and policies poses a theoretical barrier to uniquely identifying the underlying environment from an agent's observed behavior or internal value functions. The authors hypothesize that training on a sufficiently rich set of goals, as in GCRL, might introduce enough constraints to resolve this ambiguity.
- **Bridging RL Paradigms:** Demonstrating that model-free agents can implicitly learn world models could suggest a deeper, hitherto underexplored connection between model-free, model-based, and goal-conditioned RL, potentially leading to hybrid methods that combine their strengths.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** If model-free agents implicitly encode world models, these models might enable generalization to novel, out-of-distribution tasks for which the agent was not directly trained, revealing "hidden generalization capabilities."

To address these motivations, the paper aims to:
1. Introduce a methodology for extracting implicit world models from value-based agents.
2. Provide theoretical conditions under which such extraction is unique and accurate.
3. Empirically validate the effectiveness of the proposed methodology and explore the generalization capabilities of the extracted models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a combination of methodological contributions, theoretical analysis, and empirical validation.

**A. P-Learning: An Inverse Q-Learning Algorithm**
The core methodological contribution is **P-learning**, an algorithm designed to extract an implicit world model from a fixed agent's goal-conditioned Q-values, policy, and reward function. In contrast to Q-learning, which iteratively updates value estimates for a fixed environment model, P-learning iteratively updates a candidate world model (parameterized as Pφ) to be consistent with fixed Q-functions, effectively inverting the Bellman equation.

The objective function for P-learning is to minimize the Bellman residual L(φ) = ∥Tπφ(Q) - Q∥²d with respect to the world model parameters φ, where Tπφ(Q) is the Bellman operator under the candidate model Pφ. The gradient ∇φL(φ) is derived using the log-derivative trick, resulting in an update rule that pushes the model parameters towards minimizing the temporal difference (TD) error. This process is framed as a supervised learning problem for the world model.

For **tabular settings** (finite state, action, and goal spaces), the Bellman equation simplifies to a linear system M Pφ(s, a) = Q(s, a) for each (s, a) pair. In this context, tabular P-learning converges to a closed-form solution: P∞(s, a) = M⁺Q(s, a) + (I − M⁺M)P0(s, a), where M⁺ is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of M. This formula indicates that if M has full rank, P-learning converges to a unique solution M⁺Q. If the Q-values are exact, this solution corresponds to the true transition kernel P.

**B. Theoretical Analysis for Unique Identifiability**
The paper provides sufficient conditions under which the world model P can be uniquely identified from Q-values, policies, and rewards, thereby breaking value equivalence. These conditions vary across different MDP classes:

- **Finite MDPs:**
 - **Deterministic:** A single "generic" goal can be sufficient (Theorem 2). "Generic" implies rewards that lead to a column-injective M matrix, which almost all reward functions satisfy (full Lebesgue measure). For approximate Q-values, recovery is guaranteed if the approximation error ϵ is sufficiently small relative to the column separation of M.
 - **Stochastic:** At least |S| (number of states) goals are typically required. Identifiability holds for generic reward functions (Theorem 3a) or when goals "span the state space" (e.g., indicator goals, where each goal targets a distinct state) for generic policies or discount factors (Theorem 3b). Bounds for approximate Q-values are also provided.
 - **Local MDPs:** For N-local MDPs (where each state-action pair can transition to at most N next states), unique identification is possible with |G| ≥ N-1 generic goals if the support is known, or |G| ≥ 2N-1 if the support is unknown (Theorem 5).
- **Continuous MDPs:**
 - **Deterministic:** Conditions shift from count to coverage of the state space. A finite, but potentially large, number of terminating Gaussian goals (Theorem 8) or even 2d+1 non-terminating Gaussian goals if the value function is real-analytic (Theorem 9) can be sufficient. For indicator goals with termination, covering S with a ball of radius σ (G ⊇ S + Bσ(0)) is sufficient (Theorem 4).
 - **Stochastic (Unconditional Policies):** A goal set with non-empty interior for Gaussian rewards (Theorem 6) or covering S + Bσ(0) for indicator rewards (Theorem 7) can be sufficient, but these results are currently limited to unconditional policies (where the policy does not depend on the goal).

**C. Empirical Validation**
The theoretical findings are empirically validated across several benchmark RL environments:
- **FourRooms (Finite MDP):** Three variants of increasing stochasticity (deterministic, windy, teleporting) are used. Agents are trained with PQN and Hindsight Experience Replay (HER). World models are extracted using either column matching (deterministic) or linear programming (stochastic variants) for efficiency in the tabular setting.
- **Reacher (Continuous, Deterministic MuJoCo):** Agents are trained on a small set of |G|=4 position-based goals. The extracted WM is evaluated on its accuracy (MSE) and its ability to generalize by training new policies via value iteration *inside the extracted WM* for out-of-distribution (OOD) goals (e.g., target angular velocity or specific joint configurations). A systematic sweep of Q-network architectures (depth/width) is conducted to study the relationship between agent performance and WM accuracy.
- **MountainCar (Continuous, Deterministic):** Similar to Reacher, agents are trained on position-based goals, and the WM is evaluated on OOD goals, including those requiring velocity control. A second agent trained on velocity-based goals is used to compare the extracted WMs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields both theoretical and empirical findings regarding the implicit encoding of world models in model-free agents.

**A. Theoretical Breakthroughs in Value Equivalence:**
The paper formally demonstrates that training model-free agents on a sufficiently rich set of reward functions can resolve the value equivalence problem, enabling the unique identification of the underlying world model.
- **Deterministic Finite MDPs:** A single "generic" goal, or a single Gaussian reward in continuous state embeddings, is theoretically sufficient for unique identifiability (Theorem 2, Proposition 1). This is a strong result, indicating that under deterministic dynamics, even limited goal information can constrain the model significantly.
- **Stochastic Finite MDPs:** The requirement for identifiability increases to at least |S| goals. For generic reward functions, or when goals "span the state space" (e.g., indicator goals) and policies/discount factors are generic, the world model can be uniquely identified (Theorem 3). The paper provides tight error bounds for approximate Q-values in both deterministic and stochastic finite MDPs.
- **Continuous Deterministic MDPs:** Uniqueness is often driven by the coverage of the state space by the goal set. For indicator rewards with termination, a goal set covering S plus a buffer region (G ⊇ S + Bσ(0)) is sufficient (Theorem 4). Surprisingly, a finite number of goals (e.g., 2d+1 for real-analytic value functions, Theorem 9) can suffice for Gaussian rewards under certain conditions.
- **Continuous Stochastic MDPs (Unconditional Policies):** For unconditional policies, a goal set with non-empty interior for Gaussian rewards (Theorem 6) or covering S + Bσ(0) for indicator rewards (Theorem 7) can lead to identifiability.
- **Impossibility Results:** The paper also shows that using multiple discount factors or policies with a *single* goal reward function is generally insufficient to break value equivalence (Proposition 11), emphasizing the necessity of diverse goal *rewards*.

**B. Empirical Efficacy of P-Learning:**
P-learning is demonstrated to be highly effective in extracting accurate world models from agents, even when theoretical conditions are technically violated (e.g., using a finite number of goals in continuous state spaces).
- **Reacher:** Despite training on only |G|=4 position-based goals in a continuous state space (which theoretically requires infinite coverage for full guarantees), P-learning extracts a world model with extremely high fidelity (NMSE = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴), even though the Q-values themselves are imperfect (NMSE = 5.7 × 10⁻¹).
- **MountainCar:** Similarly, an agent trained on 4 position-based goals yields an implicit world model that accurately matches the true dynamics (NMSE = 6.7 × 10⁻³).
- **FourRooms Variants:** P-learning successfully recovers highly accurate WMs:
 - **Deterministic:** |G|=1 goal suffices for near-perfect recovery, consistent with theory.
 - **Windy (Local Stochastic):** |G|=4 goals lead to quasi-perfect recovery, aligning with the local MDP theory.
 - **Teleporting (Fully Stochastic):** Even with |G|=20 goals (well below the |S|=68 theoretical worst-case for full state-spanning), the extracted WM allows for quasi-optimal performance.

**C. Implicit Generalization Capabilities:**
A significant finding is that the world models extracted via P-learning reveal "hidden generalization capabilities" within the model-free agents.
- **Reacher:** Policies derived from the implicit WM (through value iteration) achieve quasi-optimal performance on out-of-distribution goals that the original agent was not trained for, including:
 - Reaching distant fingertip positions (far from training goal distribution).
 - Achieving specific joint angular configurations (target angle, a different state variable).
 - Reaching specific angular velocities (target velocity, an orthogonal variable not directly used in rewards). This suggests that the agent's Q-values implicitly encode dynamics over variables never explicitly rewarded.
- **MountainCar:** WM-derived policies achieve near-optimal performance on OOD goals such as reaching target velocities or shortest paths that could not be achieved with successor features. Furthermore, a MountainCar agent trained on velocity-based goals produced a nearly identical implicit world model to one trained on position-based goals (NMSE = 1.7 × 10⁻⁴ between them, vs. ~7.2 × 10⁻³ to ground truth for both). This leads to an informal "local-global" hypothesis: agents trained on local goals may encode accurate world models over *all* dependent environment variables.

**D. Correlation with Agent Performance:**
Empirical analysis in Reacher demonstrates a strong correlation between an agent's performance (return on training goals) and the accuracy of its internalised world model (WM MSE). A Spearman correlation coefficient of ρ = -0.98 was observed between agent return and WM error, and ρ = +0.95 between agent return and WM-policy return on unseen goals. This suggests that GCRL is an implicitly hybrid method, where performance improvements in value estimation are linked to learning better internal world dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of reinforcement learning in several ways:

**A. Bridging RL Paradigms:** The work fundamentally challenges the traditional dichotomy between model-based and model-free RL. By proving and empirically demonstrating that model-free agents trained on rich goal sets implicitly encode accurate world models, it establishes a theoretical and practical bridge between these paradigms. This suggests that model-free learning, particularly goal-conditioned variants, may not be as "model-free" as previously conceived.

**B. Enhanced Interpretability of Model-Free Agents:** P-learning offers a novel method to extract an explicit world model from trained value functions. This capability can improve the interpretability of complex model-free agents, providing insights into their understanding of environment dynamics, separate from their learned preferences or values. This addresses a common criticism of model-free approaches as "black boxes."

**C. Uncovering Implicit Generalization:** The discovery that extracted world models enable near-optimal planning for out-of-distribution goals, even those defined on state variables not directly present in training rewards (e.g., velocity goals from position-only training), is significant. This highlights previously unrecognized generalization capabilities hidden within goal-conditioned value functions, suggesting that agents learn more about the environment than what is immediately apparent from their task-specific performance.

**D. Foundations for Hybrid Methods:** The strong correlation between agent performance and the fidelity of its implicit world model, coupled with the "local-global" hypothesis, opens avenues for developing new hybrid RL methods. These methods could leverage the implicit world models learned by model-free agents to accelerate learning, improve sample efficiency, or enhance generalization, potentially combining the strengths of both model-free data efficiency and model-based planning capabilities.

**E. Implications for AI Safety and Auditing:** If agents implicitly learn world models, these models could serve as internal representations that could be inspected or audited. This has implications for AI safety, allowing researchers to probe an agent's understanding of its environment to detect biases or unintended behaviors, especially when training rewards are misspecified.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- Theoretical results for stochastic continuous MDPs are currently restricted to unconditional policies.
- The work does not cover partially observable MDPs or model-free agents that do not learn Q-values.
- Experiments for P-learning's scalability in very large finite environments have not been conducted.
- P-learning is presented as a tool for conceptual understanding and analysis, not as a direct substitute for model-based RL.

This research provides a new lens through which to understand the capabilities of model-free reinforcement learning, moving towards a more unified view of learning and planning in complex environments.
