# When More Is Less: A Systematic Analysis of Spatial and Commonsense Information for Visual Spatial Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Melbourne systematically analyzed how injecting external spatial contexts, commonsense knowledge, and reasoning instructions impacts visual spatial reasoning in vision-language models, revealing that excessive or irrelevant information often degrades performance rather than improving it. The study provides evidence that a targeted, concise approach to information injection is more effective than indiscriminate aggregation.

## Problem

- Vision-language models (VLMs) frequently struggle with ambiguous, frame-dependent, or imprecise visual spatial reasoning (VSR) despite general image-text task proficiency.
- Existing inference-time information injection techniques, used to augment VLM inputs with external cues, show inconsistent benefits and lack a systematic understanding of when they genuinely improve or degrade performance.
- There is an unexamined assumption that providing more input information inherently leads to better reasoning, which has not been consistently supported by empirical evidence.

## Method

- Conducted a hypothesis-driven empirical analysis, treating three representative VLMs (Qwen, LLaVA, BLIP-3) as fixed black-box systems.
- Systematically varied the type and quantity of ==external information injected into prompts==: nine types of spatial contexts (verbalized vs. numerical), commonsense knowledge (varying relevance and quantity), and spatial reasoning instructions (Chain-of-Thought).
- Evaluated the impact of these interventions on VSR performance using two public benchmarks: the ==VSR dataset== (fine-grained relations) and the ==EmbSpatial dataset== (cluttered scenes).

## Results

- Targeted spatial context injection yielded the most consistent improvements, with accuracy gains up to **9.6%** across models, consistently outperforming a generic spatial grounding baseline.
- Aggregating multiple spatial contexts generally led to diminishing returns and performance degradation, with models performing best with the injection of a **single** spatial context.
- Verbalized spatial contexts consistently outperformed their numerical counterparts, with numerical inputs often degrading performance by up to **9.2%** by introducing complexity; CoT prompting improved performance up to **+12.2%** on EmbSpatial but degraded it up to **-2.0%** on VSR, depending on the clarity of spatial grounding.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Injecting more information does not inherently lead to better visual spatial reasoning; excessive or irrelevant inputs can degrade VLM performance due to integration burden and cognitive overload.
> - Effective spatial grounding requires careful selection of a single, task-relevant spatial cue, which is often more beneficial in a verbalized format than as precise numerical values.
> - Commonsense knowledge and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting improve reasoning only when strictly relevant and when spatial grounding is already sufficiently precise and unambiguous, respectively.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual spatial reasoning (VSR) represents a persistent challenge for current vision-language models (VLMs). While modern multimodal systems have achieved proficiency in general image-text tasks, they frequently encounter difficulties with spatial relations that are ambiguous, dependent on the frame of reference, or necessitate precise grounding. Existing research efforts to mitigate this limitation typically pursue two primary avenues:
1. **Architectural and Dataset-focused Approaches:** This involves developing spatially-aware architectures or creating specialized datasets designed to enhance VLM capabilities in spatial understanding.
2. **Inference-time Information Injection:** This strategy augments VLM inputs with external cues during inference, without requiring additional model training. Such cues can include explicit spatial information, external commonsense knowledge, or chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning instructions.

This paper positions itself within the second category, focusing on inference-time information injection. However, it addresses a critical gap: the lack of systematic understanding regarding *when* such injected information genuinely improves reasoning and *when* it might introduce noise or degrade performance. While the intuitive assumption is that richer input leads to better reasoning, empirical evidence has shown inconsistent benefits. Information can be redundant, poorly aligned with the task, difficult for the model to integrate, or structured reasoning prompts might amplify incorrect assumptions rather than clarifying inference. Therefore, this work does not propose a new VLM architecture or training method but rather provides a systematic, hypothesis-driven empirical analysis of existing information injection techniques to delineate their effectiveness and limitations. It examines the nuances of input augmentation in VSR, questioning the assumption that "more information" is always beneficial and highlighting the importance of selective, task-aligned information provision.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to systematically investigate the conditions under which injecting additional information (spatial contexts, commonsense knowledge, and reasoning instructions) genuinely enhances visual spatial reasoning in VLMs, and conversely, when it can be detrimental. The motivation stems from the observed inconsistency in performance gains when using these augmentation techniques, prompting a need to understand the underlying mechanisms and potential pitfalls.

The authors formulate three specific hypotheses to guide their empirical analysis:

- **Hypothesis 1 (H1): Not all spatial contexts contribute equally to VSR.**
 - **Motivation:** The assumption is that providing explicit spatial cues should always help. However, different types of spatial information might vary in utility, and aggregating too many might overwhelm the model.
 - **Specific predictions:** (i) There will be substantial variance in the effectiveness of different spatial context types. (ii) Aggregating multiple spatial contexts will likely lead to diminishing returns or even performance degradation due to an increased integration burden on the VLM. This challenges the notion that a comprehensive set of spatial cues is always optimal.

- **Hypothesis 2 (H2): Excessive or weakly relevant commonsense knowledge can degrade VSR.**
 - **Motivation:** Commonsense knowledge is often considered beneficial for robust reasoning. However, an abundance of information, particularly if irrelevant or noisy, could distract the model or lead to information overload.
 - **Specific predictions:** Commonsense injection will be beneficial only when it is both relevant and concise. Providing large amounts of loosely related knowledge is expected to introduce distraction and overwhelm the VLM's reasoning process, leading to a performance peak at selective knowledge injection rather than maximal injection.

- **Hypothesis 3 (H3): Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting helps only when spatial grounding is sufficiently precise.**
 - **Motivation:** CoT prompting is designed to elicit step-by-step reasoning, which can improve performance in complex tasks. However, if the foundational spatial understanding (grounding) is flawed or ambiguous, CoT might amplify these errors rather than resolve them.
 - **Specific predictions:** CoT-style prompting will enhance VSR when the model possesses reliable premises (i.e., accurate spatial grounding and unambiguous inference requirements). Conversely, CoT is expected to amplify errors when grounding is uncertain, incomplete, or the task involves frame-dependent ambiguities.

A broader underlying motivation for this research is to move beyond superficial performance gains. The authors acknowledge that improvements from information injection might merely indicate heuristic exploitation of cues rather than a genuine development of robust spatial grounding within the VLM. Without a clear understanding of how different information types interact with internal model representations, assessing the true nature of performance improvements remains challenging. By systematically dissecting these interactions, the study aims to provide practical guidance for designing reliable multimodal reasoning pipelines and emphasize the importance of controlled information integration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a hypothesis-driven empirical analysis framework, treating vision-language models (VLMs) as fixed black-box systems. The core methodology involves systematically varying the type and quantity of external information injected into the input prompts, while keeping the VLM parameters constant. This approach aims to isolate the causal effect of different information types on visual spatial reasoning (VSR) performance.

**VLMs Evaluated:**
Three representative VLMs were selected to ensure diversity in architectural and training paradigms:
- Qwen-2-VL-7B (Qwen)
- LLaVA-Next-1.6-7B (LLaVA)
- BLIP-3-5B (BLIP)

**Benchmarks:**
Experiments were conducted on two public VSR benchmarks:
- **VSR dataset:** Designed to evaluate fine-grained spatial relations across 66 distinct categories.
- **EmbSpatial dataset:** Focuses on reasoning in cluttered, object-dense scenes, testing both explicit spatial grounding and abstract relational reasoning.

**Injected Information Types:**
The study systematically varies three categories of information appended to the image and question input:

1. **Spatial Contexts (SC):**
 - Nine distinct spatial contexts were extracted using off-the-shelf computer vision models:
 - **Spatial Values:** Bounding boxes, orientation angles, metric depth values.
 - **Interpretable Spatial Contexts (Verbalized):** Lateral (left/right), vertical (above/below), orientation (facing direction), depth (close/far), overlap (occlusion-like), and size (large/small).
 - These contexts are provided in a structured textual format.
 - **Off-the-shelf Perception Models Used:** Grounding DINO (object detection), Segment Anything 2 (segmentation), Depth Anything v2 (depth estimation), and Orient Anything (orientation estimation). These models are used solely for generating input contexts, not for VLM training or modification.

2. **Commonsense Knowledge (CK):**
 - Short commonsense statements are retrieved from the ATOMIC2020 knowledge base based on their semantic similarity to the input caption and detected entities.
 - **Control Mechanisms:**
 - **Similarity Threshold:** Controls the amount and perceived relevance of retrieved knowledge, with higher thresholds retaining fewer but more relevant statements.
 - **Knowledge Type:** Categorized into Physical-Entity (PE), Event-Centred (EC), and Social-Interaction (SI) knowledge to investigate the impact of different relational priors.

3. **Spatial Reasoning Instructions (SR) / Chain-of-Thought (CoT):**
 - These involve prepending brief, step-by-step reasoning instructions to the prompt. The purpose is to encourage the VLM to explicitly utilize the provided spatial and commonsense information before generating an answer.

**Experimental Settings:**
- **Zero-shot prompting:** Baseline performance, including only the image, caption, task description, and answer options.
- **Implicit prompting:** Adds light spatial cues without explicit external information.
- **Orient Anything (OA):** Reproduces a previously proposed method for spatial grounding.
- **Controlled Interventions:** Each spatial context type (SC), commonsense knowledge (CK), and spatial reasoning instruction (SR) is injected in isolation and in specific combinations (e.g., SC+CK+SR) to analyze individual and interaction effects.
- **Accuracy:** The primary evaluation metric reported across all experiments.

By systematically manipulating these input conditions, the methodology aims to provide empirical evidence to validate or refute the formulated hypotheses regarding the effectiveness of information injection in VSR.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments revealed consistent patterns across models and datasets, challenging the assumption that more external information invariably leads to better visual spatial reasoning.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Spatial Contexts (SC):** Injecting explicit spatial contexts yielded the most consistent and substantial improvements across all models and datasets, with accuracy gains up to 9.6%. SC consistently outperformed the `Orient Anything (OA)` baseline, which often performed worse than zero-shot prompting. This finding partially supports **H1**, indicating that targeted spatial grounding is more effective than generic or aggregated cues.
- **Commonsense Knowledge (CK):** The impact of CK was model-dependent. While Qwen and BLIP-3 generally benefited from CK, LLaVA exhibited a slight degradation in accuracy (–0.6%). This variability supports **H2**, suggesting that commonsense knowledge must be selectively injected to avoid introducing noise or overwhelming the model.
- **Spatial Reasoning Instructions (SR) / Chain-of-Thought (CoT):** SR demonstrated divergent effects. On the EmbSpatial dataset, SR significantly improved performance for LLaVA and BLIP-3 (up to +12.2%). However, on the VSR dataset, it led to accuracy degradation (up to –2.0%). This contrast supports **H3**, indicating that CoT-style prompting is beneficial primarily when clear premises are available, but can be detrimental in ambiguous settings where it might amplify errors.
- **Combined Interventions and Cognitive Overload:** Combining SC, CK, and SR did not result in additive performance gains. In several instances, the full combination (SC+CK+SR) led to a significant reduction in accuracy compared to simpler interventions. This observation suggests a cognitive overload effect, where simultaneously injecting multiple signals increases the integration burden on VLMs, leading to confusion rather than synergy. This reinforces the overarching theme that a single, task-relevant spatial context is often more effective than indiscriminate aggregation.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Spatial Context (Testing H1)

- **Individual Spatial Context Utility:** Injecting individual spatial contexts generally improved performance over zero-shot prompting, but the magnitude and direction of improvement varied significantly across different context types, models, and datasets.
 - On the VSR dataset, Qwen benefited most from depth-related cues, LLaVA from orientational context, and BLIP-3 from lateral relations.
 - On the EmbSpatial dataset, Qwen performed best with vertical context, LLaVA with overlap relations, and BLIP-3 again favored lateral context.
 - This variability indicates that spatial contexts are not interchangeable and their utility is tightly coupled to the specific reasoning demands of the dataset and the VLM's internal representations. VSR, with its focus on fine-grained relations, benefited from 3D cues, while EmbSpatial, involving cluttered scenes, found 2D relational cues more effective.
- **Verbalized vs. Numerical Contexts:** A consistent trend was that verbalized spatial contexts (e.g., "left/right", "close/far") generally outperformed their numerical counterparts (e.g., raw orientation angles, metric depth values). Numerical inputs often degraded performance (up to 9.2%), suggesting that continuous numeric cues introduce additional integration complexity, requiring models to interpret and normalize values, a burden that often outweighs the potential informational gain for current VLMs.
- **Implications for H1:** These findings strongly support H1, demonstrating that effective spatial grounding requires selecting a single, task-relevant spatial cue and expressing it in a form that the model can reliably consume. Mismatched or overly complex spatial information is frequently treated as noise.

#### 4.3. Effect of Number of Spatial Contexts (Further Testing H1)

- Further analysis on context aggregation revealed a clear trend: performance across all three models peaked with the injection of a single spatial context. As more contexts were cumulatively appended (in descending order of their individual effectiveness), performance generally declined. A notable drop occurred around five contexts, with no model recovering to earlier accuracy levels with more than three contexts.
- This provides strong evidence for H1, showing that aggregating multiple spatial cues leads to diminishing returns and often degrades performance, indicating that current VLMs struggle to integrate a large number of simultaneous spatial signals.

#### 4.4. Commonsense Knowledge Analysis (Testing H2)

- Performance for commonsense knowledge injection was analyzed by varying a similarity threshold (0.1 to 0.9) and knowledge type (Physical-Entity (PE), Event-Centred (EC), Social-Interaction (SI)).
- A consistent pattern emerged: performance peaked at high similarity thresholds (0.7–0.9), where knowledge statements were most relevant. As the threshold decreased (admitting more loosely related knowledge), accuracy declined. At suboptimal thresholds (e.g., 0.2 for Qwen and BLIP-3, 0.4 for LLaVA), accuracy fell below zero-shot levels, indicating that weakly aligned knowledge acts as noise.
- While all knowledge types showed comparable peak performance at high thresholds, PE knowledge exhibited instability at mid-level thresholds, suggesting that even spatially aligned commonsense can be detrimental if injected imprecisely or excessively.
- These findings strongly support H2, demonstrating that commonsense knowledge improves VSR only when selectively injected and relevant, while excessive or weakly aligned information induces overload.

#### 4.5. Impact of Chain-of-Thought Reasoning (Testing H3)

- Qualitative examples illustrated the context-dependent utility of CoT prompting.
 - In cases with ambiguous frames of reference (e.g., "A is on the right of B" when intrinsic vs. extrinsic perspectives differ), CoT prompting caused the model to construct an explicit but incorrect reasoning chain based on an amplified erroneous assumption, leading to a wrong answer (Figure 6a).
 - In contrast, for frame-independent relations with unambiguous grounding (e.g., "The cat is on the umbrella"), CoT helped articulate the correct reasoning path, leading to a correct prediction (Figure 6b).
- These examples provide evidence for H3: CoT prompting is beneficial only when spatial grounding is sufficiently precise and the underlying inference is unambiguous. When grounding is incomplete, frame-dependent, or uncertain, CoT can amplify errors by forcing a coherent but ultimately incorrect reasoning chain. Thus, CoT effectiveness in VSR is critically dependent on the reliability of the underlying spatial premises.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this systematic analysis contribute significantly to the understanding of visual spatial reasoning in vision-language models and have several potential impacts:

Firstly, the research empirically validates the central premise that **more information does not necessarily lead to better reasoning** in VLMs for VSR tasks. This conclusion challenges a common intuitive assumption in prompt engineering and multimodal model development, establishing a nuanced perspective on information injection. It demonstrates that indiscriminate context accumulation can hinder, rather than help, VSR performance due to factors such as integration burden and cognitive overload.

Secondly, the study provides **practical insights and actionable guidance** for designing more reliable and effective multimodal reasoning pipelines.
- For **spatial context injection**, it highlights the importance of careful selection, indicating that single, task-relevant spatial cues are often superior to aggregated, diverse cues. It also reveals that verbalized spatial contexts are generally more effective and easier for current VLMs to consume than precise numerical values, which can introduce unnecessary complexity.
- For **commonsense knowledge**, the research emphasizes the necessity of strict relevance filtering, demonstrating that only highly relevant and concise knowledge enhances performance, while weakly aligned or excessive information degrades it.
- For **chain-of-thought prompting**, it clarifies that CoT is not universally beneficial for VSR. Its effectiveness is contingent on the precision and unambiguous nature of the underlying spatial grounding. This suggests that CoT should be applied judiciously, particularly when dealing with frame-dependent or uncertain spatial relations.

Thirdly, this work contributes to a deeper understanding of **VLM limitations** regarding information processing and integration. The consistent observation of diminishing returns, overload effects, and grounding-dependent CoT behavior suggests that current VLMs may struggle with sophisticated information synthesis, particularly when faced with conflicting, redundant, or overly complex inputs. This indicates a need for future VLM architectures or prompting strategies that are more robust to noise and capable of selective information processing.

Finally, by systematically deconstructing the effects of various input interventions, the paper moves beyond reporting mere performance gains. It offers a framework for **diagnosing the robustness of VSR in VLMs**, helping researchers distinguish between genuine improvements in reasoning capabilities and the mere exploitation of superficial cues or fragile reasoning chains. This can guide the development of more principled prompt engineering techniques and contribute to the creation of VLMs with genuinely robust and interpretable spatial reasoning abilities.
