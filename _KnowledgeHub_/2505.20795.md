# Learning Generalizable Robot Policy with Human Demonstration Video as a Prompt

> [!summary] Summary
> A two-stage framework enables robots to learn dexterous manipulation skills by interpreting human demonstration videos as prompts for new tasks. This method leverages a cross-prediction video generation model to learn generalizable task representations and a ProtoDiffusion Contrastive Policy, demonstrating improved generalization to novel tasks, objects, and skills on real-world robot platforms.

## Problem

- Traditional robot learning requires extensive and costly teleoperation data collection for each new task, hindering scalability.
- Language instructions often lack the detailed spatial and temporal cues critical for precise physical manipulation, limiting robot understanding.
- Prior work using human videos for robot learning suffered from limitations like single-task focus or insufficient quantities for robust generalization.

## Method

- A two-stage framework leverages extensive human demonstration videos with sparse robot data, using a fine-tuned video generation model for cross-prediction to learn embodiment-transferable representations.
- Motion retargeting aligns human hand actions with robot hand joints, enabling skill learning with human video-action pairs and a representation-conditioned diffusion policy.
- ==The ProtoDiffusion Contrastive Policy (PDCP)== integrates contrastive, prototypical cross-entropy, and Siamese-style metric losses to enhance representation quality and task discriminability during policy training.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **0.79** for positional generalization and **0.73** for background generalization on real-world dexterous manipulation tasks, outperforming all baselines.
- Demonstrated improved success rates on novel objects, with methods incorporating human videos showing significant gains compared to robot-only trained policies.
- Enabled generalization to entirely new skills (e.g., **0.47** SR for "Grasp Bottle" where baselines achieved **0.00** SR) by learning from human demonstrations, showcasing video-based in-context learning capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The cross-prediction mechanism effectively transfers task and skill knowledge between human and robot embodiments, allowing generated videos to depict tasks by different agents.
> - Learned representations from human and robot videos exhibit vector arithmetic properties, enabling manipulation and composition of skills similar to word embeddings.
> - Integrating a prototypical contrastive loss within the diffusion policy significantly improves skill separation and multi-skill imitation performance by fostering robust, task-aware feature clustering.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traditional robot learning methodologies often rely on imitation learning from extensive datasets primarily collected through teleoperation or on language-conditioned policies. While effective for tasks within their training distribution, these approaches exhibit limitations when confronted with novel tasks. A significant challenge lies in the necessity for collecting new teleoperation data and subsequently fine-tuning policies for each new task, a process that is both time-consuming and expensive. Furthermore, language instructions, while intuitive for humans, often lack the granular spatial and temporal details crucial for physical manipulation.

In contrast, human beings possess the ability to acquire new skills efficiently by observing others. This biological capability suggests that visual demonstrations, particularly human videos, could serve as a more natural and information-rich medium for robot learning. Videos inherently capture not only the "what" but also the "how" of a task, including object relationships, motion trajectories, and timing. Moreover, human demonstration videos are more scalable to acquire than robot-specific data, whether through laboratory capture or leveraging existing online resources.

Recent research has begun to explore the integration of human videos into robot learning. Approaches like EgoMimic primarily address single-task scenarios. UniSkill uses human videos for representation learning but does not leverage them in the policy learning stage for direct generalization. Gen2Act and Human2Robot incorporate human videos, but often with limited quantities, which can restrict their ability to achieve robust generalization when combined with sparse robot demonstrations. Works like XSkill aim for cross-embodiment skill discovery through transferable representations, while Diffusion Policy and its successors like RDT-1B demonstrate the power of diffusion models in modeling complex action distributions for robot control.

This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by proposing a novel framework designed to overcome the limitations of prior work. It aims to more effectively leverage extensive human demonstration videos in conjunction with sparse robot data across two distinct stages of learning. By using human demonstration videos directly as prompts, this work seeks to enable a robot to perform new tasks without requiring new teleoperation data collection or model finetuning, thereby addressing the challenges of data efficiency, cost, and generalization in complex dexterous manipulation. It integrates video generation, cross-embodiment learning, and a novel contrastive loss within a diffusion policy framework to enhance representation quality and policy generalization, particularly for dexterous manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent limitations of current robot learning paradigms and the observed efficiency of human learning.

**Motivation:**
1. **Data Collection Burden:** Current methods often necessitate extensive teleoperation data collection for each new task, a process that is recognized as tedious, time-consuming, and expensive. This dependency creates a significant bottleneck for scaling robot capabilities.
2. **Information Richness of Prompts:** Language instructions, while convenient, provide only categorical information and lack the detailed spatial and temporal cues vital for precise physical manipulation. This limits the robot's understanding of "how" to perform a task.
3. **Human Learning Paradigm:** Humans can rapidly acquire new skills by simply observing others. This suggests that visual demonstrations, especially human videos, could offer a more natural, information-dense, and scalable medium for teaching robots, capturing critical aspects like object interaction and motion trajectories.
4. **Scalability of Human Data:** Human demonstration videos are readily available from diverse sources or can be easily self-collected, offering a virtually unlimited resource compared to robot-specific operational data.
5. **Addressing Prior Limitations:** Existing approaches leveraging human videos often face limitations such as focusing on single tasks, not fully integrating human videos into policy learning, or relying on limited human demonstration quantities, which restricts robust generalization.

### 3. Key Objectives

To address these motivations, the authors established the following objectives:
1. **Develop a generalizable robot policy** that can directly interpret a human demonstration video as a prompt to perform new tasks.
2. **Eliminate the need for new teleoperation data collection or model finetuning** when encountering novel tasks, enabling zero-shot or in-context learning capabilities.
3. **Learn a robust joint representation** that captures task, context, and embodiment information from both human and robot demonstration video data using a cross-prediction strategy.
4. **Fuse the learned representation with a shared action space** between human and robot, leveraging abundant human data alongside sparse robot data.
5. **Enhance skill separation and multi-skill imitation performance** through a novel prototypical contrastive loss.
6. **Evaluate the framework's effectiveness and generalization capabilities** across three progressive levels: basic variations (position, scene, background), novel objects, and novel skills, specifically in real-world dexterous manipulation tasks.

The core contribution is articulated as achieving elementary generalization ability to new tasks and novel skills from human prompt videos by effectively leveraging extensive human demonstration videos through a novel learning framework. This involves three concrete contributions: (1) a cross-embodiment learning strategy via cross-prediction for representation learning, (2) joint training with human video-action pairs to boost policy generalization, and (3) the ProtoDiffusion Contrastive Policy (PDCP) objective to improve representation quality.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework is a two-stage learning process designed to enable a robot to extract meaningful features and representations from human demonstration videos to perform specific dexterous manipulation tasks.

**Stage 1: Augment Video Generation by Cross Prediction (VGCP)**
The first stage focuses on learning a robust, generalizable representation from diverse video data. This is achieved by fine-tuning an existing video generation model, which initially possesses prior knowledge of physical world dynamics from extensive online video datasets, with a custom dataset relevant to robotic manipulation. The goal is for the model to capture details of human manipulation, object motion, scene context, and affordances, particularly for complex dexterous hand operations. The custom dataset comprises predominantly human manipulation videos, supplemented by robotic gripper videos (for object movement and scene understanding) and self-collected dexterous hand videos.

A key innovation in this stage is **Cross-Prediction**. The video generation model is trained to receive a prompt video of a task performed by a source embodiment (e.g., a human) and an initial image of a target embodiment (e.g., a robot hand). It then generates a video depicting the target embodiment performing the same task, effectively embedding embodiment transfer information. During training, the model randomly selects between cross-prediction (different source and target embodiments, with probability P) and normal-prediction (same embodiment, with probability 1-P). This mechanism aims to teach the model how to transfer task and skill knowledge between human and robot modalities while retaining its ability to generate videos within a single embodiment. The fine-tuned video generation model, along with its extracted representations, is subsequently frozen for use in Stage 2.

**Stage 2: Skill Learning by Human Video-Action Pair Boosting**
The second stage focuses on learning a manipulation policy for the dexterous hand, significantly reducing reliance on costly robot-specific data by leveraging abundant human hand demonstrations.

1. **Human Action Preprocessing:** To bridge the embodiment gap, human demonstration videos (third-person RGB) are aligned with robot data (third-person RGB and joint state).
 - **Hand Tracking:** The WiLoR method is employed to localize the human hand in video frames, reconstructing a 3D hand mesh and extracting 21 right-hand keypoints (J_h_i).
 - **6D Wrist Pose Extraction:** The wrist keypoint (W) provides position. Orientation is derived from a local coordinate frame constructed using W, the index-finger joint (I), and the middle-finger joint (M). This yields a 6D pose (position and rotation matrix).
 - **Coordinate Transformation:** The human hand's 3D position is transformed from the camera coordinate system to the robot's base coordinate system using intrinsic parameters.
 - **Motion Retargeting:** Following AnyTeleop, an optimization problem maps human hand joints to corresponding dexterous hand joints, establishing joint-level correspondence for a unified action space.

2. **Representation Conditioned Diffusion Policy:** A diffusion policy is used to model the conditional action distribution p(a_t | s_t, z), where:
 - `s_t`: Visual observation feature extracted by a pretrained ResNet-34 from the current robot observation.
 - `z`: The stage-one representation, which is derived from the human prompt video (human representation for human, robot representation for robot, generated by the cross-prediction model).
 - `a_t`: The 19-dimensional action vector at time t.
 The diffusion process generates denoised robot actions. The action space is decomposed into three components: 12 finger joint angles (a_finger), 3D wrist orientation (a_rot), and 3D wrist position (a_pos). Separate prediction heads in the transformer-based denoising network handle each modality, and the training loss (L_action) is a weighted sum of component losses. The input token `h_0` concatenates `s_t` and `z`.

**ProtoDiffusion Contrastive Policy (PDCP)**
To enhance representation quality and task discriminability, the Siamese Prototypical Contrastive Learning (SPCL) approach is adapted and integrated into the diffusion policy training. A learnable clustering token is added to the input of the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) encoder. The encoder's output for this token (h) serves as a prototype-aware latent representation. Joint optimization occurs across three losses:
1. **NT-Xent Contrastive Loss (L_contra):** Treats tasks sharing the same skill as positive pairs and tasks with different skills as negative pairs, analogous to data-augmented positives in vision.
2. **Prototypical Cross-Entropy Loss (L_proto):** Encourages the learned latent `h` to align with the cross-entropy distribution over its K-Means prototype label, promoting tighter intra-prototype clustering and clearer inter-prototype separation.
3. **Siamese-style Metric Loss (L_metric):** Further enforces proximity among samples from the same prototype in the metric space while repelling samples from different prototypes, aiming to reduce semantic confusion.

The total loss for PDCP combines the action loss (L_action) with a weighted sum of these three contrastive losses. This comprehensive loss objective encourages the learning of task-discriminative and modality-aligned representations, which is intended to improve cross-modal generalization. During inference, the model extracts task representations from human prompt videos, fuses them with the robot's real-time observations, and then generates actions through the Stage-2 policy.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on real-world dexterous manipulation tasks using the Xhand robot, encompassing 13 distinct skills further decomposed into over 100 concrete tasks. Generalization was evaluated across positions, scenes, objects, and skills.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Stage 1 Data:** Over 250,000 videos of human, gripper, and dexterous-hand demonstrations from datasets including RH20T, Something-Something, HOI4D, Bridge, and an in-house dexterous manipulation dataset.
- **Stage 2 Data:** Approximately 7,000 human and dexterous-hand videos, including paired in-house demonstrations and additional human videos from HOI4D. The human-to-dexterous-hand video ratio was approximately 5:1, reflecting a sparse robot data setting.
- **Inference:** Task representations were extracted from human prompt videos, fused with real-time robot observations, and fed into the Stage-2 policy (DiT from RDT-1B) to generate continuous actions at 10 Hz.
- **Metrics:** Success Rate (SR) and Task Score (quantifying task progress with partial credit for subtasks).
- **Baselines:** XSkill, UniSkill, Language-conditioned Diffusion Policy, Language+Robot+Human, Representation+Robot, and Representation+Robot+Human (without PDCP or circle tags).

**A. Evaluation for Cross Prediction Video Generation**
The cross-prediction mechanism effectively enables embodiment transfer. Examples (Fig. 4) demonstrate that the model can take a human demonstration video as a prompt and generate a video of a robot performing the identical task. The generated videos faithfully capture task details, and in cross-embodiment cases, the target embodiment's motion and scene context accurately align with the original task. This indicates that the model learned not only physical world dynamics but also how to transfer task and skill knowledge across different embodiments.

**B. Evaluation for the Learned Representations**
1. **t-SNE Visualization (Fig. 5a):** The t-SNE visualization of learned representations showed effective task differentiation. Tasks of the same category clustered closely, with identical hues indicating representations at different time points within the same task. The clusters also distinguished target object positions. This indicates that the learned representations encode embodiment, task information, object positioning, and continuous temporal details, demonstrating an advantage over categorical language prompts.
2. **Vector Properties (Fig. 5b):** The learned representations exhibited vector arithmetic properties similar to word embeddings. For instance, subtracting a human representation from a robot representation for one task and then adding a human representation for a different task resulted in a composite representation that, when used to generate a video, depicted the robot executing the second task. This suggests that the representations capture transferable semantic meaning, allowing for manipulation and composition of skills.

**C. Position, Scene, Background Generalization**
The proposed method, "Repr.+R.+H.+DPCP (Ours)," consistently outperformed all baseline approaches across positional, scene, and background generalization scenarios (Table I).
- **Positional Generalization:** Our method achieved an SR of 0.79 and a score of 85.3, compared to XSkill's 0.31 SR and 60.1 score, and Language+R.+H.'s 0.58 SR and 69.5 score.
- **Scene Generalization:** Our method showed an SR of 0.69 and a score of 88.9.
- **Background Generalization:** Our method achieved an SR of 0.73 and a score of 80.9.
This performance advantage is attributed to the richer task-relevant information encoded by the learned representations from Stage 1 and the complementary functionality of the PDCP module, which enables task-aware feature clustering and mitigates mis-grasping.

**D. Object Generalization**
Experiments on object generalization, where "new objects" were present only in human demonstrations and not in robot training data, demonstrated the benefits of incorporating human data (Figure 6).
- Methods incorporating human videos in Stage 2 (Repr.+R.+H. and Ours) showed significant improvement in success rate on novel objects compared to those trained solely on robot data (Language-Conditioned Diffusion Policy, XSkill, UniSkill).
- Our method achieved the highest success rates, indicating that human data effectively transfers knowledge of object affordances and manipulation strategies. The learned representation further complements this by providing spatial cues and operational knowledge for tasks potentially absent from the robot training set. Baselines like XSkill and UniSkill, which cannot leverage human actions during Stage 2 policy learning, performed poorly on unseen-object tasks.

**E. Skill Generalization**
The ability to generalize to entirely new skills, absent from the robot training dataset, was evaluated on four novel tasks (grasp orange juice bottle, press button, close drawer, open carton). The policy was trained using robot trajectories *excluding* the target skill, but incorporating the full set of human demonstrations *including* that skill. A preprocessing trick involving overlaying a solid circle on the wrist position in both human and robot videos was applied to reduce the visual gap and provide position-aware cues.
- Our method, "Repr.+R.+H.+DPCP (Ours)," consistently outperformed all baselines in terms of both SR and Score across all four novel skills (Table II). For instance, on "Grasp Bottle," our method achieved an SR of 0.47, while XSkill, UniSkill, and Language-conditioned Diffusion Policy all yielded an SR of 0.00.
- The results show that leveraging human demonstrations effectively enables the acquisition and execution of novel skills. The addition of circle tags further improved performance by enhancing visual alignment and providing clearer positional cues, leading to more accurate actions. This demonstrates a form of video-based "in-context learning," where the robot can successfully execute previously unseen skills by observing human demonstrations (Fig. 7).

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that significantly advances robot learning by leveraging human demonstration videos as rich, informative prompts. The work addresses several critical limitations of traditional robot learning, and its potential impact is multi-faceted:

1. **Reduced Data Dependence and Cost:** By effectively utilizing abundant human demonstration data, the framework substantially reduces the reliance on costly, time-consuming, and tedious robot-specific teleoperation data collection. This can make robot skill acquisition more scalable and economically viable.
2. **Enhanced Generalization:** The proposed two-stage learning process, particularly through cross-prediction for representation learning and the ProtoDiffusion Contrastive Policy (PDCP), enables robust generalization to novel positions, scenes, backgrounds, objects, and even entirely unseen skills. This is a crucial step towards developing more adaptable and versatile robotic systems.
3. **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability for robots to learn directly from human demonstration videos simplifies the teaching process. Humans can demonstrate tasks in a natural manner, and the robot can directly interpret these visual cues, fostering a more intuitive and efficient human-robot interaction paradigm.
4. **Enabling In-Context Learning:** The framework demonstrates a form of video-based "in-context learning," where a robot can acquire and execute novel skills simply by "watching" human demonstrations, even when those specific skills were absent from its robot-specific training data. This capability holds promise for robots operating in dynamic, unstructured environments where pre-programmed or exhaustively trained behaviors are impractical.
5. **Richer Task Understanding:** By extracting dense spatial and temporal information from video prompts, the robot gains a more comprehensive understanding of task semantics and execution details than is typically possible with categorical language instructions. The demonstrated vector properties of the learned representations suggest a deeper, manipulable encoding of skills.
6. **Advancement in Dexterous Manipulation:** The empirical evaluations on real-world dexterous manipulation tasks highlight the framework's effectiveness in controlling complex multi-fingered robot hands. This is a challenging domain where fine-grained control and adaptability are paramount.

Overall, this research contributes to the development of more autonomous, adaptable, and easily programmable robots by bridging the gap between human observational learning and robotic skill acquisition. It lays a foundation for future robotic systems that can learn and adapt to new tasks and environments with greater efficiency and less human intervention.
