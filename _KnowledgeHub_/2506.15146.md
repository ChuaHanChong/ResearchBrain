# TACT: Humanoid Whole-body Contact Manipulation through Deep Imitation Learning with Tactile Modality

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at CNRS-AIST JRL and Tokyo University of Science developed TACT, a deep imitation learning framework that enables a life-size humanoid robot to perform whole-body contact manipulation using integrated visual, tactile, and proprioceptive feedback. The system demonstrated 100% success in holding medium and large fragile paper boxes, outperforming baselines and generalizing to unseen objects.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots are typically limited to manipulation involving only hands and feet, restricting their versatility for complex tasks in unstructured environments.
- Existing model-based control methods face high computational costs for real-time generation of multi-contact, whole-body motions.
- Accurately perceiving and processing distributed tactile feedback across large robot body surfaces remains a significant challenge for complex manipulation.

## Method

- A two-layer control architecture combines a model-based lower layer for stable locomotion and kinematic retargeting with a learning-based upper layer for manipulation.
- The **TACT** (Tactile-modality extended ACT) policy, a Transformer-based ==Conditional Variational Autoencoder==, processes multimodal inputs including proprioception, vision, and distributed tactile/proximity sensor data.
- The policy is trained via deep imitation learning from human teleoperation demonstrations, predicting future human arm poses which are then translated into physically constrained robot movements by the lower layer.

## Results

- In real-world experiments, the RHP7 Kaleido robot achieved **100%** success rates in holding medium and large fragile paper boxes (3/3 trials each), significantly outperforming vision-only and tactile-only baselines.
- The TACT policy demonstrated improved generalization to unseen object sizes, successfully manipulating four out of five varied boxes, showcasing adaptability beyond training data.
- Analysis revealed that vision was crucial for object sizing and approach, while tactile feedback was indispensable for delicate contact maintenance, confirming the complementary roles of both modalities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multimodal inputs, specifically the synergistic combination of vision and tactile sensing, are essential for robust and delicate whole-body contact manipulation on a humanoid robot.
> - Tactile feedback provides critical fine force control and contact maintenance capabilities, preventing both object crushing and accidental drops during manipulation.
> - A hybrid control architecture, integrating a learning-based manipulation policy with a stable model-based locomotion system, enables dynamic and versatile robot behavior in complex tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several critical and rapidly evolving domains in humanoid robotics: whole-body loco-manipulation, tactile sensing, and learning-based control.

Traditionally, humanoid robot manipulation has been largely restricted to contacts at the extremities (hands and feet). This simplification stems from the challenges associated with modeling complex whole-body dynamics and the high computational cost of motion generation for multi-contact scenarios. Previous model-based approaches, such as optimization-based posture generation or random sampling in configuration spaces, struggle with real-time performance and generating dense sequences of poses, often limited to keyframe generation. The stabilization of multi-contact motions typically requires *a priori* knowledge of contact points, which is a significant limitation for dynamic, unconstrained interactions. This paper directly addresses these limitations by moving beyond extremity-only contact to enable robust and delicate manipulation using the entire upper body.

In the realm of tactile sensing, the deployment of distributed tactile sensors on humanoid bodies has been explored to overcome the limitations of force/torque sensors at wrists, providing direct measurement of contact. Prior work has applied such sensors to tasks like large object manipulation, multi-contact motion, and human-robot interaction. While some model-based approaches require tedious geometric calibration, learning-based methods have shown promise in exploiting the spatial structure of tactile sensors using techniques like graph neural networks. This work contributes by leveraging the high-dimensional data from distributed tactile and proximity sensors through a Transformer-based architecture, implicitly learning sensor correlations without explicit calibration, thus making the tactile modality more accessible and effective for complex tasks.

The field of learning-based humanoid control has seen substantial growth, with both reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL) being actively pursued. RL approaches often rely on extensive simulation and domain randomization to achieve robust locomotion, while IL approaches typically train policies from teleoperation data, often assuming existing lower-level controllers for walking and retargeting. This paper aligns with the latter, employing an imitation learning strategy. The authors specifically extend Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), a prominent IL model for robot manipulation, to incorporate tactile sensing, naming it TACT. By choosing IL, the authors avoid the inefficiency of RL in exploring the vast search space of whole-body contact manipulation, instead capitalizing on human expertise to generate complex, contact-rich behaviors. The integration with a robust model-based locomotion controller represents a hybrid approach that combines the strengths of both paradigms.

The core innovation lies in the **unprecedented combination** of these elements: **multimodal deep imitation learning (vision, tactile, proprioception)** applied to **whole-body contact loco-manipulation** on a **life-size humanoid robot (RHP7 Kaleido)**, driven by a **Transformer-based policy (TACT)**. This work advances the state-of-the-art by enabling humanoids to perform complex, dexterous tasks that leverage distributed physical interaction with the environment, moving closer to human-like versatility and robustness in unstructured settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the disparity between current humanoid robot capabilities and the versatility of human interaction with the environment. Humans instinctively utilize their entire body to interact with objects and surroundings, distributing loads and enhancing stability. In contrast, most humanoid robots are limited to behaviors involving contact only at their extremities, such as hands and feet. This limitation restricts their ability to perform a wide range of tasks in daily life and industrial environments that demand richer, whole-body physical interaction.

The authors identify several key challenges that hinder the realization of human-like whole-body manipulation in robots:
1. **Computational Cost:** Model-based humanoid control methods face significant computational expenses when generating motions that involve numerous, dynamically changing contact points across the robot's body.
2. **Difficulty in Measuring Broad-Area Contact:** Accurately perceiving and processing distributed tactile measurements across large body surfaces remains a complex problem. Conventional approaches often simplify these measurements to low-dimensional data, losing crucial fine-grained feedback.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this study are:
- **Develop a humanoid control system** capable of enabling delicate and rich whole-body contact loco-manipulation. This system aims to allow the robot to learn complex manipulation skills directly from human demonstrations.
- **Integrate distributed tactile sensors** across the upper body of a humanoid robot to provide comprehensive contact information, including both direct touch and proximity.
- **Propose a novel learning-based policy, TACT (tactile-modality extended ACT),** which can process multimodal sensory inputs—specifically tactile, visual, and proprioceptive (joint position) data—to generate appropriate whole-body actions.
- **Demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness** of this integrated system on a **life-size humanoid robot (RHP7 Kaleido)**, showcasing its ability to perform whole-body contact manipulation while maintaining balance and locomotion.
- **Experimentally verify the importance of multimodal inputs,** particularly the synergistic contribution of both vision and tactile modalities, for robust and delicate manipulation tasks involving broad contact.

By addressing these objectives, the researchers aim to bridge the gap between human and robotic manipulation capabilities, paving the way for more adaptable and robust humanoid robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed control system for whole-body contact manipulation is built upon a robust two-layer architecture, combining the strengths of model-based and learning-based control:

**A. Lower Layer: Model-Based Retargeting and Locomotion Control**
This layer ensures the robot's fundamental stability and basic movement capabilities, while also translating human commands into robot actions. It is implemented using a conventional, real-time control framework (`mc_rtc`).

1. **LIPM-based Biped Control:** The foundation for stable locomotion is a controller that models the robot's bipedal dynamics using the Linear Inverted Pendulum Mode (LIPM). This controller computes a dynamically consistent Center of Mass (CoM) command by solving an optimization problem to track a reference Zero Moment Point (ZMP), derived from a footstep sequence. It incorporates discrete preview control and feedback stabilization based on the Divergent Component of Motion (DCM) to maintain balance and stability even during dynamic upper-body movements or external disturbances. Contact wrenches at the soles are regulated, and foot poses are adjusted via damping control to manage contact forces.
2. **Kinematic Retargeting:** To collect training data and enable intuitive teleoperation, the system measures the posture of a human operator using pose trackers (HTC VIVE) on the waist, elbows, and wrists. These human poses are then kinematically retargeted to the robot's upper body (elbows and wrists relative to the robot's waist). A calibration procedure accounts for differences in body proportions, and a first-order lag system ensures smooth motion mapping. While implemented in simulation, model-based admittance control was designed to enhance compliance during contact by shifting IK targets proportionally to detected tactile pressure, mitigating internal forces.

**B. Upper Layer: Learning-Based Manipulation Control**
This layer is responsible for generating complex, contact-rich manipulation motions using learned policies.

1. **TACT (Tactile-modality extended ACT) Model Structure:** The core of the manipulation control is TACT, an extension of the prominent Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) imitation learning model. TACT is a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) based policy.
 - It takes **multimodal inputs:**
 - **Proprioception:** Robot joint positions.
 - **Vision:** Color images from a head-mounted camera, processed by a ResNet18 for feature extraction.
 - **Tactile Modality:** Measurements from distributed tactile sensors (e-skin) on the robot's upper body. These measurements (intensity of touch and proximity from 163 cells, flattened into a 326-element 1D vector) are linearly projected into a token.
 - The model uses **Transformers** for its encoder and decoder blocks, allowing it to learn temporal dependencies and correlations between different modalities.
 - The "condition block" extracts features from current observations to inform the decoder.
 - **Output actions:** The policy predicts future sequences of *human arm poses* (not raw robot joint positions), maintaining consistency with the teleoperation input and leveraging the lower-layer retargeting.
2. **Model Training and Deployment:**
 - **Data Collection:** Training data is gathered through online teleoperation, where a human operator performs whole-body contact manipulation tasks using the retargeting system. The dataset consists of synchronized pairs of robot joint positions, tactile measurements, camera images, and the human arm poses as target actions.
 - **Training:** The TACT policy is trained as a standard CVAE, minimizing KL divergence for latent variables and prediction error for actions.
 - **Deployment:** During deployment, the encoder is omitted. The decoder, conditioned on current sensor inputs, generates a sequence of future actions (human arm poses) using a zero latent vector. These predicted poses are then fed into the model-based retargeting controller, which translates them into physically constrained robot movements.
 - **Temporal Ensemble:** TACT employs a temporal ensemble technique (chunk size of 20, or 2 seconds) to average predicted action histories, promoting smooth and consistent control.

**C. Hardware and Software Implementation Details:**
- **Humanoid Robot:** RHP7 Kaleido (Kawasaki Heavy Industry), a life-size humanoid.
- **Tactile Sensors:** e-skin from Intouch Robotics, mounted on chest, upper arms, forearms, and wrists (11 patches, 163 cells, measuring tactile/proximity intensity and acceleration). No calibration is required as the policy learns from raw intensities.
- **Teleoperation:** HTC VIVE trackers and hand controllers for human operator pose tracking and gripper control.
- **Control Frequencies:** Lower-layer control (retargeting, locomotion) runs at 500 Hz; upper-layer (manipulation policy inference) runs at 10 Hz.

This meticulously designed methodology allows the system to combine the robust, physically-aware control of traditional methods with the flexible, data-driven learning capabilities of modern AI, making it suitable for complex real-world humanoid tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings from both real-world and simulation experiments, demonstrating the efficacy of the TACT policy for whole-body contact manipulation.

**A. Real-World Experiments: Holding Fragile Paper Boxes**
The primary real-world task involved the RHP7 Kaleido robot holding up fragile paper boxes of varying sizes (small, medium, large, and an unseen size) using both arms and forearms. This task necessitates delicate force control and broad contact to avoid crushing the box or dropping it.

- **Comparison with Baselines:** The proposed TACT policy was compared against three baselines:
 - **Replay:** Direct replay of human teleoperation data, without adaptation.
 - **TACT w/o vision:** TACT policy trained without visual input.
 - **TACT w/o tactile:** TACT policy trained without tactile input (equivalent to original ACT).
- **Success Rates (Table I, Fig. 5):**
 - **Superior Performance of TACT:** TACT demonstrated significantly higher success rates. For medium and large boxes, it achieved 100% success (3/3 trials each), whereas baselines performed poorly (e.g., TACT w/o tactile had 1/3 for medium, Replay 0/3 for large).
 - **Importance of Modalities:**
 - **TACT w/o vision** frequently crushed the boxes, indicating a lack of visual information for appropriate object sizing and approach.
 - **TACT w/o tactile** often dropped the boxes, suggesting insufficient feedback for maintaining continuous and delicate contact, leading to small gaps and loss of grip.
 - These distinct failure modes clearly highlight the complementary and essential roles of both vision (for gross pose, object recognition, and avoiding collision) and tactile sensing (for fine force control, contact maintenance, and preventing crushing) in complex, delicate manipulation.
 - **Generalization:** TACT also showed better generalization to "unseen" box sizes (4/5 success) compared to baselines, although performance on very small boxes remained a challenge for all policies, attributed to the large size variation in the training data, limiting generalization accuracy.
- **Versatility with Other Objects:** The method was successfully applied to manipulate a large plastic bag (Fig. 6) using different contact patterns (one-hand grasp with arm support, or both arms and chest), demonstrating its adaptability to various objects and contact configurations.
- **Sensor Data Analysis (Fig. 8, 9):** Visualizations of sensor data confirmed that tactile and proximity signals provided crucial information, especially when the object was close to the robot's body, obscuring visual cues. Transformer attention weights indicated that the policy actively utilized tactile input, particularly around contact events, affirming its importance in decision-making.
- **Integrated Loco-Manipulation:** Throughout all experiments, the robot maintained stable bipedal standing and was capable of walking while carrying objects (Fig. 7), validating the effectiveness of integrating the learning-based manipulation policy with the model-based locomotion controller.

**B. Simulation Experiments: Box Reorientation Task (Appendix)**
To conduct more extensive evaluations, a MuJoCo simulation environment was developed, replicating the robot and tactile sensor setup. The task involved tilting a box by 90 degrees on a table, requiring complex contact transitions.

- **Consistent Superiority:** TACT consistently outperformed TACT w/o vision and TACT w/o tactile in simulation, echoing the real-world findings. The robot also demonstrated some error recovery capabilities.
- **GCN Variants:** Exploration of Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) for tactile feature extraction (TACT-GCN1, 2, 3) showed that a simple linear projection for tactile input in the original TACT performed slightly better, or at least comparably, suggesting that integrating spatial structure effectively remains a non-trivial challenge.
- **Data Efficiency:** TACT maintained its performance advantage even with reduced training data, demonstrating graceful degradation.
- **Object Generalization:** TACT showed some generalization to different object sizes, outperforming vision-only policies for smaller objects. Notably, tactile-only policies were unaffected by changes in object texture, while vision-based policies failed, further underscoring the distinct contributions of each modality.
- **Role of Admittance Control:** Model-based admittance control in the lower layer significantly improved performance, especially for policies relying heavily on tactile input, by enhancing compliance during contact. TACT proved more resilient to the absence of admittance control but still benefited from its presence, highlighting its complementary role.
- **Fall Analysis:** Policies utilizing tactile input (TACT, TACT-GCNs, TACT w/o vision) exhibited fewer falls compared to TACT w/o tactile, suggesting that tactile information contributes to overall system stability and fall prevention.

In summary, the results conclusively demonstrate that the TACT policy, leveraging both visual and tactile modalities through deep imitation learning, enables robust and delicate whole-body contact manipulation on a life-size humanoid. The layered control architecture successfully integrates learning-based flexibility with model-based reliability, leading to unprecedented capabilities in humanoid loco-manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "TACT: Humanoid Whole-body Contact Manipulation through Deep Imitation Learning with Tactile Modality" marks a significant advancement in humanoid robotics, particularly in the domain of physical interaction and manipulation. Its primary significance lies in being the **first study to achieve whole-body contact loco-manipulation by a life-size humanoid robot based on imitation learning with multimodal sensor inputs (visual and tactile) using a Transformer-based policy.**

The potential impact of this research is substantial across several fronts:

1. **Enabling Human-like Manipulation:** By allowing humanoid robots to utilize their entire upper body for contact and manipulation, this work pushes robots closer to human-level dexterity and versatility. This capability is crucial for performing complex tasks in unstructured environments, such as assisting in households, warehouses, or disaster relief, where objects may need to be cradled, pushed, or stabilized against the body rather than merely gripped by hands.
2. **Enhanced Stability and Load Distribution:** Whole-body contact naturally distributes loads and enhances stability, which is vital for manipulating heavy, large, or irregularly shaped objects. This research demonstrates a viable path for humanoids to engage with their environment more robustly, reducing the risk of dropping objects or losing balance.
3. **Robustness in Challenging Conditions:** The multimodal approach, particularly the integration of tactile sensing, offers increased robustness. As shown in the experiments, tactile feedback is critical for delicate force control, maintaining contact, and dealing with situations where visual information might be limited (e.g., close-up interactions, occlusions, or variations in object texture). This makes robots more resilient to real-world uncertainties.
4. **Novel Control Architecture:** The proposed two-layer control system, seamlessly combining a learning-based manipulation policy with a model-based retargeting and locomotion controller, provides a powerful paradigm. It leverages the flexibility and adaptability of deep imitation learning for complex skills while maintaining the reliability and physical consistency offered by established model-based control techniques. This hybrid approach offers a blueprint for developing capable and safe humanoid systems.
5. **Advancement in Deep Imitation Learning:** Extending the prominent ACT model with tactile modality, creating TACT, is a notable contribution to the field of deep imitation learning for robotics. It demonstrates how high-dimensional, distributed tactile sensor data can be effectively integrated into Transformer-based architectures, opening avenues for future research in multimodal learning.
6. **Paving the Way for Future Humanoid Applications:** The ability to perform delicate, contact-rich loco-manipulation tasks can unlock new applications for humanoids. This includes tasks requiring gentle handling of fragile items, collaborative tasks involving physical contact with humans, or navigating constrained spaces while interacting with objects. The simulation results on data efficiency and generalization further indicate the practical viability of this approach for real-world deployment.

Looking forward, the authors outline several promising avenues for future work. Replacing the model-based lower-layer controller with a reinforcement learning policy could lead to even more robust and adaptive loco-manipulation. Expanding the method to handle stiff or heavy objects would significantly broaden its applicability, though this might necessitate hardware with higher backdrivability and enhanced locomotion controllers. Further exploration of advanced policy architectures, such as Diffusion Policy or Flow Matching, and more sophisticated graph-based networks for exploiting spatial tactile information, remains an exciting area for continued research.

Overall, this paper presents a foundational step towards enabling humanoids to operate with greater autonomy, dexterity, and human-like physical intelligence in complex, real-world environments.
