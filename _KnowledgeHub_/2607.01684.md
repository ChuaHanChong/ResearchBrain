# Imagining the Sense of Touch: Touch-Informed Manipulation via Imagined Tactile Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework enables robots to perform contact-rich manipulation using imagined tactile representations derived from visual observations, eliminating the need for physical tactile sensors at deployment. This approach improved success rates by 44.4 percentage points in real-world contact-sensitive tasks and achieved 57.5% average success in simulation, closely approaching physical tactile sensor performance.

## Problem

- Purely visual policies often struggle with contact-rich manipulation tasks due to visual ambiguities and the difficulty in inferring precise contact forces.
- Physical tactile sensors, while improving robustness, face significant practical deployment challenges such as fragility, complex calibration, and high maintenance burden.
- Existing cross-modal generation research primarily focuses on generation fidelity or perception accuracy rather than explicitly evaluating the impact on downstream manipulation performance in sensor-less scenarios.

## Method

- The **TacImag** framework employs a two-stage process: first, a ==conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM)== is trained to generate ==imagined tactile observations== (==TacRGB== or ==TacFF==) conditioned on visual and proprioceptive inputs.
- The trained and frozen ==tactile imagination model== then provides these explicit imagined tactile representations to a multimodal ==imitation learning policy== (based on ==Diffusion Policy==), alongside real visual and proprioceptive inputs.
- At deployment, the robot operates without any physical tactile hardware, relying solely on online-generated imagined tactile observations to inform its actions.

## Results

- In simulation, imagined TacFF achieved an average success rate of **57.5%** across six contact-rich tasks, closely matching that of physical TacFF (**58.2%**) and substantially outperforming the vision-only baseline (**53.6%**).
- On real-world contact-sensitive tasks (bulb installation, whiteboard wiping, belt insertion), imagined TacFF improved success rates by an average of **44.4 percentage points** over vision-only policies.
- For the real-world texture-sensitive ball sorting task using wrist-view observations, imagined TacRGB increased success rates from **33.3%** with vision-only to **73.3%**, significantly outperforming imagined TacFF (**46.7%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robots can effectively utilize tactile knowledge for manipulation without requiring physical tactile sensors at deployment, by transforming subtle visual cues into explicit imagined tactile representations.
> - Tactile imagination functions as 'contact-aware supervision,' enabling policies to leverage latent contact-relevant information from vision more effectively, rather than merely hallucinating unseen data.
> - The optimal choice of imagined tactile representation is task-dependent: imagined force fields (TacFF) are more effective for contact-sensitive tasks, while imagined tactile images (TacRGB) are better suited for texture discrimination.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation often encounters challenges in contact-rich tasks such as insertion, assembly, and sorting. While visual perception has driven significant advancements in imitation learning for such tasks, purely visual policies frequently exhibit limitations when precise contact forces and micro-interactions become critical. These limitations stem from visual ambiguities caused by tight tolerances, occlusion, lighting variations, or object appearances that make inferring crucial contact information difficult.

Tactile sensing offers complementary information about physical interactions, having been demonstrated to enhance robustness in contact-rich manipulation. High-resolution tactile sensors, such as GelSight, have become important components in modern robotic systems. However, the practical deployment of these sensors faces challenges including hardware fragility, complex calibration, additional wiring, synchronization requirements, and susceptibility to wear and damage from prolonged contact. This limits their widespread adoption in real-world scenarios.

Existing research in visuotactile manipulation typically relies on the presence of physical tactile sensors during both training and deployment. Concurrently, a distinct body of work explores cross-modal generation, demonstrating the potential to predict tactile observations from visual inputs, and vice-versa, often leveraging diffusion-based models for realistic synthesis. While these works show the feasibility of inferring tactile information, they primarily focus on generation fidelity or perception accuracy rather than evaluating the impact on downstream manipulation performance in a sensor-less deployment context.

Another related area is missing-modality learning, where privileged modalities are used during training, but policies operate with reduced sensing at deployment. This includes policy distillation and modality hallucination networks that infer missing information as auxiliary supervision. Approaches like privileged tactile latent distillation implicitly encode tactile information into deployable policies.

This work, TacImag, integrates these concepts by proposing a framework that leverages cross-modal generation to explicitly predict tactile observations, specifically addressing the scenario where physical tactile sensors are absent during robot deployment. Unlike prior efforts that primarily focus on generation quality or implicitly encode privileged information, TacImag directly investigates how *imagined* tactile observations, explicitly generated online, influence and improve downstream manipulation performance. It aims to bridge the gap between the proven benefits of tactile sensing and the practical challenges of its deployment by eliminating the need for tactile hardware at execution time while retaining tactile-informed control capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether robots can effectively utilize tactile knowledge to improve manipulation performance without requiring physical tactile sensors at the point of deployment. This addresses a fundamental practical question: how to leverage rich visuotactile training data while maintaining the simplicity and reliability of vision-only hardware during execution.

The motivation for this objective stems from two primary observations:
1. **Limitations of Purely Visual Policies:** In contact-rich manipulation, critical information about forces and micro-contacts is often difficult or impossible to infer from visual observations alone, leading to failures in tasks requiring tight tolerances, handling occlusions, or operating under ambiguous visual conditions.
2. **Challenges of Tactile Sensor Deployment:** While tactile sensors offer valuable complementary information, their practical use is hampered by hardware fragility, complex calibration, additional wiring, synchronization needs, and susceptibility to damage. These factors contribute to a significant maintenance burden.

Based on these points, the authors hypothesize that tactile imagination can serve as a form of "contact-aware supervision." This hypothesis suggests that contact-relevant information is often *weakly observable* through subtle visual cues (e.g., appearance changes, object deformation, relative motion) that are difficult for direct end-to-end policy learning to exploit. By transforming these subtle visual cues into explicit tactile representations, tactile imagination could make this latent contact information more accessible and usable for manipulation policies, rather than simply "recovering unseen information" or hallucinating data entirely absent from vision. This perspective guides the framework's design and the subsequent experimental analysis regarding the effectiveness of different tactile representations across various tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TacImag framework is designed as a two-stage process to enable touch-informed manipulation without requiring physical tactile sensors at deployment.

**A. Problem Setup:**
The work frames contact-rich manipulation as a supervised imitation learning problem using a dataset of paired multimodal observations ($o_t = \{o^{vis}_t, o^{tac}_t, o^{prop}_t\}$) and expert actions ($a_t$). The goal is to move from conventional policies requiring tactile observations at deployment to policies that utilize *imagined* tactile observations ($\hat{o}^{tac}_t$) generated from vision ($o^{vis}_t$) and proprioception ($o^{prop}_t$) inputs. Two tactile representations are considered: high-dimensional tactile RGB images (TacRGB) and compact tactile force fields (TacFF).

**B. Tactile Imagination (Stage 1):**
1. **Objective:** To generate plausible tactile observations conditioned on visual observations and proprioceptive robot states. Recognizing the inherent ambiguity in predicting tactile observations from vision alone, TacImag models a conditional distribution $p(o^{tac}_t | o^{vis}_t, o^{prop}_t)$.
2. **Model Architecture:** A conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) is employed. During training, Gaussian noise is progressively added to ground-truth tactile samples ($x^{tac}_0$) to create noisy samples ($x^{tac}_k$). The diffusion model then learns to predict the injected noise, conditioned on visual observations and robot states, using a standard DDPM objective (Eq. 5).
3. **Inference:** During deployment, tactile observations are generated by iteratively denoising from Gaussian noise, conditioned on the current visual and proprioceptive inputs. This yields an imagined tactile observation $\hat{o}^{tac}_t = G_\phi(o^{vis}_t, o^{prop}_t)$.
4. **Representation-Specific Models:** Separate imagination models are trained for TacRGB and TacFF representations to enable a controlled study of how different types of imagined tactile information influence manipulation performance. The paper emphasizes that the objective is not perfect reconstruction, but generating representations that preserve manipulation-relevant contact information, evaluated primarily by downstream manipulation performance.

**C. Policy Learning with Imagined Tactile (Stage 2):**
1. **Imagination Model Freezing:** After training, the tactile imagination model ($G_\phi$) is frozen. This is a crucial design choice that prevents gradients from propagating back into the generator during policy training, thus isolating the effect of tactile imagination and allowing for explicit analysis of its contribution.
2. **Policy Architecture:** A tactile-conditioned imitation learning policy based on Diffusion Policy is used. This policy consumes the imagined tactile observations alongside real visual and proprioceptive inputs.
3. **Observation Fusion:** Each modality (visual, imagined tactile, proprioceptive) is encoded independently, and their features are concatenated to form a shared observation representation ($z_t = \{z^{vis}_t \oplus z^{tac}_t \oplus z^{prop}_t\}$). The imagined tactile observations are processed using the same tactile encoder architecture as policies trained with real tactile sensing, facilitating direct comparisons.
4. **Deployment:** At deployment, the robot operates without any physical tactile hardware. Visual and proprioceptive states are fed into the *frozen* imagination model to generate tactile representations online, which are then used by the trained policy to inform actions.

**D. Experimental Setup (Simulation and Real-World):**
- **Simulation:** Six contact-rich manipulation tasks from the ManiFeel benchmark (USB insertion, power-plug insertion, peg-in-hole, gear assembly, bulb installation, and ball sorting) are used. These are categorized into contact-sensitive and texture-sensitive tasks. Wrist-view RGB observations are used for contact-sensitive tasks, while front-view is used for texture-sensitive sorting under dim lighting.
- **Real-World:** Four tasks are evaluated on a Franka Emika Panda robot with a parallel-jaw gripper: bulb installation, whiteboard wiping, belt insertion (contact-sensitive), and ball sorting (texture-sensitive). A GelSight R1.5 sensor is used for data collection during training, but removed and replaced with a dummy finger for deployment.
- **Policy Variants:** Five variants are compared: Vision-only, Vision+TacRGB (real), Vision+TacFF (real), Vision+TacRGB (imagined), and Vision+TacFF (imagined).
- **Evaluation:** Success rates are averaged over multiple trials and random seeds for both simulation and real-world experiments. Tactile imagination quality is also quantitatively assessed using metrics like cosine similarity for TacFF and SSIM/LPIPS for TacRGB on held-out validation data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded consistent patterns across both simulation and real-world experiments, supporting the utility of tactile imagination.

**A. Simulation Results:**
- **Overall Performance:** Imagined TacFF achieved an average success rate of 57.5% across six tasks, closely approaching that of physical TacFF (58.2%) and substantially outperforming the vision-only baseline (53.6%). Imagined TacFF surpassed the vision-only baseline in five out of six tasks.
- **Task-Dependent Effectiveness (Fig. 4):**
 - For **contact-sensitive tasks** (e.g., insertion, assembly), imagined TacFF provided the largest average improvement over the vision baseline (+4.0%), closely matching the gains from physical TacFF (+4.9%). Imagined TacRGB showed negligible or slightly negative impact on these tasks.
 - For **texture-sensitive tasks** (ball sorting), imagined TacRGB yielded a +3.6% improvement, recovering a significant portion of the +7.4% gain from physical TacRGB. Imagined TacFF offered a smaller improvement in this category.
- **Imagination Quality (Table II):** TacImag demonstrated high fidelity in generating tactile observations on held-out validation episodes. For TacFF, average force-direction cosine similarity was 0.890. For TacRGB, average SSIM was 0.953, and LPIPS was 0.157, indicating high structural and perceptual similarity to ground-truth tactile images.
- **Qualitative Examples (Fig. 5):** In peg-in-hole, imagined TacFF developed coherent force patterns reflecting contact geometry. In ball sorting, imagined TacRGB captured distinct texture patterns (smooth for ping-pong, dimpled for golf ball), aiding discrimination.

**B. Real-World Results:**
- **Imagined TacFF for Contact-Sensitive Tasks (Fig. 6b, Table III):** Imagined TacFF consistently provided substantial performance gains on contact-sensitive tasks (bulb installation, whiteboard wiping, belt insertion). For example, success rates improved from 26.7% to 86.7% on bulb installation and from 6.7% to 40.0% on belt insertion. Averaged across these three tasks, imagined TacFF improved success rates by 44.4 percentage points over vision-only policies.
- **Imagined TacRGB for Texture-Sensitive Discrimination (Fig. 6b, Table III):** On the ball sorting task, imagined TacRGB achieved the highest performance when using wrist-view observations, increasing success rates from 33.3% to 73.3%, significantly outperforming imagined TacFF (46.7%).
- **Transferability of Task-Dependent Trends:** The real-world experiments replicated the simulation findings: imagined TacFF was most effective for force-reasoning tasks, while imagined TacRGB excelled in texture discrimination tasks.
- **Viewpoint-Dependent Performance (Fig. 7):** The ball sorting task highlighted that imagined tactile quality is constrained by the information available in the visual input. With limited visual cues from a front-view camera, imagined TacRGB provided only marginal improvements (26.7% to 33.3%). However, with subtle surface details visible from a wrist-view camera during grasping, imagined TacRGB transformed these cues into explicit texture patterns, leading to substantial performance gains (33.3% to 73.3%).

**C. Discussion on Contact-Aware Supervision:**
The results consistently indicate that TacImag does not simply hallucinate information entirely absent from visual observations. Instead, it effectively transforms visually observable but often subtle and difficult-to-exploit interaction cues into explicit tactile representations that are more readily usable by manipulation policies. This supports the hypothesis that tactile imagination acts as a form of contact-aware supervision, enabling policies to identify and leverage visual features associated with physical interaction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TacImag framework represents a significant advancement in robotic manipulation by addressing the practical challenge of deploying tactile sensors while retaining the benefits of tactile feedback.

**Key areas of significance and potential impact include:**

1. **Bridging the Sensing Gap:** TacImag demonstrates that robots can achieve touch-informed manipulation without requiring physical tactile hardware at deployment. This eliminates the associated costs, fragility, calibration complexities, and maintenance burdens of tactile sensors, making robust contact-rich manipulation more accessible and feasible for real-world applications.
2. **Enhanced Robustness and Performance:** The consistent performance improvements observed across both simulated and real-world contact-rich tasks (e.g., insertion, assembly, wiping) highlight the practical utility of imagined tactile observations. This enables robots to perform tasks requiring precise contact and force reasoning with greater reliability.
3. **New Insights into Multimodal Learning:** The finding that tactile imagination acts as "contact-aware supervision" rather than information hallucination provides a novel understanding of how tactile knowledge can be recovered and exploited from visual observations. This suggests that the role of imagination models can be to transform latent information into a more policy-exploitable format, rather than generating entirely new data.
4. **Guidance for Representation Choice:** The systematic investigation of two distinct tactile representations (TacRGB and TacFF) and their task-dependent effectiveness offers valuable guidance for future research and development. It underscores that the choice of tactile representation, whether real or imagined, should align with the specific requirements of the manipulation task (e.g., force fields for contact tasks, tactile images for texture discrimination).
5. **Future Directions for Robotic Autonomy:** By decoupling tactile *sensing* from tactile *information utilization*, TacImag opens avenues for developing more generalizable and robust manipulation systems. It provides a foundation for future work investigating longer-horizon tasks, richer tactile representations, and the integration of tactile imagination with broader cognitive architectures like world models and active perception, where robots could actively seek visual cues to inform their imagined tactile state.

Overall, TacImag offers a practical and insightful approach to leveraging tactile knowledge, potentially broadening the applicability of robots in complex, contact-rich environments by making advanced manipulation capabilities less reliant on specialized and delicate hardware.
