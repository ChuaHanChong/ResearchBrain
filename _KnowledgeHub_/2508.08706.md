# OmniVTLA: Vision-Tactile-Language-Action Models with Semantic-Aligned Tactile Sensing

> [!summary] Summary
> OmniVTLA adds tactile sensing to a vision-language-action policy built on a flow-matching backbone. It uses a dual-encoder tactile path: a pretrained SigLIP vision encoder plus SA-ViT, a tactile transformer contrastively aligned to vision and language using ObjTac, a new 135K-sample tri-modal dataset of 56 objects. Real-robot results reach 96.9% pick-and-place success with a gripper and 83.3% on peg insertion.

## Problem

- Vision-language-action policies rely on vision and language alone, ignoring tactile feedback, so they fail on contact-rich tasks needing precise force-guided interaction.
- Existing tactile-augmented policies treat touch as low-level raw signal and never align it semantically with visual and linguistic context, limiting reasoning and generalization.
- Tactile pretraining work optimizes perception benchmarks like material or hardness classification but rarely studies encoder design or evaluates downstream robotic manipulation policies.

## Method

- **ObjTac** dataset: 135K tri-modal tactile-vision-text samples over 56 objects in 10 material categories, collected with a ==Hall-effect force-field sensor== at 60 Hz plus synchronized egocentric video.
- **SA-ViT** tactile encoder trained with a ==cross-modal contrastive alignment loss== combining vision-language, vision-tactile, and tactile-language terms plus a ==cross-sensor matching== ==binary cross-entropy== term.
- ==Dual-encoder== tactile path in a ==flow-matching== **OmniVTLA** policy: one pretrained ==SigLIP== branch plus **SA-ViT**, tactile fields normalized and stitched into 224x224 three-channel images yielding 256 ==tokens==.

## Results

- Gripper pick-and-place average success rose from **75.0%** baseline to **96.9%**, with completion time cut **24.2%** (**657** to **498** steps); dexterous hand went **93.8%** to **100%**.
- Peg insertion averaged **83.3%** versus **50.0%** baseline, including **90%** on the unseen round peg; parameter-matched dual-encoder ablations reached only **53.3%** and **43.3%**.
- SA-ViT scored **70.44%** material and **82.21%** roughness accuracy on the collected tactile set versus **40.21%** and **68.01%** for the strongest prior tactile encoder.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Gains came from tactile encoder architecture, not parameter count: parameter-matched dual-encoder controls without semantic alignment underperformed, isolating contrastive grounding as the causal ingredient.
> - Semantic alignment helps most where vision is occluded or contact is ambiguous; it gave no benefit in cluttered scenes dominated by visual navigation.
> - Highest success rate can mask bad behavior: unaligned variants succeeded on soft objects by crushing them, while aligned encoders grasped with far smaller gripper width.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements through Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which leverage large-scale pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) to interpret natural language instructions and visual observations. These VLA models have demonstrated potential for task generalization and intelligent behavior in various robotic tasks. However, a notable limitation of current VLA models is their predominant reliance on visual and linguistic data, often overlooking the critical role of tactile perception. This oversight significantly restricts their performance in contact-rich manipulation tasks that necessitate precise physical interaction and feedback, such as grasping fragile objects, fine assembly, or operating in occluded environments.

Concurrent research has explored tactile sensing in robotics, ranging from processing low-level physical signals for grasp stability or slip detection to learning general tactile representations for transferability across sensors and tasks. Some works focus on tactile-grounded understanding of object properties (e.g., hardness, roughness) or multi-modal alignment of tactile data. Similarly, vision-tactile fusion has been investigated for policy learning in contact-rich manipulation using reinforcement learning and imitation learning. While these approaches have achieved task-specific performance, they often lack the semantic reasoning and generalization capabilities inherent in VLA models.

Attempts to integrate tactile data into VLA frameworks exist, but these often treat tactile signals as low-level inputs, failing to establish semantic alignment with visual and linguistic contexts. This leads to a gap where advanced semantic understanding from VLA models does not fully translate into robust physical interaction capabilities. The presented work, OmniVTLA, aims to bridge this gap by explicitly addressing the challenge of semantic alignment between vision, tactile, and language modalities, thereby enhancing VLA models for contact-rich tasks through a well-designed tactile encoder and comprehensive tri-modal data. Table 1 in the paper categorizes existing models, indicating that OmniVTLA distinguishes itself by achieving semantic alignment across vision, tactile, and language modalities within an action model.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop OmniVTLA, a novel framework that integrates vision, tactile, and language modalities for end-to-end robotic control in contact-rich manipulation tasks. This framework seeks to overcome the limitations of existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which often neglect tactile perception, resulting in reduced performance during physical interactions.

The motivation for OmniVTLA stems from several observations:
- **Human Dexterity and Multimodal Integration**: Human manipulation relies fundamentally on the integration of visual and tactile senses for tasks requiring precision and adaptability, such as handling delicate items or threading a needle. Vision provides global spatial context, while tactile sensing offers complementary information about object texture, hardness, and contact forces. Current robotic systems, particularly VLA models, often lack this crucial vision-tactile integration, limiting their capabilities.
- **Limitations of Current VLA Models**: While VLA models have shown promise in interpreting natural language instructions and visual observations for task generalization, their sole reliance on vision and language renders them ineffective in scenarios demanding direct physical contact. Without tactile feedback, robots struggle with tasks such as stable grasping, precise insertion, or interacting with objects whose properties (e.g., softness, slipperiness) are not fully discernible visually.
- **Inadequate Tactile Integration Approaches**: Existing efforts to incorporate tactile sensing into VLA frameworks frequently treat tactile data as low-level signals without semantically aligning them with visual and linguistic contexts. This superficial integration prevents robots from "understanding" tactile information in a meaningful, task-relevant way, hindering effective decision-making during manipulation. Prior tactile pre-training efforts focused on tactile understanding but did not consistently investigate the role of dynamic tactile data during interactions or evaluate performance in robotic manipulation tasks.
- **Need for Semantic Alignment and Robust Tactile Encoding**: The authors hypothesize that achieving vision-language-tactile semantic alignment is critical for improving the performance of Vision-Tactile-Language-Action (VTLA) models. This necessitates the exploration of effective tactile encoder architectures that can ground tactile signals (e.g., material, roughness, hardness) within broader visual and linguistic contexts.

To address these motivations, OmniVTLA sets forth several specific objectives:
- **Develop a Novel Architecture**: Propose OmniVTLA, a unified architecture that leverages contrastive learning to align high-resolution tactile signals with visual and language concepts in a shared semantic space. This includes designing a dual-encoder path for tactile data to handle the heterogeneity of sensory inputs.
- **Create a Comprehensive Tactile Dataset**: Construct ObjTac, a novel and extensive dataset comprising textual, visual, and tactile information for a diverse set of objects. This dataset is intended to facilitate the training of semantically-aligned tactile encoders.
- **Train a Semantically-Aligned Tactile Encoder**: Utilize the ObjTac dataset to train an SA-ViT (semantically-aligned tactile Vision Transformer) that can effectively ground tactile signals within visual and linguistic contexts, providing a robust initialization for OmniVTLA.
- **Empirical Validation**: Conduct real-world experiments on contact-rich manipulation tasks (pick-and-place, peg-insertion) to demonstrate OmniVTLA’s superior performance compared to established VLA baselines in terms of success rates, task completion time, and trajectory smoothness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OmniVTLA extends a $\pi_0$ backbone with a second, tactile-specific encoder path; the sub-sections below cover the problem formulation, the dual-encoder architecture, the semantically-aligned tactile encoder, and the experimental setup.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The action model aims to predict a sequence of actions $A_t = \{a_t, a_{t+1}, \ldots, a_{t+H-1}\}$ given observations $o_t$. For standard VLA models, observations typically include RGB images ($I_i^t$), a language prompt ($l_t$), and robot proprioceptive state. The model is formulated as $A_t \sim M_{VLA}(f_\phi(I_i^t), l_t)$, where $f_\phi$ is a contrastive image encoder (e.g., CLIP, SigLIP) based on Vision Transformers (ViT).
OmniVTLA extends this by incorporating tactile data ($T_j^t$), formulating the VTLA model as $A_t \sim M_{VTLA}(f_I^\phi(I_i^t), f_T^\theta(T_j^t), l_t)$, where $f_I^\phi$ and $f_T^\theta$ are the image and tactile encoders, respectively. The core objective is to explore effective tactile encoder designs and training strategies to integrate tactile data, which differ significantly from visual data characteristics.

#### 3.2. Overall Architecture with Dual-Encoder Path

OmniVTLA is built upon the $\pi_0$ model (Black et al., 2024) and comprises three main components: tokenizers, a backbone, and an action head.
- **Tokenizers**:
 - **Language**: PaliGemma tokenizer processes language instructions ($l_t$).
 - **Vision**: A SigLiP model (Zhai et al., 2023) serves as the image encoder, projecting images ($I_i^t$) into a latent embedding space. Raw captures (third-view and wrist images) are resized to 224x224 pixels, yielding 256 tokens per image.
 - **Tactile**: A dual-encoder path is introduced for tactile data ($T_j^t$).
 - **Path 1**: A pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT) acts as one encoder, leveraging semantic representations learned from large-scale image data.
 - **Path 2**: A Semantically-Aligned Tactile ViT (SA-ViT) is specifically trained for cross-modal contrastive learning to achieve semantic alignment among tactile, visual, and textual modalities.
 - Tactile data is normalized to int8, stitched from multi-sensor inputs into single images, resized to 224x224, and processed through the ViT-like encoders to generate 256 tokens.
- **Backbone**: The Gemma-2.6B model processes the concatenated tokens from all modalities.
- **Action Head**: An action head decodes the action tokens produced by the backbone, trained with a flow matching loss, following $\pi_0$. Action representations vary by end-effector: 10 tokens (3 relative positions, 6 relative angles, 1 gripper state) for two-finger grippers, and 25 tokens (3 relative positions, 6 relative angles, 16 absolute joint positions) for four-finger hands.

#### 3.3. Semantic-Aligned Tactile Encoder

To train the semantically-aligned tactile encoder, the authors developed the **ObjTac dataset**.
- **Dataset Collection**: ObjTac contains aligned textual, visual, and tactile data for 56 distinct objects across 10 categories (plastic, glass, wood, brick, metal, fabric, leather, ceramic, paper, and others). Objects are further categorized by surface roughness (rough vs. smooth) and material hardness (rigid vs. soft).
- **Tactile Sensor**: The Paxini Gen2 sensor is utilized, which quantifies contact force fields. It uses an array of Hall effect sensors to detect magnetic field variations from displaced magnetic sources under contact forces. An embedded inverse problem solver converts magnetic field changes into 6D pose variations, which are then transformed into multi-dimensional force distributions via a continuum mechanics inversion algorithm. Force data is directly obtained from the sensor's microcontroller unit (MCU).
- **Data Collection Pipeline**:
 1. For each object, 2–5 interaction trials were conducted, each lasting 10–60 seconds, sampled at 60 Hz, yielding 270,000 force data recordings.
 2. Synchronized first-person-view visual recordings (720P, 30 FPS) were captured, resulting in 252 video sequences (average 18 seconds each).
 3. In total, 135K tri-modal samples with paired tactile and vision data were collected.
 4. Object-level annotations were added for the language modality, including object name, material type, roughness, hardness, video-level metadata, and textual descriptions.
 5. Temporal synchronization was performed using timestamps.
- **Training Strategy**: The semantic-aligned tactile encoder is trained using a second-stage training pipeline adapted from AnyTouch (Feng et al., 2025). This involves using a total alignment loss ($L_{align}$) composed of:
 - Vision-Language loss ($L_{V \leftrightarrow L}$)
 - Vision-Tactile loss ($L_{V \leftrightarrow T}$)
 - Tactile-Language loss ($L_{T \leftrightarrow L}$)
 - Additionally, a cross-sensor matching loss with binary cross-entropy is incorporated. This setup ensures that the tactile encoder learns representations that are semantically consistent across all three modalities and adaptable to the specific tactile sensors used.
- **Encoder Comparison**: The SA-ViT encoder's performance was compared against five other encoders: CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023), Tac-ViT (without alignment objective), and AnyTouch (Feng et al., 2025). CLIP and SigLIP are vision-only pre-trained encoders used for baseline comparison with similar parameter counts. Tac-ViT (w/o Align) is trained with a masked autoencoder objective but without cross-modality or cross-sensor semantic alignment.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Hardware**: A UR5 robotic arm equipped with a two-finger gripper (two tactile sensors and a wrist camera) and an 11-tactile-sensor dexterous hand (with a wrist camera). A base camera provided a third-person view.
- **Tactile Data Processing**: Tactile observations were converted into a 3-channel image representation using max-min force normalization and tensor reshaping.
- **Baselines**: Diffusion Policy (DP) (Chi et al., 2023) and $\pi_0$ (Black et al., 2024).
- **Tasks**:
 - **Pick-and-place**: Evaluated on four objects (Short Can, Square Coffee Bottle, Gum Tin, Milk Carton) using the gripper, and two objects (Coffee Bottle, Milk Carton) using the dexterous hand. Unseen objects (plastic bottle, square bottle, sponge, towel) were used for generalization testing. 40 teleoperated demonstrations per object.
 - **Peg-insertion**: Involved inserting square, round, and triangular blocks into corresponding holes with clearances of 0.9 mm, 1.2 mm, and 1.8 mm, respectively, using the gripper. The round block was reserved as an unseen object. 54 teleoperated demonstrations.
- **Evaluation Variants**: A systematic comparison of six variants was conducted:
 - VTLA-FS: Tactile encoder trained from scratch on limited teleoperated data.
 - VTLA-Pre: Tactile encoder initialized from a pre-trained vision encoder and fine-tuned on teleoperated data.
 - VTLA-SA: Tactile encoder first trained with cross-modality contrastive learning for semantic alignment (Sec. 3.3) and then fine-tuned.
 - VTLA-Pre-Pre: Dual-encoder path with two pre-trained vision encoders (SigLiP and CLIP) without tactile pre-training, matching parameter count.
 - VTLA-Pre-Tac: Dual-encoder path with SigLiP and Tac-ViT (w/o Align).
 - OmniVTLA: Dual-encoder path with SigLiP and SA-ViT.
- **Metrics**: Offline validation used Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and ground-truth trajectories. Real-world evaluation used Success Rate (SR), Completion Time (CT), spatial motion smoothness (variability of end-effector acceleration), and gripper closure smoothness (mean absolute changes of gripper actions).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Results are reported across four axes: tactile perception accuracy, offline trajectory validation, and two real-world contact-rich tasks.

#### 4.1. Tactile Perception Capability

Evaluation of tactile perception capabilities (Table 2) showed that CLIP and SigLIP, while effective for vision, exhibited lower accuracy in classifying tactile material and hardness, indicating intrinsic differences between visual and tactile data. Integrating semantic alignment (AnyTouch vs. Tac-ViT without alignment) improved material and hardness classification. The proposed SA-ViT, trained with the ObjTac dataset, achieved notably higher classification accuracy on its own collected dataset while maintaining performance on external datasets like Touch and Go, confirming its ability to align tactile signals with visual and linguistic contexts.

#### 4.2. Offline Validation

Offline validation (Fig. 5) on teleoperation-driven data indicated that VTLA-Pre-Tac achieved the lowest Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and ground-truth trajectories, with OmniVTLA achieving the second lowest. This suggests strong predictive performance from these models.

#### 4.3. Real-World Pick-and-Place Results

- **Using Two-Finger Gripper ($\pi_0$ baseline - Table 3)**: OmniVTLA demonstrated superior performance, achieving a 96.9% average success rate (SR), which is 21.9% higher than the baseline VLA model. It also achieved competitive task completion times (CT), reducing the average steps by 24.2%. VTLA-SA, a single-path variant with semantic alignment, also showed strong performance with an 87.5% average SR and the fastest CT reduction (26.3%). The variants VTLA-Pre-Pre and VTLA-Pre-Tac, despite having similar parameter counts, underperformed OmniVTLA, suggesting that the architectural choice and semantic alignment of the tactile encoder, rather than just the number of parameters, contributed to the performance gains.
- **Using Four-Finger Dexterous Hand ($\pi_0$ baseline - Table 4)**: OmniVTLA achieved a 100% SR, representing a 6.2% improvement over the VLA baseline, and reduced the average CT by 6%. Notably, OmniVTLA achieved 100% SR for unseen objects (Plastic and Square bottles), whereas the VLA baseline only reached 87.5%.
- **Using Diffusion Policy (DP) baseline (Table 5)**: Integrating tactile sensing (VTA) into the DP model resulted in an 18.7% increase in average SR (from 59.4% to 78.1%) and a 19.9% reduction in average CT (from 851 to 682 steps), affirming the universal benefit of tactile signals across different baseline architectures.

#### 4.4. Real-World Peg-Insertion Results

In peg-insertion tasks (Table 6), OmniVTLA achieved an average SR of 83.3% across in-distribution and out-of-distribution (OOD) shapes, a 33.3% improvement over the VLA baseline. VTLA-SA achieved the fastest CT. The comparative underperformance of VTLA-Pre-Pre and VTLA-Pre-Tac reaffirmed that semantic alignment in the tactile encoder contributes to better generalization and performance in contact-rich tasks.

### 5. Analysis and Discussion

- **Smoothness of Trajectories (Table 7)**: VTLA-SA exhibited the best smoothness for both end-effector motion and gripper actuation. OmniVTLA achieved the second-best performance in gripper smoothness, indicating that semantic-aligned tactile sensing contributes to more stable and controlled robot movements during interaction.
- **Qualitative Results (Figs. 6, 7)**: Visualizations illustrated that VLA models often enter trial-and-error loops or fail due to insufficient contact awareness or visual occlusion. In contrast, OmniVTLA, leveraging tactile feedback, consistently achieved stable grasps and successful insertions, demonstrating its ability to guide fine adjustments, especially when visual information is limited.
- **Generalization to Soft Objects (Table 8, Fig. 8)**: When tested on unseen soft objects (Sponge and Towel), OmniVTLA and VTLA-SA demonstrated improved "soft grasping" capabilities, indicated by a more controlled maximum gripper width compared to VLA. This suggests that the semantic-aligned tactile encoder enabled the models to recognize and adapt to the material properties of soft objects, preventing excessive deformation.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents OmniVTLA, a Vision-Tactile-Language-Action model that integrates multimodal perception to enhance robotic manipulation capabilities, particularly in contact-rich tasks. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

**Addressing a Critical Gap:** OmniVTLA effectively addresses a recognized limitation in existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which often lack robust tactile perception. By explicitly incorporating and semantically aligning tactile data with visual and linguistic contexts, the framework enables robots to perform tasks that demand intricate physical interaction, such as precise grasping, delicate handling, and accurate insertion, which were previously challenging for vision- and language-only systems.

**Demonstrated Performance Improvements:** The experimental results show substantial and quantifiable improvements over state-of-the-art VLA baselines. OmniVTLA achieves significantly higher success rates (e.g., 21.9% higher in pick-and-place with a gripper, 33.3% higher in peg-insertion), reduces task completion times, and generates smoother, more stable robot trajectories. These performance gains are demonstrated across various objects and tasks, including generalization to unseen objects and soft materials, highlighting the framework's robustness and adaptability.

**Novel Methodological Contributions:** The introduction of the ObjTac dataset, a comprehensive tri-modal dataset of textual, visual, and tactile information, provides a valuable resource for future research in multimodal perception. The dual-encoder architecture for tactile perception, particularly the semantically-aligned tactile Vision Transformer (SA-ViT), represents a notable architectural contribution. The systematic comparison of different tactile encoder designs validates the efficacy of semantic alignment in achieving superior manipulation performance.

**Enhanced Robot Dexterity and Generalization:** By enabling robots to "understand" what they feel in the context of what they see and are instructed to do, OmniVTLA moves towards more dexterous and intelligent robotic systems. This enhanced capability has implications for various applications, including:
- **Manufacturing and Assembly**: More precise handling of components, particularly in delicate or tight assembly tasks.
- **Healthcare**: Assisting in tasks requiring fine motor skills, such as surgery or patient care.
- **Logistics and Warehousing**: Improved handling of diverse items, including fragile or irregularly shaped objects.
- **Domestic Robotics**: More capable and adaptable household robots that can interact safely and effectively with varied objects in unstructured environments.

**Foundational for Future Research:** The work highlights the critical role of tactile sensing for generalization in robotics and opens avenues for further exploration. Future research can build upon OmniVTLA to investigate more efficient tactile representations, dynamic fusion architectures that capture temporal changes during interaction, and strategies for leveraging tactile signals in complex, cluttered environments where visual ambiguity is prevalent. The identified limitations, such as performance in messy clutter, offer clear directions for continued development towards more generalizable multimodal policies.
