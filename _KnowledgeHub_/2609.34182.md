# Unified Visual-Tactile-Action Modeling from Human Demonstrations for Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> UVTA uses human tactile demonstrations to train dexterous robot manipulation. A wearable tactile motion-capture glove records wrist images, fingertip pressure and 22-DoF hand motion. Human and robot data are mapped into a shared 20-D tactile space and a shared 31-D action space, and one co-trained policy predicts future actions and future touch together. It reaches 70% real-robot success, against 29% for the strongest visual-tactile baseline.

## Problem

- Tactile robot demonstrations are hard to scale because dexterous-hand teleoperation is demanding and gives operators little tactile feedback, which limits object, scene and contact diversity.
- Prior transfer from human video or retargeting depends on embodiment-specific motion correspondence, which breaks across hand morphologies, and video alone misses local contact dynamics like slip and pressure.
- World action models mostly predict visual futures, where contact onset and force are barely visible, and tactile systems learn from robot touch or need staged, aligned training.

## Method

- ==Wearable tactile motion-capture system==: a passive 22-DoF linkage exoskeleton, four-region fingertip pressure sensors, a VIVE wrist tracker and a calibrated wrist fisheye camera that matches the robot's camera geometry.
- ==Visual-Tactile-to-Piezoresistive Taxel Mapping== (**VTPM**) turns robot visual-tactile deformation maps into a 20-D pressure-like token matching the glove layout, with a shared 31-D relative-wrist, absolute-finger action space.
- Unified model: a ==ViT-S/8== visual token is joined with the tactile token, feeding a ==diffusion action head== and an MLP ==future-tactile regression head==, trained in one balanced human-robot ==co-training== run.

## Results

- Averages **70%** stage-wise success across five real-robot tasks, against T-Rex **29%**, RDP **14%** and ViTacFormer **1%**, with the biggest gains on Toggle Switch (**88% vs 24%**) and Light Bulb (**80% vs 26%**).
- With 150 robot demos held fixed, success rises steadily from **0% to 84%** as human demos grow to 1,000. The largest jump, **33 points**, comes between 750 and 1,000, so gains have not saturated.
- Ablations: removing future-tactile prediction drops success to **42%**, vision-only gives 26%, adding proprioception collapses it to **18%**, and human data alone gives **0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting future touch, not only future pixels, gives a training signal shared across embodiments, because contact physics stays the same even when hand shape changes.
> - Human actions can supervise representation learning without ever being executed. A small robot-action set grounds execution, while large human data shapes contact-aware features.
> - Conditioning on proprioception can make policies replay memorized trajectories instead of reacting to what they see and feel, so dropping state inputs can improve closed-loop dexterity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot approaches a book, slows near a page, rubs and separates one sheet, then turns it to the left. Fine contact like this depends on touch. However, collecting tactile demonstrations with a robot hand is difficult to scale. UVTA records people's hand motion, wrist-view images, and fingertip touch. It then trains on those demonstrations alongside a smaller set of robot demonstrations. On the authors' five-task real-robot evaluation, UVTA's average stage-wise score is 70%, versus 29% for T-Rex, the strongest visuo-tactile baseline in their comparison.

Page separation and turning: the robot approaches a book, slows near a page, rubs and separates a single page, then turns it to the left.

### 2. A shared lesson from different hands

Contact onset, pressure, deformation, and slip can be ambiguous or hidden in images. Robot-hand teleoperation is demanding and gives operators limited tactile feedback. A wearable system instead records synchronized wrist-centric RGB images, fingertip pressure, and hand motion without robot teleoperation.

Each of five tasks has 1,000 human and 150 robot demonstrations. The human scenes and contact patterns are more varied. The robot demonstrations provide actions executable on the target platform.

The authors' premise is that hands differ, but interaction physics such as contact, deformation, and slip has shared structure. The method uses that structure as training supervision. It does not claim that human movements can simply be copied onto a robot.

To align the two sources, robot tactile deformation maps are converted to a common 20-value fingertip representation, with four regions for each of five fingers. Both hands' actions use a common 31-value layout: relative wrist position and rotation plus 22 finger-joint targets. This shared layout is a training representation. At deployment, only robot action predictions are executed.

Tactile input is mapped by VTPM to a 20-D taxel token. Wrist vision passes through a Transformer to a 384-D visual token. Concatenation forms a 404-D interaction representation that feeds a diffusion action head and a tactile regression head.

### 3. What the robot results establish

Each of 10 real-robot rollouts per task receives the highest milestone score it reaches, with 1.0 reserved for full task completion. The five-task headline is an unweighted mean of these stage-wise scores. It is therefore not a rate of fully completed trials.

The closest prior method, T-Rex, already combines human egocentric-video pretraining with robot tactile mid-training and tactile-reactive action refinement. All methods used the same task-specific human/robot demonstration splits and evaluation protocol. Under this setup, UVTA averages 70% against T-Rex's 29% and performs best on all five tasks. These figures should not be compared with T-Rex's results on its separate benchmark.

The sharper mechanism test removes the future-tactile prediction head but keeps tactile input. Under this ablation, the average falls from 70% to 42%. This favors the claim that future-touch supervision contributes beyond simply providing current touch. It does not prove that tactile prediction alone explains the full gap over T-Rex, because the models differ in architecture and training.

A scaling study held robot demonstrations fixed at 150 per task and used the same stage-wise metric on three tasks. In that study, the average rose from 0% with no human trajectories to 84% at 1,000. The curve was still increasing at the largest tested amount.

Scores are reported percentages, not binary completion rates, from 10 rollouts per task. Ours averages 70, T-Rex 29, and the variant without tactile prediction 42.

### 4. Touch as training signal

Human touch can contribute physical supervision without being issued as a robot command. The robot still relies on robot-specific action predictions at deployment. The evidence is limited to five laboratory tasks, one robot platform, and task-specific policies, and it does not establish transfer to new robots or tasks.
