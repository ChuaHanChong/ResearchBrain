# Scale and Selection: What Makes Automatic Harness Evolution Work for Visual-Interface Robot Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> An optimizer coding agent automatically evolves the harness (prompts, MCP tools, control rules) of a frozen coding agent that controls a simulated robot through a browser-based 3D visual interface. Each round it reads full trajectories from B rollouts and proposes one bounded commit, promoted only if it strictly beats the incumbent. With B=100 and 30 rounds, held-out success rises from 51% to 67%.

## Problem

- Visual-interface robot agents built on off-the-shelf coding agents depend heavily on hand-written harnesses; a single prompt demonstration can swing one agent's success from 70% to 100%.
- Automatic harness optimization was developed for software agents with seconds-long, unit-test-graded rollouts, not long robot episodes that each yield a single noisy binary success outcome.
- Prior harness optimizers treat rollouts per decision and the revision-acceptance rule as fixed, and reported harness-evolution gains often vanish under matched budgets and held-out tasks.

## Method

- **Harness-evolution loop**: a separate Codex optimizer agent reads an ==evidence manifest== of full trajectories, tool calls, errors and verdicts from all B rollouts, then proposes one systemic, test-passing commit.
- ==Champion-Challenger selection==: a challenger harness is promoted only if its success count strictly exceeds the incumbent's on the same fixed B cases; ties and regressions are logged and discarded.
- Rejected-challenger log plus controlled batch-size study: rejection reasons are fed back so regressions are not repeated, and B is varied over 5, 10, 20, 100 with 30 rounds fixed.

## Results

- Final champions reach **47%, 54%, 51%, and 67%** held-out success for B=5, 10, 20, 100 against **a 51% baseline**; only B=100 delivers a clear **16-point** gain.
- Small batches overfit: the B=10 champion hits **70%** training success but only **54% held-out**; unconditional acceptance at B=100 falls from a **47% peak** to **34%** by round 8.
- On RoboCasa kitchens the evolved harness lifts training success from **2/100 to 37/100** in two rounds; paired with the unseen GPT-6 Astra it reaches **83/100** versus **37 baseline**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When each rollout yields one noisy binary outcome, the evaluation batch behind each accept decision matters more than optimizer cleverness; small batches promote revisions by luck.
> - LLM optimizer agents propose mostly regressions, 22 of 30 at B=100, so a strict accept/reject gate is essential rather than trusting proposals unconditionally.
> - Harness gains are not absorbed by stronger base models: an evolved harness transferred to a newer model added even more, suggesting scaffolding and model capability compound.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A coding agent can control a simulated robot by looking at screenshots of a browser interface and using simple tools to position a virtual gripper. Between the model and the robot sits a "harness": the instructions, tools, control rules, and checks around the model. That harness is normally written by hand. In this work, a second coding agent revises the harness without training either model. The process can improve success on unseen tasks. However, robot-control rollouts are long and end in noisy, binary success-or-failure verdicts, so a handful of trials can make a lucky edit look good. The authors' central interpretation is that reliable evolution needs both enough evidence per decision and a safeguard against accumulating bad edits.

The challenger is tested on the same cases and promoted only if its score is strictly higher. (c) Held-out success against rollout batch size B=5, 10, 20, and 100. The baseline is 51%, and the final champions score 47%, 54%, 51%, and 67%.

### 2. More evidence per decision

In each round, the task agent runs the episodes. The optimizer then reads the full trajectories, tool calls, errors, and verdicts and proposes one bounded harness change. The resulting challenger is tested on the same fixed training cases. Both models remain unchanged. Each episode is a costly, long sequence of model turns, yet it supplies only a binary verdict. In a small batch, an individual success or failure therefore becomes disproportionately influential.

Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol serves as both the task agent and the optimizer. **Success rate** is the percentage of rollouts whose task's built-in detector reports success. A rollout is one task-and-seed episode. The held-out set contains 100 episodes on ten tasks disjoint from training. Each campaign runs 30 rounds from the same baseline, and the optimizer never sees the held-out tasks or results.

With B=5, 10, 20, and 100 rollouts per round, final held-out success is 47%, 54%, 51%, and 67%, respectively, against the 51% baseline. The pattern is generally favorable but not strictly monotonic: B=20 dips below B=10, and the clear gain occurs at B=100. Larger B also costs more rollouts per campaign. Small batches can overfit. The B=10 champion scores 70% on its ten fixed training cases but 54% held-out. The highest training score did not belong to the harness that generalized best.

### 3. A proposal must earn its place

Under Champion–Challenger, the challenger is compared with the incumbent on the same fixed training cases. It is promoted only if it has strictly more successes. Ties and regressions are rejected and logged, and the next round starts from the unchanged champion. The alternative is unconditional acceptance, where every revision becomes the base for the next.

The B=100 runs show the difference. Under unconditional acceptance, training success starts at 42%, peaks at 47% in round 5, and falls to 34% in round 8 (38% at round 10). With Champion–Challenger, training success rises from 41% to 60% by round 29, with 8 promotions in 30 rounds. On the disjoint held-out set, the final champion reaches 67% versus the baseline's 51%.

The gate guarantees only a non-decreasing training score. The held-out evaluation is what supports generalization. The unconditional runs were stopped once the downward trend was clear: at ten rounds for B=100, and at 20 for B=5, 10, and 20.

Training success over rounds for B=5, 10, 20, and 100. Grey shows unconditional acceptance and blue shows the Champion–Challenger champion trajectory. Triangles mark promoted challengers, crosses mark rejected ones, and dotted lines mark baseline scores. The acceptance rule guarantees that the blue paths never decrease. The unconditional traces stop early, and their starting points differ slightly because baseline commits were evaluated separately.

### 4. What remains open

Harness evolution changes no model weights. In these experiments, its benefit depended on both factors: how many rollouts informed each decision and whether an edit had to beat the current champion. With 100 training rollouts per round over 30 rounds, the final harness reached 67% held-out success against a 51% baseline. All experiments were simulated, and the batch-size study stops at B=100. Whether gains continue or saturate beyond that is unknown. Comparable rollout scale is also harder to obtain on physical robots.
