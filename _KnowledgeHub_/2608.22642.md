# Mol-JEPA: A multimodal Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture for Molecules

> [!summary] Summary
> Mol-JEPA is a multimodal joint embedding predictive architecture for molecules that, instead of chemically invalid structural augmentations, randomly masks entire modalities (structure, quantum chemistry, cell-painting, binding affinity, ADMET profiles) and predicts their latent embeddings with a Transformer predictor, using SIGReg isotropic regularization against collapse. Pretrained on 4.69 million compounds across 14 modalities, it averages 0.402 MAE, beating all baselines.

## Problem

- Self-supervised molecular models augment by masking atoms or bonds, which is chemically invalid because property cliffs make small structural edits flip properties entirely.
- Molecular structure alone omits biochemical context: protein networks, metabolic processes and cellular states that determine actual drug behavior are never encoded.
- Contrastive and concatenation-based multimodal models need negative samples, suffer modality gap and modality collapse, and scale poorly to many sparse, partially paired modalities.

## Method

- ==Modality masking== as the augmentation: a ==Bernoulli mask== deactivates whole modalities per sample, and a ==Transformer predictor== with a learnable ==CLS token== reconstructs their masked embeddings under ==MSE loss==.
- ==SIGReg (Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization)== from the ==LeJEPA== framework forces embeddings toward ==isotropic Gaussians== via ==Epps-Pulley statistics== on 1024 ==random projections==, avoiding ==student-teacher== or ==stop-gradient== heuristics.
- A curated 4.69M-compound corpus with 14 modalities via three encoders (atom, graph, vector): UMA, ChemGPT, CLOOME, BioXMol, Boltz-2 affinity, MOE, ECFP, xTB, DFT, ChEMBL, PCBA, TDC.

## Results

- Average MAE of **0.402** across **23** benchmark endpoints versus **0.427** for the Transformer-probe variant, **0.453** for TabICLv2/AlvaDesc, **0.471** CheMeleon, **0.519** Chemprop, **0.576** CLAMP.
- Advantage grows with distance from training data: binned by Tanimoto similarity, Mol-JEPA has the lowest error out of distribution, and wins most on small ASAP and Biogen sets.
- Adding modalities monotonically improves downstream error while training time scales roughly linearly (**9h** for two, **13h** for four, **27h** for eight on **100k** molecules); graph, MOE and ECFP contribute most.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When augmentations are unsafe for a domain, masking whole views instead of perturbing the data preserves label semantics and removes the augmentation-invariance mismatch.
> - Latent prediction with explicit isotropy regularization scales to many sparse, partially paired modalities without negative sampling, stop-gradients or student-teacher machinery.
> - Caution: gains concentrate in low-data and out-of-distribution regimes, leave-one-out effects are small due to redundancy, and some modalities can hurt downstream performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of new drugs is a resource-intensive process characterized by substantial failure rates, often due to issues such as insufficient target binding, poor absorption, rapid metabolism, or adverse toxicity profiles. Cheminformatics has addressed these challenges through various molecular representations, ranging from physicochemical descriptors and Extended Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFP) to quantum-chemical descriptors. More recently, deep learning has introduced learned representations via Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Chemical Language Models operating on SMILES strings or molecular graphs.

Despite these advances, existing pretrained molecular representations frequently demonstrate marginal or inconsistent performance improvements over traditional baselines. This limitation is attributed partly to the quality and coverage of pretraining datasets and, significantly, to the inadequacy of self-supervised learning (SSL) augmentation strategies for molecular data. Many SSL approaches generate augmented "views" of a molecule (e.g., through atom or bond masking) and train models to produce similar representations. However, molecular property landscapes can be discontinuous, where minor structural changes lead to substantial property shifts, known as property cliffs. Such augmentations can introduce chemically invalid perturbations, misaligning the augmentation bias with desired inductive invariances and potentially hindering SSL performance.

Furthermore, the research landscape suggests that molecular structure alone is often insufficient for comprehensive understanding, as drug behavior is context-dependent, emerging from interactions with biological systems not encoded in structure. Attempts to enrich representations with additional information, such as multi-task pretraining, have encountered issues like over-specialization and sensitivity to data discontinuities. Joint embedding approaches offer a more robust alternative, but existing contrastive methods require careful negative sample selection, which impacts representation quality. Recent Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) mitigate the need for negative sampling but still rely on meaningful data augmentations, which remain a challenge for molecular structures.

Mol-JEPA aims to address these identified limitations by offering a multimodal, context-aware framework that avoids chemically invalid structural augmentations, integrates diverse biochemical information, and utilizes a JEPA-based self-supervised learning paradigm without relying on negative sampling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for developing Mol-JEPA are multifaceted:

1. **Overcome Limitations of Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) Augmentations for Molecules**: Existing SSL methods often generate chemically invalid molecular augmentations (e.g., atom or bond masking) that distort the actual molecular property landscape. This leads to a misalignment between the model's learning objective and the inherent discontinuous nature of molecular properties (property cliffs). The motivation is to devise a self-supervised strategy that provides meaningful augmentations without introducing chemical inaccuracies. Mol-JEPA addresses this by masking entire modalities instead of perturbing molecular structures directly.

2. **Incorporate Biochemical Context Beyond Molecular Structure**: The behavior of a molecule, particularly in drug discovery, is highly dependent on its biochemical environment and interactions (e.g., with protein networks, metabolic processes, cellular states). Relying solely on molecular structure for representation learning provides an incomplete picture. The motivation is to enrich molecular representations by integrating a broad spectrum of data reflecting molecular interactions and effects within biological systems. This is intended to enable models to navigate chemical space with a biological perspective and identify complex structure-property relationships.

3. **Prevent Modality Collapse and Underutilization in Multimodal Architectures**: Existing multimodal models often concatenate or aggregate modalities using attention mechanisms, which can lead to modality collapse, where some modalities are underutilized or entirely ignored. The motivation is to ensure that all available modalities contribute meaningfully to the learned representations. Mol-JEPA achieves this by employing a predictive objective in the latent space, where the model is required to reconstruct masked modalities from others, thereby encouraging comprehensive information leverage.

4. **Avoid Dependence on Negative Sampling in Joint Embedding Methods**: While joint embedding approaches are promising, many rely on contrastive learning, which necessitates careful selection of negative samples. This selection can significantly influence representation quality and may introduce "false negatives." The motivation is to develop a joint embedding framework that is robust, less prone to overfitting, and does not require negative sampling. The Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) serves as the foundation for Mol-JEPA, naturally avoiding this issue.

5. **Develop a Scalable Framework for Multimodal Integration**: Drug discovery data is inherently diverse, coming from various experimental and computational sources. The motivation is to create a scalable architecture that can seamlessly integrate numerous, potentially sparse, and partially paired modalities without complex heuristics (like alternating gradients or stop-loss mechanisms) common in other multimodal training regimes.

In essence, the overarching objective is to build a robust, multimodal molecular "world model" that learns predictive, context-dependent representations by exploiting a diverse spectrum of biochemical and physical information, thereby improving performance on critical drug discovery tasks, especially in data-scarce and out-of-distribution settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Mol-JEPA is a multimodal foundation model based on the Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA), designed to learn molecular world models by predicting masked latent representations from a diverse set of biochemical and physical modalities.

#### 3.1. Pretraining Data

The pretraining dataset integrates public and computed data, resulting in a multimodal corpus of 4.69 million unique compounds.

- **Public Datasets**:
 - **ChEMBL**: A subset of drug-like small molecules with bioactivity information (IC50, Ki, Kd, EC50, AC50), filtered for a minimum of 30 labels per assay, yielding 306 unique assays for approximately 268k molecules.
 - **Therapeutic Data Commons (TDC)**: Includes 21 ADME, 11 toxicity, and 12 high-throughput screening endpoints, aggregated per molecule to 672 attributes for around 402k molecules.
 - **PCBA-1328**: A subset of PubChem with 1,328 bioassays (Active/Inactive flags) for over 1.1 million molecules.
 - **∇²DFT**: Quantum-chemical data comprising 17 global Density Functional Theory (DFT) properties (e.g., total energy, dipole moments, HOMO-LUMO gap) for over 1.2 million molecules. Properties are normalized using a quantile transformer.
 - **Enamine REAL**: Approximately 2 million unlabeled molecules sampled to increase chemical space coverage.
 - All molecules undergo canonicalization, pH7 normalization with MoKa, stereochemistry validation, and low-energy conformer computation using ETKDGv3 and UFF.

- **Computed Data (Foundation Model Embeddings and Descriptors)**:
 - **UMA (uma-s-1p2)**: Final-layer embeddings from a large mixture-of-linear-experts graph network trained on DFT simulation data (128 dimensions).
 - **CLOOME**: Embeddings from a model jointly encoding molecular structures and cell painting data (512 dimensions).
 - **BioXMol**: Embeddings from a multimodal model trained on cell painting and transcriptomics data using contrastive learning (1024 dimensions).
 - **ChemGPT (4.7M)**: Embeddings from a general-purpose molecular language model pretrained on PubChem10M (2048 dimensions).
 - **Boltz-2**: Co-folding and affinity predictions against targets from the Bowes-44 panel (associated with adverse effects), concatenated with ensemble embeddings from the binding affinity module (total 6912 + 4 dimensions). Predictions were generated for a subset of over 100k molecules against 9 specific targets.
 - **GNN**: Embeddings from a graph transformer model (512 dimensions).
 - **GFN2-xTB**: Quantum chemical properties computed using this semi-empirical method (7 descriptive vectors) for over 3.4 million molecules.
 - **MOE Descriptors**: Expert-designed molecular descriptors (227 dimensions) incorporating substructures, charge distributions, and 3D topology.
 - **Extended Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFP)**: Circular topology fingerprints (2048 dimensions).

In total, 14 distinct modalities are utilized (Table 2 in the paper), encompassing various embedding types, descriptors, and experimental measurements.

#### 3.2. Modality Encoders

Three types of learnable encoders project the diverse modalities into a common latent space:
- **Atom Encoder**: For atomic coordinates (batch, #atoms, dim).
- **Graph Encoder**: For molecular graphs with connectivity information.
- **Vector Encoder**: For representations of shape (batch, dim).

#### 3.3. Model Architecture

Mol-JEPA's core architecture involves a Transformer-based predictor (PyTorch's TransformerEncoderLayer).
- **Modality Masking**: Instead of augmenting molecular structures, the model randomly masks entire modalities. At least one modality is always kept available. Missing modalities are represented by respective masking tokens.
- **Predictor**: A Transformer encoder predicts the latent representations of the masked modalities from the remaining inputs.
- **CLS Token**: A learnable CLS token is added as a global readout for downstream tasks. Positional encodings are applied to all predictor inputs to track which embeddings need prediction.
- **Anti-Collapse Mechanism**: Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization (SIGReg) from the LeJEPA framework is employed to prevent representation collapse, ensuring each modality contributes to training. SIGReg can be applied to target embeddings, predicted embeddings, or the CLS token.
- **Implementation**: Implemented using the stable-pretraining framework and hyperparameter-tuned with Optuna, resulting in a model with approximately 50 million parameters.

#### 3.4. Pretraining Objectives

The training objective combines two loss components:
- **Modality Prediction Loss ($L^m_{pred}$)**: For each sample $i$ and masked modality $m$, the Transformer predictor reconstructs the embedding $\hat{s}^m_i$. The loss is the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the true masked embeddings and the predicted embeddings. This encourages the model to integrate information across modalities to infer missing data.
- **Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization (SIGReg) Loss ($L^m_{SIGReg}$)**: This regularization prevents modality collapse by conditioning latent variables to approximate an isotropic Gaussian distribution. It efficiently computes divergence from a 1D normal distribution by projecting embeddings onto random 1D directions and applying the Epps-Pulley statistic. For Mol-JEPA, 1024 random projections are used.

The final Mol-JEPA objective ($L_{JEPA}$) is a weighted sum of these two losses across all modalities: $L_{JEPA} = \sum_{m \in M} (L^m_{pred} + \lambda L^m_{SIGReg})$, where $\lambda > 0$ balances the terms.

#### 3.5. Benchmark Datasets

Evaluation is conducted on high-quality, real-world relevant datasets, criticized for previous limitations in traditional benchmarks:
- **OpenADMET ExpansionRx**: 9 endpoints from a blind challenge, featuring novel compounds.
- **ASAP-Polaris**: 7 datasets, including binding against two COVID targets and ADMET endpoints.
- **OpenADMET Pregance-X Receptor (PXR)**: Activity prediction blind challenge data.
- **Biogen ADME**: Six high-quality in vitro endpoints measured under consistent experimental conditions.

Three test-train splits are generated using Taylor-Butina Clustering (Tanimoto similarity threshold of 0.65 on ECFP4 fingerprints) to group compounds, ensuring structural diversity between splits. Official competition splits are also used where available.

#### 3.6. Baselines

Performance is compared against several established methods:
- **Classical Approaches**: Random Forest and Light Gradient Boosting Machine, trained with ECFP4, AlvaDesc descriptors, and 3D pharmacophore fingerprints.
- **In-context Predictions**: TabICLv2, a tabular foundation model, using the same feature sets.
- **Deep Learning Models**:
 - **CheMeleon**: A descriptor foundation model.
 - **Chemprop**: A GNN architecture.
 - **CLAMP**: A contrastive language-assay trained molecular foundation model (used for feature extraction, then linear/non-linear predictors).
All baselines undergo finetuning and hyperparameter optimization using 5-fold cross-validation on training data.

#### 3.7. Mol-JEPA Downstream Prediction

Multiple strategies are employed to leverage Mol-JEPA embeddings for downstream tasks:
- **CLS Token Probes**: The 512-dimensional CLS token (global summary of all modalities) is fed into linear, non-linear neural network predictors, or TabICLv2.
- **Aggregative Probe on Modality-Specific Embeddings**: A 2-layer Transformer encoder processes the 512-dimensional modality-specific predicted embeddings, applies attention, mean-pools the outputs, and feeds them into a linear output layer.
- **LoRA Fine-tuning**: Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is applied to the Mol-JEPA Transformer module and output projections with a rank of 16.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Mol-JEPA's evaluation on various benchmarks and through ablation studies revealed several findings regarding its performance, generalization capabilities, and internal mechanisms.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Generalization

- **Strong Performance**: Mol-JEPA demonstrated strong performance across all benchmark families, as evidenced by aggregated mean absolute error (MAE) comparisons (Figure 2a). Its MAE averaged 0.402, outperforming all other baselines including other foundation models and classical methods (Table 3).
- **Performance on Smaller Datasets**: The model showed a clear advantage on benchmarks with smaller datasets, such as ASAP/Polaris and Biogen ADME, suggesting utility in low-data settings common in drug discovery.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Performance**: Mol-JEPA consistently outperformed baselines as the Tanimoto similarity to the training data decreased, indicating improved out-of-distribution generalization (Figure 2b). This is attributed to pretraining on a large and diverse set of drug-like compounds.
- **R² Comparison**: In terms of R² (Table 9), Mol-JEPA achieved an average R² of 0.42, again outperforming other models.

#### 4.2. Statistical Significance

- **Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Tests**: Pairwise comparisons using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests revealed that Mol-JEPA achieved superior performance in 38% of comparisons across datasets. While TabICLv2 with AlvaDesc descriptors showed a slightly higher win rate (47%), its advantage was concentrated on larger ExpansionRx datasets. Mol-JEPA, in contrast, secured more significant wins on the smaller ASAP and Biogen benchmarks, reinforcing its benefit in low-data scenarios (Figure 3a).

#### 4.3. Downstream Probe Evaluation

- **Optimal Downstream Strategies**: For utilizing Mol-JEPA embeddings, a lightweight multimodal Transformer probe operating on modality-specific embeddings and TabICLv2 applied to the CLS token were identified as the most effective downstream models.
- **Probe Complexity**: Simpler linear or non-linear probes generally resulted in lower performance, suggesting that the learned representations are complex and benefit from more expressive downstream models (Figure 3b, Figure 9, Figure 10).
- **LoRA Fine-tuning**: LoRA fine-tuning of the Mol-JEPA transformer module generally underperformed other methods, possibly due to hyperparameter sensitivity or overfitting.

#### 4.4. Modality Scaling and Contribution

- **Performance with Increasing Modalities**: Training time increased approximately linearly with the number of modalities. However, downstream task performance consistently improved as the number of integrated modalities increased (Figure 4a, Figure 15), demonstrating the effectiveness of Mol-JEPA's multimodal learning framework.
- **Leave-One-Modality-Out Analysis**:
 - The graph modality (with a trainable GNN backbone) contributed most significantly to downstream performance.
 - Molecular fingerprint modalities (MOE, ECFP4) were also highly important, indicating the value of expert-designed features.
 - Modalities capturing chemical knowledge through binding affinities (Boltz) and cellular interaction profiles (CLOOME, BioXMol) also contributed positively.
 - Minor performance differences when removing a single modality at inference time suggested a degree of redundancy in the learned representations, which is likely encouraged by the reconstruction objective (Figure 4b).

#### 4.5. Multimodal Training Dynamics

- **Stable Training**: The architecture enabled stable multimodal training, with a consistent relationship observed between prediction loss, SIGReg loss, and downstream performance (Figure 6).
- **Heterogeneous Dynamics**: Training dynamics varied across modalities (Figure 5). While the effective rank continuously increased for all modality-specific embeddings, prediction loss and isotropic regularization loss exhibited heterogeneous behavior for different modalities (e.g., experimental ChEMBL vectors showed an initial rise then steady improvement, UMA embeddings showed decreasing prediction loss but oscillating regularization loss). Sparse modalities were generally more challenging to optimize.

#### 4.6. Pretraining Data Coverage and Scaling

- **Chemical Space Coverage**: Analysis using Generative Topographic Mappings (GTM) showed that Mol-JEPA's performance was stronger on benchmark families with better representation within the pretraining data's chemical space (Figure 13a, 13b). Prediction error increased with greater molecular distance from the pretraining corpus.
- **Dataset Size Scaling**: Downstream performance steadily improved with increasing pretraining dataset size (Figure 14a), suggesting that further scaling of the corpus could lead to additional gains.

#### 4.7. Ablation Studies (from Appendices)

- **Experimental Modalities**: Using experimental modalities in a JEPA predictive setting significantly outperformed a semi-supervised approach. Removing experimental modalities entirely deteriorated performance, confirming their value despite potential noise and sparsity.
- **Regularization**: Applying SIGReg to the target embeddings resulted in stable training and optimal downstream performance, whereas applying it to predicted embeddings led to training collapse.
- **Loss Projection**: Removing a dedicated projection head (similar to SimCLR) improved downstream performance, suggesting it is not advantageous in the JEPA-based setting and might cause backbone representations to lose information.
- **Embedding Dimension**: An embedding dimension of 512 provided a favorable trade-off between representation capacity and downstream performance, with diminishing returns beyond this point.
- **Multimodal Benefits**: A controlled comparison confirmed that incorporating additional modalities (beyond just Graph and ECFP4) led to a significant reduction in MAE (13-14%), validating the multimodal approach (Figure 15).

#### 4.8. Representational Similarity (from Appendices)

- **CKA Analysis**: Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) showed clusters of related modalities (e.g., quantum chemistry modalities like Nabla, MOE, xTB; cellular profiling modalities like CLOOME, BioXMol). ChEMBL and TDC exhibited lower similarity to most other modalities, suggesting unique information contributions (Figure 16).

#### 4.9. In-Context Learning (from Appendices)

- Mol-JEPA CLS-token embeddings achieved superior performance for in-context learning with TabICLv2 across most tasks compared to other molecular representations (Figures 17-19), indicating richer, more effective representations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Mol-JEPA represents an advancement in molecular foundation models by addressing several limitations of existing approaches in drug discovery. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

1. **Robust Multimodal Representation Learning**: The core contribution is a scalable framework that enables stable self-supervised learning from diverse molecular modalities. By predicting latent representations of masked modalities, Mol-JEPA avoids the issues of chemically invalid structural augmentations that plague many SSL methods. This allows for a more accurate capture of complex molecular property landscapes, including property cliffs.

2. **Integration of Biochemical Context**: Unlike models that primarily focus on molecular structure, Mol-JEPA explicitly incorporates a wide spectrum of biochemical and physical modalities, ranging from quantum-chemical data and foundation model embeddings to experimental bioactivity, ADMET profiles, and cellular phenotypes. This comprehensive integration allows the model to learn context-dependent molecular behavior, which is critical for understanding drug-biological system interactions. This contextual understanding has the potential to lead to more effective identification of structure-property relationships.

3. **Enhanced Performance in Data-Scarce and OOD Settings**: The demonstrated strong performance on high-quality benchmark datasets, particularly in situations with smaller datasets and for out-of-distribution molecules, highlights Mol-JEPA's practical utility. In drug discovery, where acquiring large, high-quality labeled datasets is often expensive and challenging, the ability to generalize well from limited data and to novel compounds represents a significant advantage.

4. **Avoidance of Heuristics and Modality Collapse**: The use of a JEPA-based framework eliminates the need for negative sampling, which simplifies optimization and avoids potential issues of "false negatives" associated with contrastive learning. Furthermore, the predictive objective, combined with Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization (SIGReg), encourages all modalities to contribute, mitigating modality collapse and ensuring comprehensive information utilization.

5. **Foundation for Molecular World Models**: Mol-JEPA's architecture provides a general and scalable approach for integrating heterogeneous data sources. This positions it as a step towards "molecular world models" that can reason about physical dynamics and biochemical interactions comprehensively. Such models could deepen pharmacological understanding, assist in navigating vast chemical spaces, and accelerate various stages of drug discovery, from lead optimization to toxicity prediction.

6. **Broader Scientific Applications**: Beyond drug discovery, the multimodal representation learning approach demonstrated by Mol-JEPA could be applied to other scientific domains characterized by complex, multimodal, and data-scarce environments, such as materials science (predicting material properties from atomic structure, synthesis conditions, and experimental characterizations) and biology (integrating genomic, proteomic, and imaging data).

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge limitations, including the current reconstruction objective not accounting for modality interdependencies, challenges in out-of-distribution generalization to substantially different tasks, and a limited understanding of how individual modalities contribute to downstream performance. Addressing these areas in future research could further enhance the model's capabilities and expand its impact.
