# Group-Evolving Agents: Open-Ended Self-Improvement via Experience Sharing

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of California, Santa Barbara developed Group-Evolving Agents (GEA), a framework enabling open-ended self-improvement for AI systems by evolving groups of agents that share experiences. This group-centric approach led to a 71.0% success rate on SWE-bench Verified and 88.3% on Polyglot, surpassing individual-centric self-evolving methods and matching human-designed agent performance.

## Problem

- Traditional AI systems rely on pre-defined architectures and continuous human intervention, limiting their ability for autonomous, open-ended self-improvement.
- Existing open-ended self-evolving AI, often inspired by biological evolution, uses individual-centric, tree-structured approaches that isolate evolutionary branches, preventing effective experience sharing and long-term cumulative progress.
- Exploratory diversity generated by individual agents in isolation often remains transient, failing to contribute to the collective and sustained advancement of AI capabilities.

## Method

- GEA introduces a ==group-centric evolutionary paradigm== where a collection of agents, rather than individuals, forms the fundamental unit of evolution.
- The framework incorporates a ==Parent Group Selection mechanism== that selects a diverse, high-performing group of agents using a balanced performance-novelty score.
- ==Open-Ended Group Evolution== explicitly enables intra-group experience sharing, aggregating all evolutionary traces from the parent group into a shared pool for every agent to utilize in its reflection and evolution modules.

## Results

- GEA achieved a **71.0%** success rate on SWE-bench Verified and **88.3%** on Polyglot, significantly outperforming the state-of-the-art individual-centric Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) baseline (**56.7%** and **68.3%** respectively).
- The autonomously evolved GEA agents demonstrated performance competitive with or superior to human-designed state-of-the-art frameworks, such as OpenHands + GPT-5 (**71.8%**) and Aider + GPT-5 (**52.0%**).
- GEA exhibited enhanced robustness by repairing framework-level bugs in an average of **1.4** iterations, faster than DGM's **5** iterations, and demonstrated transferability of improvements across different backbone models (e.g., GPT and Claude series).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating a group of agents as the evolutionary unit, with explicit experience sharing, transforms transient exploratory diversity into sustained, cumulative progress for AI self-improvement.
> - AI agents, unlike biological organisms, are not bound by lineage and can directly share and aggregate 'trajectories, tools, and learned artifacts,' leading to more efficient evolution.
> - Meta-learning based self-improvement allows agents to autonomously evolve their own framework-level designs, matching or exceeding the performance of human-designed systems without direct human intervention.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of AI systems capable of open-ended self-improvement is positioned as a "key characteristic of scientific breakthroughs" and a "grand challenge" in AI, aiming to overcome the limitations of pre-defined architectures that currently dominate most AI systems. Existing AI often struggles to transcend the boundaries of its initial design, leading to a heavy reliance on continuous human intervention for further progress.

This paper critically assesses the current state of self-improving AI along two main trajectories:

1. **Goal-Oriented, Chain-Based Evolutionary Paradigms:** Much of the existing self-improving AI focuses on continuous, iterative refinement towards a specific objective, often using mechanisms like self-play, supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, or reflection-based methods. While effective for improvement along a particular direction, these systems inherently limit exploration of diverse evolutionary paths in open-ended solution spaces.
2. **Lineage-Based, Tree-Structured Evolutionary Strategies:** Inspired by biological evolution, more recent open-ended AI systems adopt individual-centric, tree-structured approaches, like the Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM). These frameworks allow for branching exploration, helping to avoid local optima. However, their strict isolation between evolutionary branches prevents effective information and experience sharing. This leads to a crucial inefficiency: much of the valuable "exploratory diversity" generated remains temporary and fails to contribute to long-term cumulative progress, as discoveries in one branch cannot benefit others.

"Group-Evolving Agents (GEA)" directly addresses this latter limitation. It proposes a fundamental shift from individual-centric to **group-centric evolution**. The authors argue that AI agents, unlike biological organisms, are not constrained by reproduction or lineage and can directly share "trajectories, tools, and learned artifacts." By enabling explicit intra-group experience sharing, GEA aims to convert transient exploratory diversity into sustained, cumulative progress, thereby enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of open-ended evolution. This work represents a significant conceptual departure and methodological advancement within the domain of open-ended AI, bridging the gap between fostering diversity and ensuring its long-term utility.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for introducing Group-Evolving Agents (GEA) stems from the inherent limitations of current AI paradigms. First, traditional AI systems, built on pre-defined human architectures, eventually hit a ceiling in their capabilities, necessitating constant human redesign. The goal is to develop systems that can **autonomously evolve their own structural designs** to achieve open-ended self-improvement, thus reducing dependence on human intervention.

Second, existing open-ended self-evolving systems, largely inspired by biological evolution, adopt an individual-centric, tree-structured approach. While these systems generate considerable exploratory diversity, the isolation of evolutionary branches means that many promising discoveries remain short-lived and fail to contribute to long-term progress. The authors strongly motivate a "rethinking agent evolution," arguing that AI agents are not bound by biological constraints and can directly share and aggregate experiences.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of the GEA framework are:

1. **To introduce a novel paradigm for open-ended self-improvement** that treats a group of agents, rather than an individual, as the fundamental unit of evolution.
2. **To enable explicit experience sharing and reuse** across agents within a group, thereby overcoming the inefficiency of isolated evolutionary branches in existing tree-structured methods.
3. **To more effectively consolidate and reuse exploratory diversity** generated during evolution, transforming it into sustained, cumulative progress.
4. **To achieve significant performance gains and stronger robustness** compared to state-of-the-art individual-centric self-evolving methods on challenging coding benchmarks.
5. **To demonstrate the transferability of improvements** across different coding models, showing that the enhancements are framework-level rather than model-specific.
6. **To match or surpass human-designed state-of-the-art frameworks** through meta-learning-based self-improvement, without any human intervention.

Ultimately, the goal is to develop more capable, robust, and autonomous AI agents that can continuously evolve and improve their own underlying mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GEA introduces a **group-centric evolutionary paradigm** that operates on an archive of all discovered agents. At each iteration, the process unfolds in two core stages: Parent Group Selection and Open-Ended Group Evolution.

#### 3.1. Parent Group Selection

This stage (Algorithm 1) aims to select a diverse yet high-performing set of agents to form the parent group.
- **Agent Representation:** Each agent `i` is represented by a task-success vector `z_i ∈ {0, 1}^D`, where each dimension indicates success or failure on a probe task. This binary representation characterizes the agent's coding capabilities.
- **Dissimilarity and Novelty:** The dissimilarity between two agents is measured using cosine distance. The **novelty** of an agent `i` (`nov(i)`) is defined as the average cosine distance to its `M` most similar neighbors. This encourages selection of agents exploring less common solution spaces.
- **Performance–Novelty Score:** Agents are ranked using a combined score: `score(i) = α_i * sqrt(nov(i))`. Here, `α_i` is the agent's performance on downstream coding tasks, and `sqrt(nov(i))` provides a mild bias towards novelty without dominating performance. This balanced approach ensures both exploitation of high-performing agents and exploration of novel directions.
- **Selection:** The top-K agents based on this score are selected to form the parent group `G`.

#### 3.2. Open-Ended Group Evolution

This is the core stage (Algorithm 2) where the selected parent group jointly produces an offspring group, fundamentally differing from individual-centric evolution by explicitly enabling experience sharing.

- **Evolutionary Trace Collection:** For each agent `a_i` in the parent group `G`, a set of evolutionary traces `T_i` is collected. These traces include:
 - Code modification patches previously applied to `a_i`.
 - A predicted task patch generated by `a_i` for a randomly sampled unsolved task.
 - Corresponding task execution logs (tool invocation history, workflow).
 - Evaluation outcomes for the same task, highlighting failure modes and improvement directions.
- **Group-Level Experience Aggregation and Sharing:** Unlike other methods, GEA aggregates all collected traces from all agents in the parent group into a shared pool `S = ⋃ a_j ∈ G T_j`. This aggregated experience `S` is then provided as shared input to *every* agent in the group for its evolution.
- **Reflection:** Each agent `a_i` utilizes its "reflection module" to analyze the shared group-level experience `S` (along with its own history). This module produces "evolution directives" (`∆_i`) that suggest improvements to the agent's workflow, tool usage, or prompting strategies.
- **Evolution:** The "evolution module" of each agent then uses `∆_i` to generate framework-level patches (`π'_i`).
- **Acting and Evaluation:** The generated patch `π'_i` is applied to `a_i` to create an offspring agent `a'_i`. This new agent is then evaluated on programming tasks.
- **Archive Update:** Agents that successfully compile and demonstrate basic coding functionality are added to the archive for potential future selection.

This iterative process of group selection and evolution allows agents to integrate complementary discoveries and adaptations from other group members, transforming individual exploratory variations into collective, cumulative progress. The methodology emphasizes meta-learning for self-improvement without human intervention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on challenging coding benchmarks demonstrate the significant advantages of GEA:

#### 4.1. Performance Against State-of-the-Art Open-Ended Self-Evolving Systems

- **Superior Performance:** GEA significantly outperforms the state-of-the-art individual-centric self-evolving method, Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM), on both benchmarks.
 - On SWE-bench Verified, GEA achieved a 71.0% success rate, compared to DGM's 56.7%.
 - On Polyglot, GEA reached an 88.3% success rate, substantially higher than DGM's 68.3%.
- **Efficiency of Improvement:** GEA shows faster and more pronounced performance gains in the mid-to-late stages of evolution, indicating that as the archive accumulates diverse experiences, the group-level consolidation becomes increasingly effective.

#### 4.2. Performance Against State-of-the-Art Human-Designed Agents

- **Comparable or Exceeding Human Design:** GEA's autonomously evolved agents demonstrate performance competitive with or superior to carefully engineered human-designed state-of-the-art frameworks.
 - On SWE-bench Verified, GEA achieved 71.0%, very close to OpenHands + GPT-5 (71.8%).
 - On Polyglot, GEA's 88.3% significantly surpassed Aider + GPT-5 (high) (52.0%).
- This highlights the potential for fully autonomous agent improvement through meta-learning, matching or exceeding human expert performance.

#### 4.3. Evolution Analysis

- **Effective Consolidation of Diversity:** GEA is more effective at consolidating tool-level innovations. The best GEA agent integrated 8 out of 9 key functionalities, whereas the best DGM agent integrated only 5. Crucially, tools discovered in isolated DGM branches failed to propagate, while GEA systematically consolidated dispersed capabilities.
- **Broader Ancestor Integration:** The best GEA agent incorporated experiences from 17 unique ancestor agents (28.3% of the population), nearly double the 9 ancestors for the best DGM agent. This wider integration indicates successful cross-pollination of improvements.
- **Population-Wide Quality Improvement:** The worst-case performance among GEA's top-5 agents (58.3%) exceeded the single best agent produced by DGM (56.7%). This confirms that GEA systematically elevates the overall quality of the agent population, not just producing outliers.
- **Adaptive Evolutionary Patterns:** GEA adapts its evolutionary behavior to task complexity, producing larger, more concentrated patches for simpler tasks (Polyglot) and smaller, more distributed patches for complex tasks (SWE-bench Verified).

#### 4.4. Transferability

- **Model Agnostic Improvements:** The improvements achieved by GEA's best-discovered agent consistently transfer across different backbone models, including various GPT-series and Claude-series models. This indicates that the evolutionary gains are primarily due to enhancements in the agent's workflow and tool usage, rather than specific model-dependent prompting strategies.

#### 4.5. Robustness

- **Faster Bug Repair:** GEA demonstrated superior robustness to framework-level bugs. Injected errors were repaired in an average of 1.4 iterations, significantly faster than the 5 iterations required by the self-evolving DGM baseline. This is attributed to GEA's ability to leverage experience from better-performing, bug-free agents within the group to guide the repair of faulty ones.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Group-Evolving Agents (GEA) marks a significant step forward in the pursuit of truly open-ended self-improving AI systems. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating that **treating a group, rather than an individual, as the fundamental evolutionary unit** leads to more efficient and robust cumulative progress. By enabling explicit experience sharing, GEA effectively addresses the critical limitation of isolated evolutionary branches, transforming transient exploratory diversity into sustained, actionable improvements.

The **main findings underscore GEA's substantial impact**:
- **Enhanced Performance and Efficiency:** GEA significantly outperforms existing self-evolving methods, achieving state-of-the-art results on challenging coding benchmarks with the same computational budget (number of evolved agents). This indicates a more efficient utilization of evolutionary resources.
- **Autonomous Agent Development:** By matching or exceeding human-designed frameworks without human intervention, GEA demonstrates the viability of **fully autonomous meta-learning-based self-improvement**. This capability could dramatically accelerate AI development cycles and reduce reliance on manual engineering efforts.
- **Robustness and Transferability:** The framework's ability to swiftly repair framework-level bugs through group-level experience sharing, coupled with the transferability of its improvements across different backbone models, highlights its inherent robustness and generality. This suggests that GEA evolves fundamental agent-level improvements rather than superficial, model-specific optimizations.

**Potential Impact:**
The implications of GEA extend beyond coding tasks. The paradigm of open-ended group evolution could potentially be applied to diverse domains, enabling AI systems to:
- **Continuous Learning and Adaptation:** Develop more advanced and generalist agents capable of lifelong learning and adapting to novel situations and environments.
- **Mitigation of Biases:** Potentially allow systems to self-improve in areas like fairness and ethical alignment by evolving mechanisms to identify and correct biases.
- **Accelerated Scientific Discovery:** Enable AI to autonomously explore and refine experimental designs, algorithms, or theories, contributing to scientific breakthroughs.
- **Reduced Human Bottlenecks:** Minimize the need for human experts to continuously design, debug, and optimize complex AI architectures, shifting human roles towards setting high-level objectives and ethical guidelines.

**Ethical and Societal Considerations:**
The authors acknowledge crucial considerations within the "Impact Statement." Open-ended exploration, while powerful, carries inherent risks:
- **Misalignment with Human Intent:** The evolutionary process might inadvertently generate solutions or directions that deviate from human values or objectives, necessitating robust alignment mechanisms.
- **Computational Resources:** Open-ended evolution, especially at scale, can be computationally intensive, raising concerns about environmental impact and accessibility.
- **System Complexity:** Autonomously evolved systems could become increasingly complex and difficult for humans to fully understand or audit, posing challenges for interpretability and control.

The paper suggests the importance of establishing "appropriate boundaries" and guiding the system to "preserve exploratory diversity while ensuring alignment with human intent," conducting experiments in "isolated sandbox environments" to mitigate immediate risks. This responsible approach highlights the need for ongoing research into the governance and safety of powerful, self-evolving AI. Overall, GEA represents a significant conceptual and practical advancement, paving the way for more autonomous, capable, and adaptable AI systems, while also underscoring the critical need for careful ethical consideration as these technologies mature.
