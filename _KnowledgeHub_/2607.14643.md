# NavCMPO: Critic-Guided MeanFlow Policy Optimization for Adaptive Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Zhejiang University developed NavCMPO, a two-stage adaptive navigation framework that utilizes critic-guided MeanFlow policy optimization for low-latency visual local planning. This approach reduces inference latency to 60 ms while achieving a 74.7% average success rate across simulation scenes and a 66.7% success rate in real-world scenarios, surpassing existing diffusion-based methods.

## Problem

- Existing end-to-end diffusion-based navigation policies, like NavDP, suffer from high inference latency (e.g., 85 ms for 10 denoising steps), hindering real-time deployment.
- Aggressively reducing generation steps in flow-based models for speed leads to a substantial degradation in trajectory quality, particularly in obstacle avoidance.
- Policies trained purely through behavior cloning are limited by expert demonstration quality and struggle with novel situations or optimal path planning beyond the training data.

## Method

- A two-stage framework: **MeanFlow** policy pre-training for efficient, obstacle-aware trajectory generation, and online ==reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning== for performance enhancement and adaptation.
- Incorporates ==Obstacle Proximity Prediction (OPP)== as an auxiliary task during pre-training to learn spatially-aware visual features.
- Utilizes ==Critic-Guided Trajectory Refinement (CGTR)== during inference to leverage ==critic gradients== for safety correction, refining intermediate trajectory states to avoid obstacles.

## Results

- NavCMPO reduced inference latency from **85 ms** (NavDP) to **60 ms** on an NVIDIA RTX 4090, achieving a **74.7%** average success rate (SR) and **65.1%** Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) in simulation, outperforming NavDP (**68.3%** SR, **60.3%** SPL).
- Ablation studies showed that removing Critic-Guided Trajectory Refinement (CGTR) caused the largest drop in average SR (**13.5%**), emphasizing its critical role in ensuring safety with few-step generation.
- Demonstrated robust sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot, achieving the highest average SR of **66.7%** across real-world scenarios, surpassing baselines like ViPlanner (**33.3%**) and NavDP (**56.7%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MeanFlow, augmented with obstacle-aware representation learning and critic guidance, can achieve superior trajectory quality with significantly fewer generation steps than traditional diffusion models, enabling real-time performance.
> - Combining behavior cloning with reinforcement learning fine-tuning (PPO with BC regularization) allows the policy to overcome the performance ceiling of imitation learning while maintaining a strong prior for navigation.
> - A continuously updated critic network, coupled with CGTR, effectively adapts to embodiment-specific observation changes and improves safety without explicit retraining for new robot platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous visual navigation in complex, unstructured environments is a foundational challenge in robotics. Historically, classical navigation systems relied on modular pipelines for mapping, localization, and path planning, which often required significant engineering effort and struggled with adaptability across diverse environments and robot platforms. In contrast, end-to-end learning-based approaches have emerged as an alternative, directly mapping sensor observations to actions or trajectories, thereby bypassing explicit map construction and potentially reducing the sim-to-real gap.

Within end-to-end visual navigation, two primary paradigms exist:
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods:** These learn policies through trial-and-error interaction. However, vision-to-action RL often suffers from low sample efficiency, requiring extensive environmental interaction to learn both visual perception and motion planning.
- **Behavior Cloning (BC):** This approach learns directly from expert demonstrations. Diffusion-based policies have achieved state-of-the-art results in this area, exemplified by Diffusion Policy for visuomotor control and NavDP, which adapted this framework for mapless visual navigation with sim-to-real transfer.

Despite the successes of diffusion-based behavior cloning, several limitations have been identified:
1. **High Inference Latency:** Multi-step denoising processes, inherent in many diffusion models, introduce substantial inference latency. For example, NavDP requires 10 iterative denoising steps per planning cycle, hindering real-time deployment on resource-constrained platforms.
2. **Performance Ceiling of Behavior Cloning:** Policies trained solely on expert demonstrations cannot surpass the quality of those demonstrations and can be brittle to distributional shifts encountered during deployment.
3. **Degradation in Quality with Fewer Denoising Steps:** While techniques like flow matching and MeanFlow aim to reduce generation steps, aggressively reducing steps in navigation tasks can significantly degrade trajectory quality, particularly in obstacle avoidance, as models may not sufficiently reason about spatial structures.

The present work, NavCMPO, positions itself within this landscape by addressing these limitations. It integrates few-step MeanFlow trajectory generation to reduce latency, introduces critic-guided refinement and obstacle-aware representation learning to compensate for quality degradation from fewer steps, and incorporates reinforcement learning fine-tuning to overcome the performance ceiling and enhance adaptability. This approach aims to provide a low-latency, high-performance, and adaptive solution for visual navigation, bridging the gap between efficient generative models and robust robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a low-latency and adaptive visual navigation framework, named NavCMPO, that can effectively operate in cluttered, unstructured environments while addressing the inherent limitations of existing end-to-end learning-based navigation policies.

The motivation behind NavCMPO stems from several key challenges:

1. **Reducing Inference Latency for Real-time Operation:** End-to-end diffusion-based policies, despite their strong performance in mapless visual navigation, typically involve an iterative denoising process that introduces significant inference latency. For instance, the NavDP baseline, requiring 10 denoising steps, incurs an 85 ms latency per planning cycle. This latency is a barrier to real-time deployment, especially on robots with limited computational resources. The authors are motivated to explore techniques like MeanFlow, which offer the potential for few-step trajectory generation, to drastically reduce this latency.

2. **Compensating for Quality Degradation from Few-step Generation:** A critical observation is that simply reducing the number of denoising steps in flow-based models (e.g., to one step) leads to a substantial degradation in trajectory quality, particularly in obstacle avoidance. Such trajectories tend to prioritize the direct path to the goal, ignoring potential collisions. This motivates the development of mechanisms to restore or enhance obstacle avoidance capabilities when using efficient, few-step generation.

3. **Surpassing the Performance Ceiling of Behavior Cloning:** Policies trained purely through behavior cloning are inherently limited by the quality and diversity of the expert demonstrations. They may struggle with novel situations or optimal path planning that goes beyond what was explicitly demonstrated. This limitation motivates the integration of reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning, which has shown promise in pushing generative policies beyond the imitation learning ceiling, enabling agents to learn more optimal and robust behaviors through environmental interaction.

4. **Enabling Efficient Adaptation to Embodiment-Specific Changes:** Mobile robots operate on various platforms with differing camera placements, fields of view, and dynamics. Retraining a visual navigation policy from scratch for each new robot embodiment is computationally expensive and time-consuming. The authors aim to develop a framework that allows for efficient specialization to a target robot, accommodating embodiment-specific observation changes without requiring a complete overhaul of the policy. This motivates a two-stage approach with targeted adaptation mechanisms.

In summary, NavCMPO is motivated by the need for visual navigation policies that are simultaneously fast enough for real-time deployment, safe and effective in obstacle-rich environments even with reduced computational steps, and robustly adaptable to new robotic platforms, going beyond the limitations of purely imitation-based learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NavCMPO proposes a two-stage adaptive navigation framework designed for low-latency visual local planning using RGB and depth camera inputs to generate navigation trajectories.

**A. Stage 1: MeanFlow Policy Navigation Pre-Training**

The first stage focuses on establishing a robust, obstacle-aware trajectory generation policy using MeanFlow, augmented with specific mechanisms to ensure safety and quality with few-step generation.

- **Problem Formulation:** The task is framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The robot receives observations ($o_t$) including historical RGB images, current depth image, and the navigation goal. The policy ($\pi_\theta$) outputs a trajectory (sequence of waypoints, $a_t = \tau_t$).
- **Multi-Modal Encoder:** Two DINOv2 ViT-S encoders process RGB and depth observations independently. Historical RGB images are fed to the RGB encoder, while the current depth image is replicated to three channels for the depth encoder. The resulting visual tokens are fused using a lightweight Transformer decoder, yielding a conditional representation ($h_{cond}$).
- **Obstacle Proximity Prediction (OPP):** An auxiliary task introduced to encourage the visual encoder to learn spatially-aware features. The space around the robot is divided into 12 angular sectors. An MLP projects $h_{cond}$ to predict the minimum distance to an obstacle within each sector ($d_{pred}$). Ground truth distances are derived from obstacle point clouds. The loss is SmoothL1, focusing on capturing local obstacle geometry. This auxiliary task is removed during inference.
- **Policy Head (MeanFlow):** This component predicts the velocity field $u_\theta(z_t, t, h_{cond})$ for a MeanFlow process. Trajectory generation is modeled as a deterministic flow from a standard Gaussian noise ($\epsilon \sim N(0, I)$) to the expert trajectory distribution ($a_{tgt}$). The MeanFlow loss minimizes the squared difference between the predicted velocity field and the target velocity field ($u_{tgt} = (\epsilon - a_{tgt})$). While MeanFlow typically aims for one-step generation, the authors use multi-step integration (e.g., 5 steps) to improve trajectory quality for navigation, updating the flow state iteratively.
- **Critic Head:** During inference, this network $C(z_t, h_{cond})$ evaluates $N$ parallel candidate trajectories generated by the policy. It shares the Transformer decoder with the policy head. The critic predicts a quality score based on obstacle proximity and goal progress. The scoring function penalizes waypoints violating a collision-safety threshold ($d_{safe}$) and rewards progress towards the goal, with obstacle avoidance being prioritized. The critic is trained via mean squared error against ground-truth scores derived from obstacle point cloud supervision on expert and augmented trajectories.
- **Critic-Guided Trajectory Refinement (CGTR):** To mitigate potential safety risks from few-step denoising, CGTR refines the intermediate trajectory state in the penultimate generation step. It uses gradients from the pre-trained critic, $z_\tau \leftarrow z_\tau + \eta \cdot \text{Clip}(\nabla_z C(z_t, h_{cond}), \delta)$. This positive critic gradient provides a local direction to improve the safety and goal-directedness of the intermediate trajectory, analogous to classifier guidance but tailored for navigation.
- **Total Pre-training Loss:** A weighted sum of the MeanFlow loss, OPP loss, Critic loss, and a positional encoding loss.

**B. Stage 2: Online RL Fine-tuning for MeanFlow Policy**

The second stage fine-tunes the pre-trained MeanFlow policy using reinforcement learning to enhance performance and adapt to specific robot embodiments and environments.

- **Action Distribution for Exploration:** Stochastic noise is injected into the final steps of the flow process during training, parameterizing the action distribution as a Gaussian centered on the MeanFlow prediction.
- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** PPO is employed to update the policy parameters. The log-probability of actions is adjusted to account for the CGTR correction term.
- **Reward Function:** A dense reward function is designed:
 - $R_{progress}$: measures the reduction in Euclidean distance to the goal.
 - $R_{safety}$: penalizes collisions and unsafe proximity using Euclidean Signed Distance Field (ESDF) map values.
 - $R_{goal}$: a bonus for reaching within a specified distance of the goal.
- **Critic Update:** The critic head is continuously updated during Stage 2 using the same score definition and supervised loss as in Stage 1. This ensures the critic adapts to embodiment-specific observation changes encountered during deployment.
- **Behavior Cloning (BC) Regularization:** To prevent catastrophic forgetting and maintain the navigation prior learned during pre-training, a behavior cloning loss ($L_{BC}$) is added. It penalizes deviations of the fine-tuned policy's trajectories from those generated by a frozen reference policy ($\pi_{ref}$, the pre-trained BC policy).
- **Final Stage 2 Optimization Objective:** The total loss for fine-tuning combines the PPO clipped surrogate objective, the BC regularization loss, and the critic update loss.

This two-stage approach leverages the efficiency of MeanFlow for trajectory generation, enhances safety through obstacle-aware representation and critic-guided refinement, and improves adaptability and robustness through online reinforcement learning with targeted regularization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results from simulation experiments and real-world deployments to evaluate NavCMPO's performance.

**A. Simulation Results**

- **Comparative Performance (Table I):**
 - NavCMPO achieved an average success rate (SR) of 74.7% and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) of 65.1% across Cluttered-Hard, Home, and Commercial simulation scenes, outperforming all baselines.
 - It exceeded the retrained NavDP baseline (68.3% SR, 60.3% SPL) by 6.4 percentage points in SR and 4.8 percentage points in SPL.
 - NavCMPO reduced inference latency from NavDP's 85 ms to 60 ms on an NVIDIA RTX 4090, while maintaining higher performance.
 - MP1 (one-step MeanFlow) demonstrated the lowest latency (30 ms) but suffered from significantly lower success rates in complex environments (e.g., 46.4% SR in Home scenes), confirming the degradation in quality with aggressive step reduction.
 - NavCMP (the pre-trained NavCMPO without RL fine-tuning) achieved performance comparable to NavDP (69.2% SR, 62.2% SPL) at a reduced latency of 60 ms, indicating the effectiveness of its pre-training components.
 - Qualitative comparisons (Fig. 2) showed NavCMPO generating safer and less tortuous paths, successfully avoiding obstacles where other baselines struggled.

- **Ablation Studies (Table II, Fig. 3, 4, 5, 6):**
 - **Generation Method:** Replacing MeanFlow with Flow Matching (FlowNav) resulted in the lowest average SR (51.0%), highlighting MeanFlow's superiority for few-step generation quality.
 - **Obstacle Proximity Prediction (OPP):** Removing OPP ("w/o OPP") led to a 9.0% drop in average SR (from 73.5% to 64.5%), confirming its role in learning obstacle-aware visual representations.
 - **Critic-Guided Trajectory Refinement (CGTR):** Disabling CGTR ("w/o CGTR") caused the largest average SR drop (13.5%, from 73.5% to 60.0%), underscoring its critical role in ensuring safety and compensating for fewer denoising steps.
 - **Reinforcement Learning Fine-tuning (RFT):** Without RFT ("w/o RFT"), the average SR decreased by 7.5%, demonstrating that RL fine-tuning significantly improves task performance and robustness.
 - **Critic Update:** Not updating the critic during fine-tuning ("w/o CriUp") resulted in a 1.0% SR and 3.0% SPL decrease, indicating the importance of critic adaptation to embodiment-specific observation changes.
 - **Behavior Cloning (BC) Regularization:** Removing BC loss ("w/o BC Loss") increased SR slightly in easy scenes but reduced average SPL by 7.4%, suggesting that BC regularization helps maintain path efficiency and prevents long-term drift.
 - **Optimal CGTR Configuration:**
 - Analysis of denoising steps (Fig. 3) showed that 5-step MeanFlow with CGTR achieved similar SR to 10-step MeanFlow without CGTR (59.4% vs. 59.2%), but with significantly lower latency (60ms vs. 85ms). Single-step MeanFlow achieved only 36.3% SR.
 - Single-step CGTR guidance at the penultimate generation step was found to be optimal, effectively steering trajectories around obstacles without causing over-correction or instability observed with multi-step guidance (Fig. 4).
 - Sensitivity analysis of the guidance coefficient $\eta$ (Fig. 5) identified an optimal value of $\eta=0.4$, maximizing both SR (61%) and SPL (57.1%).
 - **Fine-tuning Steps in RL:** Experimentation with the number of flow integration steps during PPO fine-tuning (Fig. 6) revealed that a 5-step configuration achieved the highest and most stable reward growth, outperforming 1-step and 3-step configurations.

**B. Real-World Results**

- **Sim-to-Real Transfer (Table III, Fig. 7):**
 - Experiments on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot demonstrated effective sim-to-real transfer.
 - NavCMPO achieved the highest average SR of 66.7% across Easy, Outdoor, and Hard real-world scenarios, outperforming ViPlanner (33.3%), NavDP (56.7%), and NavCMP (53.3%).
 - NavCMPO showed particular strength in challenging environments, achieving 8/10 SR in Outdoor scenes and 5/10 SR in Hard scenes, significantly better than baselines.
 - Qualitative examples (Fig. 7c) illustrated NavCMPO successfully navigating narrow passages and avoiding furniture where NavDP failed, generating safer trajectories with better obstacle clearance. This suggests improved robustness to real-world distributional shifts, particularly in scenarios requiring precise obstacle avoidance not fully covered by expert demonstrations.

In summary, NavCMPO demonstrates a superior quality-latency tradeoff, significantly enhances obstacle avoidance for few-step generative policies, and exhibits robust sim-to-real transfer capabilities, leading to higher success rates in diverse real-world navigation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The NavCMPO framework represents an advancement in end-to-end visual navigation, with several notable aspects of significance and potential impact:

1. **Improved Efficiency for Real-time Deployment:** By effectively combining few-step MeanFlow generation with critic-guided refinement, NavCMPO significantly reduces inference latency (from 85 ms in NavDP to 60 ms) without compromising performance. This efficiency is critical for real-time robotic applications, enabling responsive and agile navigation on resource-constrained platforms, which can accelerate the deployment of autonomous mobile robots in various practical scenarios.

2. **Enhanced Safety and Robustness in Cluttered Environments:** A key challenge with efficient few-step generative models is the potential degradation in obstacle avoidance. NavCMPO addresses this through the Obstacle Proximity Prediction (OPP) auxiliary task, which fosters obstacle-aware visual representations, and the Critic-Guided Trajectory Refinement (CGTR) mechanism. CGTR uses learned critic gradients to explicitly correct unsafe intermediate trajectories, ensuring reliable obstacle avoidance even with aggressive denoising step reduction. This enhancement in safety is paramount for reliable robot operation in complex, unstructured, and dynamic environments.

3. **Overcoming Behavior Cloning Limitations through RL Fine-tuning:** NavCMPO successfully integrates reinforcement learning (PPO) fine-tuning to push the policy's performance beyond the ceiling imposed by expert demonstrations. This allows the robot to learn more optimal behaviors, adapt to subtle environmental dynamics, and exhibit greater robustness to distributional shifts not captured in the initial dataset. This paradigm of RL fine-tuning generative policies is a promising direction for creating more capable and generalizable robotic agents.

4. **Adaptive and Generalizable Framework:** The two-stage adaptation scheme, which includes continuous critic adaptation and behavior-cloning regularization during RL fine-tuning, supports efficient specialization to new robot embodiments. Instead of requiring extensive retraining from scratch, the system can adapt to changes in camera placement, field of view, or robot dynamics. This significantly reduces the computational burden and time required for deploying navigation policies across a fleet of diverse robots, potentially lowering the barrier to entry for various robotic applications.

5. **Contribution to Generative Models in Robotics:** The work demonstrates the effective application of MeanFlow for visual navigation, providing insights into its quality-latency trade-off and the necessary augmentations for safe deployment. It contributes to the growing body of research on using generative models for robot control, particularly in addressing the practical challenges of speed and safety.

6. **Real-world Applicability:** The successful sim-to-real transfer and superior performance on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot in diverse indoor and outdoor real-world scenarios underscore the practical applicability of NavCMPO. Its ability to handle complex situations and avoid obstacles effectively in real-world settings suggests its potential for widespread use in logistics, inspection, service robotics, and other domains where autonomous navigation is essential.

While the current evaluation focuses on a matched-compute setting and a specific robot, the framework establishes a solid foundation for future work exploring broader cross-embodiment evaluation, more explicit geometric guidance baselines, and safer online policy adaptation strategies directly on physical robots.
