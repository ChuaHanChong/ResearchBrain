# TAROT: Targeted Data Selection via Optimal Transport

> [!summary] Summary
> TAROT introduces a targeted data selection framework that leverages Whitened Feature Distance (WFD) and Optimal Transport (OT) to identify data subsets that optimally enhance model performance on specific target tasks. The method consistently achieved superior results in semantic segmentation, motion prediction, and LLM instruction tuning, often using significantly less data than full datasets or prior methods.

## Problem

- Existing targeted data selection methods, often relying on influence estimation and greedy heuristics, struggle with complex, multimodal data distributions.
- Influence estimation in high-dimensional data is susceptible to bias from dominant feature components, leading to inaccurate data selection.
- Prior greedy selection strategies often assume a restrictive linear additive impact of training samples, which fails to capture non-linear interactions within data.

## Method

- TAROT frames targeted data selection as an ==Optimal Transport (OT)== problem, aiming to select a subset of candidate data that minimizes the OT distance to the target distribution.
- It introduces ==Whitened Feature Distance (WFD)== as a novel pairwise cost metric, based on whitened and normalized model loss gradients, to robustly measure data similarity and influence.
- A greedy algorithm, utilizing the sparsity of ==gradient features== and optionally incorporating data weighting via OT potentials, iteratively selects the most relevant data points to minimize the OT distance.

## Results

- TAROT's Whitened Feature Distance (WFD) consistently outperformed state-of-the-art data attribution methods (e.g., TRAK) in terms of Linear Data Modeling Score (LDS) across various tasks like image classification and motion prediction.
- On semantic segmentation (GTA5 to Cityscapes), TAROT's Optimal Transport Minimization (OTM) scheme automatically selected **~24%** of the data, achieving higher mIoU than models trained on the full dataset or other baselines.
- For motion prediction (multi-source to nuScenes) and LLM instruction tuning, TAROT achieved state-of-the-art performance using significantly reduced data subsets (e.g., **<0.5%** for LLMs), demonstrating high data efficiency and transferability across different model architectures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal Transport offers a principled framework for targeted data selection by directly minimizing the distributional discrepancy between selected candidate data and a target domain.
> - Whitened Feature Distance (WFD), derived from whitened and normalized model gradients, provides more accurate and robust influence estimates compared to prior methods, especially in high-dimensional and correlated feature spaces.
> - Selecting data based on its distributional alignment with the target task, rather than just individual influence scores, enables models to achieve higher performance with significantly smaller, carefully curated datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid expansion of model capacity and data volume has become a defining characteristic of modern deep learning, particularly in computer vision (CV) and large language models (LLMs). While more data often correlates with better performance, training on "all available data" is frequently suboptimal, computationally expensive, and often infeasible due to varying data quality and resource constraints. This context has spurred significant research into **data selection**, aiming to identify efficient and effective subsets of data for model training.

Within data selection, a distinction is drawn between "coresets" and "targeted data selection." Coreset selection focuses on identifying a subset that optimizes *in-domain* performance, assuming the training, validation, and candidate data share the same distribution. In contrast, **targeted data selection**, which is the focus of TAROT, aims to select data from a candidate pool that maximizes model performance on a *specific target task or domain*, even if the candidate data differs distributionally from the target. This is particularly relevant in scenarios like transfer learning, domain adaptation, or fine-tuning on specialized datasets.

Prior methods for targeted data selection predominantly rely on a two-step approach:
1. **Influence Estimation:** Quantifying how individual candidate data points affect the model's performance on target data. This often involves gradient-based approximations (e.g., Pruthi et al., 2020) or "datamodels" (e.g., Park et al., 2023; Engstrom et al., 2024).
2. **Greedy Heuristics:** Selecting data with the highest estimated influence scores.

TAROT directly addresses critical limitations identified in these existing influence-based greedy heuristics. The paper points out two primary weaknesses:
- **Dominant Feature Bias:** In high-dimensional data (like gradients), certain feature components can disproportionately impact influence estimation due to correlations and varying scales, leading to distorted representations of data relationships.
- **Linear Additive Assumptions:** Greedy selection strategies often implicitly assume that the influence of multiple training samples on a target is simply additive. This assumption, while computationally convenient, is frequently violated in complex, multimodal data distributions (e.g., motion prediction, diverse LLM instruction sets), where interactions are non-linear and emergent. As highlighted by Hu et al. (2024), this can lead to suboptimal data selection.

TAROT positions itself as a significant advancement by moving beyond these limitations. It adopts a **distribution-matching approach** grounded in **Optimal Transport (OT) theory**. OT has recently gained traction in machine learning for comparing probability distributions and estimating dataset similarity (Alvarez-Melis & Fusi, 2020; Kang et al., 2024). By leveraging OT, TAROT explicitly aims to minimize the distributional discrepancy between the selected data and the target domain. This aligns with recent findings (Just et al., 2023) that smaller OT distances between source and target data improve domain-specific performance. A concurrent work (Liu et al.) also explores OT for data selection but uses a different gradient distance estimation and a continuous OT formulation, which TAROT distinguishes itself from by introducing a novel distance metric and a more efficient discrete greedy selection. Thus, TAROT contributes to the frontier of data-centric AI, pushing the boundaries of how we efficiently and effectively curate training data for complex modern AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the TAROT framework is to **develop a more effective and robust method for targeted data selection** that overcomes the limitations of existing influence-based greedy heuristics. Specifically, the authors aim to select a subset of data from a large candidate pool that optimally enhances model performance on a predefined target task or domain.

The key motivations driving this research stem directly from the identified shortcomings of current targeted data selection approaches:

1. **Addressing Ineffectiveness on Complex, Multimodal Data:** Current methods, while successful on limited and unimodal data (e.g., single-task LLM fine-tuning), struggle when target data exhibits complex, diverse, or multimodal distributions. This is crucial for real-world applications like autonomous driving (motion prediction) or diverse language generation tasks where multiple inherent patterns exist. The motivation is to create a method that performs reliably across such complex scenarios.
2. **Mitigating Bias from Dominant Feature Components:** Influence estimation in high-dimensional spaces (e.g., model gradients) can be skewed by features that dominate due to correlations or disproportionate scales. This leads to inaccurate influence scores and suboptimal selection. A core motivation is to develop an influence measure that provides a more reliable and unbiased representation of data relationships.
3. **Overcoming Restrictive Linear Additive Assumptions:** Greedy selection methods often assume that the impact of multiple training samples can be simply summed. This linear additivity fails to capture complex interactions and non-linear dependencies within data, especially when dealing with diverse distributions. The authors are motivated to move beyond this simplistic assumption by adopting a more holistic, distribution-level matching approach.
4. **Optimizing Data Efficiency and Resource Utilization:** With ever-growing datasets and computational costs for training, the ability to train high-performing models on smaller, more relevant datasets is a significant practical motivation. This translates to reduced training time, lower carbon footprint, and enhanced accessibility for researchers with limited resources.
5. **Ensuring Broad Applicability:** The authors aim for a versatile framework that is not confined to a single domain but can consistently improve performance across various deep learning tasks, including traditional computer vision, complex robotic perception, and large language model instruction tuning.

To address these motivations, TAROT proposes a novel approach that integrates a new influence estimation method, Whitened Feature Distance (WFD), with Optimal Transport theory for distribution matching, thereby offering a principled solution to these challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TAROT frames targeted data selection as a **distribution matching problem**, where the goal is to select a subset of candidate data that minimizes the Optimal Transport (OT) distance to the target distribution. The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

#### 3.1. Data Representation via Model Gradients

The foundation of TAROT, similar to many influence-based methods, is to represent data points (feature-label pairs `z`) using their **model loss gradients**. Specifically, for a data point `z`, its embedding `ϕ(z)` is defined as the sum of loss gradients with respect to model parameters `θ` across multiple training checkpoints `T`:
`ϕ(z) = Σ_{i=1}^{T} ∇L(z; θ_i)`
This gradient feature captures the task-specific relationship between data points and the model’s optimization objective, making it a suitable basis for measuring data influence.

#### 3.2. Whitened Feature Distance (WFD)

A critical innovation in TAROT is the **Whitened Feature Distance (WFD)**, designed to address the challenges of correlated, high-dimensional gradient features. Direct L2 distance on raw gradients can be biased. WFD tackles this in three steps:
1. **Random Projection:** To reduce the high dimensionality of raw gradients, `ϕ(z)` is projected into a lower-dimensional space using a random projection matrix `P^T`. This is a common technique for efficiency.
2. **Whitening (Cholesky Whitening):** The projected gradients `ϕ(z)_proj` are still correlated, creating an anisotropic feature space where some directions dominate distance calculations. To decorrelate features and scale them to unit variance, Cholesky whitening is applied. This involves centering the projected gradients (`~ϕ(z)_proj`), computing their covariance matrix `Σ`, performing a Cholesky decomposition `Σ = LL^T`, and then transforming the gradients using `L^-1`. This ensures each feature contributes equally to the distance. (Note: For influence estimation evaluation, ZCA whitening is used due to its property of preserving data structure, though Cholesky is used for selection due to efficiency and comparable performance).
3. **Normalization:** Finally, each whitened gradient vector `ϕ(z)_w` is normalized to unit length to ensure consistent gradient scales across different samples.
The **WFD** between two samples `z` and `z'` is then calculated as the L2 distance between their normalized, whitened gradient embeddings: `d_w_Z(z, z') = ||ˆϕ(z) - ˆϕ(z')||_2`.

#### 3.3. Optimal Transport between Datasets

Using the pairwise WFD as the cost function `c(z, z')`, TAROT lifts this metric to a dataset-level Optimal Transport distance. For a candidate dataset `D_c` and a target dataset `D_t`, the OT distance `d_OT(D_c, D_t)` is defined as the minimum cost of transporting mass from the distribution of `D_c` to that of `D_t`. This is typically solved using the Sinkhorn algorithm for computational efficiency, which approximates the computationally expensive linear programming formulation.

#### 3.4. Data Selection via OT Distance Minimization

The core challenge is to select a subset `D_s` from `D_c` of a given size `S` (or an optimal size) that minimizes `d_OT(D_s, D_t)`. Since exhaustive search is combinatorial, TAROT proposes a **greedy algorithm** that leverages the sparsity of gradient-based features in high-dimensional space, implying that most transport mass is concentrated on nearest neighbors.

Two selection schemes are implemented:
1. **Fixed-Size Selection:** For a predefined target subset size `S`, the algorithm iteratively adds the `k`-th nearest candidates from `D_c` for each point in `D_t`. If adding all `k`-th nearest candidates would exceed `S`, the method computes "potential" scores for new candidates using the dual OT problem (Equation 13), which estimates the benefit of adding a sample to minimize OT distance. It then ranks candidates by potential and selects the top `S - |D_s|` samples.
2. **OT-Distance Minimization Selection (OTM):** This scheme automatically determines a potentially optimal selection ratio. It employs a **k-fold cross-validation strategy** on the target dataset `D_t`. In each fold, 1/k of `D_t` is used for selection, and the OT distance is evaluated against the remaining (k-1)/k data. The algorithm iteratively adds nearest neighbors from `D_c` until the OT distance *increases*, serving as an early stopping criterion. This prevents overfitting to specific target points and estimates the optimal size. The selected subsets from all folds are then combined.

#### 3.5. Data Weighting with OT Potential (Optional)

To further leverage the insights from Optimal Transport, TAROT can assign weights to selected data points based on their OT potentials (derived from Equation 13). These weights determine how many times each data point is duplicated within an epoch during training, effectively emphasizing more "important" samples. The total sum of weights can be normalized to match the training steps of using the full dataset or adjusted based on specific requirements.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

TAROT was rigorously evaluated across three diverse deep learning tasks: semantic segmentation, motion prediction, and instruction tuning, consistently demonstrating superior performance over state-of-the-art baselines.

#### 4.1. WFD for Influence Estimation

- **Performance:** WFD consistently outperformed TRAK (Park et al., 2023), a state-of-the-art data attribution method, in terms of Linear Data Modeling Score (LDS) across varying ensemble sizes for both image classification (CIFAR-10) and motion prediction (nuScenes). This suggests WFD provides more accurate and reliable influence estimates by better correlating with "datamodels."
- **Qualitative Analysis:** Visualizations (Figure 3, Appendix F) confirmed that WFD successfully identifies training examples that are semantically aligned with target samples (most helpful) and those that are semantically divergent (most detracting) in complex domains like motion prediction and semantic segmentation.
- **Ablation Study:** The ablation study (Table 2) confirmed the crucial role of both whitening and normalization in WFD, as removing either component significantly decreased LDS scores.

#### 4.2. TAROT for Semantic Segmentation (GTA5 to Cityscapes)

- **Domain Alignment:** Training a DeepLabV3 model on the full synthetic GTA5 dataset actually *degraded* performance (mIoU) on the real-world Cityscapes dataset, highlighting the issue of distributional misalignment.
- **Superior Selection:** TAROT consistently outperformed baselines (LESS, DsDm, random selection) across various selection ratios. Notably, TAROT's OTM (Optimal Transport Minimization) scheme automatically selected approximately 24% of the GTA5 data, achieving the highest mIoU, even surpassing models trained on the full GTA5 dataset. This demonstrates TAROT's ability to identify relevant synthetic data that enhances real-world performance.
- **Distributional Capture:** T-SNE visualizations of selected gradient features (Figure 6) revealed that TAROT selects data that captures the broader complexity and diversity of the target distribution, unlike DsDm, which tended to cluster narrowly around the target center. This supports TAROT's advantage in handling multimodal distributions.

#### 4.3. TAROT for Motion Prediction (Multi-source to nuScenes)

- **Generalizability:** In this highly complex task involving diverse input types (maps, trajectories) and cross-dataset transfer (WOMD, Argoverse 2, nuPlan to nuScenes), TAROT again consistently outperformed baselines.
- **Optimal Selection Ratio:** The OTM scheme effectively found optimal selection ratios (e.g., ~29.8% of the full candidate dataset), leading to models that outperformed those trained on the entire dataset and other fixed-ratio selections. TAROT achieved the top performance on the nuScenes leaderboard (Appendix D), setting a new state-of-the-art with less data.
- **Transferability:** Data selected by a lightweight AutoBots model (for selection) significantly improved the performance of a larger Wayformer model (for training), despite differences in model capacity and architectures. This indicates that TAROT's selected datasets possess strong model-agnostic properties.

#### 4.4. TAROT for Instruction Tuning (Large Language Models)

- **Effectiveness in LLMs:** Even in LLM tasks where target distributions might be simpler, TAROT proved highly effective. It consistently outperformed existing methods (LESS, TSDS, random) and models trained on the full dataset (FLAN V2, COT, DOLLY, OPEN ASSISTANT 1) on MMLU and BBH benchmarks.
- **Subtask Label Agnostic:** Unlike some baselines (e.g., LESS), TAROT does not require subtask labels for target samples, simplifying its application.
- **Extreme Data Efficiency with OTM:** TAROT-OTM was exceptionally efficient, selecting **less than 0.5%** of the candidate data while achieving performance comparable to or even surpassing methods that used 5% of the data. This highlights TAROT's ability to pinpoint critical samples and drastically reduce training costs.
- **Transferability Across LLMs:** Data selected by LLAMA-3.1-8B successfully enhanced the performance of QWEN-2.5-7B, further demonstrating strong transferability across different LLM architectures.

#### 4.5. Computational Complexity

TAROT's primary computational overhead lies in the one-time gradient feature computation, which scales linearly with dataset size, number of checkpoints, and gradient dimension. The data selection process itself, especially with the greedy OT approach, is shown to be fast, outperforming TSDS in wall-clock runtime (Table 8, Appendix G).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of TAROT represents a significant advancement in the field of data-centric AI, particularly for optimizing the training of large and complex deep learning models. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Overcoming Core Limitations of Prior Methods:** TAROT directly addresses the critical shortcomings of previous influence-based greedy data selection heuristics, specifically their failure on multimodal distributions, the bias from dominant features, and the restrictive linear additive assumptions. By leveraging Optimal Transport and Whitened Feature Distance, TAROT provides a more principled and robust approach to understanding and leveraging data relationships.
2. **Enhanced Data Efficiency and Performance:** The most striking impact of TAROT is its consistent ability to achieve superior model performance using significantly smaller, carefully selected subsets of data. This was demonstrated across diverse domains: semantic segmentation, motion prediction, and large language model instruction tuning. In some cases, models trained on TAROT-selected data even surpassed those trained on the full, much larger datasets (e.g., nuScenes leaderboard performance, LLM instruction tuning with <0.5% data).
3. **Reduced Computational Costs and Environmental Impact:** By drastically cutting down the required training data volume, TAROT directly translates to substantial reductions in computational resources (GPU hours) and, consequently, the energy consumption and carbon footprint associated with training large-scale AI models. This is crucial for sustainable AI development.
4. **Broader Applicability and Versatility:** TAROT's effectiveness across highly varied tasks, from pixel-level CV to complex trajectory prediction and text-based LLM fine-tuning, highlights its generalizability. This versatility makes it a valuable tool for a wide range of researchers and practitioners dealing with large, diverse datasets.
5. **Model-Agnostic Data Selection:** The demonstrated transferability of data selected by a smaller model (AutoBots) to a larger one (Wayformer), and between different LLM architectures (LLAMA-3.1-8B to QWEN-2.5-7B), indicates that TAROT selects inherently valuable data rather than data specifically optimized for a single model. This enhances the reusability and robustness of the selected datasets.
6. **Addressing Data Quality and Relevance:** TAROT's distribution-matching objective allows it to implicitly filter out irrelevant or redundant data, focusing on samples most pertinent to the target task. This is particularly valuable when dealing with noisy or heterogeneous candidate data sources.
7. **Future Directions for Data-Centric AI:** The paper itself identifies a limitation: potential overfitting on small or highly specific target datasets. This opens promising avenues for future research, such as incorporating distribution diversification into the selection objective, which could lead to even more generalizable and robust data selection frameworks.

In conclusion, TAROT represents a significant step towards more efficient, effective, and responsible data selection in machine learning. By enabling high performance with smaller, more relevant datasets, it has the potential to democratize access to high-quality model training and accelerate AI development across various complex domains.
