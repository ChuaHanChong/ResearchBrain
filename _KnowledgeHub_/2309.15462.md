# DTC: Deep Tracking Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich's Robotic Systems Lab developed Deep Tracking Control (DTC), a hybrid architecture that combines model-based trajectory optimization with deep reinforcement learning. This system enables quadrupedal robots like ANYmal to achieve precise foot placement (2.3 cm tracking error) and robust recovery skills, successfully navigating challenging and unstructured terrains like slippery surfaces, narrow beams, and large gaps in real-world environments.

## Problem

- Model-based control methods for legged robots, while precise for planning, are often brittle and susceptible to real-world uncertainties like model mismatch, unmodeled contact dynamics, and state estimation errors, leading to failures in unstructured environments.
- Reinforcement learning (RL) policies for locomotion, while robust to disturbances, often struggle with tasks requiring precise foot placement due to sparse reward signals and show limited generalization to terrains not specifically encountered during training.
- There is a need for legged robot controllers that combine both the planning accuracy of model-based approaches and the inherent robustness and adaptability of data-driven learning methods across diverse and challenging real-world terrains.

## Method

- The **Deep Tracking Control (DTC)** architecture employs a ==hierarchical hybrid control== system, integrating a high-level model-based ==Trajectory Optimization (TO)== planner (==TAMOLS==) with a low-level ==reinforcement learning (RL)== policy.
- The TO planner generates dense, optimal ==reference motions== (footholds, base trajectory, contact schedules) that serve as explicit targets for the RL policy, guiding its learning process and providing continuous reward signals.
- A deep neural network policy, trained using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== with an ==asymmetric actor-critic== structure and extensive ==domain randomization==, learns to track these references and produce target joint positions, enabling robust recovery and precise movements.

## Results

- The **Deep Tracking Control (DTC)** framework enabled the ANYmal robot to achieve **2.3 cm** average foothold tracking error on flat ground, demonstrating high precision.
- DTC allowed ANYmal to successfully traverse challenging real-world obstacles such as slippery surfaces, deformable objects, narrow beams (**1.8m**), and consecutive large gaps (**0.6m**), outperforming pure RL baselines.
- The trained policy generalized zero-shot to different model-based planners, allowing ANYmal to climb a **0.48m** box (**50% higher** than a baseline RL method) and recover from severe disturbances like a collapsing floor scenario solely using proprioception.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining model-based planning with deep reinforcement learning effectively leverages the planning foresight and accuracy of trajectory optimization to provide dense, meaningful reward signals for RL, while simultaneously imbuing the robot with robustness and adaptability to handle unmodeled dynamics.
> - Utilizing a model-based planner to generate optimal reference motions and footholds transforms the challenging sparse reward problem of pure reinforcement learning into a more manageable and sample-efficient tracking problem for the RL agent.
> - A low-level tracking policy trained on specific reference signals (like desired footholds and joint positions) can generalize to motions generated by different high-level model-based planners without retraining, indicating a more universal learned skill.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged locomotion, particularly for quadrupedal robots, has been a long-standing challenge in robotics, requiring systems that are both accurate in complex environments and robust to real-world uncertainties. Historically, two dominant paradigms have emerged to address this: model-based control and model-free (reinforcement learning, RL) approaches.

**Model-based control**, epitomized by Trajectory Optimization (TO) and Model Predictive Control (MPC) with Whole-Body Control (WBC), has been extensively researched for over a decade. Its strengths lie in explicit planning, intuitive cost function tuning, and theoretical optimality guarantees derived from a robot's kinematic and dynamic models. Methods like those using single rigid body, inverted pendulum, centroidal, or full-body dynamics have enabled agile motions. However, a critical limitation is their susceptibility to model mismatch (e.g., unknown friction, unmodeled contact dynamics, state estimation errors, vision occlusions) and violation of assumptions, leading to brittle performance in unpredictable real-world scenarios. Sophisticated hand-engineered state machines are often required to manage edge cases.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, on the other hand, has gained prominence in recent years for synthesizing highly robust and adaptive locomotion policies. RL policies, typically represented by deep neural networks, are learned through extensive interaction with a simulated environment, often mapping raw observations to actions directly. This approach bypasses the need for explicit models and hand-engineered state machines, as the policy inherently learns to handle various corner cases by experiencing them during training. However, RL faces its own set of challenges. A significant hurdle is the "sparse reward problem," where meaningful feedback is rare in environments with limited valid interaction points (e.g., gaps, stepping stones). This often leads to poor exploration and difficulties in learning precise foot placement, sometimes requiring specialized policies that overfit to specific scenarios.

The current work, "DTC: Deep Tracking Control," directly addresses the limitations of both paradigms by proposing a **hybrid control architecture**. It positions itself at the intersection of these two fields, aiming to synthesize a controller that combines the predictive capabilities and optimality guarantees of online model-based planning with the inherent robustness and recovery skills attributed to offline reinforcement learning. This aligns with a growing trend in robotics research that seeks to leverage the complementary strengths of different control methodologies, moving beyond a purely model-based or purely data-driven approach. Previous attempts at combining these (e.g., supervised learning to warm-start solvers, RL imitating or correcting TO motions, or model-based methods tracking learned profiles) exist, but this paper specifically focuses on using a model-based planner to provide dense, accurate reference motions for an RL-trained low-level tracking controller, thereby tackling the precision and robustness trade-off in a novel manner for general locomotion tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a hybrid control architecture for legged robots that simultaneously achieves **greater robustness, foot-placement accuracy, and terrain generalization** than existing state-of-the-art model-based or reinforcement learning methods alone.

The motivation stems from the inherent limitations of current control paradigms:
- **Addressing Model-Based Control's Brittleness:** Model-based methods, while accurate in ideal conditions and excellent for planning, are highly sensitive to real-world uncertainties such as model mismatch (e.g., unmodeled friction, dynamic obstacles, deformable ground) and imperfect state estimation. This often results in faulty operation or failure in challenging, unstructured environments.
- **Overcoming Reinforcement Learning's Precision and Generalization Gaps:** Purely data-driven RL approaches often struggle with scenarios requiring precise foot placement due to sparse reward signals in environments like stepping stones or gaps, where valid footholds are rare. Moreover, despite their robustness to unmodeled dynamics, they frequently exhibit limited generalization to terrain types not explicitly seen or learned during training, often requiring specialized policies.
- **Unifying Strengths:** The authors hypothesize that by combining the strengths of both worlds – the predictive capabilities and optimality guarantees of online planning (model-based) with the inherent robustness and learned reflex behaviors of offline learning (RL) – they can achieve superior performance across a wider range of challenging real-world scenarios.

Specifically, the work aims to leverage a model-based planner to generate optimal reference motions and footholds, providing a dense and accurate "expert" signal. A deep neural network policy is then trained in simulation to track these optimized footholds and reference motions. This effectively transforms a hard exploration problem into a more manageable tracking problem for the RL agent. The ultimate goal is to enable legged robots like ANYmal to reliably traverse highly challenging terrains encountered in real-world applications such as search-and-rescue, construction, or maintenance, which demand both accurate foot placement and robust recovery skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces "Deep Tracking Control (DTC)," a hybrid control architecture structured hierarchically.

**Hybrid Architecture Overview:**
DTC integrates a high-level model-based planner with a low-level reinforcement learning policy.
- **High-Level Planner:** A model-based Trajectory Optimization (TO) method, specifically an enhanced version of **TAMOLS (Terrain-Aware Motion Optimization for Legged Systems)**, serves as the high-level planner. TAMOLS optimizes over footholds and base pose simultaneously, enabling operation at kinematic limits. Its key features include CPU parallelization, a Python interface for simulator integration, and independence from contact estimation by assuming measured contact state matches desired. During training, TAMOLS is updated less frequently (mainly at foot touch-down or after disturbances) to reduce computational cost, but during deployment, it runs at its maximum possible rate (e.g., 400 Hz for TAMOLS, 100 Hz for the comparative MPC planner) to account for real-time uncertainties. The planner is provided with a discretized 2.5D elevation map of the environment.
- **Low-Level Controller (RL Policy):** A deep neural network policy, trained using **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, acts as the low-level tracking controller. This policy maps observations to target joint positions, which are then tracked by a PD controller. This is an end-to-end learning approach, directly from reference signals to joint positions, differentiating it from methods that learn corrective torques around an existing Whole-Body Control (WBC) module.

**Training Environment and Paradigm:**
- **Simulation-Based Training:** The policy is trained entirely in simulation, leveraging massive parallelization (4096 robots simultaneously) to collect vast amounts of experience (equivalent to 23 years of optimized trajectories).
- **Reference Motion Guidance:** Unlike pure RL, where exploration is unguided, DTC utilizes the TO solution as a "reference motion" to guide exploration. The TO provides desired foothold positions, joint positions at touchdown, contact schedules, and base trajectory references. This makes the reward signal dense, facilitating learning.
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic:** The training employs an asymmetric actor-critic structure. The policy (actor) observes noisy, realistic measurements (e.g., proprioception, partial height scans), while the value function (critic) receives privileged ground truth information (e.g., optimized base pose, external wrenches, friction coefficients, height map drift from simulator) in addition to policy observations. This helps the critic learn a better value estimate, guiding the actor effectively.
- **Observation Space:**
 - **Policy Observations:** Include proprioceptive data (base twist, gravity vector, joint positions/velocities, previous actions), and critical information from the planner: planar foothold coordinates (xy), desired joint positions at touchdown (computed via analytical Inverse Kinematics (IK) for the swing leg), desired contact state, and time left in the current phase. A novel "height scan" observation samples terrain heights along a line connecting the current foot to its desired foothold, focusing perception on relevant areas and modeling per-foot drift. Notably, the policy *does not* observe the full base pose reference from the planner to reduce sensitivity to mapping errors and promote planner-independence.
 - **Privileged Observations (for Critic):** Include optimized base pose/twist/acceleration, consistency reward, external base wrench, external foot forces, friction coefficients, and height map drift.
- **Reward Functions:** The total reward is a weighted combination designed to encourage:
 - **Tracking:** Soft tracking rewards for base pose, twist, and acceleration (exponential decay of error) and a hard tracking reward for foothold accuracy (logarithmic penalty on error). Foothold rewards are given only upon successful contact to prevent dragging.
 - **Consistency:** A unique reward term that encourages the agent to produce actions consistent with consecutive optimization solutions, preventing "hesitant" behavior often seen in RL.
 - **Regularization:** Penalty terms for foot power (to prevent scuffing/collisions), joint power (to prevent arbitrary motions), action rate, and joint acceleration, ensuring smooth and physically plausible movements for sim-to-real transfer.
- **Domain Randomization:** Extensive randomization is applied during training to enhance sim-to-real transfer and robustness. This includes noise on observations (except privileged), elevation map noise and drift (per-foot), external pushes on the robot, randomized friction coefficients, and external forces on feet. Noise is introduced to desired joint positions proportional to height drift to prevent the policy from implicitly learning to remove drift.
- **Terrain Curriculum:** A curriculum learning approach is used, where robots start on easier terrain patches and progressively face harder ones (12 terrain types, 10 difficulty levels, 1200 unique patches, summing to 76800 m²). This ensures comprehensive skill acquisition for diverse locomotion behaviors.
- **Deployment:** The trained policy is deployed zero-shot at 50 Hz on ANYmal robots, querying the latest solution from the separate, faster-running motion optimizer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors present extensive real-world and simulation experiments comparing DTC against state-of-the-art model-based and RL baselines.

**Evaluation of Robustness:**
- **Moving Plane (Collapsing Floor):** ANYmal successfully balanced on a pitching cover plate, demonstrating recovery skills solely from proprioception when visual data was inconsistent, replicating a "collapsing floor" scenario. Repeated three times successfully.
- **Slippery, Rolling, Deformable Objects:** DTC showcased superior recovery on an obstacle parkour with a slopped box, wet whiteboard, soft foam box, and rolling cart. Despite not being trained on movable/deforming obstacles, the robot recovered in all five trials without falls.
- **Walking Blind Upstairs:** The policy, trained with perceptive feedback, generalized to completely blind locomotion up and down a two-tread stair set. Learned swing reflexes enabled success even when a stair tread was higher than the foot's swing motion, clearing all trials. This matched the robustness of a more complex teacher/student RL baseline trained for similar scenarios.
- **Collapsing Floor vs. Model-Based:** In simulation, DTC with the TAMOLS planner successfully navigated an invisible gap where baseline-to-1 (pure TO) failed due to model assumption violations and ineffective heuristic recovery. DTC's learned recovery skills, robust to map drift, allowed it to traverse the trap. Similar performance was observed on soft materials.

**Evaluation of Accuracy:**
- **Precise Turn-in-Place:** ANYmal performed a complex turn-in-place maneuver on a small elevated table (0.94 × 0.44 m²) with high precision before descending, showcasing accurate foothold tracking in a constrained environment.
- **Foothold Tracking Error:** On flat ground, DTC achieved a very high precision of **2.3 cm average tracking error** with a standard deviation of 0.48 cm across various heading velocities.

**Deployment with Different Model-Based Planners (Generalization):**
- **Higher Obstacle Climbing:** DTC demonstrated zero-shot generalization by successfully deploying with baseline-to-2 (a more complex MPC planner) despite being trained only with TAMOLS. This allowed ANYmal to climb a 0.48 m box (50% higher than baseline-rl-1, 380% higher than baseline-rl-2) in five successful trials. The average foothold tracking error with baseline-to-2 was 0.03 m, only slightly higher than with TAMOLS, attributed to the similarity of foothold patterns despite different base pose trajectories. This highlights the policy's independence from specific planner details when crucial observations are limited to desired footholds and joint positions.

**Benchmark Against Model-Based Control:**
- **Obstacle Parkour (Simulation):** DTC successfully traversed a complex obstacle parkour in simulation at 1 m/s, 20% faster than baseline-to-1, demonstrating that the combined approach can exceed the agility of pure TO in certain conditions.

**Benchmark Against RL Control:**
- **Gap Crossing:** Baseline-rl-1 failed to cross a small 0.1 m gap, while DTC achieved a 100% success rate over ten repetitions. Furthermore, DTC successfully crossed four consecutive 0.6 m wide gaps in the real world (100% success over four repetitions), a task only previously achieved by specialized, pre-mapped RL policies.
- **Narrow Beam Traversal:** Baseline-rl-1 struggled to walk along a 1.0 m long beam, whereas DTC successfully traversed a significantly longer 1.8 m beam (100% success over four repetitions).
- **Stepping Stones:** DTC consistently traversed fields of flat, varying height, and even loose/movable stepping stones in the real world (e.g., 10/10 successes on flat stones, 4/4 on varying height, high success on loose stones despite one miss-step). This contrasts with baseline-rl-1's inability to cross similar terrains, demonstrating DTC's superior generalization and foot placement accuracy on sparse-contact terrains.

**Simulation-Based Ablation Study:**
- **Optimizer Update Rate:** Increasing the optimizer update rate from 1 Hz to 50 Hz during deployment (after training) significantly improved performance: failure rates dropped by 7.11%, and success rates increased by 4.25%, demonstrating the benefit of real-time planning updates.
- **Comparison to RL Baselines on All Terrains:** DTC showed substantially higher success rates across 120 diverse terrains compared to baseline-rl-2. While baseline-rl-3 (baseline-rl-2 retrained in DTC's environment) improved, DTC still outperformed it, especially on "sparse-structured" terrains (stepping stones, beams, gaps, pallets), suggesting the effectiveness of the hybrid approach itself beyond just the training environment.
- **Effect of Elevation Map Drift:** DTC's tracking error linearly increased with map drift on both flat and rough ground, but its success rate remained constant for drifts up to 0.1 m on rough terrains and was unaffected on flat ground, confirming its robustness to perception errors.
- **Importance of Desired Joint Positions (from IK):** Providing desired joint positions from IK for the upcoming touchdown greatly improved training convergence time and foothold tracking performance (higher average terrain level and foothold reward), simplifying the learning process by encoding foothold location in joint space.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Deep Tracking Control" (DTC) framework represents a significant step forward in legged locomotion control, offering a compelling solution to the long-standing challenges of achieving both accuracy and robustness in complex, real-world environments.

**Key Significance:**
- **Bridging the Gap:** The most significant contribution is the successful marriage of model-based planning (Trajectory Optimization) with model-free learning (Reinforcement Learning). This hybrid approach effectively leverages the planning foresight and accuracy of TO to provide dense, meaningful reward signals for RL, while simultaneously imbuing the robot with the inherent robustness, reflex behaviors, and adaptability characteristic of RL policies to handle unmodeled dynamics and disturbances.
- **Enhanced Real-World Performance:** The rigorous evaluation, primarily through extensive real-world experiments on the ANYmal robot, demonstrates superior performance compared to leading pure model-based or pure RL controllers. DTC's ability to navigate slippery surfaces, deformable objects, narrow beams, large gaps, and varying-height stepping stones, even blindly in some cases, highlights its practical utility in unstructured and uncertain environments.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** The work showcases impressive generalization:
 - **Across TO methods:** The trained policy can track motions generated by different TO planners (TAMOLS vs. MPC from baseline-to-2) without re-training, suggesting a more universal low-level tracking skill.
 - **To unseen challenges:** The policy generalizes to blind locomotion despite being trained with visual perception, and to movable/deformable obstacles not explicitly included in training.
 - **Across terrain types:** It creates a "single unifying locomotion policy" that effectively tackles both dense and sparse-structured terrains, which has been a major challenge for purely RL-based methods.
- **Practical Implications for Robotics Development:** By improving both accuracy and robustness, DTC reduces the gap between simulation and reality and the need for extensive hand-engineering or specialized policies. This could accelerate the deployment of autonomous legged robots in domains currently limited by control fragility.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Robotics Applications:** This work has direct and substantial impact on fields requiring highly capable mobile robots:
 - **Search and Rescue:** Robots can more reliably traverse debris, unstable ground, and navigate confined spaces to locate survivors.
 - **Construction and Maintenance:** Enables robots to inspect sites, carry materials, and perform tasks in hazardous, unpredictable construction environments.
 - **Logistics and Inspection:** Improved navigation over varied indoor/outdoor terrains for automated delivery or infrastructure inspection.
 - **Exploration:** Allows robots to explore unknown and challenging environments with greater confidence and autonomy.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper opens several promising avenues for future research, including:
 - **Dynamic Stride Duration:** Allowing the policy to suggest arbitrary contact schedules to the motion optimizer, potentially increasing survival rate and technical skills.
 - **Policy-Modified Cost Functions:** Enabling the policy to directly modify the planner's cost function for more diversified motions.
 - **Analytical IK Integration:** Exploiting prior knowledge of analytical inverse kinematics within network structures to improve sample efficiency and generalization across platforms.
 - **Offline Trajectory Sampling:** Leveraging benefits of sampling trajectories from offline buffers to reduce training time and use more accurate TO methods or even real expert data.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
While highly impactful, the authors acknowledge that the proposed architecture is not a "universal recipe" for optimal integration of TO and RL for generic problems. They also observed that the proposed training environment was not more sample-efficient than comparable unifying RL approaches, suggesting that foothold accuracy is inherently complex to learn. Despite these, the demonstrated capabilities and the clear roadmap for future improvements solidify DTC's role as a foundational contribution to robust and agile legged locomotion.
