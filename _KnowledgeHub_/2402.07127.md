# Learning by Watching: A Review of Video-based Learning Approaches for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of robot manipulation learning from passive human video. It reviews foundations (video representation learning, affordance and hand-object understanding, 3D hand/body modeling, large-scale datasets and VLA models), then organizes methods into five families: foundational perception and translation, reinforcement learning, imitation learning, hybrid RL-IL, and multi-modal foundation models, comparing them in per-family tables and mapping challenges, open-source tooling and benchmark gaps.

## Problem

- Curated robot datasets are collected in controlled settings, so they stay costly, biased and narrow, and policies trained on them generalize poorly to real environments.
- Deep RL and behavioral-cloning methods need reward engineering or action labels, and degrade under distribution shift when demonstrations are diverse and uncurated.
- Prior surveys treat learning from demonstration, reinforcement learning or foundation models separately; none cover the intersection of in-the-wild video and manipulation skill learning.

## Method

- A five-family taxonomy of video-based approaches: foundational perception and domain translation, ==reinforcement learning==, ==imitation learning==, ==hybrid RL-IL==, and ==multi-modal transformer== or ==vision-language-action models==.
- A foundations review of ==representation learning== (time-contrastive, masked pretraining, ==R3M==), affordance and hand-object interaction, 3D hand/body models such as ==MANO== and ==FrankMocap==, plus dataset and VLA catalogs.
- Per-family comparison tables rating task performance, sample efficiency, compute cost and embodiment handling, a macro-level trade-off table, open-source resource tables, and a proposed ==Causal-CALVIN== or ==Interventional-RLBench== protocol.

## Results

- Compiled evidence shows video pretraining pays: GR-1 reports **94.9%** success on CALVIN while fine-tuning on **10%** robot data, and GR-2 pretrains on **38 million** clips.
- Human-video-only pipelines stay competitive: general-flow prediction reaches **81%** across **18** real tasks, BC-Z gets **44%** zero-shot on **24** unseen tasks, R3M adds **~20%** over MoCo and CLIP.
- Scale alone is not decisive: **7B** OpenVLA beats **55B** RT-2-X using **~1M** Open X-Embodiment trajectories, while the benchmark review rates RLBench, CALVIN and RoboTube low for causal evaluation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Passive human video is the cheapest scaling axis for manipulation data, but only pays off once a representation absorbs the human-robot embodiment gap.
> - Capability and accessibility are diverging: the strongest multi-modal policies demand compute beyond most labs, so match method to deployment constraints, not leaderboards.
> - High benchmark success can be pure correlation; only perturbed physics or rewired mechanisms reveal whether a policy learned causes rather than statistics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics, particularly robot manipulation, has historically lagged behind other AI domains like computer vision (CV) and natural language processing (NLP) due to a critical scarcity of diverse, high-quality datasets. Traditional robot learning methods, including classical planning, deep reinforcement learning (RL), and behavioral cloning (BC), often struggle with generalizability and real-world transfer when data is limited or heavily curated. Existing robotics datasets, while beneficial, are typically collected in controlled environments, limiting their representativeness of the complex, unstructured "in-the-wild" scenarios robots encounter in reality. This leads to issues such as reduced generalizability, dataset biases, high data collection costs, and ethical concerns regarding human-robot interaction in controlled settings.

This paper addresses this fundamental challenge by positioning "video-based learning" as a paradigm-shifting solution. Inspired by the tremendous success of computer vision models trained on vast, uncurated "in-the-wild" video datasets (e.g., YouTube), the authors propose that leveraging abundant passive videos can provide scalable supervision, reduce dataset bias, and foster greater generalization for robotic manipulation. The work directly contributes to the burgeoning interdisciplinary field where computer vision, natural language processing, and robot learning converge.

Crucially, this survey distinguishes itself by being "the first of its kind to explore the landscape of learning robot manipulation skills specifically from video data." While other surveys have covered broader fields like robot manipulation or learning from demonstration, this paper's unique focus on video as the primary data modality fills a significant gap in the literature. It systematically reviews and compares methodologies across imitation and reinforcement learning that specifically utilize video demonstrations, including recent advancements in vision-language models (VLMs), foundation models, and large-scale data resources. By synthesizing the state-of-the-art and identifying open challenges, the review provides essential context for researchers navigating this rapidly evolving subfield. It highlights the transition from controlled, expert-driven data collection to more scalable, observation-based learning, moving the field closer to developing generalist robots capable of learning a wide array of skills from human demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this comprehensive review stems from the recognized limitations within current robot learning paradigms, chiefly the scarcity of diverse and unbiased datasets for robot manipulation. The authors highlight how this data bottleneck hinders progress in generalizability and real-world applicability, contrasting it sharply with the data-rich environments of computer vision and natural language processing. The success of self-supervised learning on large-scale "in-the-wild" video datasets in computer vision serves as a compelling impetus for translating this paradigm to robotics. The core idea is that if robots can "learn by watching" human activities from readily available online videos, they can overcome many of these data-related challenges.

The paper articulates three key objectives, which also serve as its main contributions:

1. **Detailed Review and Analysis of Current Approaches:** To systematically dissect and evaluate the capabilities of existing methodologies in video-based robot manipulation across various robotic tasks. This involves categorizing and scrutinizing different techniques to understand their strengths and weaknesses.
2. **Overview of Open-Source Resources and Tools:** To provide researchers with a practical guide to available open-source frameworks, tools, and datasets that can facilitate the implementation and advancement of video-based robot manipulation learning. This aims to lower the barrier to entry and encourage reproducible research.
3. **Discussion of Current Challenges and Future Directions:** To identify the persistent obstacles facing the field and to propose promising avenues for future research. This includes outlining theoretical and practical bottlenecks that need to be addressed to realize the full potential of learning from videos.

The scope of the survey is intentionally narrow yet deep, focusing "solely on vision-based manipulation." While broader topics like navigation, locomotion, or non-visual approaches are mentioned for perspective, the central aim is to thoroughly discuss the techniques, advantages, and challenges associated with acquiring robust manipulation skills specifically from video data. This focused approach ensures a comprehensive and insightful analysis of this particular learning paradigm.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a structured and systematic approach to survey the extensive literature on video-based learning for robot manipulation. The report is organized into distinct sections that progressively build from foundational concepts to advanced methodologies, culminating in a critical analysis of current challenges and future directions.

The methodology begins by establishing the **Foundations of Learning from Videos** (Section II). This foundational segment delves into the essential components required for robots to learn from video data. It covers:
- **Representation Learning:** Discussing techniques for extracting meaningful visual features from videos, including video-centric (e.g., TCN, DPC) and general-purpose (e.g., MAE, R3M) approaches.
- **Object Affordance and Human-Object Interaction:** Exploring how robots understand the functional properties of objects by observing human interactions.
- **Human Action and Activity Recognition:** Reviewing methods for identifying and interpreting human actions in videos, crucial for imitation.
- **3D Hand Modeling:** Examining techniques for bridging the embodiment gap by representing human hands in 2D/3D to enable robot imitation.
- **Datasets:** Providing a comprehensive overview of influential datasets, categorized into Large-Scale Video, Egocentric (First-Person) Video, Task-Specific/Multi-Modal, and Embodied AI/Interactive datasets.
- **Large-Scale Robotic Resources:** Detailing the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models like RT-1, RT-2, and more recent diffusion/flow-matching based architectures (e.g., OpenVLA, CogACT, GR00T N1), which leverage vast amounts of multimodal data.

Following this foundational overview, Section III, **Approaches to Learning from Videos**, categorizes the core methodologies into five main groups:
1. **Foundational Perception and Representation Methods:** Encompassing feature extraction (CNN-based, pose/keypoint detection) and domain bridging techniques (image and context translation) to address the human-robot domain gap.
2. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Approaches:** Discussing how RL can be integrated with visual features from videos, including visual RL with feature extraction and structured/hierarchical RL.
3. **Imitation Learning (IL) Approaches:** Covering behavioral cloning (BC), meta-imitation/few-shot IL, and cross-domain/human-to-robot transfer methods.
4. **Hybrid Approaches:** Reviewing models that combine RL, IL, and other techniques to overcome individual limitations.
5. **Multi-Modal Approaches:** Exploring methods that integrate vision with other modalities, primarily natural language, including vision-language grounding, vision-action alignment, and large-scale multi-modal transformers/foundation models.

For each approach, the authors review notable literature, highlighting innovations, benefits, and interconnections, often utilizing tables for concise comparisons of different methods within a category (e.g., Table 2 for representation learning, Table 5 for feature extraction).

A **Comparative Analysis** (Section IV) provides a macro-level synthesis, comparing the primary methodological subgroups based on their advantages, disadvantages, key bottlenecks, and representative applications using a comprehensive table (Table 12). This section critically assesses the trade-offs between capability and practicality across different approaches.

The paper then offers an **Overview of Open-Source Tools** (Section V), listing specific implementations, frameworks, libraries, and simulators (Tables 13-18) that are crucial for researchers.

Finally, the authors dedicate significant attention to **Challenges** (Section VI) and a **Future Outlook** (Section VII). The challenges are categorized into data availability, domain shift, computational cost, sample efficiency, evaluation, and causal reasoning. The future outlook identifies promising research avenues, including active learning for data annotation, advanced domain adaptation, emerging architectures like diffusion models and world models, and critically, the development of robust evaluation metrics and benchmarks that specifically assess *causal understanding*. This emphasis on causality, including a detailed discussion of causal discovery methods (observational and interventional) and causal representation learning, marks a unique and forward-looking aspect of this survey.

Overall, the methodology is comprehensive, systematic, and analytical, providing a clear roadmap through the complex landscape of video-based robot manipulation learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core findings and results presented in this review emanate from a comprehensive synthesis of the current literature, offering both a broad overview and granular insights into video-based robot manipulation.

**1. Evolution of Methodologies:** The survey effectively maps the progression of approaches, starting from foundational perception techniques to highly complex multi-modal foundation models. Initially, methods focused on basic **feature extraction** (CNNs, pose/keypoint detection) and **domain bridging** (image/context translation) to interpret visual data and reduce the human-robot embodiment gap. These laid the groundwork for integrating more sophisticated learning paradigms.

**2. Strengths and Weaknesses of Core Learning Paradigms:**
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches** (Section III-B) are powerful for long-horizon and complex tasks, with hierarchical RL showing promise for compositional learning. However, they are hampered by high computational costs, sensitivity to reward engineering, and the need for extensive interaction data.
- **Imitation Learning (IL) approaches** (Section III-C), particularly behavioral cloning and its meta-learning variants, excel in sample efficiency and rapid skill acquisition from demonstrations. Yet, they face persistent challenges with distribution mismatch, embodiment differences, and generalizing beyond the training data.
- **Hybrid approaches** (Section III-D) aim to combine the strengths of RL and IL, often incorporating elements like residual learning or domain adaptation. While robust to demonstration noise and effective in cross-domain transfer, they introduce design complexity and often lack broad real-world validation.
- **Multi-Modal approaches** (Section III-E) represent the forefront of the field, integrating vision, language, and sometimes other modalities. They enable natural language task specification, leverage internet-scale pretraining, and offer strong potential for zero-shot generalization and embodied reasoning. However, their primary drawbacks are extreme computational demands, model complexity, and reliance on vast, richly annotated multimodal datasets.

**3. Critical Role of Foundations:** The review highlights that advances in core areas are indispensable. **Representation learning** (Section II-A) techniques like R3M provide universal visual features that are crucial for efficient imitation. Progress in **object affordance understanding** (Section II-B) and **3D hand/body modeling** (Section II-D) directly improves a robot's ability to interpret human actions and map them to its own kinematics. The availability of **large-scale datasets** (Section II-E) such as Ego-4D, Open X-Embodiment, and narrated web videos (HowTo100M) is foundational, akin to ImageNet's impact on CV, enabling the training of more generalist robot policies.

**4. Emergence of Large-Scale VLA Models:** A significant finding is the rapid evolution of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (Section II-F, III-E.3). Models like RT-1, RT-2, and more recent architectures (e.g., OpenVLA, GR00T N1, GR-2) are leveraging web-scale vision-language pretraining to achieve unprecedented dexterity and generalization. These models are shifting from simple action discretization to more expressive action modeling using techniques like diffusion and flow-matching, and employing multi-component architectures for high-frequency control and robust reasoning.

**5. Persistent Challenges:** Despite impressive advancements, the survey identifies several overarching challenges (Section VI) that are pervasive across all methodological categories:
- **Data Availability and Annotation:** The continuous need for diverse, unbiased, and often expert-labeled data remains a bottleneck.
- **Domain Shift and Embodiment Gap:** The fundamental difficulty in transferring skills directly from human demonstrations to robots due to inherent differences in appearance, dynamics, and action spaces.
- **Computational Cost:** The increasing resource demands of large-scale models limit their real-world deployability on resource-constrained platforms.
- **Sample Efficiency:** Many models still require massive amounts of data or interaction steps, making them impractical for real-world scenarios.
- **Evaluation and Benchmarking:** A critical lack of standardized metrics and benchmarks hinders fair comparison and reproducible progress.
- **Causal Reasoning and Policy Abstraction:** A major limitation is the models' current inability to move beyond statistical correlations to understand true cause-and-effect relationships, which is crucial for robust generalization and planning in novel situations.

**6. Future Outlook on Causal Reasoning and Benchmarking:** A particularly insightful finding, emphasized in the future outlook (Section VII), is the urgent need to integrate **causal reasoning** into robot learning. The authors argue that current models, largely reliant on correlations, are brittle when faced with distribution shifts. They propose that methods for causal discovery from visual data (observational and interventional) and causal representation learning are essential for building robots that can genuinely generalize, adapt, and perform counterfactual reasoning. This is tied directly to the need for new **robust evaluation metrics and benchmarks** (e.g., "Causal-CALVIN" or "Interventional RLBench") that can specifically assess an agent's causal understanding, moving beyond simple task success rates to a more meaningful assessment of physical intelligence.

In essence, the main findings highlight a dynamic field making significant strides by leveraging passive video data, but also one grappling with foundational issues of generalization, efficiency, and intelligence that require a shift towards deeper understanding beyond mere pattern recognition.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This detailed review, "Learning by Watching: A Review of Video-based Learning Approaches for Robot Manipulation," holds significant implications for the robotics community and beyond, with potential impacts across research, development, and practical applications.

**1. Guiding Research and Future Directions:** As the first comprehensive survey specifically dedicated to learning robot manipulation from video data, this paper serves as an invaluable resource for researchers. It systematically maps the complex landscape, categorizing diverse approaches, highlighting their interconnections, and articulating a clear taxonomy. By doing so, it provides a roadmap for both seasoned and nascent researchers, helping them to navigate the vast literature, identify promising avenues, and avoid redundant efforts. The in-depth discussion of current challenges, particularly the persistent problems of domain shift, data scarcity, and the lack of causal reasoning, is crucial for directing future research towards fundamental breakthroughs rather than incremental improvements.

**2. Accelerating Generalist Robot Development:** The central premise of "learning by watching" is to overcome the data bottleneck that has historically hindered robot learning. By showcasing how abundant "in-the-wild" human videos can provide scalable, unbiased supervision, the review champions a paradigm that could lead to more generalist robots. These robots would be capable of acquiring a broad repertoire of manipulation skills without the costly and labor-intensive process of collecting tailored robot demonstrations. This shift is critical for moving robotics out of controlled lab environments and into real-world, unstructured settings like homes and workplaces.

**3. Fostering Interdisciplinary Collaboration:** The review explicitly positions video-based robot learning at the intersection of computer vision (CV), natural language processing (NLP), and robot learning. By outlining the foundational contributions from CV (e.g., representation learning, 3D modeling) and NLP (e.g., vision-language models), it underscores the necessity of interdisciplinary approaches. This perspective will encourage researchers from these distinct fields to collaborate, cross-pollinate ideas, and jointly tackle complex problems, thereby accelerating innovation.

**4. Promoting Reproducible Research and Accessibility:** The inclusion of a dedicated section on open-source tools, frameworks, datasets, and simulators is highly impactful. By cataloging these resources, the authors lower the barrier to entry for researchers, enabling easier replication of experiments and fostering a more collaborative and transparent research ecosystem. This practical guidance can significantly accelerate the pace of development by providing readily available tools for implementing and testing new ideas.

**5. Influencing Evaluation and Benchmarking Paradigms:** Perhaps one of the most significant potential impacts lies in the emphatic call for a re-evaluation of current benchmarking protocols, particularly the push for metrics that assess **causal understanding**. The authors' critique that existing benchmarks (like CALVIN and RLBench) primarily reward correlational learning, leading to brittle policies, is profound. Their proposal for "Causal-CALVIN" or "Interventional RLBench" suggests a paradigm shift in how robot intelligence is measured. If adopted, such benchmarks would compel researchers to develop models that learn the underlying cause-and-effect relationships in the world, leading to more robust, adaptive, and genuinely intelligent robotic systems capable of reasoning about interventions and novel situations.

In conclusion, this review is more than a summary of existing work; it is a strategic document that maps a rapidly evolving landscape, identifies critical challenges, and articulates a compelling vision for the future of robot manipulation. Its comprehensive nature, coupled with its forward-looking perspective on causal reasoning and evaluation, positions it to significantly influence the trajectory of research and development in embodied AI, ultimately contributing to the creation of more capable and intelligent robots that can seamlessly integrate into human environments.
