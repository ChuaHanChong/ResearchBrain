# An Unbiased Look at Datasets for Visuo-Motor Pre-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> A dataset-centric study of visuo-motor pre-training: 15 masked-autoencoder ViT representations are trained on 1M-image samples from ImageNet, Ego4D, Kinetics, 100 Days of Hands and RoboNet, then fine-tuned by behavior cloning on 13 simulated and 3 real Franka tasks. Curated internet image datasets beat egocentric and robot data, and a balanced data soup plus extra hands data reaches 60% real success.

## Problem

- Prior visuo-motor pre-training work fixates on algorithms and architectures while nearly all methods train on the same egocentric dataset, leaving dataset choice empirically untested.
- Earlier representation papers compared only against supervised ImageNet features, never an apples-to-apples self-supervised ImageNet baseline trained with identical algorithm, architecture and hyperparameters.
- The field evaluates pre-trained representations mostly in simulation benchmarks, assuming those rankings predict real robot performance, an assumption never rigorously checked on hardware.

## Method

- Controlled pre-training protocol: an identical ==Masked Auto-Encoder ViT== recipe and hyperparameters applied to 1M frames sampled from each of five datasets, isolating dataset effect from algorithm.
- Fine-tuning stack: ==behavior cloning== of a two-layer Gaussian-Mixture policy head atop the encoder, trained end-to-end with Adam, adding dropout regularization and random crop/blur image augmentation.
- Data-mixing schemes: **Soup-1M** balances 200K frames from each dataset, **Soup-2M** naively scales it, and Soup-1M plus 1M frames of one dataset measures each source's marginal value.

## Results

- Real-robot success averaged **41%** for ImageNet, **44%** Kinetics and **38%** hands data versus **28%** Ego4D and **30%** RoboNet; baselines VC-1 and MVP reached **33%** and **25%**.
- Soup-2M matched Soup-1M at **41%** real success, whereas Soup-1M plus 1M hands frames reached **60%**, a **20-point** gain and roughly **30%** over the strongest baseline.
- Simulation scores correlated weakly with real performance (R-squared **32%**, **34%** on the closest task pair); dropout hurt sim but lifted adversarial block-stacking success from **10%** to **24%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pre-training image distribution matters more than dataset size or domain similarity; curated, diverse internet images can beat data that superficially resembles the robot's own observations.
> - Simulation benchmarks are unreliable proxies for real manipulation: design choices such as dropout that help on hardware can look harmful under simulated evaluation.
> - When mixing heterogeneous sources, keep them balanced and add data selectively; naive scaling gave zero real-world gain while one well-chosen source gave large gains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics, particularly in manipulation tasks within unstructured environments, faces a challenge in developing robust visual perception. Robots must identify objects, understand their properties, and adapt to diverse scenarios (e.g., varying objects, lighting, environments) using visual input. Hand-engineering vision pipelines for such complexity is largely unfeasible. The computer vision and representation learning communities have addressed similar challenges by learning end-to-end neural representations from data, a paradigm the robotics community is adopting for policy learning.

However, collecting large-scale, diverse, and task-specific robotic data (i.e., trajectories for imitation or reinforcement learning) is often expensive and challenging on real hardware. Furthermore, the homogeneity of data collected in single lab settings limits generalization. To overcome these limitations, the prevailing approach in robot learning has shifted towards pre-training visual representations on large-scale, unlabeled, out-of-domain vision datasets (e.g., videos of egocentric human interactions like Ego4D) using self-supervised learning algorithms such as Masked Auto-Encoders (MAEs) or contrastive learning. These pre-trained representations aim to decouple policy learning from perception, theoretically enabling the learning of behaviors with less task-specific robotic data.

Prior work in this area, exemplified by studies like R3M and MVP, has primarily focused on developing novel neural architectures and algorithms for learning these visual priors. These studies often utilize the same or similar large-scale datasets, notably Ego4D, based on intuitive reasoning regarding its egocentric viewpoint, focus on human-object interaction, and sheer volume of data. However, these works have often compared their self-supervised representations with supervised ImageNet representations, rather than conducting "apples-to-apples" comparisons with self-supervised representations derived from other diverse datasets.

This paper addresses a critical gap in this landscape by shifting the research focus from refining pre-training *algorithms* to conducting an empirical, *dataset-centric analysis*. It questions the underlying assumptions about which types of pre-training data are most beneficial for robotics, especially in light of the scarcity and homogeneity of robotics-specific datasets. By systematically comparing representations learned from various common vision and robotics datasets, the work aims to provide empirical evidence regarding the optimal choice and combination of pre-training data, thereby informing future directions in data-driven robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically investigate the impact of pre-training dataset choice on the efficacy of visuo-motor representation learning for robotic manipulation tasks. The study aims to provide an unbiased analysis to challenge or validate common intuitions and practices within the field.

The motivation behind this investigation stems from several factors:

- **Scarcity and Homogeneity of Robotics Data:** Learning visual representations alongside action policies for robots often requires substantial amounts of data that are expensive and difficult to collect in real-world settings. When collected, this data tends to be homogenous, hindering a robot's ability to generalize to novel scenarios. This motivates the use of pre-training on readily available, large-scale, out-of-domain vision datasets.
- **Decoupling Perception and Policy Learning:** Self-supervised pre-training on diverse vision datasets can learn useful general priors, allowing policy learning to proceed with significantly less robot-specific data. This approach is central to recent advancements in robot manipulation.
- **Unexamined Dataset Biases:** A common trend in the field is to utilize datasets like Ego4D for pre-training, based on intuitive factors such as its first-person camera views, focus on human-object interactions, and large data volume. However, the empirical validity of these intuitive biases, especially in a direct comparison with other dataset types, has not been rigorously established. Prior benchmarks, while comparing self-supervised approaches, often lack a direct comparison of different *self-supervised* pre-training *datasets*.
- **The "What Data?" Question:** The paper explicitly poses the question: "What Data Should Robots Be Pre-Trained On?" This highlights the researchers' motivation to move beyond algorithmic advancements and investigate the fundamental role of the input data itself in shaping the learned representations. The implicit hypothesis is that the characteristics and distribution of the pre-training data might be as, or more, critical than the specific pre-training algorithm.
- **Reliability of Simulation Benchmarks:** The motivation also extends to assessing the transferability of insights gained from simulated environments to real-world robot performance. Many existing evaluations primarily occur in simulation, and the researchers aim to determine if these simulated trends reliably predict success on physical robots.
- **Identifying Practical Improvements:** Through this systematic analysis, the study seeks to identify practical strategies for improving visuo-motor representation learning, such as optimal dataset combinations or simple implementation details, to achieve higher performance on real-world robotic tasks.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for a foundational understanding of data's role in robot learning, moving beyond assumptions to empirically guide the selection and composition of pre-training datasets for robust and generalizable robotic policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic, two-step experimental methodology to compare different pre-training datasets for visuo-motor representation learning in robotics.

**A. Visual Pre-Training:**

1. **Representation Learning Algorithm:** The study exclusively utilizes Masked Auto-Encoders (MAEs) as the self-supervised learning algorithm for pre-training. MAEs were selected due to their scalability, ability to operate on heterogeneous data, and demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across various vision and robotics tasks.
2. **Network Architecture:** The MAE encoder is a Vision Transformer (ViT) network, producing an embedding vector of R^768 for an input image. During pre-training, the encoder processes only 25% of randomly sampled image patches, while a separate decoder network attempts to reconstruct the full image. Both the encoder and decoder are trained end-to-end by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) reconstruction objective. This process encourages the encoder to learn spatial reasoning and efficient image descriptors. Hyperparameters for MAE training and the ViT architecture are directly adopted from the original MAE work and prior robotics studies to ensure comparability.
3. **Pre-training Datasets:** The investigation focuses on five widely used datasets, representing different domains and characteristics. For initial "apples-to-apples" comparisons, 1 million (1M) frames were randomly sampled from each dataset:
 - **ImageNet-1K:** A classical computer vision dataset known for its curated internet images, 1000 balanced classes, and focus on centered, single-object, high-quality images.
 - **Ego4D:** A modern, large-scale (3.6K hours) egocentric video dataset showing humans performing daily tasks. Frames were sub-sampled at 3 FPS to obtain the 1M images.
 - **100 Days of Hands (DoH):** Curated YouTube videos primarily featuring human hands manipulating household objects, with balanced action classes and distinct video content. Frames were sub-sampled at 3 FPS.
 - **Kinetics(-700):** Similar to DoH, curated from YouTube, but with a broader distribution of human actions involving objects and other humans. Frames were sub-sampled at 3 FPS.
 - **RoboNet:** Contains over 13M image observations of robots interacting randomly with objects in a bin. 1M image observations were sampled.
4. **Dataset Scaling and Combination Strategies:**
 - **"Soup-1M":** A composite dataset created by proportionally mixing images, containing 200K images randomly sampled from each of the five core datasets, totaling 1M images.
 - **"Soup-2M":** A scaled version of Soup-1M, containing 400K images from each of the five datasets, totaling 2M images, to investigate naive data scaling.
 - **"Soup-1M + 1M X":** Combinations where the Soup-1M dataset is augmented with an additional 1M frames from a specific individual dataset (e.g., ImageNet, Ego4D, Kinetics, DoH, or RoboNet). This approach explores the marginal value of adding more data from specific sources while shifting the overall distribution.
 - **Diversity Ablation (Appendix E):** An additional experiment compared two 500K ImageNet subsets: one with 500 classes (1000 images/class) and another with 1000 classes (500 images/class) to probe the effect of dataset diversity while keeping total image count constant.
5. **Replication with SimCLR (Appendix D):** To test the generality of the findings beyond MAE and ViT, a subset of real-world evaluations was repeated using the SimCLR contrastive learning algorithm and a ResNet-18 architecture on 1M images from each target dataset.

**B. Fine-Tuning with Behavior Cloning (BC):**

1. **Policy Learning Framework:** The pre-trained visual representations are fine-tuned for downstream robotic manipulation tasks using Learning from Demonstration (LfD) via Behavior Cloning (BC). The goal is to learn a policy (π) that maps observations (o_t) to an optimal action distribution (a_t).
2. **Observation and Action Space:** Observations (o_t) consist of the current image (i_t) and robot joint information (j_t). Actions (a_t) are Cartesian velocity commands sent to the robot controller.
3. **Policy Architecture:** The policy (π) is parameterized by a 2-layer MLP network (p) built on top of the pre-trained encoder (E). The input image is first encoded by E(i_t), then concatenated with j_t, and finally fed into the MLP p.
4. **Output Distribution:** The policy network p predicts the parameters (mean, mixing parameters, standard deviation) of a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) distribution. The number of modes (m) was set to 5 for RoboSuite and real-world tasks, and 1 (standard Gaussian) for Franka Kitchen and MetaWorld for consistency with prior work.
5. **Training:** The entire policy network (p and E) is fine-tuned end-to-end using the Adam optimizer for 50,000 iterations, minimizing the negative log-probability of expert actions.
6. **Key Modifications:** Two important modifications were introduced based on empirical testing:
 - **Dropout:** Applied to the policy network (p) with a probability of 0.2 after ReLU activations, primarily to improve robustness in the real world.
 - **Data Augmentation:** Random crops and random blur were applied to the input image (i_t) before passing it to the encoder, following recommendations for best practices.

**C. Evaluation Procedure:**

1. **Consistent Evaluation:** Each policy's final checkpoint is evaluated on a fixed number of test rollouts (N) per task, using novel initializations (e.g., test objects, new initial positions). All evaluation hyperparameters are kept fixed within a task to ensure maximally fair comparison.
2. **Simulation Tasks:** Evaluations were conducted on 13 tasks across three MuJoCo environments frequently used by the robot learning community:
 - Meta-World (5 tasks)
 - RoboSuite (3 tasks)
 - Franka Kitchen (5 tasks)
 The setups, including task rewards, success criteria, camera positioning, object sets, and demonstration trajectories, were adopted directly from prior work. Demonstrations used were n=25 for MetaWorld/Franka Kitchen and n=200 for RoboSuite. Success rates were empirically estimated over 50 test trajectories.
3. **Real-World Tasks:** Three distinct, challenging manipulation tasks were designed and validated on a Franka Panda Robot:
 - **Block Stacking:** Pick a red block and place it on a green block. Evaluated on 25 fixed novel test positions for the blocks.
 - **Pouring:** Lift a cup and pour almonds into a target bowl. Evaluated on 15 trials using 3 novel cup/bowl pairs in 5 random positions.
 - **Toasting:** Pick an object, place it in a toaster, and shut the toaster. Evaluated on 10 trials using 2 novel object pairs with the toaster in 5 random orientations.
 For all real-world tasks, 50 tele-operated demonstrations were collected for fine-tuning, and performance was rigorously evaluated with 50+ robot rollouts per task.
4. **Baselines:** Performance was benchmarked against a "Scratch" model (no pre-training) and two state-of-the-art baselines, VC-1 and MVP. These baselines share the ViT architecture and MAE pre-training recipe but were trained on significantly more frames (2.5M+), primarily from Ego4D. R3M (ResNet-18, Ego4D, contrastive learning) was also used as a baseline for SimCLR comparisons.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The investigation yielded several findings concerning dataset choice, data scaling, and the reliability of simulation for robotic pre-training.

**A. Pre-training Dataset Choice and Effectiveness:**

- **Traditional Vision Datasets Outperform Egocentric/Robotic Data (Table 1):** In an "apples-to-apples" comparison using 1M images and MAE pre-training, representations learned from traditional computer vision datasets (ImageNet, Kinetics, 100 Days of Hands (DoH)) generally yielded stronger performance on real-world robotic tasks compared to those trained on domain-specific datasets like Ego4D and RoboNet.
 - Average real-world success rates: Kinetics (44%), ImageNet (41%), and DoH (38%) were higher than Ego4D (28%) and RoboNet (30%). This contradicts the intuition that egocentric human interaction data or direct robot interaction data would be superior.
- **Competitiveness Against SOTA Baselines:** ImageNet representations were found to be competitive with, and in some cases outperformed, state-of-the-art (SOTA) baselines like VC-1 (33%) and MVP (25%), despite these baselines being trained on significantly more data (2.5M+ frames, predominantly Ego4D).
- **Generality Across Pre-training Methods (Appendix D):** The observed trends were not exclusive to MAE and ViT. Replication experiments using SimCLR (contrastive learning) and a ResNet-18 architecture also showed that vision datasets (ImageNet/Kinetics/DoH) produced stronger visuo-motor features than Ego4D/RoboNet. MAE representations generally exhibited stronger average performance than SimCLR.

**B. Image Distribution Versus Dataset Size:**

- **Image Distribution Matters More Than Raw Size:** The study indicates that the distribution and quality of the pre-training images are more crucial than simply increasing the total number of frames.
 - **Naive Scaling Insufficient (Table 2):** Doubling the size of a mixed dataset ("Soup-2M" with 2M images) did not lead to a significant average improvement in real-world performance compared to "Soup-1M" (both averaged 41% success).
 - **Targeted Data Addition (Table 2):** Adding specific datasets to the "Soup-1M" base yielded varied results:
 - "Soup-1M + 1M DoH" achieved the highest real-world average success rate (60%), representing a 20% improvement over Soup-1M and a 30% improvement over the strongest baseline (VC-1). This suggests a strong marginal value for the DoH dataset.
 - Adding more ImageNet, Ego4D, or RoboNet data to Soup-1M resulted in slightly worse or similar performance, while adding more Kinetics data showed a slight improvement. These outcomes align with the individual dataset performance observed initially.
- **Diversity Hypothesis (Appendix E):** An ablation comparing ImageNet subsets of equal size but varying class diversity showed that a more diverse dataset (1000 classes, 46% average success) marginally outperformed a less diverse one (500 classes, 37% average success) on pouring and toasting tasks, providing some support for the hypothesis that diversity aids representation learning.

**C. Reliability of Simulation Benchmarks:**

- **Poor Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The study found a significant disconnect between performance trends observed in simulation and those in the real world.
 - **Contradictory Rankings (Table 1):** Simulation benchmarks often predicted Ego4D as a strong performer, a finding that consistently diverged from real-world evaluations.
 - **Impact of Regularization (Figure 4):** Applying dropout (p=0.2) to the policy network consistently improved real-world performance (e.g., Block Stacking), leading to smoother robot motions and better generalization (Block Stacking Robust task success increased from 10% to 24%). However, the same dropout frequently *decreased* performance in simulation, indicating that simulation results might be misleading for certain design choices.
 - **Weak Correlation (Figure 5):** A comprehensive plot of simulated performance versus real-world performance across all tested models revealed a low correlation (R^2 = 32%), indicating that simulation metrics are poor predictors of real-world success. Even for tasks deemed conceptually similar (RoboMimic's block-lift vs. the stacking task), the correlation remained low (R^2 = 34%).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides empirical findings with implications for the field of data-driven robotics, particularly in the domain of visuo-motor pre-training.

- **Re-evaluation of Dataset Selection Paradigms:** The most significant impact lies in challenging the prevailing, often intuitive, biases regarding the choice of pre-training datasets for robotics. The finding that traditional, curated internet image/video datasets (ImageNet, Kinetics, 100 Days of Hands) can yield stronger visuo-motor representations than specialized egocentric or robot-specific datasets (Ego4D, RoboNet) suggests a need for re-evaluating current practices. This implies that researchers might broaden their consideration of data sources beyond those perceived as "closest" to robotic observations.
- **Emphasis on Data Distribution Over Sheer Volume:** The demonstration that the *distribution* and diversity of images in a pre-training dataset are more critical than merely increasing the *number* of images is a key insight. This can guide future data collection and curation efforts, encouraging a focus on breadth and quality of visual priors rather than simply accumulating more data from a narrow domain. The notable success of combining "Soup-1M" with "1M DoH" (a 30% improvement over baselines) provides a concrete example of this principle, suggesting strategies for effective dataset mixing.
- **Improved Real-World Robotic Performance:** The identification of optimal dataset combinations and simple regularization techniques (like dropout) that lead to substantial performance gains (up to 30% over baselines) on real-world robotic tasks has direct practical implications. These findings can contribute to developing more robust and generalizable policies for physical robots.
- **Caution Regarding Simulation Benchmarks:** The empirical evidence demonstrating the weak correlation between simulated and real-world performance is a critical finding. This calls for increased skepticism towards conclusions drawn solely from simulation studies in robot learning. It underscores the necessity for more rigorous real-world evaluation of pre-trained representations and policies, or for the development of more sophisticated sim-to-real transfer methods and evaluation benchmarks (e.g., standardized cloud robotics platforms) that bridge this gap more effectively.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** By shifting the focus to dataset analysis, this work opens new avenues for research into understanding *why* certain data distributions are more effective. Future studies could delve deeper into the specific features or priors learned from different datasets and their transferability characteristics. It also emphasizes the need for faster and more reproducible methods for evaluating representations directly on hardware.
- **Contribution to Open Science:** The public release of pre-trained representations and evaluation code fosters reproducibility and lowers the barrier for other researchers to build upon these findings, accelerating progress in the field.

In summary, this paper provides a robust, data-centric empirical analysis that offers practical guidance for selecting and combining pre-training datasets, while simultaneously issuing a significant caution regarding the reliance on simulation benchmarks for real-world robotic applications.
