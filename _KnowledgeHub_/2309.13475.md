# Detecting and Mitigating System-Level Anomalies of Vision-Based Controllers

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework integrates offline Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis to identify system-level failures in vision-based controllers with an online deep learning anomaly detector and a safety-preserving fallback mechanism. This system reduced the failure space, represented by the Backward Reachable Tube volume, and maintained high detection performance in an autonomous aircraft taxiing application.

## Problem

- Autonomous systems with vision-based controllers are susceptible to failures when encountering novel or out-of-distribution inputs, posing safety risks.
- Existing anomaly detection methods primarily focus on component-level errors, which are insufficient to prevent cascading system-level failures.
- Automatically acquiring diverse datasets of system-level anomalous inputs for high-dimensional visual data and varied environmental conditions is challenging.

## Method

- An offline phase uses ==Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) Reachability analysis== in a simulator to automatically discover and label system-level failure states (==Backward Reachable Tube - BRT==) and their corresponding visual inputs under diverse environmental conditions.
- A ==deep neural network (DNN) classifier== is trained on this automatically generated dataset of labeled safe and anomalous images to serve as an ==online anomaly detector (AD)==.
- During online operation, the trained AD continuously monitors input images; upon detection of an anomaly, the system switches from its primary vision-based controller to a ==safety-preserving fallback controller== to mitigate potential failures.

## Results

- The learned anomaly detector achieved high recall (**90.34%-97.44%**) and accuracy (**89.72%-98.19%**) when tested on unseen environmental conditions and new airports, demonstrating strong generalization capabilities.
- The system-level anomaly detector outperformed component-level baselines (prediction error and ensemble uncertainty-based methods) in accurately identifying true system-level failures and understanding their context.
- The integrated anomaly detection and fallback mechanism demonstrably prevented system failures, reducing the Backward Reachable Tube volume (e.g., from **25.75%** to **18.24%** for one runway in an aircraft taxiing scenario), while introducing acceptable computational overhead.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hamilton-Jacobi Reachability analysis can effectively and automatically identify system-level failure states and their corresponding visual inputs within a simulator, providing crucial training data for anomaly detection.
> - A deep learning-based anomaly detector, trained on reachability-derived system-level failure labels, can generalize effectively to unseen environmental conditions and implicitly learn semantic failure modes without explicit programming.
> - Integrating a system-level anomaly detector with a simple fallback controller creates a robust safety pipeline that significantly reduces the system's failure space and prevents catastrophic outcomes with minimal latency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous systems, including self-driving cars and drones, increasingly rely on vision-based controllers and machine learning for decision-making and control. While these systems demonstrate high performance, they remain susceptible to errors when encountering novel or out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs, which can lead to system failures. The broader research landscape in this area focuses on enhancing the safety and robustness of such systems, particularly through runtime anomaly detection (AD) and mitigation strategies.

Existing anomaly detection methods for learning components generally fall into two categories: distributional shift methods and functional uncertainty methods. Distributional shift methods aim to identify discrepancies between training and test data distributions. These include techniques involving artificial noise injection, distributionally robust optimization to guarantee OOD performance, and domain randomization during training. Functional uncertainty methods, on the other hand, focus on detecting inputs that are either dissimilar to training data or result in low-confidence or erroneous predictions. Examples of such methods include dissimilarity-based detection and techniques leveraging predictive uncertainty from ensembles of neural networks or Bayesian approximations.

However, a significant limitation of many existing approaches is their focus on *component-level* anomaly detection. Detecting errors at the level of individual modules, such as image classification errors, may not be sufficient to prevent *system-level* faults. Minor component errors can cascade into catastrophic system failures, necessitating a broader, system-level perspective on safety. This paper addresses this gap by proposing an approach that moves beyond component-level monitoring to detect and mitigate anomalies that pose a threat to the safety of the overall closed-loop system. It builds upon previous work, such as that in, which uses Hamilton-Jacobi Reachability within a visual simulator to expose closed-loop system failures, but extends it by developing an online, computationally feasible anomaly detection and mitigation framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent challenge of ensuring safety and reliability in autonomous systems driven by vision-based controllers. Despite advances in deep learning and computer vision, such controllers cannot account for all possible real-world scenarios, leading to potential failures when faced with uncommon "corner cases" or out-of-distribution inputs. Errors made by individual vision modules can propagate and culminate in catastrophic system-level failures, highlighting the inadequacy of merely monitoring individual component performance. Therefore, there is a critical need for runtime anomaly detection systems that can provide early warnings of potential system breakdowns and facilitate mitigation.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Develop a Runtime Anomaly Monitor:** To introduce a system that can detect closed-loop, system-level failures of vision-based controllers during operation. This monitor is designed to identify inputs that might cause the overall system to fail, rather than just indicating individual module errors.
2. **Automate System-Level Failure Mining:** To leverage a reachability-based framework, specifically Hamilton-Jacobi Reachability analysis, offline. This framework is used to systematically stress-test the vision-based controller in a simulated environment and automatically identify states and corresponding input image sequences that lead to system-level failures. This process aims to overcome the challenge of manually acquiring diverse datasets of system-level anomalous inputs, especially for high-dimensional visual data and varied environmental conditions.
3. **Train an Anomaly Detector for Online Use:** To utilize the automatically mined system-level failure data to train a classifier. This classifier, operating as an anomaly detector, is intended for online deployment to flag input images that have the potential to trigger a system breakdown, without requiring privileged information about the environment or real-time reachability computations.
4. **Design a Safety-Preserving Fallback Controller:** To integrate the anomaly detector with a fallback control mechanism. This fallback controller is designed to robustly handle detected anomalies, trading off system performance for safety, thereby preserving system integrity when the primary vision-based controller is deemed unsafe.
5. **Validate the Approach on a Realistic Autonomous System:** To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed anomaly detection and mitigation framework using a concrete case study: an autonomous aircraft taxiing system ("TaxiNet") that employs a vision-based controller. This validation includes comparing the proposed system-level anomaly detector against component-level anomaly detection techniques, such as those based on prediction error or ensemble uncertainty, to demonstrate its advantages in terms of safety and robustness.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a practical solution for enhancing the safety and reliability of autonomous systems operating in complex and unpredictable environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology proposed in this paper integrates offline reachability analysis with online machine learning for detecting and mitigating system-level anomalies in vision-based controllers. The approach consists of an offline phase for data generation and model training, and an online phase for real-time anomaly detection and mitigation.

**Problem Setup:**
The system is modeled with dynamics $\dot{x} = f(x, u)$, where $x$ is the state and $u$ is the control input. A sensor $S$ provides visual inputs $I = S(x)$ from the surroundings. A vision-based controller $\pi$ maps $I$ to $u$, i.e., $u = \pi(I)$. The goal is to identify input images $I$ that lead the closed-loop system to a set of undesirable (failure) states $O$. Such images are defined as anomalous, $I_{unsafe}$. The objective is to learn a binary mapping $\sigma: I \to \{0, 1\}$, where 1 indicates an anomaly, and 0 indicates a safe input. The challenges include the difficulty of obtaining $I_{unsafe}$, handling high-dimensional visual inputs, and accounting for variations in environmental conditions.

The approach is demonstrated using an autonomous aircraft taxiing system, TaxiNet. The aircraft's state is $(p_x, p_y, \theta)$ representing crosstrack error, downtrack position, and heading error, respectively. The control $u$ is angular velocity. The vision-based controller $\pi$ is a composition of a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) that estimates $(p_x, \theta)$ from an image and a Proportional-Controller (P-Controller) that generates $u$. Unsafe states $O$ are defined as $|p_x| \ge B$, where $B$ is the runway width, indicating the aircraft leaving the runway. Images are rendered using the X-Plane flight simulator under various conditions.

**Offline Phase: Learning System Failures Using Reachability Analysis**

1. **Automatic Labels for System-Level Anomalous Inputs:**
 - **Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) Reachability Analysis:** The core of the offline data generation is the computation of the Backward Reachable Tube (BRT). The BRT is defined as the set of initial states $x$ from which the system, following policy $\pi(I)$, can reach the undesirable set $O$ within a given time horizon $[t, T]$. This is computed by solving a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Variational Inequality (HJB-VI), which determines a value function $V(x,t)$. States where $V(x,t) \le 0$ are considered part of the BRT, meaning they lead to failure.
 - **Simulator-based BRT Computation:** Since an analytical model for the vision sensor $S$ and the controller $\pi(I)$ is typically unavailable, the approach from is adopted. This involves using a photorealistic simulator (X-Plane) to render images $I$ for sampled states $x$. These images are then fed to the vision-based controller $\pi$ to obtain the control input $u$, which is used in the HJB-VI solver.
 - **Diverse Environment Conditions:** To enhance generalization, BRTs are computed for a diverse set of environmental conditions, including different times of day (e.g., 9 AM, 9 PM), cloud conditions (clear, overcast), and runways. This ensures that the generated dataset encompasses a wide range of visual inputs that lead to system failures.
 - **Dataset Generation:** After computing the BRTs, a large number of states are randomly sampled. For each sampled state, an image is rendered using the simulator. If the state falls within the computed BRT, the image is labeled as "anomalous" (system-level failure); otherwise, it is labeled "safe." For the TaxiNet case study, 12 different BRTs were computed (2 times of day x 2 cloud conditions x 3 runways), yielding a dataset of 240,000 labeled images. The Level Set Toolbox (LST) was used for numerical solution of the HJB-VI.

2. **Learning an Anomaly Detector (AD):**
 - A deep neural network (DNN)-based binary classifier is trained on the generated dataset. The DNN takes a 224x224x3 input image and outputs softmax scores indicating the probability of the image being anomalous.
 - A pre-trained EfficientNet-B0 model is used as the backbone, with the final layer replaced by a fully connected layer leading to a binary softmax output. The classifier is trained using cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimizer.

**Online Phase: Mitigating System Failures**

1. **Fallback Controller:**
 - During runtime, the trained anomaly detector $\sigma(I)$ continuously monitors the input images.
 - If $\sigma(I)$ classifies an image as anomalous (i.e., $\sigma(I) = 1$), the system switches from its default vision-based controller $\pi$ to a predefined fallback controller $\pi^*$. If the image is classified as safe, the default controller $\pi$ remains active.
 - The fallback controller $\pi^*$ is designed to prioritize safety, potentially at the cost of performance. In the TaxiNet example, $\pi^*$ reduces the aircraft's linear velocity by 0.01 m/s each time an anomaly is detected, effectively slowing it down and potentially bringing it to a stop if anomalies persist. This allows the system to remain on the runway and avoid catastrophic failure.

**Evaluation and Baselines:**
The anomaly detector's generalization capabilities are tested on unseen environmental conditions (different time of day, new airports). Its performance is measured using recall and accuracy.
The proposed method is compared against two component-level anomaly detection baselines:
- **Prediction Error-based Labels:** Anomaly labels are assigned if the TaxiNet's estimated state error (difference between predicted and true $p_x, \theta$) exceeds a certain threshold.
- **Ensembled Predictions:** An ensemble of five TaxiNet models (with different weight initializations) is used. An image is labeled anomalous if the variance between the ensemble's predictions exceeds a threshold, indicating high predictive uncertainty.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the efficacy of the proposed system-level anomaly detection and mitigation framework.

**Anomaly Detector Performance:**
The learned anomaly detector demonstrated consistent performance across various unseen environments.
- **Generalization:** When tested on unseen times of day (5 PM) and entirely new airports (KEWR, KSFO), alongside different cloud conditions (clear and overcast), the AD maintained high recall and accuracy.
 - Recall values ranged from 90.34% to 97.44%.
 - Accuracy values ranged from 89.72% to 98.19%.
- This indicates the AD's ability to generalize beyond the specific conditions present in its training data, identifying system-level anomalies in novel visual contexts.

**Qualitative Analysis of Detected Anomalies:**
The trained AD exhibited an implicit understanding of system failure modes without explicit programming or heuristics.
- **Runway Boundaries:** The AD successfully flagged images where the aircraft was close to the runway boundaries, recognizing these states as leading to system failure (e.g., Fig. 4a, 4b).
- **Semantic Failure Modes:** It identified runway markings as sources of confusion for the TaxiNet controller, leading to system failure (e.g., Fig. 4c, 4d). This suggested an emergent understanding of visual cues that mislead the controller.
- **Contextual Understanding:** The AD distinguished between similar visual inputs based on environmental context. For example, a state near the runway boundary might be classified as anomalous during the day but safe at night if runway lights are illuminated, as the lights aid the vision controller (e.g., Fig. 4e vs. 4f). This illustrates the AD's ability to learn how environmental factors influence the controller's performance and system safety.

**Comparison with Baselines:**
The system-level anomaly detector significantly outperformed component-level detection methods.
- **Prediction Error-based Detection:** This method was found to be insufficient for predicting system-level failures. It produced both false positives (states with high prediction error but not leading to system failure) and false negatives (states with low prediction error but eventually leading to system failure). Its unpredictable nature and lack of correlation with true system-level failures (as defined by BRT) demonstrated its inadequacy.
- **Ensembled Predictions:** While often used to quantify uncertainty, the ensemble-based approach also failed to accurately predict system-level anomalies. The ensemble either confidently made incorrect predictions for some states or incorrectly flagged safe states as anomalous due to disagreement, highlighting its limitations in capturing complex system-level failure dynamics.

**Fallback Mechanism Efficacy:**
The integration of the AD with a simple fallback controller demonstrably enhanced system safety.
- **Failure Prevention:** When an anomaly was detected, the fallback controller (reducing linear velocity) successfully prevented the aircraft from leaving the runway. This was observed in trajectories where the nominal TaxiNet controller failed, while the system with the safety pipeline safely decelerated and avoided the failure state (Fig. 7a, 7b).
- **Reduced Failure Space:** Quantitatively, the Backward Reachable Tube (BRT), representing the set of failure states, was substantially reduced when the safety pipeline was active. For a test case on the KMWH runway, the BRT volume decreased from 25.75% to 18.24%, indicating a significant reduction in the states from which system failure would occur.
- **Minimal Latency:** The combined AD and fallback controller introduced minimal computational overhead, allowing the system to operate at 25Hz compared to 33Hz for the vanilla TaxiNet, which is acceptable for online deployment without severe latency issues.

**Failure Modes of AD:**
Despite its strengths, the anomaly detector exhibited certain limitations.
- **BRT Boundary Ambiguity:** The AD struggled with states near the BRT boundary, where visually similar images could lead to drastically different system outcomes (failure vs. success). This inherent ambiguity at the decision boundary posed a challenge for accurate classification (Fig. 8a-c).
- **Novel Semantic Failure Modes:** The AD did not perform well when exposed to entirely new semantic failure modes not represented in its training dataset (e.g., runway markings with colors not seen during training, Fig. 8d). This highlights the need for continuous data acquisition and model updates.

**Incremental Training (Preliminary):**
Preliminary results suggest that incrementally training the TaxiNet model on the collected anomalous data can further improve its robustness and reduce the BRT size, indicating a potential avenue for direct controller improvement using the identified failure scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous systems by addressing a critical gap in current safety assurance methodologies. Its core significance lies in shifting the focus from component-level anomaly detection to a more holistic, system-level perspective, which is paramount for the reliable deployment of vision-based controllers in safety-critical applications.

The potential impact of this work is multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced System Safety and Robustness:** By automatically identifying and mitigating system-level failures, the proposed framework directly contributes to making autonomous systems safer and more robust. The demonstrable reduction in the Backward Reachable Tube (BRT) volume signifies a quantifiable improvement in safety margins, reducing the likelihood of catastrophic events caused by novel or out-of-distribution inputs.
- **Automation of Failure Analysis:** The methodology automates the challenging process of discovering and labeling system-level anomalies. This eliminates the need for manual identification of failure modes, which is often tedious, subjective, and prone to human error, especially in high-dimensional state and observation spaces. This automation streamlines the development and testing pipeline for autonomous systems.
- **Improved Trustworthiness of Autonomous Systems:** By providing a runtime monitoring and mitigation system, the research contributes to building more trustworthy autonomous systems. Operators and regulators can have higher confidence in systems equipped with such an anomaly detection and fallback mechanism, knowing that potential system breakdowns are actively monitored and addressed.
- **Generalizability Across Environments:** The successful validation on unseen environmental conditions (different times of day, new airports, various cloud cover) suggests that the framework possesses strong generalization capabilities. This is crucial for real-world applications where autonomous systems must operate reliably in diverse and unpredictable environments.
- **Foundational for Future Research:** The work lays a foundation for several promising future directions. These include exploring more sophisticated fallback mechanisms, incorporating temporal information into anomaly detection, investigating unsupervised learning methods for anomaly detection, and leveraging the identified failure data for targeted incremental training of the primary controller. The integration of high-dimensional reachability techniques, such as DeepReach, could also extend the applicability of this approach to more complex systems.
- **Applicability to Various Domains:** While validated on an aircraft taxiing system, the underlying principles of combining reachability analysis for system-level failure mining with machine learning for online detection and mitigation are broadly applicable to other autonomous domains, including self-driving cars, robotics, and industrial automation, where vision-based control is prevalent.

In summary, this research offers a practical and principled approach to enhance the safety and reliability of vision-based controllers, moving beyond module-specific error detection to address the more critical issue of overall system integrity. This advancement has the potential to significantly impact the development, verification, and deployment of autonomous technologies.
