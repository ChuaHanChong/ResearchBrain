# K-VIL: Keypoints-based Visual Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> K-VIL learns manipulation skills from a handful of human demonstration videos by extracting sparse object-centric keypoints and the geometric constraints they satisfy, using dense visual descriptors and principal manifold estimation, plus via-point movement primitives and a priority-aware keypoint admittance controller. A humanoid robot reproduces five daily tasks in cluttered novel scenes, reaching sub-millimeter accuracy and over 90% success from four demonstrations.

## Problem

- Pixel-level context translators for visual imitation are computationally expensive to train and do not scale to categorical objects with large shape and pose variation.
- Descriptor and graph based representations learn task constraints implicitly, so demonstrations with large pose or shape variation get averaged into an unusable mean configuration.
- Prior explicit-constraint methods need around thirty demonstrations, and many keypoint methods require robot state or matched camera viewpoints, breaking embodiment and viewpoint independence.

## Method

- ==Principal Constraint Estimation== jointly selects sparse keypoints and fits five constraint types (point-to-point, line, plane, curve, surface) via ==PCA== and ==principal manifold estimation==, with hierarchical agglomerative clustering removing redundant keypoints.
- Preprocessing densely samples candidates via ==Mask R-CNN==, tracks them with ==Dense Object Net== correspondences and ==MediaPipe== hands, assigns master/slave object roles by motion saliency, and builds object-centric local frames.
- ==Via-point movement primitives== encode keypoint motion in local frames; a keypoint-based ==admittance controller== drives virtual spring-damper forces on prioritized constraints, ranking lower-dimensional manifolds first.

## Results

- With four demonstrations, press-button and fetch-tissue reach sub-millimeter control accuracy and precision with at least **90%** success, versus **0%** when learned from fewer than four.
- One-shot representations still succeed thanks to controller priorities: hanging a hat scored **95%** and stick insertion **62%**, the latter limited by an extreme stick-length change.
- Removing controller priorities dropped one-shot insertion success to **25%**; on generalizable representations success held but accuracy and precision degraded on pouring and insertion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit geometric constraints on principal manifolds beat implicitly learned ones: they survive large pose and shape variation instead of averaging demonstrations into a mean.
> - Constraint dimensionality is a natural priority ordering for control; without it, competing spring-damper terms equilibrate and the most task-critical keypoint loses accuracy.
> - A few demonstrations with genuine pose and shape variation carry more information than many repeated ones; low variance yields over-constrained, unreachable targets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper operates within the field of **Visual Imitation Learning (VIL)**, a sub-area of Imitation Learning (IL) where robots learn new skills solely from visual sensory input, typically human demonstration videos. VIL offers an intuitive and efficient paradigm for skill acquisition, but it faces several significant challenges. The authors categorize these challenges into:
(i) **Context Translation:** Establishing correspondences between the demonstrator's (human) and imitator's (robot) visual contexts. Prior approaches often use pixel-level translators, which are computationally expensive and time-consuming, or goal-level translators that still require specific robot play data.
(ii) **Fine-grained Understanding of Scene Structure:** This involves representing objects and their functional parts in a viewpoint-invariant manner, extracting hierarchical scene structures, and defining generalized task constraints. Many existing methods struggle with scaling to categorical objects, requiring large datasets, or are embodiment-dependent. Neural Descriptor Fields (NDFs) and Dense Object Net (DON) offer dense correspondence, but their application often requires access to robot state or specific view setups. Approaches that implicitly learn task constraints, often using deep learning or reinforcement learning (RL), can suffer from the "averaging problem" where specific details of constraints are lost when dealing with large object variations.
(iii) **Sample-efficient and Scalable Control Policies:** Developing control policies that can effectively reproduce learned skills and adapt to novel scenes. RL-based policies, while powerful, typically demand extensive training data.

K-VIL positions itself as a solution that addresses these challenges by focusing on **sparse, object-centric, viewpoint-invariant, and embodiment-independent task representations**. It explicitly aims to overcome the limitations of prior work by:
- **Leveraging pre-trained, task-agnostic dense visual correspondence models (Mask R-CNN, DON, MediaPipe):** This allows K-VIL to acquire representations from a single or few demonstrations, a significant improvement over methods requiring large datasets or robot-specific play data.
- **Explicitly extracting geometric task constraints:** Unlike methods that implicitly learn constraints (e.g., Visual Entity Graphs), K-VIL identifies specific point-to-point, point-to-line, point-to-plane, point-to-curve, and point-to-surface constraints. This explicit representation is key to avoiding the averaging problem, leading to better generalization across categorical objects with shape and pose variations.
- **Combining geometric constraints with movement primitives (VMPs):** This provides a flexible and adaptable control policy that enables temporal scaling and reliable via-point adaptation, contrasting with less flexible or more sample-hungry RL-based controllers.
- **Focusing on few-shot and one-shot learning:** K-VIL aims for highly sample-efficient learning, reducing the burden of data collection compared to many data-intensive deep learning approaches in VIL.

By providing an interpretable and generalizable task representation, K-VIL contributes to making robotic skill acquisition more practical and scalable for real-world manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable robotic systems to acquire novel manipulation skills efficiently and intuitively from human demonstrations. The authors highlight the current challenges in simultaneously learning geometric task constraints and control policies from visual inputs alone.

The core motivation and key objectives of the K-VIL approach can be broken down as follows:

- **Automatic and Incremental Task Representation Extraction:** To develop a method that automatically extracts sparse, object-centric, viewpoint-invariant, and embodiment-independent task representations. A crucial aspect is the ability to extract these representations from a *single* demonstration video (one-shot learning) and *incrementally update* and refine them as more demonstrations become available (few-shot learning). This addresses the need for sample efficiency in imitation learning.
- **Generalizable Geometric Task Representation:** To define a task representation composed of keypoint-based geometric constraints on principal manifolds, their associated local frames, and movement primitives. This representation should be generalizable, allowing skills learned from one set of objects/poses to be transferred to novel scenes with new instances of categorical objects and varying poses and shapes. This tackles the averaging problem and enhances extrapolation capabilities.
- **Robust Task Reproduction and Adaptation:** To enable robots to reproduce manipulation skills using the learned set of prioritized geometric constraints in novel and cluttered scenes, handling challenges such as viewpoint mismatch and large object variations. This necessitates a robust control mechanism that can leverage the extracted geometric information.
- **Flexibility and Efficiency:** To formulate and learn a wide variety of geometric constraints (point-to-point, point-to-line, point-to-plane, point-to-curve, point-to-surface) to ensure the task representation is flexible and efficient.

In essence, the authors are motivated by the goal of making robot learning from human demonstrations more practical, robust, and less data-intensive by focusing on an interpretable and structured representation of manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The K-VIL (Keypoint-based Visual Imitation Learning) approach is structured into two main phases: **Task Representation Learning** (K-VIL itself) and **Task Reproduction** (via the Keypoint-based Admittance Controller, KAC).

**Phase 1: Task Representation Learning (K-VIL)**

K-VIL processes N RGB-D demonstration videos to extract a comprehensive task representation.

- **A. Preprocessing:**
 1. **Sampling of Candidates:** Uses Mask R-CNN to identify objects and densely samples candidate points from their visible surfaces. For human hands, MediaPipe is used. These candidates are potential keypoints or local frame origins.
 2. **Trajectories of Candidate Points:** Employs a pre-trained Dense Object Net (DON) model to obtain dense visual descriptors for each candidate point. These descriptors are used with a correspondence function ($f_c$) to track candidate points in 3D across demonstration videos, forming their trajectories.
 3. **Object Properties:** Defines a canonical shape for each object category based on candidate positions and a spatial scale (maximum distance between candidates) to normalize variability.
 4. **Object Roles:** Determines "master" and "slave" objects based on motion saliency. Local frames are primarily constructed on master objects, and keypoints are extracted from slave objects. This is crucial for object-centric task representation.
 5. **Local Frame Detection:** Defines canonical local frames on the master object, parameterized by neighboring candidate descriptors. During reproduction, these local frames can be detected on new object instances by minimizing displacement with reference values.

- **B. Principal Constraints Estimation (PCE):** This is the core of extracting keypoints and their geometric constraints.
 1. **Distance Criteria (for N=1 demonstration - One-shot IL):** Since variability information is absent, a heuristic approach is used. For each slave object, the closest candidate to the master object ($k_1$), the farthest point from $k_1$ ($k_2$), and a third point farthest from both ($k_3$) are selected. These three keypoints are subject to linear point-to-point (p2p) constraints to fully constrain the slave object's pose in 3D.
 2. **Variance Criteria for Linear Constraints (for N>1 demonstrations - Few-shot IL):** For multiple demonstrations, K-VIL leverages the variability of candidate point trajectories in the master object's local frames. PCA is applied to compute "explained variance" and "spatial variability." Thresholds ($\xi_1, \xi_2$) are used to identify:
 - **p2p constraint:** Low spatial variability in all components ($\eta < \xi_1$).
 - **p2l constraint (point-to-line):** Low variability in the second and third components, high in the first ($\eta_2 < \xi_1, \eta_1 > \xi_2$). Requires N>2.
 - **p2P constraint (point-to-plane):** Low variability in the third component, high in the first two ($\eta_3 < \xi_1, \eta_2 > \xi_2$). Requires N>3.
 3. **Variance Criteria for Nonlinear Constraints (for N>10 demonstrations):** For more complex tasks and sufficient data, Principal Manifold Estimation (PME) is used. Candidate points not satisfying linear constraints are considered. PME models the keypoint trajectories as low-dimensional principal manifolds. Based on explained variance in tangential and orthogonal directions ($\nu_{\parallel}, \nu_{\perp}$), p2c (point-to-curve, d=1) and p2S (point-to-surface, d=2) constraints are identified.
 4. **Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering (HAC):** Resolves redundancy in selected keypoints due to adjacent timesteps, neighboring keypoints, or equivalent local frames. Keypoints with the lowest variability are preferred within clusters, ensuring robustness.

- **C. Extraction of K-VIL's Complete Task Representation:**
 The final task representation comprises:
 - **Keypoint Descriptors (D):** Visual descriptors for each selected sparse keypoint.
 - **Geometric Constraints (C):** The identified (non-)linear constraints for each keypoint.
 - **Movement Primitives (Ω):** Via-point Movement Primitives (VMPs) are trained for each keypoint. Crucially, for non-p2p constraints, VMPs are trained on trajectories *projected onto the orthogonal direction* to the principal manifold. This allows the keypoints to approach the manifold while permitting extrapolation of target positions along the manifold.

**Phase 2: Task Reproduction (Keypoint-based Admittance Controller - KAC)**

KAC translates the learned task representation into real-time robot control commands.

- **A. Attraction Force:** For each keypoint, its real-time position is identified using DON. A virtual spring-damper system computes an attraction force towards an attractor position generated by the corresponding VMP. For non-p2p constraints, the VMP generates motion orthogonal to the principal manifold.
- **B. Density Force:** For non-p2p constraints, a density function of demonstrated target positions on the principal manifold is estimated using kernel density estimation. This creates a "density field" that generates an additional force, driving the keypoint towards regions of higher demonstrated probability (i.e., demonstrated targets), balancing extrapolation with fidelity to demonstrations.
- **C. Priority:** To handle potential interference between different constraint types (especially with large object shape variations), KAC assigns priorities. p2p constraints have the highest priority. For lower-priority constraints, the density force is projected onto the tangent space of a sphere centered at the higher-priority keypoint, ensuring it does not interfere with the higher-priority constraint. For one-shot learning, where all are p2p, KAC assigns $Pri_1 > Pri_2 > Pri_3$ (e.g., contact point $k_1$ has highest priority).
- **D. Admittance Control:** The total control force for each keypoint (attraction + projected density force) is aggregated to control a virtual Tool-Center-Point (TCP). This virtual TCP's desired acceleration is then mapped to the robot end-effector's control command using a task-space inverse dynamics controller, enabling the robot to physically interact with objects and fulfill the learned task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluate K-VIL across six diverse daily manipulation tasks (Press Button, Fetch Tissue, Insert Stick, Pour Water, Hang Hat, Clean Table) involving categorical objects with significant pose and shape variations.

- **Efficient and Generalizable Task Representation:**
 - K-VIL successfully extracts interpretable geometric constraints (e.g., p2p for pressing a button, p2l for stick insertion, p2c for pouring) that align with human intuition.
 - The learned task representations *converge* and become *generalizable* with significantly fewer demonstrations compared to state-of-the-art approaches (e.g., 3-11 demonstrations vs. ~30). For instance, Press Button and Fetch Tissue converged at N=4, Hang Hat and Insert Stick at N=3, and Pour Water at N=11.
 - **Impact of Demonstrations and Variability:** The number and diversity (pose and shape variations) of demonstrations are crucial. Scarcity can lead to embodiment-dependent representations (robot cannot reproduce), potential collisions, or reduced control accuracy/precision. Adequate variations, particularly in master and slave objects, facilitate the efficient and joint extraction of robust local frames, keypoints, and geometric constraints.
 - **Viewpoint Mismatch:** K-VIL effectively handles demonstrations collected from different viewpoints by aligning them into a common local frame defined on the master object, ensuring the robustness of constraint extraction. The robot can also reproduce skills from novel viewpoints.

- **Robust Task Reproduction with KAC:**
 - **One-shot Imitation Learning (N=1):** While limited in generalization for complex tasks (e.g., failure in PB/FT due to unreachable targets, collisions with extremely long sticks in IS), K-VIL can still reliably adapt the task representation to new scenes and perform simple tasks thanks to the robust keypoint identification and VMP generation. The prioritized KAC significantly improves success rates and collision avoidance even in these challenging scenarios (e.g., IS with priority achieved 62% success vs. 25% without).
 - **Few-shot Imitation Learning (N>1):** With sufficient demonstrations, KAC achieves high control accuracy, precision, and success rates.
 - **Control Accuracy and Precision:** For generalizable tasks (e.g., PB 4, FT 4, PW 4, PW 11, HH 3, IS 3, CT 3), KAC achieves sub-millimeter to millimeter-level regulation errors (control accuracy) and high repeatability (precision). For instance, PB 4 achieved 0.67mm accuracy and 0.34mm precision.
 - **Success Rate:** Tasks with generalizable representations consistently achieve high success rates (≥90%, often 100%).
 - **Ablation Studies:** Removing KAC's priority mechanism (e.g., IS np 1, PW np 11) leads to a significant drop in success rate (IS np 1: 25% vs. 62%) or a notable decrease in control accuracy and precision, highlighting the criticality of prioritized control for robustness and performance.
 - **Extrapolation Capabilities:** The decomposed control (orthogonal vs. tangential to manifold) and density force mechanism in KAC allow for effective extrapolation of keypoint target positions, accommodating large object shape variations (e.g., successfully handling various stick lengths in IS).

In summary, K-VIL demonstrates a strong capability to learn generalizable robotic manipulation skills from limited visual data by combining robust visual perception models with an innovative approach to explicit geometric constraint extraction and a prioritized admittance controller.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The K-VIL approach represents a significant step forward in the field of visual imitation learning and robot skill acquisition. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency:** K-VIL's ability to extract generalizable task representations from a *small number* of demonstrations (as few as 3-4 for many tasks, and even one for basic tasks) is a major breakthrough. This drastically reduces the data collection burden, a common bottleneck in data-intensive learning approaches, making robotic skill acquisition more practical and accessible for real-world deployment.
- **Improved Generalization and Robustness:** By focusing on explicit, object-centric, and embodiment-independent geometric constraints, K-VIL overcomes the "averaging problem" often seen in implicitly learned policies. This allows robots to robustly perform tasks with novel instances of categorical objects, large shape and pose variations, and in cluttered scenes, even with viewpoint mismatch between demonstration and execution. This level of generalization is critical for deploying robots in dynamic, unstructured environments.
- **Interpretable Task Representations:** Unlike "black-box" deep learning policies, K-VIL's task representation (keypoints, explicit geometric constraints like p2p, p2c, and VMPs) is inherently interpretable. This allows human operators or designers to understand *what* the robot has learned and *how* it is trying to achieve the task, facilitating debugging, refinement, and trust in autonomous systems.
- **Flexible and Prioritized Control:** The proposed Keypoint-based Admittance Controller (KAC), with its decomposed control strategy and priority mechanism, is a novel contribution. It intelligently balances the need for task generalization (extrapolating keypoint targets on manifolds) with fidelity to demonstrated styles (density force) while managing potential constraint interference. This flexible and robust control is crucial for dealing with the inherent variability of real-world scenarios.
- **Foundation for More Complex Skills:** K-VIL lays a strong foundation for learning increasingly complex manipulation tasks. The authors acknowledge limitations, such as handling transparent/reflective objects, occlusions, or multi-manual manipulation. However, the core framework could be extended to these areas by integrating more advanced perception models (e.g., NeRF-based) or hierarchical master-slave relationships.
- **Bridging Symbolic and Sub-symbolic AI:** The paper implicitly touches upon the crucial challenge of bridging symbolic (semantic) and sub-symbolic (geometric motion) representations. K-VIL provides the sub-symbolic component, and its keypoints and constraint distributions could potentially be linked to higher-level concepts like affordances, spatial relations, and grasping points in future work, paving the way for more comprehensive task models that combine abstract reasoning with precise physical execution.

In conclusion, K-VIL represents a significant advancement in making robots learn from human observations more efficiently and reliably, pushing closer to a future where robots can intuitively acquire and adapt a wide range of dexterous manipulation skills.
