# First Deployable Dynamic-CoM: A Unified Policy and Method-Agnostic Benchmark for Humanoid Single-Leg Balance

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a novel method for directly incorporating dynamic balance signals into humanoid robot control policies deployable on hardware, paired with a standardized benchmark for single-leg balance. Their proposed FDDC policy achieves a 95.6% perfect single-leg balance rate, while existing generalist policies consistently fail this task.

## Problem

- Existing unified humanoid policies struggle to maintain stable, clean single-leg balance, often resorting to reactive recovery behaviors like stepping instead of proactive prevention.
- Integrating dynamic balance signals, such as the capture point, directly into hardware policies is challenging due to the difficulty in measuring the robot's absolute base linear velocity on-board, necessitating complex distillation processes.
- The absence of a standardized, method-agnostic benchmark for single-leg balance hinders objective evaluation, comparison, and tracking of progress across different humanoid control methods.

## Method

- A novel ==deployable dynamic-CoM observation== is developed, allowing the ==capture point== to be reconstructed on-board from joint encoders and IMU readings, eliminating the need for teacher-student distillation.
- A human-science-inspired reward library is designed, incorporating capture-point margins, an ==ankle-knee-hip response hierarchy== for graded action penalties, and smoothness terms to promote preventive and human-like balance strategies.
- A method-agnostic sim2sim benchmark, **'Dynamic-CoM'**, is established with a stratified motion set, shared ==MuJoCo kernel==, and comprehensive metric suite for reproducible and objective evaluation of single-leg balance policies.

## Results

- The **FDDC** policy achieved a **95.6%** 'Perfect' single-leg balance rate on the held-out test set under clean sim2sim conditions, significantly outperforming all eight tested state-of-the-art generalist policies, which scored **0%**.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that the deployable dynamic-CoM observation was the most critical component, contributing a **40 percentage point** improvement to the 'Perfect' success rate and proving essential for robustness under observation noise.
- The benchmark-selected **FDDC** policy was successfully deployed onto a physical Unitree G1 robot, maintaining stable single-leg balance across various poses using only on-board sensor data and confirming the real-world deployability of the dynamic-CoM observation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic balance signals, specifically the capture point relative to the support foot, can be made directly deployable on hardware by leveraging the cancellation of unmeasurable base velocity components when calculating relative CoM velocity.
> - Generalist humanoid policies, despite their complex whole-body motion tracking capabilities, fundamentally lack the competence for stable, clean single-leg balance, often relying on reactive recovery mechanisms.
> - A reward function systematically crafted from human postural control principles can effectively train reinforcement learning policies to exhibit preventive, stable, and biomechanically sound single-leg balance behaviors.
> - Training reward is an unreliable indicator of a policy's true performance in deployment-relevant balance tasks, underscoring the necessity of objective, sim2sim evaluation for robust checkpoint selection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has seen progress in developing unified policies capable of executing complex whole-body motions, such as tracking dances, performing runs, and executing acrobatic maneuvers like backflips. These policies often leverage large motion datasets and advanced reinforcement learning techniques. However, a recurrent challenge within this landscape is the stable execution of fundamental tasks, particularly single-leg balance. While humans rely on balance as a prerequisite for most motor skills, existing state-of-the-art general humanoid policies frequently exhibit limitations when faced with the demand of maintaining a clean single-leg stance. Many policies, even those demonstrating robustness against falls, tend to recover from imbalance by initiating steps or hops rather than preventing the loss of equilibrium proactively.

A significant contributing factor to this limitation is the historical difficulty in integrating dynamic balance signals, such as the capture point (xCoM), directly into deployed hardware policies. The capture point, a concept central to human biomechanics and push-recovery strategies for robots, requires knowledge of the base's absolute linear velocity. This velocity is typically not directly measurable by on-board humanoid sensors, leading to a "deployability gap." Consequently, dynamic balance signals have often been confined to privileged critic inputs during training or used solely within reward functions, necessitating a "teacher-student distillation" process to transfer this knowledge to a deployable, proprioception-only actor. This indirect approach can introduce complexity and potential performance compromises.

Furthermore, the evaluation and comparison of humanoid balance methods, especially for specialized single-leg balance, have faced a "reproducibility gap." Many research efforts release neither their code nor trained policies, and report performance metrics using self-selected or non-standardized demonstrations. This practice hinders objective comparison across different methods and makes it challenging to assess genuine progress or the single-leg competence of increasingly generalist locomotion policies.

This work addresses these identified gaps by proposing a solution to make dynamic balance observations directly deployable on hardware and by introducing a standardized, method-agnostic benchmark. It aims to elevate single-leg balance from a task-specific trick to a measurable, foundational capability within the broader framework of general humanoid control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research was driven by several key objectives aimed at enhancing humanoid single-leg balance capabilities and the methodologies for evaluating them:

- **Objective 1: Achieve Stable, Clean Single-Leg Balance on Real Hardware Without Distillation.**
 - **Motivation:** Current unified humanoid policies, despite their proficiency in complex movements, struggle with the fundamental task of maintaining a stable, clean single-leg stance. They often rely on reactive strategies like stepping or hopping to recover balance rather than preventing its loss. This suggests a deficiency in their root-level balance competence. The necessity of distilling privileged balance knowledge from a teacher policy to a student policy for hardware deployment adds complexity and potential for information loss. The researchers aimed to develop a policy that could directly transfer to hardware, simplifying the deployment pipeline and improving performance fidelity.

- **Objective 2: Enable Direct Use of Dynamic Balance Signals (Capture Point) in Deployed Hardware Policies.**
 - **Motivation:** The capture point (xCoM), which extrapolates the Center of Mass (CoM) using its velocity, is a critical dynamic signal for predicting and preventing falls, as established in human postural control. However, its computation traditionally requires the base's absolute linear velocity, which is not directly provided by typical on-board humanoid sensors. This "deployability gap" has historically prevented the capture point from directly informing the deployed actor, relegating it to training-only components like privileged critics or reward functions. A core motivation was to overcome this limitation, making this vital dynamic information accessible to the policy running on the robot.

- **Objective 3: Develop a Reward Library Grounded in Human Postural Control Principles.**
 - **Motivation:** Decades of research in human postural control offer a detailed understanding of how humans maintain balance, including concepts such as the ankle→knee→hip correction hierarchy, spatial (Margin of Stability) and temporal (time-to-boundary) measures of stability, and the importance of movement smoothness. Existing humanoid balance methods have only partially leveraged these principles. The researchers were motivated to systematically translate these biomechanical insights into a comprehensive reward library to encourage more human-like, preventive balance strategies in the robot, focusing on preventing imbalance rather than merely recovering from it.

- **Objective 4: Establish a Method-Agnostic, Reproducible Benchmark for Humanoid Single-Leg Balance.**
 - **Motivation:** The absence of a standardized, objective evaluation framework for single-leg balance has made it difficult to compare the performance of different methods and to track progress in the field. Specialized policies often report results on self-selected demonstrations or with non-standard metrics, hindering reproducibility and cross-comparison. General locomotion policies are rarely systematically tested for their intrinsic single-leg competence. The objective was to create a robust, open-source benchmark that could objectively score any released policy under identical conditions, facilitate quantitative comparisons, and serve as a tool for identifying strong checkpoints for deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology integrates three main components: a novel deployable dynamic-CoM observation, a human-science-inspired reward library, and a method-agnostic sim2sim benchmark.

**Deployable Dynamic-CoM Observation:**
The core of the method addresses the deployability gap of dynamic balance signals. Single-leg balance is conceptualized as a Linear Inverted Pendulum (LIP) problem, where the capture point ($\xi$) determines balance. The capture point is defined as $\xi = c + \dot{c}/\omega_0$, where $c$ is the Center of Mass (CoM) and $\dot{c}$ is its velocity. Recognizing that the absolute CoM velocity is challenging to measure on-board, the authors reformulated the capture point relative to the support foot, $s$: $\xi - s \approx r + \dot{r}/\omega_0$, where $r \triangleq c - s$. They demonstrated that the support-relative dynamic-CoM state, represented by the relative position $(r_B)$ and its velocity $(\dot{r}_B)$ in the base frame (horizontal components), can be reconstructed entirely from joint encoders and the Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) gyroscope. This is because the base's linear velocity ($v_b$), which is typically unmeasurable, cancels out when calculating the difference between CoM and support-foot velocities. This deployable dynamic-CoM observation ($o_{bal} \in \mathbb{R}^4$) is directly fed into the actor network. The actor's full observation also includes standard proprioception (joint positions, velocities, base angular velocity, projected gravity), motion commands, and a short future-reference window to enable anticipatory adjustments. A privileged critic, used only during training, receives additional world-frame quantities that are not deployable, and is discarded at deployment, thereby avoiding distillation.

**Human-Science Reward Library:**
The reward system is designed based on principles from human postural control to promote preventive balance. The library includes:
- **Capture-point margins:** Two soft penalties are applied. A spatial "Margin of Stability" term penalizes the xCoM when it approaches a safety margin around the support foot boundary. A temporal "Time-to-Boundary (TTB)" term penalizes the xCoM if its projected time to cross the support boundary falls below a reaction threshold. These soft penalties encourage the policy to maintain the capture point within a safe region and act proactively.
- **Ankle→knee→hip response hierarchy:** This is implemented as graded action-rate penalties on the stance leg. The ankle joint incurs the heaviest penalty on action rate, the knee a lighter one, and the hip none. This design encourages the ankle to provide smooth, sustained torque for initial CoM stabilization, mirroring the human distal-first control strategy for small perturbations.
- **Smoothness:** An action jerk penalty is included to suppress high-frequency motor chatter, promoting smoother control which is indicative of higher-quality postural control and better for hardware deployment.
The reward weights for these terms, along with standard contact, tracking, and regularization terms, are specifically tuned.

**Asymmetric FastSAC Training:**
The policy, termed FDDC (First Deployable Dynamic-CoM), is trained using FastSAC (Fast Soft Actor-Critic) in an asymmetric configuration. This means the actor's observations are limited to deployable, on-board reconstructible signals, while the critic has access to privileged, world-frame information during training. This approach is designed to eliminate the need for teacher-student distillation for hardware deployment.

**Method-Agnostic Sim2Sim Benchmark:**
To address the reproducibility and comparability issues, the authors developed a benchmark for single-leg balance.
- **Stratified Motion Set:** A dataset of 900 single-leg balance motions was distilled from the AMS synthetic dataset. These motions were stratified into a 3x3 grid based on two orthogonal pose axes: support-leg squat depth (pelvis height) and swing-foot absolute height. This stratification ensured uniform coverage of the pose space and allowed for evaluation across varying difficulty levels. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets to ensure unbiased evaluation.
- **Shared Kernel and Per-Method Adapter:** The benchmark ensures method-agnostic evaluation by running all policies within a single, shared MuJoCo kernel with a Unitree G1 model, a fixed control rate (50 Hz), and a standardized PD controller interface. Each policy interacts via a thin "per-method adapter" that translates the shared state into the policy's specific observation format, executes its network, and decodes actions to joint targets for the shared PD controller.
- **Sim2Sim Evaluation:** Crucially, evaluation is performed in a MuJoCo simulator distinct from the simulators where policies were originally trained. This "sim2sim" approach is intended to be more challenging and predictive of real-robot transfer than in-training-simulator reporting.
- **Metric Suite:** The benchmark employs a comprehensive suite of metrics grounded in postural-control science.
 - **Outcome Tiers:** Trials are categorized as "Perfect" (clean single-leg stance, no hops/touchdowns, within tracking gate), "Marginal" (upright but recovers by hopping support foot or touching swing foot down), or "Failure" (falls or drifts outside tracking gate). These tiers are mutually exclusive.
 - **Continuous Diagnostics:** Additional metrics include Margin of Stability, time-to-boundary, actual-CoM margin, xCoM out-of-support duration, 12-keypoint tracking errors (position, velocity, acceleration), control jerk (of joint velocities), support-foot slippage, and time-to-fall. These diagnostics provide a detailed quantitative assessment of balance quality.

**Real-Robot Deployment:**
The benchmark-selected FDDC checkpoint is directly deployed on a physical Unitree G1 robot. All actor observations are reconstructed on-board from encoders and IMU readings, confirming the real-world deployability of the dynamic-CoM observation without needing distillation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding FDDC's performance, the limitations of existing generalist policies, the impact of the proposed methodological components, and real-world deployability.

**FDDC’s Single-Leg Performance:**
FDDC demonstrated a high success rate in maintaining clean single-leg balance. On the held-out test set, FDDC achieved a Perfect single-leg balance rate of 95.6% (86 out of 90 test motions) under clean, deterministic sim2sim conditions. The remaining trials were classified as 3.3% Marginal (3 motions) and 1.1% Failure (1 motion). The continuous metric suite indicated that FDDC maintained its capture point effectively within the support polygon, showing a mediolateral/fore-aft Margin of Stability (MoS) of -0.030 / +0.030. The capture point spent only 0.10 seconds outside the support polygon during the approximately 3.3-second single-support window. Furthermore, FDDC exhibited low support-foot slippage (3.9 mm/s) and smooth control (0.23 RMS jerk), consistent with biomechanically sound balance. Performance was consistent across the stratified pose classes, with slight dips in Perfect success (8/10) observed only in challenging deep-squat/high-lift poses, where the policy still maintained upright stability.

**Generalist Policies Fail Single-Leg Balance:**
In contrast to FDDC, all eight state-of-the-art general-purpose humanoid whole-body motion-tracking policies, when evaluated under the identical clean, deterministic sim2sim protocol, achieved a Perfect single-leg balance rate of 0/90. These baselines included policies such as SONIC, HoloMotion, and Humanoid-GPT. The generalist policies rarely fell outright; instead, most achieved "Marginal" success by employing recovery behaviors such as re-planting the swing foot or hopping the support foot. For instance, SONIC achieved 81.1% Marginal success and 18.9% Failure, indicating an emergent robustness but a lack of clean single-leg competence. Continuous metrics for these generalist policies were substantially inferior to FDDC, with capture-point margins ranging from -0.16 to -0.74 (compared to FDDC's near-zero values), xCoM out-of-support durations around 3 seconds, significantly higher slippage (84-578 mm/s), and control that was 3-11 times jerkier. The failure rate of baselines increased predictably with pose difficulty, highlighting their limitations in demanding balance scenarios.

**Ablation Study: Component Contributions:**
An ablation study quantitatively assessed the contribution of each design component to FDDC's performance.
- **Deployable Dynamic-CoM Observation:** This was identified as the most significant driver. Removing it reduced the Perfect success rate from 95.6% to 55.6% (a 40 percentage point drop). Merely making the observation static (removing velocity information) cost 31 points (64.4% Perfect success). Under deployment-relevant observation noise, removing the dynamic-CoM observation caused a drastic collapse to 8.6% Perfect success (from 62.2%), emphasizing its critical role in maintaining robustness under uncertainty.
- **Soft Time-to-Boundary (TTB) Reward:** This was the second most impactful component, costing 24 percentage points (dropping to 71.1%) when removed.
- **Polygon Margin of Stability Reward:** Its removal resulted in an 11 percentage point drop.
- **Ankle/Knee Action-Rate Penalties:** Removing the knee action-rate penalty cost 11 points, and removing the ankle rate alone cost 4.5 points.
- **Jerk Penalty:** Removal resulted in a 5.6 percentage point drop.
- **Future-Reference Observation:** This contributed the least in clean conditions (2.3 points) but showed increased value under noise (5.2 points).
The study confirmed that all proposed components contributed positively to the policy's performance and that the configuration was non-redundant.

**Checkpoint Selection:**
The research indicated that training reward is an unreliable predictor of a policy's real-world or sim2sim balance performance. By utilizing the benchmark to sweep hundreds of checkpoints, the authors demonstrated that the measured-best checkpoint (e.g., step 262k) for deployment was neither the final nor the highest-reward one from training. This finding underscores the importance of deployment-aligned evaluation for robust policy selection.

**Real-Robot Deployment:**
The benchmark-selected FDDC policy was directly deployed onto a physical Unitree G1 robot. The policy operated stably, maintaining single-leg balance across various poses. This successful transfer to hardware without any distillation confirmed the efficacy of the deployable dynamic-CoM observation and the overall design approach. All actor observations were reconstructed on-board using only joint encoders and IMU data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents several advancements with implications for the field of humanoid robotics:

- **Enabling Direct Hardware Deployment of Dynamic Balance Control:** By demonstrating that the crucial dynamic-CoM observation can be reconstructed on-board from standard robot sensors, this work removes a significant barrier to the direct implementation of advanced balance control principles in deployed humanoid policies. This eliminates the need for complex and potentially lossy teacher-student distillation processes, simplifying the development pipeline and potentially improving real-world performance.

- **Advancement in Humanoid Balance Capabilities:** The FDDC policy's ability to achieve stable, clean single-leg balance across a range of stratified poses, including challenging ones, significantly surpasses the performance of existing generalist policies. This indicates progress in teaching humanoids to prevent, rather than merely react to, imbalance, a foundational capability for agile and robust locomotion.

- **Integration of Biomechanical Principles into Robot Control:** The systematic translation of human postural control principles (e.g., Margin of Stability, time-to-boundary, ankle-knee-hip hierarchy, jerk minimization) into the reward library highlights the value of interdisciplinary approaches. This method demonstrates how insights from human motor control can be effectively leveraged to design more capable and robust robotic controllers.

- **Establishment of a Standardized Evaluation Benchmark:** The release of a method-agnostic, sim2sim benchmark for single-leg balance provides a crucial tool for the robotics community. This benchmark offers a reproducible and objective platform for:
 - **Cross-Method Comparison:** Allowing researchers to rigorously compare the performance of different single-leg balance policies under identical, deployment-predictive conditions.
 - **Competence Assessment:** Providing a standardized probe for evaluating the intrinsic single-leg balance competence of generalist humanoid policies, which often lack explicit balance-specific training.
 - **Deployment-Oriented Checkpoint Selection:** Enabling data-driven selection of the most robust policy checkpoint for real-world deployment, as demonstrated by the findings on the poor correlation between training reward and actual balance performance.

- **Foundation for Future Generalist Humanoids:** By treating single-leg balance as a measurable, foundational capability, this work contributes to the development of more generalist humanoid robots. Achieving robust balance is essential for versatile locomotion, manipulation, and interaction in unstructured environments. The benchmark and the proposed control design provide tools for future policies to integrate deep balance competence, rather than treating it as a specialized, context-dependent skill.

- **Practical Implications for Robot Development:** The successful qualitative deployment on a Unitree G1 without distillation demonstrates practical feasibility, suggesting potential for more rapid and reliable development of balance-aware controllers for real-world humanoid applications.
