# Robotic Foundation Models for Industrial Control: A Comprehensive Survey and Readiness Assessment Framework

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper presents a comprehensive survey of Robotic Foundation Models (RFMs) for industrial control and introduces an implication-grounded framework to assess their readiness for industrial deployment. An evaluation of 324 manipulation-capable RFMs using this framework revealed consistently low industrial maturity scores, highlighting significant gaps in areas like safety, real-time performance, and cost-effectiveness.

## Problem

- Industrial robotics, particularly with collaborative robots (cobots), requires adaptive and intuitive control methods to handle variability and facilitate rapid deployment, which traditional control struggles to provide.
- Despite advancements in generalist Robotic Foundation Models (RFMs), a systematic, industrially grounded assessment of their deployment readiness is lacking in the existing literature.
- Real-world industrial environments impose stringent constraints, including safety-critical operation, real-time performance on edge hardware, robustness, and cost-effectiveness, which are often overlooked in academic RFM evaluations.

## Method

- The authors conducted an extensive literature review using an ==automated pipeline==, followed by ==LLM-based filtering== and manual refinement, to build comprehensive corpora on **RFMs** and industrial implications.
- A novel, multi-layered ==industrial readiness framework== was developed, deriving 11 general industrial implications, 47 deployment-relevant attributes, and a detailed catalogue of 149 assessable criteria.
- Three hundred twenty-four manipulation-capable RFMs were evaluated against these 149 criteria using an automated, ==LLM-based evaluation pipeline== (==GPT-5.1==) with a ==conservative scoring== approach, designed to yield a cautious, lower-bound estimate of industrial maturity.

## Results

- The evaluation of **324 RFMs** showed low overall industrial maturity, with even the highest-rated models achieving total scores of only **0.11-0.12** (out of **1.0**), indicating a substantial gap from industry-grade deployment.
- Top-performing RFMs exhibited 'pronounced peaks' in only **one or two** specific industrial implications (e.g., Adaptability & Flexibility, Data Requirements), rather than comprehensive readiness across all criteria.
- Critical industrial implications such as Safety & Compliance (**3.4%** breadth, **0.019** average depth for top-50 models), Real-time Performance (**4.9%** breadth, **0.036** average depth), and Cost-Effectiveness & Integration (**7.1%** breadth) were consistently under-addressed across the entire RFM corpus.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current RFM research demonstrates a ceiling effect and a strong imbalance in addressing industrial requirements, with a consistent focus on specific technical aspects like adaptability and benchmarking, while largely neglecting crucial areas such as safety, real-time performance, and cost-effective integration.
> - Industrial readiness requires a holistic view beyond model performance, encompassing the entire RFM ecosystem, including software stacks, integration interfaces, monitoring, and safety infrastructure.
> - Future RFM development should prioritize layered system designs that treat planning, memory, action generation, verification, and safety gating as central, independently testable components to facilitate deployment in complex industrial settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of industrial robotics is undergoing a transformation, moving from highly optimized, isolated automation systems towards more flexible, collaborative paradigms. This shift is driven by the increasing adoption of Collaborative Robots (cobots) in environments requiring rapid commissioning, frequent redeployment across changing processes, and closer human-robot proximity. While cobots offer mechanical precision and repeatability, their utility is constrained by the availability of adaptive and intuitive control methods. This context has led to a renewed focus on learning-based robot intelligence capable of handling variability while adhering to industrial constraints such as safety, reliability, low-latency operation, and cost-effectiveness.

Recent advancements in large-scale learning have given rise to Foundation Models (FMs), which aim to reduce manual engineering effort through knowledge transfer across tasks. Within this development, Robotic Foundation Models (RFMs) are emerging as a promising avenue to provide generalist competence for embodied decision-making. This paper specifically focuses on RFMs capable of generating low-level actions for direct robot actuation, termed "RFMs for control" or "integrated RFMs," due to their strong coupling with real-world industrial constraints like latency and safety.

Existing literature includes numerous surveys on RFMs, particularly Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs), which are a distinct sub-category of RFMs. These surveys often focus on architectural taxonomies, challenges, learning paradigms, and post-training strategies. However, the authors identify a notable gap: a systematic, industrially grounded assessment of RFMs' deployment readiness is currently lacking. Previous industrially focused reviews are either too general, not addressing specific model types, or are limited to narrow aspects like VLMs in Human-Robot Collaboration. This research aims to bridge this gap by systematically connecting RFM methodological progress to the practical industrial constraints that determine deployability. It provides an implication-grounded assessment framework that aims to consolidate the rapidly expanding RFM landscape through an industrial lens, moving beyond isolated benchmark successes to a view of what constitutes credible candidates for industrial deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the industrial applicability of Robotic Foundation Models (RFMs) for control, providing a comprehensive overview of the RFM landscape and assessing its readiness for industrial deployment. This central objective is motivated by several factors:

1. **Transition in Industrial Robotics**: Industrial robotics is evolving towards flexible and collaborative deployment, especially with the rise of cobots in high-mix, low-volume settings and Small and Medium-sized Enterprises (SMEs). This transition necessitates robust, adaptive, and intuitive control methods that can cope with task, environment, and hardware variability, which traditional rule-based or specialized robotic control struggles to provide.
2. **Potential of RFMs**: RFMs promise to address the limitations of conventional control by offering generalist competence, cross-task/embodiment adaptability, and instruction/demonstration-driven reconfiguration, thereby reducing manual engineering effort and commissioning time. The paper specifically highlights the importance of RFMs for control, as this interface directly exposes industrial constraints like latency and safety.
3. **Lack of Industrial Applicability Assessment**: Despite the rapid growth in RFM research, a critical and systematic investigation of their practical applicability in industrial contexts is absent in the existing literature. Current surveys tend to focus on architectural or functional aspects, overlooking the multifaceted requirements imposed by real-world industrial environments.
4. **Complex Industrial Constraints**: Industrial environments present unique and stringent demands, including safety-critical operation (especially in human-robot shared workspaces), real-time performance on resource-constrained edge computing hardware, robustness to disturbances, precision and accuracy, cost-effectiveness, and the need for explainability and trust. These factors collectively create deployment pressures that expose the limitations of current RFMs when moving beyond laboratory settings.
5. **Need for a Structured Evaluation Framework**: The rapid pace of RFM development risks fragmentation and inflated claims without a structured approach to assess industrial readiness. A framework is needed to systematically analyze how industrial domains and use cases shape RFM requirements, with particular attention to collaborative robot platforms, heterogeneous sensing and actuation, edge-computing constraints, and safety-critical operation.

To achieve its primary objective, the paper outlines several specific contributions:
- Categorization of robotic control approaches, situating RFMs within the progression to generalist intelligence.
- A concrete definition and clustering of RFMs.
- A comprehensive overview of the current manipulation-capable RFM literature.
- An industrially motivated perspective on cobots and the amplified need for adaptable control methods.
- Synthesis of industrial deployment viewpoints into eleven interdependent implications.
- Development of an extensive, implication-grounded criteria catalogue (149 items) for industrial maturity assessment of RFMs and their ecosystems.
- A large-scale evaluation of the current RFM landscape against this catalogue to identify strengths, weaknesses, and research gaps.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-stage methodology to systematically survey the RFM landscape and assess its industrial readiness. The approach combines automated literature acquisition with a structured, multi-layered framework for deriving industrial implications and evaluating RFMs.

**A. Literature Acquisition and Corpus Composition:**
The authors developed a modular Python pipeline for automated article retrieval and filtering, ensuring comprehensive coverage and reproducibility.
1. **Automated Retrieval (DB-Search)**: The pipeline uses a structured Academic Search Query Syntax (ASQS) to query five academic databases (arXiv, Google Scholar, OpenAlex, Scopus, Semantic Scholar) within a specified date range (2022 onwards for RFMs, 2015 onwards for industrial implications). It retrieves metadata, merges results, and performs duplicate removal. Retrieval limits (e.g., 400 articles per database-query combination) were set to manage volume.
2. **LLM-based Filtering (Filter Process)**: A GPT-4o LLM instance was used for semantic relevance scoring, filtering articles based on title and abstract to ensure relevance to robotics and then specifically to RFMs or industrial implications. The LLM provided a binary relevance label, a confidence score, and a justification.
3. **Manual Refinement**: The automatically retrieved and filtered corpora were subjected to manual in-depth review, further filtering, enrichment (e.g., through chain-searching references), and correction to ensure quality and completeness.

This process generated several corpora:
- **RFM-Related Corpus (Main Corpus)**: Comprising 1,025 unique and highly relevant works, categorized into: 341 control or integrated RFMs (324 manipulation-focused or generalist), 321 related works, 231 high-level approaches (e.g., LLM-based planning), 77 data/benchmark papers, and 55 survey papers.
- **Industrial Implication Corpus**: Comprising 125 manually reviewed articles (from an initial 296 filtered records) that described industrial requirements, constraints, and deployment challenges for robotic systems.
- **Further Corpora**: 113 auxiliary publications on industrial robots, cobots, compute hardware, sensors, etc., gathered through additional automated and manual searches.

**B. Industrial Readiness Assessment Framework Development:**
The core of the methodology lies in developing a novel, multi-layered framework for industrial readiness assessment:
1. **Derivation of General Industrial Implications (11)**: An iterative qualitative synthesis of the 125 manually reviewed industrial implication papers was conducted. Recurring requirements, constraints, and deployment challenges were extracted, grouped, and refined into eleven interdependent, domain-agnostic industrial implications (e.g., Adaptability & Flexibility, Safety & Compliance, Real-Time Performance, Cost-Effectiveness).
2. **Identification of Deployment-Relevant Attributes (47)**: To operationalize the qualitative implications, 47 structured attributes capturing principal degrees of freedom for industrial RFM deployment were identified (e.g., Hardware, Security, Actions/Outputs, Precision & Accuracy, Documentation).
3. **Creation of a Criteria Catalogue (149)**: The 11 implications and 47 attributes were mapped to generate 11 x 47 = 517 conceptual implication-attribute combinations. These combinations guided the translation of industrial requirements into a comprehensive catalogue of 149 concrete, assessable criteria. These criteria cover both RFM-centric capabilities (e.g., online adaptation, multimodal input) and ecosystem requirements (e.g., documentation, integration interfaces), with intentional redundancy across implications to ensure thorough diagnosis of immaturity.

**C. RFM Evaluation Procedure:**
1. **LLM-assisted Evaluation Pipeline**: All 324 manipulation-capable RFMs were evaluated against the 149 criteria using an automated, LLM-based pipeline (GPT-5.1). Each of the 324 x 149 = 48,276 criterion-level decisions was performed in strict isolation: the LLM received one criterion and one paper (full text, including figures) at a time. This process was executed on private, secure infrastructure to prevent data leakage.
2. **Conservative Scoring**: The LLM was instructed to make conservative judgments, marking a criterion as "not fulfilled" unless clearly and explicitly supported by the paper's text. This approach yields a cautious, lower-bound estimate of industrial maturity.
3. **Structured Output**: For each criterion-paper pair, the LLM generated a binary fulfillment judgment, a flag for direct statement, a confidence score, an evidence availability signal, extracted quotes, and a brief rationale.
4. **Expert Agreement Comparison**: To validate the pipeline, three representative papers were independently annotated by human experts. The LLM's performance was then compared against these expert judgments. The pipeline achieved an accuracy of 0.966 and an F1-score of 0.762 for the positive class, with a low false-positive rate (1.9%) and a higher false-negative rate (22.6%), confirming the conservative bias. This validation supported the pipeline's reliability for generating corpus-level conclusions.

This methodical approach allowed for a scalable and transparent assessment of industrial readiness across a large body of RFM literature, providing both quantitative scores and qualitative insights into current gaps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 324 manipulation-capable Robotic Foundation Models (RFMs) against the 149 criteria revealed a consistent pattern of limited and uneven industrial maturity across the field.

**A. Overall Industrial Maturity:**
- **Low Overall Scores**: Even the highest-rated models achieved a total maturity score of only 0.11-0.12, meaning they satisfied approximately one out of ten criteria in the comprehensive catalogue. This indicates a significant gap between current RFM capabilities and the requirements for industry-grade deployment.
- **Ceiling Effect**: This pronounced ceiling effect suggests that no single model currently approaches comprehensive industrial readiness, despite impressive advancements in specific technical aspects.

**B. Performance of Highest-Rated RFMs:**
- **Implication-Specific Peaks**: The top five highest-rated RFMs did not demonstrate uniformly higher values across all implications. Instead, their maturity profiles exhibited "pronounced peaks" in one or two specific implications. This suggests that current research tends to address individual industrial bottlenecks rather than integrating broader requirements.
 - **Gemini Robotics 1.5** achieved the highest total score (0.12) and the strongest performance in **Adaptability & Flexibility (I1)** (0.44). This was attributed to its multi-component design, combining a VLA and a VLM, indicating that layered architectures may enhance flexibility.
 - **CollabVLA** scored highest within the top group for **HRI & HRC (I3)** (0.20) and **Sensor Fusion & Perception (I9)** (0.21), aligning with its explicit objective to integrate human guidance into action generation.
 - **GF-VLA** showed strong performance in **Data Requirements & Usage (I11)** (0.27), consistent with its focus on efficient utilization of demonstration data for generalization.
 - **OneTwoVLA** also showed high flexibility (I1: 0.33) through its dual-system architecture (fast acting, slower reasoning).
 - **SC-VLA** achieved high scores in **Robustness & Reliability (I4)** (0.21) and **Precision & Accuracy (I5)** (0.23), attributed to its self-correction mechanisms for failed actions.
- **Absolute Limits**: Even the best per-implication results remained limited, with the highest single implication fulfillment being 0.44 (Gemini Robotics 1.5 on I1), the only value exceeding 0.4 across all evaluated RFMs.

**C. Industrial Applicability Overview (Corpus-Level Trends):**
- **Temporal Stability of Research Focus**: An analysis of criteria coverage by publication year revealed a consistent profile. The research consistently concentrates on a small subset of implications, while others remain persistently low. This suggests a lack of convergence towards progressively more holistic industrial coverage over time.
- **Breadth vs. Depth Mismatch**:
 - **1+-Coverage (Breadth)**: A significant portion of the full corpus (324 RFMs) touched upon at least one criterion for **Adaptability & Flexibility (I1)** (75.6%), **Standardised Benchmarking & Evaluation (I10)** (75.3%), and **Data Requirements & Usage (I11)** (70.7%). However, critical implications like **Safety & Compliance (I2)** (3.4%), **HRI & HRC (I3)** (5.2%), **Real-time Performance (I6)** (4.9%), and **Cost-Effectiveness & Integration (I7)** (7.1%) showed very low breadth.
 - **Average Criteria Coverage (Depth)**: Even for the implications with high breadth, the average depth remained low, with ACC values below 0.10 for the full corpus (I1: 0.089, I10: 0.099, I11: 0.097). This indicates that while many papers acknowledge certain industrial concerns, they rarely provide multi-criterion, implementation-relevant coverage.
 - **Top-50 Subset**: Restricting the analysis to the top-50 models improved depth but did not fundamentally alter the picture. Breadth became near-universal for seven implications (I1, I4, I5, I8, I9, I10, I11), but I2, I3, I6, and I7 still lagged significantly. Depth improved (I1: 0.238, I10: 0.232, I11: 0.227), but **Safety & Compliance (I2)** (0.019) and **Real-time Performance (I6)** (0.036) remained particularly low.
- **Most Frequently Fulfilled Criteria**: The ten most commonly fulfilled criteria clustered heavily in I1, I10, and I11. These include "Multi-Task Generalisation" (49.38%), "Flexible Data Type Acceptance" (48.77%), "Repeatability & Reproducibility" (44.75%), and "Low-Data Adaptation & Few-Shot Requirements" (41.98%). This highlights a research focus on task generality, data flexibility, and benchmark reporting.
- **Evidence-Fulfilled-Gap Analysis**: A significant gap (often exceeding 0.8) was observed between the rate at which evidence for a criterion was found in a paper (EvidRate) and the rate at which the criterion was marked as fulfilled (FulfillRate). This indicates that many industrial desiderata are acknowledged or discussed in the literature, but rarely satisfied robustly or comprehensively under the conservative evaluation policy.

In summary, the results underscore that while RFM research is advancing in specific areas, comprehensive integration of industrial requirements, particularly regarding safety, real-time operation, and cost-effective integration, remains largely unaddressed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant implications for both the academic development of Robotic Foundation Models (RFMs) and their practical adoption in industrial settings.

**A. For RFM Research and Development:**
- **Shift from Benchmarks to Deployability**: The study highlights that isolated benchmark successes, while indicative of progress, are insufficient for industrial deployment. It advocates for a shift in research focus from mere performance metrics to an integrated consideration of deployability aspects, including safety, real-time feasibility, robustness, and cost-effectiveness.
- **Identification of Research Gaps**: The detailed criteria catalogue and subsequent evaluation clearly pinpoint areas of RFM research that are systematically underrepresented. Specifically, safety and compliance (I2), human-robot interaction and collaboration (I3), real-time performance (I6), and cost-effectiveness and integration capabilities (I7) consistently demonstrate low maturity. These areas represent critical research frontiers for advancing RFMs towards industrial applicability.
- **Guidance for Future RFM Design**: The implication-grounded assessment framework provides a transparent and structured reference for designing future RFMs. It encourages the development of layered systems where aspects like planning, memory, action generation, verification, and safety gating are treated as central, independently testable components, rather than monolithic visuo-motor policies.
- **Emphasis on Ecosystem Requirements**: The framework acknowledges that industrial readiness is not solely determined by the model but also by its surrounding ecosystem (software stack, interfaces, safety infrastructure, monitoring, logging, documentation). This encourages a holistic development approach that considers the entire deployment stack.
- **Call for Industrially Relevant Benchmarking**: The findings suggest leveraging the strong emphasis on benchmarking in the field (I10) to develop protocols that reflect deployment realities. These should include evaluating RFMs under distribution shifts, during failure recovery, with human supervision, and across long-horizon operations, moving beyond curated tasks to diagnose why a model fails under industrial conditions.

**B. For Industrial Adoption and Automation:**
- **Reference for Industrial Stakeholders**: The comprehensive criteria catalogue and the maturity assessment provide industrial practitioners and decision-makers with a clear, objective reference to evaluate emerging RFM technologies. It enables them to understand the current limitations and focus on technologies that address their specific deployment needs.
- **Informed Investment and Development**: By highlighting critical gaps, the report can inform investment strategies in both internal R&D and external collaborations. Industries can direct resources towards RFM developments that prioritize safety, real-time operation, and integration, which are paramount for productive use.
- **Accelerating Cobot Potential**: RFMs are identified as a critical enabling technology to unlock the full potential of cobot-centered automation. By addressing flexibility, reconfigurability, and intuitive instructioning, RFMs can help overcome current bottlenecks in cobot deployment, especially in cost- and changeover-sensitive manufacturing.
- **Bridging Lab-to-Fab Gap**: The research provides a structured bridge between academic laboratory advancements and industrial production environments. It emphasizes that translating RFMs from controlled settings to complex, variable, and safety-critical industrial operations requires deliberate design choices informed by real-world constraints.
- **Foundation for Regulatory and Standardization Efforts**: By systematically identifying and categorizing industrial requirements, the work contributes a foundational basis for future regulatory discussions and the development of industry standards for AI-driven robotics. This can help ensure that RFMs are auditable, safe, and compliant with evolving industrial norms.

In conclusion, this survey moves the discussion beyond isolated technical successes, providing a critical and comprehensive assessment that can guide the design and evaluation of RFMs towards reliable, industrially viable autonomous systems.
