# A Photonic Physically Unclonable Function's Resilience to Multiple-Valued Machine Learning Attacks

> [!summary] Summary
> A study from Southern Methodist University and Anametric, Inc. assessed a photonic Physically Unclonable Function's resistance to machine learning attacks using multiple-valued logic representations. Results indicate that approximately 1,000 Challenge-Response Pairs are required for models to achieve above-chance prediction accuracy, with higher non-power-of-two radices further impeding attack success.

## Problem

- Many electronic Physically Unclonable Functions (PUFs) are vulnerable to machine learning (ML) attacks capable of predicting responses from known Challenge-Response Pairs (CRPs).
- There is a need for new PUF architectures, such as photonic PUFs, that can inherently resist sophisticated ML modeling attacks.
- The specific resilience of photonic PUFs, particularly when their outputs are represented using Multiple-Valued Logic (MVL), against ML attacks had not been thoroughly quantified.

## Method

- A computational model of a ==24-cell photonic PUF== was developed to efficiently generate large datasets of ==Challenge-Response Pairs (CRPs)== for ML attack training and testing.
- A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) neural network was designed and trained as an ML attack model, evaluating its performance across various ==Multiple-Valued Logic (MVL) radices== for both challenges and responses.
- The methodology involved training **6,600 models**, systematically evaluating resilience across diverse CRP training set sizes (**10 to 100,000**) and numerous radix combinations using 5-fold cross-validation.

## Results

- Approximately **1,000** Challenge-Response Pairs (CRPs) were needed for ML models to achieve an average prediction accuracy above **50%** (random chance) across individual response bits.
- Even with **10,000** CRPs, ML models rarely surpassed **60%** average prediction accuracy in **31 out of 33** radix combinations, indicating strong overall resilience.
- ML attack models demonstrated the lowest prediction accuracies for radix-3 challenges (R_c=3) and higher non-power-of-two response radices, while binary responses (R_r=2) were the most susceptible to prediction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Photonic PUFs demonstrate inherent resilience against ML attacks, demanding a substantial number of CRPs to achieve prediction accuracy significantly better than random chance.
> - The choice of radix for representing challenges and responses in Multiple-Valued Logic (MVL) plays a critical role in determining the effectiveness of an ML attack.
> - Lower radices and those that are powers of two generally allow for more predictable PUF responses, whereas higher, non-power-of-two radices enhance the PUF's security by reducing attack efficacy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Physically Unclonable Functions (PUFs) are critical components in modern hardware security, offering a unique "fingerprint" for integrated circuits (ICs). They exploit microscopic, uncontrollable variations introduced during the manufacturing process to generate challenge-response pairs (CRPs) that are difficult to predict but easy to verify. This inherent randomness makes PUFs highly valuable for applications such as IC authentication, trusted system development, and hardware-level cybersecurity. The ideal "strong PUF" is designed to be unclonable, impossible to characterize completely, and to produce responses that are numerically unpredictable for unseen challenges, even when a large number of CRPs are known.

However, the broader research landscape has seen a significant challenge to the security of conventional, electronic PUFs: machine learning (ML) attacks. These attacks leverage known subsets of CRPs to train ML models (such as neural networks, support vector machines, or genetic algorithms) that can then predict responses to unknown challenges with accuracy better than random chance. Many PUF designs initially deemed "strong" have proven vulnerable to these sophisticated ML-based modeling attacks, undermining their security promises. This vulnerability has driven continuous efforts to either enhance existing PUF designs to resist ML attacks or explore entirely new PUF paradigms.

This paper addresses this crucial challenge by investigating **photonic PUFs** as a promising alternative. Photonic PUFs harness the highly sensitive manufacturing tolerances of photonic integrated circuit (PIC) components, using light as input and output. This optical nature offers several inherent advantages over conventional electronic PUFs:
1. **Complexity of Modeling:** The underlying physics of photonic interactions can be mathematically represented using potentially "infinitely many matrix-vector calculations," making it extremely difficult to create an explicit mathematical model for predicting CRPs.
2. **Difficulty of CRP Acquisition:** Collecting a large number of CRPs from a photonic PUF is inherently more challenging for an attacker due to the specialized optical interface and reduced susceptibility to physical and electromagnetic interference attacks, which are common for electronic PUFs.

The current work directly builds upon previous research by MacFarlane et al. (2023), which demonstrated that photonic PUF outputs can be modeled using Multiple-Valued Logic (MVL). The paper's contribution is to assess the resilience of this specific photonic PUF design against ML attacks that utilize MVL representations, thereby positioning it at the cutting edge of hardware security research focused on next-generation, ML-resistant PUFs. It aims to determine if the unique properties of photonic PUFs, particularly when combined with MVL encoding, provide a robust defense against state-of-the-art ML modeling attacks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **comprehensively investigate the susceptibility of a specific photonic Physically Unclonable Function (PUF) to Multiple-Valued Logic (MVL)-based machine learning (ML) attacks.** Specifically, the authors aim to determine the number of Challenge-Response Pairs (CRPs) required to train ML models that can predict responses from the photonic PUF with an accuracy significantly better than random chance.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical points in the cybersecurity and hardware security landscape:
1. **Vulnerability of Conventional PUFs:** As discussed, many electronic PUF implementations have been compromised by ML attacks. This necessitates the exploration of new PUF architectures that can inherently resist such attacks.
2. **Promise of Photonic PUFs:** Photonic PUFs offer unique physical characteristics that make them inherently more difficult to model and to extract CRPs from, positioning them as a strong candidate for next-generation secure hardware. However, their specific resilience against ML attacks, particularly those exploiting different data representations, needs thorough validation.
3. **Exploration of Multiple-Valued Logic (MVL):** Previous work indicated that photonic PUF outputs could be represented using MVL. This paper is motivated to explore whether MVL-based representations, both for challenges and responses, could either inadvertently create a "smoother optimization space" for an attacker's neural network to exploit, leading to improved predictive performance, or conversely, make the relationships harder to discern, thereby enhancing PUF security. Understanding this dual potential of MVL is crucial.
4. **Quantifying Resilience:** A key practical motivation is to quantify the minimum number of CRPs an attacker would need to gather to successfully compromise the photonic PUF. If this number is prohibitively high for a physical device, it substantiates the PUF's real-world resilience. The authors acknowledge that obtaining a vast number of CRPs from a physical photonic PUF is inherently challenging, making this quantification a direct measure of its practical security.

In essence, the research seeks to validate the hypothesis that photonic PUFs offer superior security against advanced ML attacks, specifically by investigating the impact of MVL representations on attack efficacy and determining the practical threshold of CRPs required for an attack to succeed.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a comprehensive experimental approach involving the simulation of a photonic PUF, the design and training of a neural network for ML attacks, and extensive evaluation across various parameters.

#### 3.1. A. Modeling the Photonic PUF

Given the impracticality of rapidly acquiring vast numbers of CRPs from a physical photonic PUF for ML training, the researchers developed a **computational model** of the photonic PUF.
- **Architecture:** The model simulates a 24-cell photonic PUF architecture, where each cell is provided the same 24-bit challenge and contributes one bit to an overall 24-bit response. This design aims to enhance statistical independence of CRPs compared to single-cell designs.
- **Underlying Principles:** The model is based on Jones calculus, a mathematical framework for representing the polarization of light, and incorporates insights from past experimental characterizations of PIC PUF components.
- **Data Generation:** The model accepts a 24-bit challenge and produces a 24-bit response, both derived from decimal-value representations of optical signals. This simulation capability allowed for the efficient generation of the large datasets of CRPs necessary for training and testing ML models.
- **Model Validation:** While not detailed in this paper, the authors mention that the model was validated by analyzing properties of the CRP distribution, including autocorrelation, bijectivity, challenge-response relationship, and quantile distribution, ensuring its fidelity to a real photonic PUF.

#### 3.2. B. The Machine Learning Attack Network

The core of the ML attack methodology involves a **neural network** designed to predict PUF responses.
- **Architecture:** A standard **multi-layer perceptron** (MLP) architecture was employed, consisting of six layers. This included five fully-connected hidden layers, each with 1024 neurons, followed by a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function and batch normalization. The final layer was also a fully-connected layer.
- **Input and Output Representation (MVL):**
 - **Challenge Input:** Challenges were represented as D<sub>c</sub>-digit vectors, encoded using a specific radix (R<sub>c</sub>). D<sub>c</sub> is determined by the number of digits needed to represent the maximum 24-bit value (2^24 - 1) in radix-R<sub>c</sub>.
 - **Response Output:** The network was designed to predict responses as D<sub>r</sub>-digit vectors, represented using a specific radix (R<sub>r</sub>). D<sub>r</sub> is similarly calculated based on R<sub>r</sub>. Model outputs were passed through a sigmoid activation scaled by (D<sub>r</sub> - 1) to constrain logits to the range [0, D<sub>r</sub> - 1].
 - **Radix Variations:** The study explored various combinations of R<sub>c</sub> = {2, 3, 4} and R<sub>r</sub> = {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 16, 27, 64}.
- **Model Parameters:** While the core architecture remained constant, the number of trainable parameters varied slightly (e.g., from 4,269,080 to 4,250,630) due to changes in D<sub>c</sub> and D<sub>r</sub>, ensuring a fair comparison across different radix encodings.

#### 3.3. C. Training Phase

- **Loss Function:** Mean-squared error (MSE) was used to compute the loss between the ground truth response and the predicted response, both represented in radix-R<sub>r</sub>.
- **Optimizer:** The Adam optimizer was used, coupled with a cosine learning rate schedule that included warm restarts (initial learning rate 0.001, 1,000 initial decay steps, multiplier of 2.0 for subsequent restarts).
- **Batch Size and Termination:** Batch size was set to max(number of training samples, 128) with a minimum of 1,000 training steps. Training terminated if loss did not decrease by more than 0.0001 over 100 steps.

#### 3.4. D. Evaluation Phase

- **Accuracy Metric:** To ensure a fair comparison across models using different MVL response representations, all predictions were converted to a standard 24-bit binary representation (D<sub>r</sub> = 24, R<sub>r</sub> = 2).
 1. **Rounding:** Continuous-valued predictions were rounded to the nearest integer.
 2. **Base-10 Conversion:** The D<sub>r</sub>-digit, radix-R<sub>r</sub> integer response was converted to a base-10 integer.
 3. **24-bit Binary Conversion:** The base-10 integer was then converted to a 24-bit binary response vector.
- **Accuracy Calculation:** Accuracy was computed by averaging the binary accuracy across all 24 bits of the predicted and ground truth responses. This ensured that a 50% accuracy always represented chance-level prediction.

#### 3.5. E. Experimental Design

- **Scale:** A total of **6,600 models** were trained and evaluated.
- **PUF Realizations:** Data from four simulated PUF realizations was used to account for potential variations.
- **CRPs Training Set Sizes:** A wide range of training set sizes was considered: {10, 30, 50, 100, 300, 1,000, 3,000, 5,000, 10,000, 30,000, 50,000, 100,000} CRPs.
- **Cross-Validation:** For each combination of PUF realization, R<sub>c</sub>, R<sub>r</sub>, and training set size, 5-fold cross-validation was applied. Each fold used a test set of 2 × 10^5 CRPs, disjoint from the training set.

This rigorous methodology allowed the researchers to systematically assess the photonic PUF's resilience under a vast array of ML attack scenarios and data representations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive evaluation of 6,600 trained models yielded several key findings regarding the photonic PUF's resilience to MVL-based machine learning attacks:

#### 4.1. A. Number of CRPs Required for Above-Chance Prediction

- **Threshold for Average Accuracy:** A crucial finding is that **approximately 1,000 Challenge-Response Pairs (CRPs) are necessary** for ML models to predict individual response bits with an average accuracy better than random chance (50%). Below this threshold, models generally perform no better than a coin flip.
- **Limited Efficacy with More CRPs:** Even with significantly more data, model performance often remained limited. In 31 out of 33 radix combinations tested, even 10,000 CRPs were not enough to train models that predict above 60% accuracy on average.
- **Maximum Accuracy vs. Average:** While maximum individual bit accuracies could reach much higher levels (e.g., 98.5% with 100,000 CRPs for R<sub>c</sub>=2, R<sub>r</sub>=2), the *average* accuracy across all 24 bits of the response remained substantially lower for practical training set sizes. This implies that even if an attacker could predict some bits with high accuracy, many other bits would remain unpredictable, maintaining the PUF's overall resilience.

#### 4.2. B. Impact of Radix Representation

- **Lower Radix Responses Perform Better for Attacks:** Models using lower-radix response representations (R<sub>r</sub>) generally achieved better prediction accuracy. Notably, models with R<sub>r</sub> = 2 (binary) consistently performed the best across all challenge radices (R<sub>c</sub>). Conversely, models with higher non-power-of-two radices, such as R<sub>r</sub> = {5, 6, 9, 10, 27}, were consistently among the worst performers.
 - **Hypothesis:** The authors hypothesize that higher-radix encodings might "obfuscate response bit relationships," making the learning task more challenging for the neural network. Lower radices, especially binary, might retain clearer, more separable relationships.
- **Powers of Two are More Predictable:** Both challenge (R<sub>c</sub>) and response (R<sub>r</sub>) radices that are powers of two generally led to more easily predictable responses.
 - Models trained with R<sub>r</sub> values that are powers of two (e.g., R<sub>r</sub> = 2, 4, 8, 16, 64) were more easily predicted than most other non-power-of-two radices.
 - Models with R<sub>c</sub> = 3 (not a power of two) were notably more difficult to predict across all response radices. For R<sub>c</sub> = 3, average and maximum accuracies only marginally increased above chance even with the largest number of CRPs considered (10^5).
 - **Hypothesis:** This effect might be linked to the native 24-bit (radix-2) representation of CRPs and the resolution constraints of measurement devices. The inherent radix-2-determined resolution could inform challenge-response relationships in ways that are lost or diluted when translated to higher radices not based on powers of two.

#### 4.3. C. Training Set Size and Accuracy Trends

- **Monotonic Increase:** Accuracy (average, maximum, and minimum) consistently increased or stayed the same as the number of training CRPs increased, as expected.
- **Rapid Increase in Maximum Accuracy:** Maximum individual bit accuracies increased more rapidly than average accuracies, especially for R<sub>c</sub>=2 and R<sub>c</sub>=4. For instance, maximum accuracy could be above chance with training sets of ~10-100 CRPs, whereas average accuracy required ~1,000 CRPs. This suggests that certain bits might be more vulnerable to prediction than others.

In summary, the most significant practical finding is the requirement of at least 1,000 CRPs to enable above-chance average prediction accuracy for the photonic PUF. Given the inherent difficulty in acquiring such a large dataset from a physical photonic PUF, this strongly supports its resilience against MVL-based ML attacks in practical scenarios. The study also offers valuable insights into the influence of radix choices on attack efficacy, suggesting potential avenues for future PUF design enhancements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant implications for the future of hardware security and the development of robust Physically Unclonable Functions (PUFs). Its potential impact spans both theoretical understanding and practical implementation:

#### 5.1. A. Validation of Photonic PUF Resilience

The primary significance lies in providing empirical evidence for the **resilience of photonic PUFs against Multiple-Valued Logic (MVL)-based machine learning (ML) attacks**. By demonstrating that an attacker would, on average, require at least 1,000 Challenge-Response Pairs (CRPs) to predict responses better than random chance, the authors strengthen the case for photonic PUFs as a viable and secure alternative to traditional electronic PUFs. This finding is particularly impactful because:
- **Practical Attack Difficulty:** The inherent challenges of acquiring a large number of accurate CRPs from a physical photonic PUF (due to its optical nature and resistance to conventional probing) make the 1,000 CRP threshold a significant barrier for attackers in real-world scenarios.
- **Novel Attack Vector Considered:** By specifically investigating MVL-based ML attacks, which could potentially exploit "smoother optimization spaces" for ML models, the research addresses a sophisticated and less-explored attack vector, thereby broadening the scope of validated resilience.

#### 5.2. B. Guidance for Photonic PUF Design and Optimization

The study provides valuable insights that can guide the design and optimization of future photonic PUFs:
- **Radix Choice as a Security Feature:** The finding that higher, non-power-of-two radices (e.g., R_c=3, or various R_r values) lead to lower prediction accuracies for ML models is a crucial design consideration. If future photonic PUFs can be engineered to natively or effectively represent their challenges and responses using these harder-to-predict radices, it could significantly enhance their inherent security against ML attacks, requiring even more effort from potential adversaries.
- **Focus on Statistical Properties:** The observed differences between maximum and average prediction accuracies highlight that some bits within the 24-bit response might be more predictable than others. Future research, as suggested by the authors, can focus on identifying and understanding these "less-resilient bits" and modifying PUF structures (e.g., combining PUF cells, as was done in this paper) to ensure more uniform unpredictability across all response bits.

#### 5.3. C. Contribution to Hardware Cybersecurity

In the broader context of hardware cybersecurity, this work contributes by:
- **Advancing Secure Hardware Primitives:** It pushes the envelope in developing new and more robust hardware security primitives. As IoT devices and critical infrastructure increasingly rely on hardware-level security, PUFs that can withstand advanced attacks are essential.
- **Informing Standard Development:** The quantitative assessment of CRP requirements can inform future standards and best practices for PUF design and evaluation, particularly for emerging optical PUF technologies.
- **Bridging Theory and Practice:** The collaboration between academia (SMU's Darwin Deason Institute) and industry (Anametric, Inc.) underscores a practical, application-oriented approach, ensuring that the research findings are relevant to real-world security challenges.

#### 5.4. D. Future Research Directions

The paper itself lays out several important avenues for future work, indicating its foundational nature:
- **Deeper MVL Model and Bit Performance Analysis:** Further investigation into *why* certain MVL representations and specific response bits are more or less predictable can lead to fundamental insights into PUF design principles.
- **Longer PUF Responses:** Exploring PUF designs with longer response lengths, potentially suitable for cryptographically secure applications, will be critical.
- **Statistical Property Analysis:** A detailed analysis of the PUF's statistical properties, such as uniqueness and uniformity, will further characterize its robustness and guide structural adjustments for improved resilience.

In conclusion, this research significantly bolsters the understanding of photonic PUFs' capabilities as a resilient hardware security primitive against sophisticated ML attacks. It not only validates their current robustness but also provides a clear roadmap for further enhancements, positioning them as a promising technology for future secure integrated circuits.
