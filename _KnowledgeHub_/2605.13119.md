# Towards Long-horizon Embodied Agents with Tool-Aligned Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers propose the "VLAs-as-Tools" framework to enable Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to reliably execute long-horizon embodied tasks by reframing them as callable tools for a high-level Vision-Language Model agent. This approach, using a VLA tool-family interface and Tool-Aligned Post-Training (TAPT), demonstrates improved task success rates (e.g., +35.5 points for OpenVLA-OFT on RoboTwin) and enhanced instruction following fidelity in simulated environments.

## Problem

- Current VLA models face a "dual burden" for long-horizon tasks, requiring extensive closed-loop planning and diverse physical operations within a single monolithic policy, which is difficult with current data and model scale.
- End-to-end VLAs exhibit limitations in long-horizon reasoning and planning and can be prone to biases from visual priors or demonstration regularities, overriding explicit language instructions.
- Existing embodied agentic systems, while capable of high-level planning, often rely on less capable, frequently manually specified, or task-specific robotic tools for physical execution.

## Method

- The "VLAs-as-Tools" strategy formalizes VLAs as a family of bounded, callable executors within an embodied agentic control loop, managed by a high-level Vision-Language Model (VLM) agent.
- A bidirectional VLA tool-family interface is established, enabling the VLM agent to send discrete invocation messages to select specific VLA tools and receive continuous progress feedback for efficient, event-triggered replanning.
- Tool-Aligned Post-Training (TAPT) adapts base VLAs to function as reliable tools by structuring training data around bounded invocations and employing tool-family residual parameterization, which provides distinct execution paths for each tool while preserving shared representations.

## Results

- The framework improved long-horizon task success rates, with gains such as +35.5 points for OpenVLA-OFT on RoboTwin and +5.2 points for OpenVLA on LIBERO-Long when compared to standalone SFT baselines.
- Invocation fidelity was substantially enhanced, demonstrating +34.6 points in Faithful Rate and +16.2 points in Non-biased Rate for OpenVLA-OFT on the LIBERO-CF-Long counterfactual benchmark.
- Agent planning efficiency increased significantly, reducing the number of high-level VLM calls per task from 109.5 to 1.988 for OpenVLA, while achieving effective replanning success rates (e.g., 61.4% for 0.5 ).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reframing VLAs as specialized, callable tools within an agentic framework effectively distributes the computational and cognitive burden of long-horizon tasks, allowing high-level planning and low-level execution to be handled by distinct components.
> - Explicit invocation-aligned training through TAPT is critical for improving a VLA's fidelity to instructions, ensuring it faithfully executes commanded subtasks rather than defaulting to visual priors or dataset regularities.
> - Continuous progress feedback from VLA tools to the high-level VLM agent provides a lightweight and efficient mechanism for timely intervention and replanning, significantly reducing the frequency and cost of VLM queries compared to constant monitoring.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of embodied artificial intelligence, specifically focusing on the capabilities of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robot control. Recent advancements in VLA models, such as OpenVLA-OFT, RDT-1B, and GR00T N1, have demonstrated effectiveness in general-purpose robot control and cross-embodiment generalization. However, their application to long-horizon tasks is constrained by the demands of extensive closed-loop planning, diverse physical operations, and the limitations of current embodied data and model scale.

The broader landscape features two primary strategies for addressing long-horizon embodied tasks. One approach involves extending end-to-end VLA policies to incorporate internal planning mechanisms, such as semantic subtask prediction and task-level planning tokens, as seen in models like π0.5. The second approach adopts an agentic formulation, wherein large language models (LLMs) or vision-language models (VLMs) act as high-level planners, selecting skills, composing programs, or invoking robot APIs, a paradigm exemplified by systems like SayCan and Code as Policies. This agentic approach has shown success in digital domains by leveraging high-capacity LLMs for goal decomposition, state tracking, replanning, and error recovery, with tools providing bounded and reliable execution interfaces.

A recognized gap exists between these two paradigms in embodied AI: end-to-end VLAs offer robust physical execution capabilities but exhibit limitations in long-horizon reasoning, while existing embodied agents provide stronger planning capabilities but often rely on less capable, frequently manually specified, physical tools. Prior research has also indicated that VLA policies may exploit visual priors or dataset regularities rather than faithfully grounding language instructions, leading to issues like counterfactual failures where visual cues override specified instructions. The current work positions itself to bridge this gap by integrating the strengths of both paradigms: leveraging a planning-capable VLM agent for high-level reasoning and recovery, while employing specialized VLA models as robust, instruction-faithful physical tools for bounded subtask execution. This aims to enhance the usability and reliability of VLAs within an agentic framework for complex, multi-step robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to effectively handle long-horizon embodied tasks. The motivation stems from observed limitations of current VLA models in this domain. While VLAs have advanced general-purpose robot control, their direct application as end-to-end controllers for complex, multi-step tasks faces a "dual burden." This burden involves the need for extended closed-loop planning, which current VLA models struggle with due to data and scale constraints, and the requirement for diverse physical operations within a single monolithic policy, making tasks like decomposition, progress tracking, failure recovery, and skill composition challenging.

The authors identify a "complementary gap" in existing approaches:
1. **End-to-end VLAs:** Possess strong physical execution capabilities but exhibit weaknesses in long-horizon reasoning and planning. They can be prone to biases from scene priors or demonstration regularities, potentially failing to strictly follow invoked instructions.
2. **Embodied Agentic Systems:** Offer robust high-level planning, state tracking, and error recovery through large language or vision-language models (LLMs/VLMs), but typically rely on weaker, often manually specified or task-specific, robot tool sets for physical execution.

The core motivation is to bridge this gap by combining the strengths of both paradigms. The proposed "VLAs-as-Tools" strategy aims to distribute the computational and cognitive burden of long-horizon tasks. A high-level Vision-Language Model (VLM) agent is envisioned to manage temporal reasoning, global planning, and recovery, while a family of specialized VLA tools is designed to execute diverse, bounded local physical operations. This distribution allows each VLA tool to operate within a focused execution scope, where it can be efficiently adapted and reliably map visual-language inputs to continuous robot actions.

To realize this strategy, two core challenges are addressed:
1. **Making VLAs callable and reliable physical tools:** This involves ensuring that VLA execution faithfully adheres to invoked instructions, rather than being biased by visual context or demonstration priors. It also requires preventing task-specific adaptation from degrading pretrained semantic understanding and generalization.
2. **Integrating VLAs effectively into an embodied agentic system:** This necessitates a seamless communication mechanism between the high-level VLM agent and the low-level VLA tools, enabling efficient event-triggered replanning without continuous, costly agent polling.

In summary, the detailed motivation is to transform general VLA models from limited standalone long-horizon agents into scalable, instruction-faithful physical tools that can be reliably invoked, monitored, and composed by a high-level VLM agent, thereby improving overall task success, robustness, and efficiency for complex robot manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the "VLAs-as-Tools" strategy, which formalizes VLAs not as monolithic long-horizon policies, but as a family of bounded, callable executors within an embodied agentic control loop. This strategy is instantiated through two main components: a VLA tool-family interface and Tool-Aligned Post-Training (TAPT).

#### 3.1. Bidirectional Agent–Tool Interaction via a VLA Tool-Family Interface

The interface, denoted as I = (C, R), defines the communication between the high-level VLM agent and the VLA tool family.

##### 3.1.1. Invocation Messages (C)

- **Structure:** Each message `c_k` sent by the agent to a selected VLA tool comprises two fields: `c_k = (g_k, z_k)`.
 - `g_k` is a discrete tool-family label (e.g., "grasp," "open," "place") that explicitly selects a member from the VLA tool family T = {T_g}.
 - `z_k` is a scene-grounded subtask instruction (e.g., "grasp the red block") that specifies the object, relation, and desired local effect for the selected tool family.
- **Rationale:** This factorization makes the agent's request transparent. The explicit `g_k` allows for distinct action distributions and termination conditions for visually similar but functionally different instructions. It also provides a stable signal for TAPT to organize data and parameters for tool-family specialization.

##### 3.1.2. Feedback Messages (R)

- **Structure:** Feedback messages are returned by the VLA tool to the agent for monitoring its execution. The primary feedback mechanism is a continuous progress signal `p_t ∈`.
 - Values near 0 indicate minimal progress, while values near 1 suggest near completion of the current invocation.
 - This signal is local to the bounded call `c_k`, measuring progress on that specific subtask, not the overall long-horizon task.
- **Rationale:** Continuous progress feedback provides timely information, allowing the agent to detect stagnation or deviation before a subtask fully succeeds or fails. This enables low-frequency but responsive replanning, avoiding the computational cost of continuous querying of a large VLM or the latency of a binary completion signal. An auxiliary head `ˆp_t = ψ_ω(b_t)` attached to the VLA backbone predicts this progress signal.

#### 3.2. Improving VLA Tool Usability with Tool-Aligned Post-Training (TAPT)

TAPT is a VLA-level adaptation method designed to train a base VLA to function as a reliable, callable tool, aligning its behavior with the VLA tool-family interface.

##### 3.2.1. Invocation-Aligned Training Units

- **Data Preparation:** TAPT re-structures training data around bounded invocations that match the agent's calls during inference.
 - **Imitation Learning (IL):** Demonstrations are segmented into bounded windows, each annotated with an invocation `c = (g, z)` and a progress target `p*_1:T`. These segments are derived from task annotations, state changes (e.g., gripper state, relative velocity), contact events, and automatic multimodal labeling, ensuring a clear correspondence between invocation and intended behavior. An example IL unit is `x_IL = (o_1:T, z, g, a_1:T, p*_1:T)`.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** For each invocation `(g, z)`, a bounded subtask rollout is constructed. The rollout starts from states (`S_0_z,g`) where that invocation should begin, obtained from demonstration boundaries or successful preceding subtask executions. A local completion predicate `ψ_z,g(s)` (e.g., contact, containment, relative pose) evaluates success within a horizon `H_k`. The RL reward is 1 if the predicate becomes true within `H_k`, else 0.
- **Shared Objective:** Both IL and RL are framed to optimize for the reliable execution of the current bounded invocation, teaching actions, progress prediction, and task completion at the subtask level.

##### 3.2.2. Tool-Family Residual Parameterization

- **Architecture:** The VLA tool family is implemented using a shared pretrained VLA backbone (`f_Θ`) combined with deterministic tool-family residual adapters.
 - For each selected linear layer `W` in the backbone, a bank of low-rank residual adapters (`{∆W_g}_g∈G`), one for each tool family, is added. Each adapter `∆W_g` is a low-rank matrix decomposition (`B_g A_g`), where `r` (rank) is significantly smaller than `min(d_in, d_out)`.
 - Given a hidden activation `x` and a selected family `g`, the adapted layer computes `y = Wx + ∆W_gx`.
- **Functionality:** The tool-family label `g` deterministically routes the call through its specific lightweight LoRA residual adapter. This provides each tool family with a distinct execution path while preserving shared visual-language representations from the backbone. This approach introduces a small parameter overhead for specialization.

##### 3.2.3. Tool-Aligned Post-Training Objective

- **Multi-task Loss:** TAPT post-trains the VLA on a broad invocation-aligned corpus (`D_inv_mid`) before benchmark-specific adaptation. The objective combines two supervised signals:
 - **Action-cloning term:** Trains the selected residual path (`ϕ_g`) to reproduce the actions for the invocation, minimizing `−∑ log π_θ,ϕg(a_t | o_t, z, h_t)`.
 - **Progress term:** Trains the auxiliary feedback head to predict invocation progress, minimizing `λ_prog ∑ ||ˆp_t − p*_t||^2_2`.
- **Joint Optimization:** This objective jointly updates the tool-family residual adapters and the progress head, ensuring the resulting VLA learns both to execute the invocation and to return accurate progress feedback. Downstream Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and RL then reuse these same interface and objectives on benchmark-specific data.

The overall approach creates a VLA tool family `T_θ,Φ = {T_g}_g∈G` that can accept an invocation `c = (g, z)`, execute the selected tool, and provide progress feedback, facilitating robust and efficient long-horizon embodied task completion.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the "VLAs-as-Tools" strategy at both the policy level (invocation-aligned execution) and the agent-loop level (overall effectiveness in long-horizon control) across various VLA backbones (OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, π0.5) and benchmarks (LIBERO-Long, RoboTwin, CALVIN, LIBERO-CF-Long).

#### 4.1. Improved Long-Horizon Embodied Performance (Supervised Downstream Adaptation)

- **Comparison:** The study compared three deployment modes: a standalone SFT-trained VLA, a standard SFT-trained VLA with a direct planner (periodically issuing language instructions), and a TAPT-trained VLA with the VLA tool-family interface.
- **Results (Table 1):** Deploying a standard SFT policy with a direct planner was insufficient; it often led to performance degradation (e.g., -11.8 points for OpenVLA-OFT on LIBERO-Long). In contrast, TAPT with the VLA tool-family interface consistently improved success rates over standalone SFT baselines. Notable gains included +5.2 points for OpenVLA on LIBERO-Long, +35.5 points for OpenVLA-OFT on RoboTwin, and +23.1 points for π0.5 on RoboTwin.
- **Conclusion:** The benefit stems from making the VLA executor tool-aligned and reliable under agent–tool interaction, rather than merely wrapping a VLA with a planner.

#### 4.2. Improved Long-Horizon Embodied Performance (Reinforcement Learning in Target Domain)

- **Comparison:** Experiments included Standard RL (full-task RL without invocation-aligned units), Invocation-aligned RL (IA RL), TAPT, and the full TAPT + VLA tool-family interface.
- **Results (Figure 3):** The full configuration outperformed Standard RL, with +3.8 and +11.2 points improvements for OpenVLA-OFT and π0.5 on LIBERO-Long, respectively. The VLA tool-family interface further added +2.6 and +2.0 points over TAPT alone. On CALVIN, success increased from 76.0% (Standard RL) to 82.3% (full TAPT).
- **Conclusion:** The proposed strategy also enhances RL-based training, and its benefits are observed across different subtask structures (both custom splits and CALVIN's native structure).

#### 4.3. Improved Invocation Fidelity

- **Evaluation:** Tested on LIBERO-CF-Long, a counterfactual benchmark designed to assess whether the VLA executor faithfully follows the invoked instruction rather than defaulting to source-task biases. Metrics included "Faithful Rate" (completing the counterfactual goal) and "Non-biased Rate" (avoiding the original source-task bias).
- **Results (Table 2):** From SFT to full TAPT, OpenVLA-OFT showed improvements of +34.6 points in Faithful Rate and +16.2 points in Non-biased Rate. Similarly, π0.5 improved by +30.0 and +15.0 points, respectively.
- **Conclusion:** TAPT strengthens the invocation–behavior binding, which is essential for reliable tool use, leading to higher instruction alignment.

#### 4.4. Core Design Ablation

- **Component Contributions (Table 3):**
 - Invocation-aligned SFT (subtask-centric supervision) alone improved success rates by approximately 1%.
 - TAPT (IA Post-train and tool-family residual adapters) steadily improved OpenVLA-OFT. For π0.5, IA Post-train alone initially reduced success, suggesting a data mismatch, but residual adapters recovered this and led to overall improvement.
 - The interface-only setting (planner wrapping without tool-aligned training) performed poorly (e.g., 80.2% for OpenVLA-OFT), confirming that tool-alignment is critical for executor reliability.
 - The full configuration achieved the highest success rates (95.6% for OpenVLA-OFT, 97.2% for π0.5), indicating the synergistic benefits of TAPT and the VLA tool-family interface.

#### 4.5. Agent Planning Efficiency

- **Comparison:** Progress-based replanning via the VLA tool-family interface was compared against a direct VLM monitoring baseline (querying the VLM every 5 steps).
- **Results (Table 4):** The VLA tool-family interface substantially reduced the number of high-level VLM calls per task (e.g., from 109.5 to 1.988 for OpenVLA, from 58.2 to 0.304 for OpenVLA-OFT). Despite lower query frequency, progress-triggered intervention achieved significant replan success rates (e.g., 61.4% for π0.5).
- **Conclusion:** Progress feedback provides a lightweight and efficient alternative to frequent VLM monitoring for timely intervention and replanning.

#### 4.6. Tool Family Residual Adapter Cost

- **Overhead (Table 5):** The residual adapters introduced a minor overhead. For OpenVLA-OFT, parameters increased by 9% and inference time by 7%. For the lighter π0.5 backbone, the adapted model remained small (0.50x the OpenVLA parameter baseline), though its inference time was slightly higher.
- **Conclusion:** The tool-family parameterization enables specialization with minimal additional computational cost, avoiding the need for a full, separate VLA model for each tool family.

#### 4.7. Qualitative Analysis

- **Failure Recovery (Figure 4):** A qualitative example illustrated how the agent–tool loop prevents cascading failures. In a baseline rollout, a local error (dropping an object) led to a complete task failure because the monolithic policy continued from an invalid state. In contrast, the proposed method's progress monitoring detected the failure, triggered replanning, and allowed the agent to re-invoke the tool for recovery, demonstrating enhanced robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "VLAs-as-Tools" framework represents a significant contribution to the field of embodied AI by addressing critical limitations in how Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are utilized for complex, long-horizon tasks.

**Key Significance:**

- **Enhanced Composability and Modularity:** The framework re-conceptualizes VLAs from monolithic, end-to-end controllers into callable, specialized executors. This modular design allows a high-level VLM agent to decompose complex goals, plan sequences of actions, monitor progress, and manage recovery, while specialized VLA tools handle the bounded, low-level physical executions. This separation of concerns significantly enhances the composability of robotic systems, making them more adaptable to diverse and extended tasks.
- **Improved Instruction Following and Reliability:** The Tool-Aligned Post-Training (TAPT) methodology directly addresses the challenge of VLA models being biased by visual priors or demonstration regularities. By aligning training data, model architecture (residual adapters), and objectives with explicit invocation units, TAPT strengthens the binding between agent commands and the resulting physical behavior. This leads to higher "invocation fidelity," where the VLA tools more faithfully execute specified instructions, a crucial property for reliable tool use in agentic systems.
- **Increased Efficiency and Scalability:** The VLA tool-family interface, particularly through its continuous progress feedback, allows for efficient event-triggered replanning by the high-level VLM agent. This significantly reduces the frequency of costly VLM queries compared to continuous monitoring baselines, improving the overall computational efficiency of the agentic loop. Furthermore, the tool-family residual adapters enable parameter-efficient specialization, allowing the creation of multiple specialized VLA tools on top of a shared backbone with minimal overhead, promoting scalability.
- **Robustness in Dynamic Environments:** By enabling timely progress feedback and agent-level replanning, the framework improves the system's robustness to execution failures. Local errors can be detected and corrected through subtask-level recovery, preventing cascading failures that often plague monolithic long-horizon policies. This capability is vital for deploying robots in real-world, unpredictable environments.

**Potential Impact:**

The principles and methods introduced in this paper have the potential to impact the development of generalist robot policies and intelligent autonomous agents. By enabling VLAs to function as reliable, extensible tools, this work contributes to building more capable and robust robots that can:
- **Perform more complex tasks:** The ability to handle long-horizon tasks with improved reliability opens doors for robots to tackle intricate manipulation sequences in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and domestic settings.
- **Be more easily programmed and adapted:** The clear agent-tool interface and invocation-aligned training could simplify the process of specifying and adapting robot behaviors, potentially lowering the barrier for developers and users.
- **Facilitate generalization:** By separating high-level reasoning from low-level execution, the framework might promote better generalization across different tasks and environments, as the VLM agent can abstract away task-specific physical details.
- **Inspire future research:** The identified "complementary gap" and the proposed solution provide a clear direction for integrating large foundation models with robotic control, encouraging further research into robust agent-tool interaction, improved invocation alignment, and real-world deployment.

A recognized limitation, focusing on simulated manipulation benchmarks, highlights an important direction for future work: extending this robust tool interface and post-training methodology to diverse real-robot settings will be crucial for realizing its full potential in practical applications.
