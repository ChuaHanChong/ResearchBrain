# Learning to Reason Efficiently with Discounted Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Amazon and the University of Alberta developed a discounted reinforcement learning approach to train Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) for efficient reasoning. This method enables LRMs to generate significantly shorter reasoning sequences, reducing mean response length by 13-37%, while consistently maintaining or slightly improving Pass@1 accuracy across various mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) generate excessively long 'chains of thought,' leading to high computational costs, memory usage, inference latency, and reduced serving throughput.
- A common belief in the field suggests an inherent trade-off where shorter reasoning paths inevitably lead to reduced accuracy.
- Existing methods for LRM efficiency often rely on heuristic length penalties or specific data curation, lacking a robust theoretical foundation for simultaneously optimizing accuracy and conciseness.

## Method

- The work models LRM reasoning as a finite horizon ==discounted Markov Decision== Process (MDP) with a binary correctness reward applied by a verifier.
- It leverages Blackwell optimality, which theoretically guarantees that policies optimal for discount factors sufficiently close to one maximize undiscounted success (accuracy) and, among these, minimize expected trajectory length.
- A practical training recipe is introduced using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), applying discounting only to the extrinsic correctness reward, incorporating KL regularization, and masking non-reasoning tokens from discounting.

## Results

- Mean response length was reduced by 13-**22%** on GSM8K and MATH for Qwen2.5 **7B**-Instruct and Llama 3 **8B**-Instruct models, while Pass@1 accuracy was maintained or slightly increased.
- On advanced math benchmarks (AMC 2023, AIME 2025, MINERVA, OLYMPIAD), Phi-4 and Qwen2.5 **14B**-Instruct achieved 21-**37%** length reductions, with some instances showing notable Pass@1 accuracy improvements (e.g., Phi-4 on AMC 2023 from **51%** to **61%**).
- Experiments varying the discount factor demonstrated that stronger discounting reduces length, but overly aggressive discounting eventually compromises accuracy, confirming the theoretical boundary for optimal conciseness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Blackwell optimal policies inherently achieve maximal accuracy and, among accuracy maximizers, minimal expected trajectory length, challenging the assumption of an unavoidable length-accuracy trade-off within certain policy classes.
> - A 'Blackwell discount factor' (γ_bw < 1) exists, defining a range for the discount factor where optimal policies simultaneously maximize correctness and minimize response length.
> - Empirical findings confirm that with a properly chosen discount factor, LRMs can generate substantially shorter reasoning traces without sacrificing accuracy, and sometimes even improving it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses the computational and latency challenges associated with Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) that generate extensive intermediate reasoning tokens. This issue positions the work within the broader field of efficient artificial intelligence, particularly concerning the deployment and scalability of large language models (LLMs). The paper frames the process of LRM reasoning as a goal-reaching sequential decision problem, where the objective is to achieve a correct answer quickly.

Existing research has explored several avenues to promote efficient reasoning in LLMs. These include:
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) with length-based penalties:** Methods that introduce explicit costs for each token or step taken during policy optimization (Arora & Zanette, 2025; Su & Cardie, 2025; Ling et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025).
- **Curated data approaches:** Techniques involving fine-tuning models on datasets of variable-length or compressed reasoning traces, aiming to internalize concise reasoning patterns (Fatemi et al., 2025; Hammoud et al., 2025; Qiao et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Shrivastava et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2025).
- **Prompt control:** Strategies that manipulate prompts to explicitly instruct models to generate more concise reasoning (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025; Dumitru et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025).

While RL post-training has been shown to improve the accuracy of LRMs, it often inadvertently leads to longer responses, which exacerbates the problems of increased inference cost and latency (Liu et al., 2025a). The present work challenges the common assumption that longer responses inherently lead to improved accuracy, suggesting that there is no fundamental trade-off between length and accuracy up to an instance-dependent threshold.

This paper proposes "plain old discounting" from reinforcement learning theory as a principled and instance-aware mechanism for efficient reasoning. It draws a connection between discounted RL and stochastic shortest path problems, where each reasoning token is analogous to an extra step, and the objective is to find the shortest successful trajectory. The theoretical foundation leverages Blackwell optimality (Blackwell, 1962), a concept from dynamic programming that deals with policies optimal for discount factors sufficiently close to one. This approach aims to unify the objectives of maximizing accuracy and minimizing reasoning length, thereby positioning the work as a theoretical and practical contribution to the development of more efficient and capable reasoning models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a method for training Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) that can reach correct answers effectively (i.e., with high accuracy) and efficiently (i.e., with concise reasoning), without sacrificing accuracy.

The motivation for this objective stems from several practical and theoretical considerations:

- **Computational Cost and Latency:** LRMs, especially when engaged in complex reasoning tasks such as solving math or code problems, generate a substantial number of intermediate "reasoning tokens" before producing a final answer. This token generation process is computationally intensive, consuming significant compute and memory resources. The quadratic attention mechanism and the growing key-value (KV) cache further contribute to increased inference costs and latency, ultimately reducing serving throughput in deployment scenarios.
- **Trade-off between Accuracy and Length:** While reinforcement learning (RL) post-training has demonstrated success in improving LRM accuracy, it frequently results in longer response chains (Liu et al., 2025a). The paper highlights a contested debate regarding the inherent relationship between the length of a chain of thought and its accuracy (Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b; Lu et al., 2025; Fatemi et al., 2025). The authors hypothesize that a direct trade-off is not always necessary and that response length can be reduced up to a certain instance-dependent threshold without a corresponding drop in accuracy.
- **Analogy to Stochastic Shortest Path (SSP) Problems:** The problem of reaching a correct answer with minimal reasoning tokens can be conceptualized as a stochastic shortest path problem. Each emitted reasoning token represents a step, and the goal is to find the shortest successful trajectory to a correct terminal state. This analogy provides a theoretical lens for applying reinforcement learning techniques designed for efficiency.
- **Leveraging Blackwell Optimality:** The authors are motivated by the concept of Blackwell optimality (Blackwell, 1962; Puterman, 2014; Grand-Clément & Petrik, 2023). This principle suggests that for discount factors (γ) sufficiently close to 1, an optimal policy will prioritize maximizing the undiscounted reward (accuracy) and, among policies achieving maximal accuracy, will then select those with the shortest expected trajectory length. This provides a formal theoretical justification for using discounting to encourage conciseness without compromising correctness.
- **Principled Mechanism:** The paper aims to introduce "plain old discounting" as a principled, instance-aware mechanism for achieving efficient reasoning, differentiating it from ad-hoc length penalties or data curation methods. It builds on the understanding that a small per-step negative reward in finite horizon MDPs with binary rewards is mathematically equivalent to discounting (Bertsekas, 2012).

In summary, the motivation is to address a critical practical bottleneck in LRM deployment by theoretically establishing and empirically validating a method that encourages concise reasoning while maintaining or improving accuracy, using a fundamental concept from reinforcement learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research integrates theoretical insights from Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) and Blackwell optimality with practical training strategies for Large Reasoning Models (LRMs).

#### 3.1. Theoretical Framework

The core theoretical approach models LRM reasoning as a finite horizon discounted Markov Decision Process (MDP), specified by the tuple $M = (S, A, P, r, H, \gamma, \mu)$.
- **States ($S$)**: Represent token sequences.
- **Actions ($A$)**: Correspond to vocabulary tokens, including a special `eos` (end-of-sequence) action.
- **Transitions ($P$)**: Deterministically append the chosen token to the current sequence, forming the next state.
- **Reward ($r$)**: A binary terminal verifier. In the "RL with verifiable rewards" (RLVR) setting, $r(S_t, \text{eos}) = 1$ if $S_t$ contains a correct answer, and $0$ otherwise. For non-`eos` actions, $r(S_t, a) = 0$.
- **Horizon ($H$)**: A finite integer representing the maximum sequence length.
- **Discount Factor ($\gamma$)**: A value in $[0, 1)$, central to the proposed method.
- **Initial State Distribution ($\mu$)**: Distribution over questions.

The undiscounted finite horizon return ($J_1(\pi)$) under this reward structure directly corresponds to the Pass@1 accuracy, defined as the probability of generating a correct solution within $H$ steps.

The theoretical analysis centers on **Blackwell optimality**, a stronger notion of optimality where a policy is optimal for all discount factors sufficiently close to one.
- **Uniform Taylor Expansion (Lemma 3.5)**: The paper demonstrates that the discounted return $J_\gamma(\pi)$ can be expressed as $p(\pi)[1 - \epsilon (L(\pi) - 1)] + R_\pi(\epsilon)$, where $\epsilon = 1 - \gamma$, $p(\pi)$ is the success probability (accuracy), and $L(\pi)$ is the conditional successful path length. This expansion makes explicit the Stochastic Shortest Path (SSP) structure induced by discounting, revealing that as $\gamma \to 1$, the objective prioritizes accuracy and then, among accuracy maximizers, minimizes path length.
- **Theorem 3.6 (Blackwell Optimality and Shortest Path)**: This theorem formally establishes that in finite-horizon MDPs with a deterministic binary terminal verifier, every Blackwell optimal policy is a shortest path policy. This means it simultaneously maximizes the probability of success and, among all policies that achieve maximal success, minimizes the expected trajectory length.
- **Existence for Restricted Policy Classes (Theorem 3.8 and Lemma 3.10)**: The paper considers training with softmax policies and deploying their greedified versions. The resulting deployment policy class ($\Sigma$) is finite. For such finite policy classes, the existence of a Blackwell discount factor ($\gamma_{bw} < 1$) is proven, implying that for any $\gamma \in (\gamma_{bw}, 1)$, the $\gamma$-optimal policies are constant and correspond to the Blackwell optimal set. This ensures that a single choice of $\gamma$ (sufficiently close to 1) can consistently achieve the desired accuracy-then-length objective across instances.
- **Blackwell Discount Factor ($\gamma_{bw}$)**: This factor quantifies how "hard" it is for discounting to implement SSP tie-breaking without sacrificing success probability. The existence of a uniform critical discount factor ($\gamma_{crit}$) for any finite collection of instances implies that a single $\gamma$ choice can preserve instance-wise SSP optimality.

#### 3.2. Practical Training Recipe

Guided by the theoretical framework, the authors propose a four-component training methodology:

1. **Selective Discounting of Rewards**: A discount factor $\gamma \in (0, 1)$ is applied exclusively to the *environment (correctness) reward* ($r_e(\tau)$). Intrinsic formatting or shaping rewards ($r_f(\tau)$) provided by the learner are kept undiscounted. The objective optimized is $R(\tau) = \gamma^{K(\tau)} r_e(\tau) + r_f(\tau)$, where $K(\tau)$ is the count of reasoning tokens. This design aims to preserve structural output quality while promoting concise problem-solving.
2. **KL Regularization to a Moving Reference Policy**: To prevent rapid policy collapse due to aggressive shortening, a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty is added to the objective: $E_{S_1 \sim \mu, \tau \sim \pi(S_1)}[R(\tau)] - \beta \text{KL}(\pi | \pi')$. The reference policy $\pi'$ is periodically updated to the current policy, acting as a dynamic trust region.
3. **Discounting only Reasoning Tokens**: The discount exponent $K(\tau)$ only counts tokens identified as part of the "reasoning span" (e.g., delimited by `<reasoning>` tags in the prompt). Tokens required for prompt adherence, formatting, or final answer presentation are explicitly excluded from discounting ($m_t=0$ for such tokens). Empirical observation indicated that discounting all tokens could negatively impact accuracy by leading to omitted formatting.
4. **Comparable Token Budgets**: To ensure fair comparisons, discounted methods, which produce shorter traces, are given an increased number of rollouts. This equalizes the total tokens processed during training with undiscounted baselines, ensuring that observed performance differences are due to the objective function rather than differing amounts of training data.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Policy Optimization Algorithm**: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) is employed for fine-tuning.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning**: The discount factor $\gamma$ is selected via bisection search, seeking the value farthest from 1 that maintains or slightly exceeds the undiscounted training accuracy. Other hyperparameters, like the KL regularization strength ($\beta$) and reference policy update frequency, are tuned to maximize the undiscounted baseline's performance and then applied to discounted methods.
- **Greedy Decoding**: Inference uses greedy decoding (temperature $\nu=0$), consistent with the analysis of greedy deployment policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results empirically validate the theoretical predictions, demonstrating that discounted reinforcement learning, when applied as described, encourages efficient reasoning in large language models by reducing response length while preserving or enhancing accuracy.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Models:** Qwen2.5 7B-Instruct, Llama 3 8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5 14B-Instruct, and Phi-4.
- **Training Method:** GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) with and without discounting.
- **Datasets:**
 - **Common Benchmarks:** GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) for Qwen2.5 7B and Llama 3 8B.
 - **Generality Test:** A subset of the DeepScaleR math dataset (Luo et al., 2025) for Qwen2.5 14B and Phi-4. Evaluation on AMC 2023, AIME 2025, MINERVA (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and OLYMPIAD (He et al., 2024) to assess generalization beyond the training distribution.
- **Metrics:** Pass@1 (accuracy, defined as the fraction of problems where the first generated solution is correct) and mean response length (in tokens).
- **Evaluation Robustness:** To address variance, each training run was repeated with 3 random seeds, and each trained model was evaluated with 10 independent sampling seeds on GSM8K and MATH (5 seeds for other benchmarks). Averages over these runs were reported, with evaluation seeds fixed across methods for paired comparisons.
- **Discount Factor Selection:** A single discounted configuration per model/dataset was chosen based on matching or exceeding the undiscounted training Pass@1, prioritizing the shortest mean response length.

#### 4.3. Effect of Discount Factor (γ)

- **Experiment:** Additional runs with Qwen3 1.7B on GSM8K explored the impact of varying $\gamma$.
- **Observation (Figure 2):** As $(1-\gamma)$ increases (i.e., $\gamma$ decreases), mean response length reliably decreases. However, if $\gamma$ becomes too small (overly aggressive discounting), accuracy begins to decline. This empirically confirms the theoretical prediction that while discounting encourages conciseness, overly aggressive discounting can shift policy preference towards shorter trajectories even at the cost of success probability, outside the Blackwell optimal region.

In summary, the experiments consistently demonstrate that applying the proposed discounted reinforcement learning methodology enables LRMs to achieve reasoning efficiency, producing shorter and more concise responses without a detriment to their accuracy, and in some cases, even leading to minor accuracy improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of large language models and reinforcement learning by providing both a theoretical framework and practical methodology for improving reasoning efficiency.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Significance

- **Principled Framework for Efficiency:** The work introduces Blackwell optimality as a principled mechanism for simultaneously maximizing accuracy and minimizing reasoning length in verifier-based MDPs. This provides a formal theoretical justification for using discounting to achieve these dual objectives, moving beyond heuristic length penalties.
- **Challenging the Length-Accuracy Trade-off:** The paper's core theoretical finding, supported by empirical evidence, challenges the common assumption that there is an inherent trade-off between reasoning length and accuracy in LRMs. It demonstrates that, within a specific regime (determined by the model class and problem instance, bounded by the Blackwell discount factor), response length can be significantly reduced without compromising accuracy. This refines the understanding of how reasoning complexity relates to performance.
- **Connection to Existing Methods:** By establishing that small per-token penalties in finite-horizon MDPs with binary rewards are equivalent to discounting in the near-undiscounted regime, the research provides a unified theoretical lens for understanding and potentially improving several existing length penalty methods (Arora & Zanette, 2025; Su & Cardie, 2025; Xiang et al., 2025).
- **Blackwell Discount Factor for Restricted Classes:** The proof of existence for a Blackwell discount factor ($\gamma_{bw} < 1$) for finite restricted policy classes (such as those induced by greedy deployment of softmax policies) is significant. It clarifies that a single, appropriately chosen discount factor can ensure Blackwell optimality across a range of problems, guiding practical hyperparameter selection.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact

- **Improved LRM Efficiency:** The primary practical impact is the substantial reduction in mean response length observed across various models and math benchmarks (e.g., 13-22% on GSM8K/MATH, and up to ~42% on other benchmarks). This directly translates to:
 - **Reduced Computational Cost:** Fewer tokens generated mean lower computational resources (GPU cycles, memory) per inference.
 - **Decreased Latency:** Shorter generation times improve real-time application responsiveness.
 - **Increased Serving Throughput:** More efficient token generation allows for processing more queries per unit time.
 These benefits are critical for deploying powerful LRMs at scale in real-world applications where operational costs and user experience are key considerations.
- **Maintained/Improved Accuracy:** Crucially, these efficiency gains are achieved while maintaining or, in some cases, slightly improving Pass@1 accuracy. This demonstrates that efficiency does not necessitate a sacrifice in performance under this methodology.
- **Adaptive Reasoning:** The approach implicitly enables the model to adapt to the "inherent token complexity" of a given question (Lee et al., 2025). By operating within the Blackwell region, the learner is steered towards the shortest successful reasoning trajectories permitted by its policy class, effectively optimizing for problem-specific efficiency.
- **Generalizability:** The consistent results across different model architectures (Qwen2.5, Llama 3, Phi-4) and diverse math reasoning datasets, including those distinct from training, suggest broad applicability of the proposed method.

#### 5.3. Future Work Directions

The paper outlines several promising avenues for future research:
- **Generalization through Compression:** Investigating whether shorter, more compressed reasoning traces, as produced by this method, lead to improved generalization capabilities in reasoning tasks. This connects to Hutter's (2007) hypothesis linking compression to improved generalization.
- **Combining Reasoning Strategies:** Exploring hybrid approaches that integrate methods promoting longer, exploratory reasoning (for strategy discovery) with methods that emphasize shorter, compressed reasoning (for path optimization). A pipeline akin to distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), where initial long traces are compressed, could potentially yield more robust and efficient reasoning policies.

In summary, this research provides a theoretically grounded and empirically validated method to enhance the efficiency of large reasoning models, offering significant practical benefits in terms of cost, latency, and throughput, while contributing to a deeper understanding of reasoning dynamics in LLMs.
