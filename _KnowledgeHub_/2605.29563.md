# Planning with the Views via Scene Self-Exploration

> [!summary] Summary
> The research introduces VI E W SU I T E, a new benchmark for 6-DoF multi-turn view planning in real 3D scenes, revealing a "planning gap" where frontier Vision-Language Models struggle to compose single-step view-action understanding into multi-turn plans. An iterative self-exploration and view graph distillation framework is proposed, which improves a VLM's interactive view planning success rate from 2.5% to 47.8%, surpassing leading proprietary models.

## Problem

- Current visual reasoning benchmarks treat VLMs as passive observers, lacking interactive control for active view planning in 3D environments.
- Existing embodied navigation benchmarks focus on physical affordances and arrival, not isolating 6-DoF viewpoint control and multi-turn view composition.
- Frontier Vision-Language Models exhibit a significant "planning gap" in multi-step view planning in complex 3D environments, struggling to compose single-step view-action knowledge into multi-turn plans.

## Method

- Introduces VI E W SU I T E, a benchmark featuring 6-DoF viewpoint control in real ScanNet 3D scenes, and three diagnostic tasks: Path-to-View, View-to-Path, and Interactive View Planning.
- Proposes an iterative training framework that alternates between self-exploration using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and "view graph distillation."
- All self-generated exploration trajectories, including failed ones, are converted into a view graph, from which paths are sampled and reformulated into dense supervised training instances.

## Results

- Frontier VLMs achieved approximately **50%** accuracy on single-turn tasks (P2V/V2P) but only below **10%** average success on multi-turn Interactive View Planning (IVP), with the best proprietary model reaching **21.4%**.
- The proposed iterative framework improved the Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct model's IVP success rate from a baseline of **2.5%** to **47.8%**, outperforming all evaluated frontier models, including GPT-5.4 Pro (**18.5%**) and Gemini 3.1 Pro (**21.4%**).
- The trained model developed a more effective two-phase exploration strategy and demonstrated transferable spatial priors, leading to 8-12 percentage point gains on P2V/V2P and ~10 percentage point gains on the external MindCube benchmark.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frontier VLMs exhibit a "planning gap," performing reasonably on single-turn view-action understanding but significantly worse on multi-turn planning, particularly for 3D translation.
> - Direct reinforcement learning with sparse rewards is ineffective for teaching multi-turn view planning to VLMs.
> - Distilling knowledge from all self-generated trajectories, even unsuccessful ones, through a view graph provides dense and effective supervision for view planning.
> - Interactive view planning, enabled by the iterative framework, fosters transferable spatial priors, improving VLM performance on related view-understanding tasks and external benchmarks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the capability of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to perform complex spatial reasoning in 3D environments, specifically focusing on "view planning." This task involves actively manipulating a camera viewpoint to localize an unobserved target view. The paper situates this as an instance of active perception, where an agent dynamically chooses where to look and how to move its camera.

Previous research in view search has primarily focused on tasks within 2D image regions or 360-degree panoramas, often with limited action spaces (e.g., 2-DoF control). The current work extends this by introducing multi-step view planning within real-world 3D scenes, granting the agent full six degrees of freedom (6-DoF) camera pose control. A key distinction of this research is its emphasis on localization accuracy (estimating the target view's 6-DoF camera pose) rather than physical arrival at a semantic goal, which differentiates it from typical embodied navigation or object goal tasks. This framing isolates the cognitive aspect of reasoning about viewpoints from physical affordances and traversability.

The paper contrasts VI E W SU I T E with existing view reasoning and 3D scene benchmarks in several ways. While many benchmarks probe view-dependent reasoning from static images or involve passive observation, VI E W SU I T E introduces active, multi-turn interaction. Embodied agent benchmarks often focus on agents physically reaching semantic destinations. In contrast, view planning in VI E W SU I T E requires mentally simulating unobserved viewpoints and inferring their camera poses based on observed views, aligning with concepts from spatial cognition such as building cognitive maps, combining egocentric and allocentric representations, and prospective spatial reasoning. The study explores whether such cognitive capabilities, including the ability to form internal world models of view transitions, are present in frontier VLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the "view planning" ability of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). View planning is defined as the process of localizing a target view by composing a sequence of camera actions. The authors decompose this ability into two coupled components:
1. **Tracking how a given path changes the view:** This involves local reasoning, understanding view transitions relative to the current viewpoint.
2. **Actively composing a path to localize an unseen target view:** This requires a more global, prospective spatial reasoning capability, necessitating the anchoring of current and target views within a shared allocentric frame, mentally simulating the effects of camera actions on future observations, and formulating a multi-turn plan.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several factors:
- **Fundamental for 3D Scene Understanding:** A comprehensive understanding of a 3D scene requires an agent to comprehend its position within the scene, actively decide where to look next, and reason about how camera movements alter observations.
- **Probing Prospective Spatial Reasoning:** View planning serves as a diagnostic probe for advanced cognitive abilities in VLMs, specifically their capacity for "mentally looking ahead," predicting future observations resulting from viewpoint changes, and inferring the camera pose of a target view before it is fully observed.
- **Identifying VLM Limitations:** The research aims to assess whether current frontier VLMs possess this prospective spatial reasoning, hypothesizing that they may lack it despite advancements in other multimodal tasks.
- **Addressing Learning Challenges:** Standard reinforcement learning (RL) methods face significant challenges in complex planning tasks with sparse rewards. The authors are motivated to develop an effective training methodology to enable VLMs to learn view planning from self-exploration without relying on extensive expert demonstrations.
- **Developing Transferable Spatial Priors:** A further objective is to determine if the spatial priors acquired through interactive view planning are generalizable and can benefit performance on other view-understanding tasks.

The study aims to quantify the "planning gap" in VLMs, explore the factors contributing to this gap (e.g., turn budget, rendering quality, types of distance), differentiate between "view matching" and true "mental localization" as modes of success, and propose a scalable solution to mitigate the identified deficiencies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors developed **VI E W SU I T E**, a view-planning environment built upon 286 real indoor scenes from the ScanNet dataset, to study view planning. This environment offers full 6-DoF camera pose control, allowing agents to manipulate their viewpoint through 12 discrete, step-size-parameterized actions (6 translations and 6 rotations). Rotations are subject to discrete snapping to ensure invertible action sequences. The environment renders views from point cloud data using Open3D, with additional experiments conducted using 3D Gaussian Splatting for comparison.

The benchmark comprises three diagnostic tasks, totaling approximately 165,000 instances derived from 55,000 view pairs:
- **Path-to-View (P2V):** A single-turn multiple-choice task where the model predicts the resulting view after executing a given action sequence from an initial view, provided with a top-down reference map. This tests the model's ability to mentally simulate view transitions.
- **View-to-Path (V2P):** Another single-turn multiple-choice task where the model identifies the action sequence that connects a given initial view to a target view, also with a top-down reference. This probes view-transition tracking in reverse.
- **Interactive View Planning (IVP):** A multi-turn task where the agent is given an initial view, a target view, and a top-down map. The agent must issue a sequence of camera actions within a fixed turn budget (10 turns) to localize the target view, observing its current view and pose after each action. The task concludes with the submission of a 6-DoF camera pose estimate for the target view. Unlike P2V and V2P, IVP requires the agent to compose a plan actively.

Data collection involved sampling initial-target view pairs from ScanNet video frames, followed by scene-level and pair-level filtering to ensure data quality and relevance (e.g., removing scenes with poor top-down views, filtering out trivial or excessively long paths). The unified view distance, which combines translation and rotation distances, was used to categorize task difficulty (Short and Long horizon splits).

For evaluation, P2V and V2P tasks use accuracy. IVP is evaluated using a Success Rate, which is determined by how closely the agent's submitted 6-DoF camera pose estimate matches the ground truth. The success thresholds (0.5 m for position and 30 degrees for rotation) were calibrated against human judgments, ensuring alignment with human perception of viewpoint similarity.

The core methodological contribution is an **iterative self-exploration with view graph distillation framework** designed to overcome the sparse reward problem inherent in reinforcement learning for view planning. This framework alternates between two stages:
1. **Self-Exploration Stage:** The VLM agent interacts with the VI E W SU I T E environment, performing actions and receiving a sparse reward (+1 for success, +0.1 for correct format). All generated trajectories, regardless of success, are continuously compressed into a **view graph**. This graph stores viewpoints as nodes and actions between them as directed edges, with nodes and edges deduplicated to manage graph size. This process effectively builds an empirical world model of view transitions from the agent's own experience.
2. **View Graph Distillation Stage:** Paths are sampled from the accumulated view graph. These paths are then reformulated into supervised demonstrations for view planning. Specifically, any path $P = (v_0, a_1, v_1..., a_K, v_K)$ from the graph is converted into an IVP demonstration where $v_0$ is the initial view, $v_K$ is the target view, and $(a_1..., a_K)$ is the ground-truth action sequence. This technique extracts dense, meaningful supervision from even failed exploration attempts. The VLM is then fine-tuned using supervised learning (SFT) with a standard cross-entropy loss, using these reformulated demonstrations and additional auxiliary tasks like view-difference estimation. The fine-tuned model then initializes the next self-exploration iteration, progressively bootstrapping the policy by expanding the view graph into regions the model can actually plan over.

The framework was instantiated using Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct as the primary base model, with Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct used for robustness checks. Various baselines, including proprietary models (GPT-5.4 Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro), standard RL algorithms (Direct PPO, Direct GRPO), and ablations of the proposed framework (e.g., Success-Only Bootstrapping, Random-graph construction), were used for comparative evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the spatial reasoning capabilities of frontier VLMs and the effectiveness of the proposed training methodology:

#### 4.1. Planning Gap in Frontier VLMs

- **Discrepancy in Abilities:** Frontier VLMs demonstrated a notable disparity between their ability to track local view transitions and their capacity for multi-turn view planning. On P2V and V2P tasks, which involve tracking given paths, the best models achieved approximately 50% overall accuracy, rising to over 70% on short-horizon samples. This indicates partial knowledge of view-action mappings. However, performance significantly deteriorated on the Interactive View Planning (IVP) task, where models must compose a plan. The best frontier model, Gemini 3.1 Pro, achieved only 21.3% success, with most models falling below 10%, and even lower on long-horizon samples.
- **Limited Impact of Turn Budget and Rendering Quality:** Increasing the turn budget for IVP from 10 to 20 or 30 turns yielded only marginal improvements, suggesting that the bottleneck is rooted in planning ability rather than a lack of exploration opportunities. Similarly, switching to a higher-fidelity renderer (3D Gaussian Splatting) resulted in only minor gains on IVP, indicating that visual fidelity is not the primary limiting factor; rather, the challenge lies in the models' reasoning capabilities.
- **Differentiated Difficulty Drivers:** Analysis of unified view distance revealed that P2V and V2P performance primarily degraded with increasing *rotation distance*, as mentally simulating cumulative rotations is challenging. In contrast, IVP success collapsed with increasing *position distance*, highlighting the difficulty models face in understanding 3D spatial layout and planning paths for translation.
- **View Matching vs. Mental Localization:** A critical finding was that over 90% of successful IVP rollouts across frontier VLMs occurred only after the target view became visually observable to the agent. This suggests that models predominantly succeed by physically approaching and then matching the target view, rather than by inferring its precise 6-DoF pose through prospective spatial reasoning before direct observation. This pattern was termed a "cognitive gap."

#### 4.2. Self-Exploration Closes the Gap

- **Significant Performance Improvement:** The iterative self-exploration with view graph distillation framework substantially improved performance. For Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, IVP success rate increased from a baseline of 2.5% to 47.8%. This outcome surpassed all evaluated frontier models.
- **Robustness Across Backbones:** When applied to Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, the framework achieved 32.5% success, still outperforming other prompting and training baselines, demonstrating the robustness of the approach across different pre-trained backbones.
- **Superiority Over RL Baselines:** Direct PPO, GRPO with filtering, and Success-Only Bootstrapping (which only uses successful trajectories for SFT) all remained below 7% success on IVP. This indicates that direct RL is ineffective due to sparse rewards, and that the view graph construction and task reformulation are crucial for extracting dense, useful supervision from overwhelmingly unsuccessful exploration.
- **Importance of On-Policy Graph Construction:** Ablation studies showed that progressive improvement in IVP success correlated with the number of iterations (12.0% after 1 iteration, 27.9% after 2, and 47.8% after 3). The Random-graph variant, which built its view graph from random actions rather than on-policy exploration, achieved only 13.0% success. This confirmed that on-policy graph construction, where the graph grows with the agent's evolving capabilities, is critical for effective supervision.

#### 4.3. Learned Model Characteristics and Transferability

- **Learned Exploration Strategy:** The trained model developed a distinct "two-phase explore-then-approach" strategy. Scene coverage expanded rapidly in early turns, followed by an acceleration in the target intersection ratio during middle turns as the agent moved closer to the target view. This pattern was unique to the trained model and was not observed in base or frontier models.
- **Shift in Attention Mechanisms:** Training led to a discernible shift in the model's attention, with the trained model allocating a greater proportion of its attention to image tokens across most layers and turns compared to the base model. This suggests that the model learned to ground its decisions more effectively in visual evidence gathered during interaction.
- **Transferable Spatial Priors:** The spatial priors acquired through interactive view planning were found to be transferable. After identical GRPO post-training, the IVP-trained model outperformed its base counterpart by 8–12 points on other VI E W SU I T E tasks (P2V and V2P). Furthermore, it showed approximately a 10-point gain over the base model on MindCube, an external benchmark with no shared scenes, actions, or rendering pipeline, demonstrating that the learned priors generalize to different view-understanding contexts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of artificial intelligence by introducing "view planning" as a distinct and challenging problem for Vision-Language Models, and by providing a novel benchmark (VI E W SU I T E) designed for its assessment. The benchmark, featuring real 3D scenes, full 6-DoF camera control, and multi-turn interaction, offers a more comprehensive evaluation of spatial reasoning than many existing datasets.

The identification of a "planning gap" in frontier VLMs, where they demonstrate proficiency in tracking local view changes but struggle with multi-turn planning and proactive spatial inference, highlights a fundamental limitation in current models. The finding that most successes in such models rely on "view matching" rather than genuine "mental localization" provides specific insights into the nature of their current spatial understanding.

The proposed iterative self-exploration with view graph distillation framework offers a substantial methodological advancement. By effectively leveraging self-generated, diverse, and often unsuccessful trajectories to create dense supervision, the framework overcomes the sparse reward problem commonly encountered in complex embodied tasks. The demonstrated performance gains, significantly elevating VLM capabilities in interactive view planning beyond existing state-of-the-art models, indicate a viable path toward bootstrapping advanced planning abilities.

Furthermore, the evidence that the spatial priors learned through view planning are transferable to other view-understanding tasks underscores the potential for this approach to foster more generalized spatial intelligence in VLMs. This suggests that rather than learning task-specific skills, models can acquire foundational knowledge about 3D space and viewpoint manipulation.

The work opens several avenues for future research, including applying the framework to more complex environments (e.g., outdoor, dynamic), exploring continuous control, and scaling to larger models. More fundamentally, it sets the stage for developing VLMs that can truly perform prospective spatial cognition, capable of localizing unseen target views through inference rather than mere observation and matching. Ultimately, this research contributes to the broader goal of endowing AI agents with more human-like spatial reasoning capabilities, enabling them to navigate, understand, and interact with the 3D world in a more sophisticated manner.
