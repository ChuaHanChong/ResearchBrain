# TIC-VLA: A Think-in-Control Vision-Language-Action Model for Robot Navigation in Dynamic Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> TIC-VLA, developed by UCLA, introduces a vision-language-action (VLA) framework that explicitly addresses the challenge of VLM inference latency for robot navigation in dynamic environments. It leverages a delayed semantic-control interface and a latency-consistent training pipeline, achieving a 55.29% success rate and 28.24% collision rate in simulation and robust real-world performance on edge devices despite multi-second reasoning delays.

## Problem

- Existing VLA systems often assume temporal alignment between slow semantic reasoning (VLMs) and fast robot control, leading to stale semantic information for real-time actions.
- VLM inference delays (hundreds of milliseconds to several seconds) hinder continuous robot operation in dynamic environments, often requiring robots to pause during VLM processing.
- Prior benchmarks for language-guided navigation frequently lack dynamic agents, visual realism, and physics-based interactions needed for comprehensive evaluation.

## Method

- A dual-system "Think-in-Control" (TIC)-VLA framework that asynchronously processes VLM semantic reasoning and high-frequency action control without blocking robot execution.
- A delayed semantic-control interface that conditions the action policy on VLM's cached semantic states, explicit latency metadata (effective delay), and corresponding ego-motion offsets.
- A three-stage latency-consistent training pipeline involving VLM supervised fine-tuning, imitation learning with synthetic delay injection, and ==reinforcement learning== with asynchronous guidance in a custom simulation environment.

## Results

- Achieved a **55.29%** success rate and **28.24%** collision rate in the DynaNav simulation, outperforming prior VLA baselines in language-guided navigation under latency conditions.
- Demonstrated robust real-world performance on edge devices (e.g., Unitree Go2 with Jetson Orin NX), reaching an **85%** success rate despite multi-second VLM inference latencies.
- Confirmed that incorporating ego-motion offsets into the latency-aware action policy substantially improved navigation performance (**47.06%** SR versus **41.18%** SR without offset).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling and compensating for VLM inference latency allows robots to maintain robust real-time control and language-guided navigation in dynamic environments.
> - A delayed semantic-control interface, combining VLM key-value (KV) cache features and ego-motion offsets, is crucial for the control policy to effectively reinterpret stale semantic information.
> - A multi-stage training pipeline, including synthetic delay injection during imitation learning and asynchronous guidance during reinforcement learning, is essential for policy robustness to variable reasoning delays.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotics research is increasingly focused on enabling autonomous agents to operate effectively in complex, dynamic, and human-centric environments. A key capability for such robots is the ability to interpret and follow natural language instructions while simultaneously maintaining real-time reactive control to navigate safely and efficiently. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a framework for integrating perception, language understanding, and robot control within a unified learning-based system, often leveraging large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for semantic grounding and task reasoning.

However, existing VLA systems frequently operate under an assumption of temporal alignment between reasoning and control. This assumption is often impractical in real-world scenarios, particularly for mobile robots with limited computational resources. VLM inference can introduce substantial delays, sometimes spanning multiple seconds, whereas robot control loops typically operate at much higher frequencies (e.g., tens of Hertz). This temporal mismatch results in the robot's control policy acting on semantic information that pertains to a past state of the world, rather than its current observation.

Previous approaches to embodied navigation have largely not addressed this specific challenge directly. Traditional Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) models often abstract navigation into discrete viewpoint transitions, thereby sidestepping the complexities of real-time embodiment, dynamics, and timing. More recent VLA systems may rely on high-performance computing to minimize delays or even pause robot execution during VLM inference, which is not viable in dynamic environments where continuous motion is required. While dual-system or asynchronous VLA architectures exist to decouple high-level reasoning from low-level control, they typically still implicitly assume that semantic outputs are sufficiently fresh, not explicitly modeling or compensating for the staleness introduced by inference latency.

This research positions itself by identifying inference latency not merely as an engineering concern but as a fundamental modeling problem. It argues that policies trained without explicit consideration of delayed semantic inputs will degrade when deployed under realistic asynchronous conditions. Furthermore, while social navigation has gained attention, existing evaluation benchmarks often lack the combination of dynamic agents, language-guided navigation, and visual realism needed for comprehensive assessment. TIC-VLA seeks to bridge these gaps by introducing a latency-aware framework, a corresponding training methodology, and a robust simulation environment designed to address these limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from a critical challenge in deploying VLA models for robot navigation in real-world, dynamic environments: the inherent temporal mismatch between slow semantic reasoning performed by large VLMs and the need for fast, real-time reactive control. While VLMs are powerful for interpreting complex natural language instructions and providing high-level semantic guidance, their computational demands often lead to inference latencies ranging from hundreds of milliseconds to several seconds. In contrast, robot control systems must operate at much higher frequencies to ensure smooth, safe, and efficient movement in rapidly changing environments.

The existing paradigm of assuming temporal alignment between reasoning and control, or pausing robot execution during VLM inference, is deemed impractical for robots interacting with humans or navigating complex dynamic scenes. When semantic information is delayed, it describes a past state of the robot and its environment, creating a systematic misalignment between "thinking" (semantic understanding) and "control" (action generation). If this delay is not explicitly modeled, policies trained under idealized synchronous conditions are likely to perform poorly when exposed to realistic asynchronous inference latencies during deployment.

Given this fundamental problem, the key objectives of the TIC-VLA research are:

1. **Develop a Latency-Aware VLA Framework:** To introduce a novel architectural framework, named Think-in-Control (TIC)-VLA, that explicitly acknowledges and accounts for the inference delay of semantic reasoning during action generation. This framework aims to decouple slow semantic processing from fast reactive control without blocking robot execution.

2. **Define a Delayed Semantic-Control Interface:** To design a specific interface that conditions the action generation policy on delayed vision-language semantic states, along with explicit latency metadata and ego-motion offsets. This allows the control policy to effectively interpret and compensate for the temporal staleness of the semantic information relative to the current robot state and observations.

3. **Propose a Latency-Consistent Training Pipeline:** To implement a multi-stage training methodology that ensures robustness to variable reasoning delays. This pipeline involves injecting realistic inference delays during both imitation learning (to align training inputs with asynchronous deployment conditions) and online reinforcement learning (to adapt to closed-loop interactions and dynamic environments).

4. **Create a Realistic Simulation Suite (DynaNav):** To develop a physics-accurate, photo-realistic simulation environment to support robust and reproducible evaluation of language-guided navigation in dynamic scenarios. DynaNav aims to overcome limitations of existing benchmarks by incorporating diverse human agents, realistic rendering, and physics-based robot execution, suitable for teleoperated data collection, online RL, and benchmarking.

5. **Demonstrate Robust Performance:** To empirically validate that TIC-VLA consistently outperforms prior VLA models, maintaining robust real-time control and language-guided navigation even under significant (multi-second) reasoning latencies in both simulation and real-world robot deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TIC-VLA framework employs a dual-system execution paradigm, wherein high-level semantic reasoning and low-level control operate asynchronously. A central aspect of this methodology is the explicit modeling of inference delay as a core variable, which is then exposed to the control policy.

**VLM Semantic Reasoning:**
The framework utilizes InternVL3-1B (comprising an InternViT-300M vision encoder and a Qwen2.5-0.5B language model) as its VLM backbone. The VLM performs semantic reasoning on visual observations that are temporally lagged, corresponding to the time `t - ∆t`, where `∆t` is the effective reasoning latency (sum of VLM inference time `t_infer` and elapsed time `t_elapse` since the last completed reasoning update). The VLM processes historical frames (e.g., at `t - ∆t`, `t - ∆t - 3s`, `t - ∆t - 6s`, `t - ∆t - 9s`) to produce high-level semantic outputs, including scene understanding, critical object identification, intent prediction, and future target waypoints. These reasoning results (`R_{t-∆t}`) are explicitly anchored to their generation time, providing crucial temporal context for the control policy.

**Latency-Aware Action Policy:**
The action policy (`π_θ`) is a Transformer-based network that operates at a high frequency (e.g., 10 Hz) for real-time control. At each timestep `t`, it receives four categories of inputs:
1. **Current visual observation (`x_t`)**: Processed by a shared vision encoder.
2. **Current robot state (`s_t`)**: Includes ego-motion information like linear and angular velocities.
3. **Most recent semantic hidden state (`S_{t-∆t}`)**: The key-value (KV) cache from the final Transformer layer of the VLM, representing the delayed semantic understanding.
4. **Explicit latency metadata**: This includes the effective latency (`∆t`) and the corresponding ego-motion offsets (`∆p_t = (∆x, ∆y, ∆θ)`) accumulated by the robot since `S_{t-∆t}` was generated.

This combination of inputs forms the **delayed semantic-control interface**, which is designed to enable the control policy to reinterpret stale semantic features in the context of the robot's current state and movement during the delay. The action policy outputs a short horizon of future actions (e.g., 3 seconds discretized into 30 action chunks), each parameterized as relative motion increments (`d_x, d_y, d_θ`). These chunks are integrated to form a short-horizon trajectory, from which a target point is selected for execution.

**Asynchronous Execution:**
TIC-VLA maintains an asynchronous operational loop. The VLM periodically updates its cached semantic hidden state and reasoning results whenever an inference job completes. Concurrently, the action policy continuously executes, always using the most recently available cached semantic information, without blocking for new VLM inference. The explicit latency metadata allows the policy to account for the temporal gap between the cached semantic state and the current real-time observations.

**Latency-Consistent Training Pipeline:**
A three-stage training pipeline is employed to ensure that the policy is robust to realistic inference delays:
1. **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) of the VLM:** The InternVL3-1B VLM is fine-tuned on diverse datasets (SCAND, GND, and the newly collected DynaNav data). This stage involves automatically generating natural language instructions and detailed reasoning traces (Chain-of-Thought style, covering current situation, critical objects, motion, task progress, and short-horizon plan) using GPT-5. The VLM is trained autoregressively to produce these reasoning tokens and waypoint predictions from visual tokens and instructions. The vision encoder is kept frozen during this stage.
2. **Imitation Learning (IL) under Reasoning Latency:** To make the action policy robust to delays, expert demonstrations are perturbed by synthetically introducing reasoning delays. During IL, `∆t` values are sampled uniformly (e.g., seconds), and the policy is trained to generate actions conditioned on the current image and robot state, the delayed VLM KV cache features, and the explicit latency metadata. A smooth L1 loss is used to match predicted trajectories with ground-truth expert trajectories. Token dropout is applied to VLM KV-cache features and robot-state inputs to enhance robustness.
3. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) with Asynchronous Guidance:** The action policy is further fine-tuned using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in a custom simulation environment (DynaNav). The VLM and shared vision encoder remain frozen. This stage allows the policy to adapt to the closed-loop dynamics induced by asynchronous VLM interactions, handle dynamic agents, and mitigate variability. Stochastic inference delays are injected after VLM updates during RL training to closely mimic real-world edge hardware latency characteristics. The reward function encourages goal reaching and progress while penalizing collisions and suboptimal speeds.

**DynaNav Simulation Suite:**
To facilitate realistic and reproducible evaluation, the authors introduce DynaNav, a simulation suite built on Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab. Key features include:
- **Realistic Environments:** Four diverse scenes (warehouse, hospital, office, outdoor sidewalk) with photo-realistic rendering and controllable dynamic human agents.
- **Physics-based Control:** Supports both wheeled (Nova Carter) and quadruped (Boston Dynamics Spot) robots with physics-accurate interactions.
- **Data Collection & Scalable RL:** Allows human teleoperation for expert demonstration collection and utilizes GPU-accelerated simulation for efficient PPO training across multiple parallel environments.
- **Diverse Benchmark Tasks:** 85 test cases varying in crowd density (0-200 agents), navigation distance, and scene type, each with standardized initial/goal positions and language instructions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TIC-VLA was conducted through extensive experiments in both the DynaNav simulation environment and on a real robot platform, comparing its performance against several baselines across various metrics.

**Simulation Testing on DynaNav Benchmark:**
Experiments were performed with the action policy running at 10 Hz and asynchronous VLM reasoning at 0.5 Hz.
- **Overall Performance (Table 1):** TIC-VLA, particularly after RL fine-tuning, demonstrated strong performance. It achieved the highest success rate (SR) of 55.29% and the lowest collision rate (CR) of 28.24% among all language-guided navigation methods. While NavDP, a point-goal method with privileged state access, achieved a slightly lower navigation error (NE) and higher Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), TIC-VLA operates solely on egocentric observations and language instructions. TIC-VLA significantly outperformed prior VLN baselines (e.g., Uni-NaVid, NaVILA, DualVLN) and stronger VLA baselines (OmniVLA, MobileVLA) in terms of both SR and CR. A synchronous variant of TIC-VLA, which blocked control for VLM inference, experienced a substantial performance degradation (SR 32.94%), confirming the negative impact of such an approach in real-time settings. The imitation learning-only TIC-VLA variant also showed competitive results (SR 47.06%) before RL fine-tuning.
- **Latency Robustness Analysis (Figure 5):** The RL-fine-tuned TIC-VLA policy exhibited greater robustness to increasing VLM reasoning inference latency (up to 5 seconds). While the IL-based policy showed a decline in success rate with increasing latency, the RL-fine-tuned policy maintained consistently higher success rates across all tested latency settings, indicating the effectiveness of RL in mitigating performance degradation due to delays. Collision rates remained relatively stable across latency variations, suggesting that the asynchronous policy preserved its reactive capabilities.
- **Influence of Semantic-Control Interface and Latency Awareness (Table 2):** The choice of semantic interface and explicit latency awareness were found to be influential. Using VLM key-value (KV) cache features as the semantic interface significantly improved navigation success compared to sparse waypoint-based guidance. Furthermore, incorporating explicit latency-aware modeling and training consistently enhanced performance. The combination of KV-cache features with latency-aware training yielded the best results, validating the design choice of the delayed semantic-control interface.

**Real-world Testing:**
TIC-VLA was deployed on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot using NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX and RTX 4060 Laptop GPU, with comparisons against baselines on an RTX A6000.
- **Performance on Edge Devices (Table 3):** TIC-VLA achieved an average success rate of 0.85 on the RTX 4060 and 0.75 on the Jetson Orin NX, despite experiencing multi-second VLM reasoning latencies (e.g., ~4.8 seconds on Orin NX for VLM, while action policy ran at ~120ms). This demonstrated its suitability for deployment on resource-constrained edge platforms. The RL fine-tuning stage improved the real-world success rate from 0.70 to 0.85, confirming its benefits for physical deployment robustness.
- **Comparison with Baselines:** TIC-VLA outperformed DualVLN (SR 0.50) and NaVILA (SR 0.35) even when these baselines were executed on a more powerful NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU, highlighting the efficacy of explicit latency modeling and efficient control over raw computational power. Communication overhead was noted as a factor preventing the A6000 setup from surpassing local RTX 4060 deployment performance.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Influence of Reasoning at Test Time (Table 4):** Enabling explicit VLM reasoning during inference significantly improved navigation performance (SR 55.29% vs. 40.00% without reasoning). While disabling reasoning reduced VLM overhead, it led to reduced goal completion and navigation progress, indicating that test-time reasoning is beneficial and TIC-VLA successfully mitigates its associated latency.
- **Influence of Action Prediction Horizon (Table 5):** A 3-second action prediction horizon was found to be optimal, balancing reactivity and planning context. Shorter (1s) horizons showed better collision rates but lower overall task success, while longer (5s) horizons reduced control accuracy.
- **Influence of Ego-motion Offset (Table 6):** Incorporating ego-motion offset into the latency-aware action policy substantially improved performance (SR 47.06% vs. 41.18% without offset). This confirmed that explicitly providing information about robot displacement during inference delay is crucial for the policy to accurately reinterpret delayed semantic features.
- **Additional Ablations (Supplementary Materials):** Further analysis revealed that the query-based Transformer action policy outperformed diffusion-based and flow-based alternatives (Table 11). InternVL3 (1B) provided the best balance of VLM capacity and efficiency compared to smaller and larger backbones (Table 12). A 6-layer Transformer for the action expert was found to be optimal (Table 13). TIC-VLA also demonstrated reasonable robustness to odometry drift, maintaining stable performance under typical noise conditions (Table 14).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TIC-VLA framework represents a notable advancement in the field of embodied AI and robot navigation, particularly for VLA models operating in dynamic, human-centric environments. Its primary significance lies in explicitly addressing the fundamental challenge of inference latency, a problem often either overlooked or circumvented by prior VLA systems. By demonstrating that robust, real-time control can be maintained despite multi-second delays in semantic reasoning, TIC-VLA offers a more practical and realistic approach to deploying VLM-powered robots.

The introduction of the delayed semantic-control interface is a key methodological contribution. This interface, coupled with the latency-consistent training pipeline, enables the control policy to effectively interpret and compensate for temporally stale semantic information. This capability is crucial for enhancing the reliability and safety of robots in real-world scenarios where continuous, reactive action is paramount and VLM inference cannot be instantaneous or blocking.

The development of DynaNav, a physics-accurate, photo-realistic simulation suite for language-guided navigation in dynamic environments, is another significant contribution. This benchmark provides a standardized and realistic platform for future research, addressing limitations of previous simulators that often lacked dynamic agents, fine-grained physics, or comprehensive language-conditioned tasks. DynaNav is poised to facilitate the development and rigorous evaluation of more advanced and robust navigation policies.

The potential impact of TIC-VLA extends to various real-world applications. By enabling VLM capabilities on resource-constrained edge platforms (e.g., Jetson Orin NX), the framework could accelerate the deployment of intelligent robots in domains such as service robotics (e.g., hospital delivery, office assistance), logistics (e.g., warehouse automation), and assisted mobility. The demonstrated zero-shot transferability to real-world robot platforms further underscores its practical value.

However, the authors also acknowledge certain limitations. The current system is not yet fully optimized for runtime efficiency, indicating scope for further improvements in inference speed. The real-world evaluation, while promising, is limited in scale and would benefit from larger and more diverse studies to fully validate robustness across a broader range of complex conditions. Additionally, the research is currently focused on navigation, and extending the latency-aware principles to other robotic domains, such as manipulation, remains a future area of work. The impact statement also responsibly highlights the inherent safety and ethical risks associated with robots operating in human spaces, emphasizing that while latency-aware control helps mitigate risks like collisions, perception, reasoning, and control failures are still possible, necessitating continued human oversight in safety-critical applications.
