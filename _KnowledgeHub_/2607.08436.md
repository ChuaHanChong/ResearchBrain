# EgoWAM: World Action Models Beyond Pixels with In-the-Wild Egocentric Human Data

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Georgia Institute of Technology developed EGOWAM, a framework for human-robot co-training that utilizes diverse egocentric human data by augmenting policies with an auxiliary world-model head to predict future scene states. This approach demonstrated that world models, particularly those based on DINO features or 3D motion flow, enable more robust transfer and out-of-distribution generalization compared to traditional behavior cloning, which often struggles with the embodiment gap.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning (BC) co-training often struggles to effectively leverage in-the-wild egocentric human data for robot manipulation due to the
- embodiment gap
- where human actions introduce non-transferable motions.
- Traditional BC models conflate transferable task content (e.g., objects, scenes) with non-transferable execution factors (e.g., human morphology, head motion), leading to degraded robot performance.
- Existing World Action Models (WAMs) primarily rely on pixel-based prediction, which can entangle motion with appearance and may not adequately abstract scene dynamics for robust cross-embodiment transfer.

## Method

- Introduced **EGOWAM**, a controlled human-robot co-training framework that augments a ==Heterogeneous Pretrained Transformer (HPT)== policy backbone with an auxiliary world-model head to predict future scene states.
- Systematically investigated three world representations as targets for the world-model head: ==Pixel VAE== for reconstruction, ==DINO features== for semantic abstraction, and ==3D motion flow== for spatial abstraction and ego-motion factoring.
- Employed a joint training objective where the ==world-model loss== supervises the shared policy trunk using dynamics from both human and robot data, allowing for robust knowledge transfer while maintaining action-only inference efficiency.

## Results

- WAM co-training consistently translated human data into performance gains, even with misaligned human demonstrations, while BC co-training often degraded performance under similar conditions.
- DINO feature-based WAMs achieved substantial improvements, up to **4x**, in out-of-distribution generalization for unseen objects and novel scenes.
- 3D motion flow-based WAMs delivered the largest in-domain spatial gains (**20-30%** improvement) and maintained robustness even when human demonstrations contained deliberately unaligned or inexecutable actions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - World Action Models (WAMs) enable a shared latent representation between human and robot data, driven by task-relevant dynamics, making them more robust to embodiment differences than action-only behavior cloning.
> - Effective world representations for human-to-robot transfer should prioritize appearance abstraction, cross-embodiment consistency of physical effects, and ego-motion factoring.
> - Different world representations offer complementary strengths: DINO features excel in out-of-distribution generalization by providing semantic abstraction, while 3D motion flow yields robust in-domain spatial gains through geometrically grounded and ego-motion-factored dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The utilization of human demonstration data is a recurring theme in robot learning, offering a scalable alternative to costly robot-specific data collection. Egocentric human video, captured from a first-person perspective, is particularly valuable due to its diversity in objects, scenes, and behaviors. A common approach to leverage this data is behavior cloning (BC) co-training, where human demonstrations are retargeted to a robot's action space and used to jointly train an imitation policy.

However, existing research indicates that this BC co-training paradigm often struggles when human data is not carefully aligned with robot capabilities in terms of viewpoint, motion speed, or behavioral style. This "embodiment gap" means that the action-level supervision from human data can introduce non-transferable human-like motions, which robots cannot execute, leading to degraded performance. This phenomenon highlights a limitation where the shared action decoder in BC models conflates transferable content (e.g., objects, scenes, task semantics) with non-transferable execution factors (e.g., human morphology, head motion, behavioral style).

World Action Models (WAMs) represent an alternative approach by augmenting policies with an auxiliary world-model head that predicts future states. This additional supervision channel operates on observations, which are hypothesized to be less sensitive to the embodiment gap compared to actions. Prior WAM research has primarily focused on pixel-based prediction, often through video VAEs, which can entangle motion with appearance and thus may not adequately abstract scene dynamics for cross-embodiment transfer. This paper addresses the need for a systematic investigation into optimal world representations for WAM co-training in the context of human-robot transfer, explicitly acknowledging the limitations of pixel-level prediction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether World Action Models (WAMs) can provide a more effective training signal for robot manipulation by utilizing in-the-wild egocentric human data, specifically by requiring policies to predict not only actions but also how the scene evolves. The motivation stems from the recognized limitations of behavior cloning (BC) in human-robot co-training.

The authors observe that traditional BC co-training methods often suffer from an "embodiment gap." When human demonstrations, particularly those collected "in-the-wild," are used to train robots, they inevitably contain non-transferable factors such as human morphology, idiosyncratic head motions, and unique behavioral styles. These elements can become entangled with transferable task-relevant content like objects, scene layouts, and general task semantics within the shared action decoder. This entanglement frequently results in the robot attempting to execute human-like motions that are kinematically impossible or sub-optimal for its own embodiment, leading to performance degradation.

The core hypothesis is that WAMs can overcome this limitation by introducing a second, "world-model" supervision channel. This channel, by predicting future scene states or dynamics, is posited to be less sensitive to embodiment-specific factors than action prediction. The belief is that task-relevant dynamics, such as how an object moves or a scene changes due to an interaction, are more readily transferable across embodiments than the precise actions that cause them. By decoupling supervision into "how the world evolves" (through the world model) and "how each embodiment acts" (through the action head), WAMs are hypothesized to induce a more shareable representation in the policy's shared backbone, thereby enabling more effective scaling from diverse, large-scale human data.

A critical, yet systematically unaddressed, question for the promise of WAMs in human-robot co-training is the nature of the "world representation" itself. Most existing WAMs rely on pixel-based prediction, often via video VAEs, which are optimized for photometric reconstruction. The authors argue that such pixel-level targets can entangle motion with appearance, hindering effective transfer. They hypothesize that an effective world representation for human-to-robot transfer should satisfy three desiderata:
1. **Appearance Abstraction (D1):** The representation should abstract away embodiment-specific visual appearance to focus on task-relevant structure.
2. **Cross-Embodiment Consistency (D2):** The representation should consistently capture physical effects regardless of the agent (human hand vs. robot gripper).
3. **Ego-Motion Factoring (D3):** The representation should separate changes in the environment from changes caused by camera (head) motion, which varies significantly between a moving human head and a static robot camera.

These desiderata motivate the controlled study of different world representations—Pixel VAE, DINO features, and 3D motion flow—within the EGOWAM framework to determine which best facilitates human-to-robot knowledge transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **EGOWAM**, a controlled human-robot co-training framework designed to systematically evaluate the impact of different world representations on cross-embodiment transfer. The framework builds upon a Heterogeneous Pretrained Transformer (HPT) backbone, enabling a consistent comparison by fixing key architectural components while varying only the world prediction target.

#### 3.1. Aligned Action Channel as a Strong Baseline

To establish a robust baseline, the action channel is aligned between human and robot data as much as possible.
- **Data Sources:** Egocentric human dataset (D_H) collected with Project Aria glasses and bimanual teleoperated robot dataset (D_R) with ego-RGB and wrist-mounted views.
- **Action Unification:** Actions are unified into a 14-D end-effector space (per-arm 6-DoF SE(3) pose + 1-D gripper command).
- **Coordinate Alignment:** Robot actions, derived from joint angles, are re-expressed in the static ego-camera frame. Human hand poses, natively in the moving device frame, are re-expressed in the instantaneous device frame at time *t* to factor out head ego-motion.
- **Residual Mismatches:**
 - **Speed:** Embodiment-specific time windows (T_H = 1s, T_R = 1.5s) are used, spanning comparable progress and discretized into *k* steps.
 - **Workspace Range:** Quantile normalization maps action dimensions to [-1, 1], increasing robustness to outliers.
- **Behavior Cloning (BC) Co-training:** A shared encoder and action decoder are trained end-to-end with a cross-embodiment BC objective using conditional flow-matching losses for both robot and human data. This serves as the strongest action-aligned baseline.

#### 3.2. WAM as a World-Level Transfer Interface

EGOWAM augments the BC policy with an auxiliary future-prediction head to create a WAM.
- **Shared Encoder:** `f_ϕ` maps current observation `o_t` to a latent `z_t`.
- **Parallel Heads:**
 - **Action Head (`π_θ`):** Produces an action chunk `a_t:t+k`.
 - **World-Model Head (`g_ψ`):** Predicts a future state `s_t+T` at horizon `T`.
- **Joint Training:** Both heads are trained jointly under `L_WAM = L_action + λ L_world`, where `λ` balances action fidelity and future prediction. The world-model loss `L_world` supervises the shared trunk `f_ϕ` through dynamics from both embodiments, enabling transfer even when human action labels are problematic.

#### 3.3. EGOWAM: A Controlled Study of World Representations

The paper investigates three world targets, each addressing the three desiderata (Appearance Abstraction, Cross-embodiment Consistency, Ego-motion Factoring) to different degrees. All world-model heads are trained under a shared linear flow path `s_τ = (1-τ)ϵ + τs`.

- **Single-Backbone Architecture and Controlled Variables:**
 - **HPT Backbone:** A Heterogeneous Pretrained Transformer with modality-specific stems (ego vision, proprioception, wrist vision) and a single transformer trunk operating on a unified token stream.
 - **Tokens:** Observation, action, and future-prediction tokens coexist in the same stream.
 - **Action Head:** `π_θ` is a multi-block transformer decoder trained with conditional flow matching. It denoises action tokens conditioned on trunk context.
 - **Swappable World-Model Head:** `g_ψ` is conditioned on trunk embeddings and predicts `s_t+T`.

- **World-Model Target Instantiations:**
 - **Pixel VAE (Reconstruction Baseline):**
 - **Target:** Latent space `s = VAE(I_ego_t+T)` of a pretrained video VAE (Wan VAE) optimized for photometric reconstruction.
 - **Head:** Diffusion transformer (DiT) (Pixel) or VACE-1.3B (Pixel-PT) trained to predict noise.
 - **Desiderata:** Violates all three desiderata (D1-D3) as it directly reconstructs appearance and entangles motion with appearance/ego-motion.
 - **DINO Features (Semantic Abstraction):**
 - **Target:** DINOv2-B patch features of the future ego frame `s = DINO(I_ego_t+T)`.
 - **Head:** Wide-DDT design from RAE, a diffusion transformer denoiser.
 - **Desiderata:** Addresses D1 (semantic prior abstracts appearance) and partially D2 (semantic alignment), but remains spatially indexed, thus partially violating D3.
 - **3D Flow (Spatial Abstraction):**
 - **Target:** Dense 3D motion field `s = F_[t,t+T]` over `[t, t+T]`, expressed in the camera-stabilized frame at time `t`.
 - **Data Processing:** Uses Track4World for 3D point tracking, then re-expresses future positions using Aria VIO camera poses to stabilize the 3D flow. Static background yields near-zero flow, while manipulated objects show motion proportional to physical displacement.
 - **Head:** Alternating self/cross-attention decoder that takes query points `q` from the current ego frame and regresses velocity at those points.
 - **Desiderata:** Satisfies all three desiderata by construction (abstracts appearance, captures physical displacement, factors out ego-motion).

#### 3.4. Joint Training and Action-Only Inference

- **Joint Training:** EGOWAM is trained end-to-end with `L_EGOWAM = (L_robot_action + L_human_action) + λ(L_robot_world + L_human_world)`. Both robot and human data contribute to the world-model loss, shaping the shared trunk `f_ϕ`.
- **Action-Only Inference:** At inference, the world-model head `g_ψ` is discarded. Only the shared trunk `f_ϕ` and action head `π_θ` are used to decode actions `a_t:t+k` from observations, maintaining the latency of a BC policy while leveraging the representation learned through world-model supervision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a systematic study of EGOWAM for human-robot co-training, evaluating it across three real-world bimanual tasks and in simulation. The main findings address three core questions:

#### 4.1. Q1: WAM vs. BC Co-Training under Natural Human Data

- **Behavior Cloning (BC) Limitations:** When the action patterns between robots and humans are misaligned (e.g., different grasping styles), BC co-training often degrades performance by introducing inexecutable human-like actions. With larger amounts of human data, BC tends to overfit to robot-specific data, failing to generalize effectively. For instance, in the "fold-clothes" task, BC+EgoVerse still struggles with novel scenes like lower table heights.
- **WAM Benefits:** WAM co-training consistently translates human data into performance gains, even with misaligned human demonstrations. The UMAP visualization of trunk embeddings (Figure 4) indicates that BC separates human and robot representations, while WAM aligns them into a shared latent space through task-relevant dynamics supervision. This suggests that the world model provides a robust channel for transferring knowledge that is less sensitive to embodiment-specific action differences.

#### 4.2. Q2: World Representation Choice for Cross-Embodiment Transfer

The choice of world representation significantly impacts transfer effectiveness, with different representations offering complementary strengths.
- **Pixel-based Prediction (Pixel VAE):** This method demonstrates weak transfer. Reconstructing raw appearance entangles embodiment- and scene-specific details that do not generalize well. Qualitatively, this led to issues like hallucinating free-space grasps in "bag-grocery" or failing to complete the final fold in "fold-clothes" due to geometric confusion (Figure 5). Pixel-PT (pretrained VAE decoder) also exhibited hallucination in "bag-grocery," often predicting an already-open bag, leading to policies skipping the opening stage.
- **DINO Features (Semantic Abstraction):** DINO-based WAMs, which predict semantic features, yield the strongest out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. They show substantial improvements (up to 4x) in handling unseen objects and novel scenes (Figure 3). The semantic prior helps abstract appearance and align predictions across embodiments, mitigating issues seen with pixel-level targets.
- **3D Flow (Spatial Abstraction):** 3D-flow WAMs, predicting camera-stabilized motion, deliver the largest in-domain spatial gains (20-30% improvement, Figure 3). This representation, by factoring out ego-motion and focusing on physical displacement, enables precise manipulation across the workspace, as observed in successful cup placements for "cup-on-saucer" (Figure 6).
- **World Prediction Quality:** Qualitative comparison of world predictions (Figure 7) shows that pixel-based prediction changes minimally, while DINO-based RAE refines object shape from human data. The 3D-flow target benefits most significantly, accurately recovering object motion that robot-only predictions otherwise leave static.

#### 4.3. Q3: Ablation on Unaligned Human Data

- **Impact of Misaligned Data on BC:** When human demonstrations contain deliberately unaligned or inexecutable actions (e.g., unusual grasps for "bag-grocery"), BC policies collapse, performing worse than a robot-only baseline.
- **Robustness of WAMs:** In contrast, the 3D-flow WAM maintains robustness and continues to outperform the robot-only baseline even with such misaligned human data (Figure 8). This confirms the hypothesis that the world-model channel is less sensitive to action-level misalignment, allowing human data to shape the shared representation effectively.
- **Aligned Human Data (Appendix A.1):** When human data is manually aligned in viewpoint and grasp strategy, BC performance improves substantially (from below robot-only to above). Pixel and DINO also see significant gains (20-30 percentage points), demonstrating their sensitivity to ego-motion. However, the 3D-flow WAM shows invariant performance, achieving high success rates regardless of manual alignment, because its camera-stabilized target inherently factors out ego-motion.

#### 4.4. Robot-to-Robot Transfer in Simulation (Appendix D)

Simulated experiments on the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark confirm the real-world findings:
- Cross-embodiment co-training consistently outperforms single-embodiment training.
- World-model variants (Pixel/DINO/3D Flow) surpass action-only BC policies, indicating that the world-model head drives transfer, not just the action space alignment.
- DINO and 3D Flow demonstrate strong object generalization, particularly in tasks with diverse object pools or appearance shifts, reflecting their appearance-abstraction capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces EGOWAM, a framework that advances the understanding and application of World Action Models (WAMs) for human-robot co-training using in-the-wild egocentric human data. The work addresses a key challenge in robot learning: effectively leveraging diverse human demonstrations despite the inherent embodiment gap between humans and robots.

The primary significance lies in the demonstration that WAM co-training can effectively scale and transfer from large-scale, natural egocentric human data, a scenario where traditional behavior cloning (BC) often falters or even degrades performance. By introducing a world-model head that predicts future scene evolution, EGOWAM provides a robust alternative supervision channel that is less susceptible to non-transferable human-specific motions. This finding positions WAMs as a crucial component for enabling robots to learn from the vast and varied reservoir of human activity videos.

A key contribution is the systematic investigation into the choice of world representation. The authors establish three desiderata—appearance abstraction, cross-embodiment consistency, and ego-motion factoring—and demonstrate that different representations fulfill these to varying degrees, leading to complementary strengths:
- **DINO features** provide strong object and scene generalization, indicating the value of semantic abstraction for out-of-distribution scenarios.
- **3D motion flow** yields robust in-domain spatial gains, highlighting the importance of geometrically grounded, ego-motion-factored dynamics for precise manipulation.
- **Pixel-based reconstruction**, while a common baseline, proves less effective for transfer due to its entanglement of appearance and motion.

The research's implications for robot learning are substantial. It provides a concrete "recipe" for designing transfer mechanisms for robot policies, emphasizing the need for world targets that abstract appearance, ensure cross-embodiment consistency of effects, and factor out ego-motion. This moves beyond simply "using human data" to a more nuanced understanding of *how* to extract transferable knowledge from it. By showcasing that the benefits of human data can be unlocked at the cost of a BC policy during inference, EGOWAM offers a practical pathway for deploying more capable and generalizable robot manipulation policies.

The authors acknowledge limitations, including the focus on context-level gains rather than learning novel motion primitives, and the use of one policy per task rather than multi-task scaling. They also highlight that the "best" world representation remains an open question. These limitations indicate directions for future research, such as developing more unified action representations, exploring multi-task co-training with diverse human datasets, and continuing the quest for even more generalizable world representations. This work serves as a foundational step towards enabling scalable robot learning in complex, open-world environments.
