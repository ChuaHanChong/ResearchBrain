# Combinatorial Rising Bandits

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced the Combinatorial Rising Bandit (CRB) framework to model online learning scenarios involving combinatorial actions and dynamically improving rewards that are partially shared among components. They developed the Combinatorial Rising Upper Confidence Bound (CRUCB) algorithm, which demonstrated near-optimal regret bounds theoretically and empirically outperformed existing baselines in both synthetic and deep reinforcement learning environments.

## Problem

- Existing combinatorial online learning models assume stationary reward distributions, failing to capture scenarios where rewards improve over time.
- Traditional rising bandit models lack combinatorial action structures and the 'partially shared enhancement' where improvements in sub-components benefit multiple complex actions.
- Real-world applications in robotics, network routing, and recommendation systems require models that account for both combinatorial actions and rising, shared rewards simultaneously.

## Method

- The research formalizes the ==Combinatorial Rising Bandit== (CRB) framework, defining problems with combinatorial super arms, rising and concave base arm rewards, and monotone expected reward functions.
- It proposes the Combinatorial Rising Upper Confidence Bound (**CRUCB**) algorithm, which uses a 'Future-UCB' index to optimistically estimate future base arm outcomes by considering recent averages, predicted improvements, and an adaptive exploration bonus.
- CRUCB selects super arms by maximizing the expected reward based on these optimistic future estimates, using a modular solver for various combinatorial structures.

## Results

- CRUCB consistently achieved lower cumulative regret across various synthetic combinatorial tasks, including Online Shortest Path and k-MAX problems, outperforming five state-of-the-art baselines.
- The algorithm demonstrated superior performance in complex deep reinforcement learning AntMaze environments, maintaining robustness even when initial reward growth violated strict concavity assumptions.
- Visualizations confirmed CRUCB's efficient exploration strategies, showing that it quickly identified and exploited optimal paths by leveraging shared improvements, unlike baselines that exhibited inefficient or overly broad exploration patterns.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Constant policies, which can be optimal in non-combinatorial rising bandits, are generally sub-optimal in CRB due to the complex dependencies introduced by partially shared enhancement among base arms.
> - The proposed Future-UCB index, with its adaptive sliding window, effectively balances exploration and exploitation by robustly estimating future rising rewards and their increments for base arms.
> - CRUCB's regret upper bounds closely match the derived theoretical lower bounds for the CRB problem, demonstrating its near-optimality and adaptivity to varying reward growth patterns without requiring prior knowledge of instance difficulty.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of online learning, specifically addressing multi-armed bandit problems with combinatorial structures and evolving rewards.

Existing literature in **combinatorial online learning** focuses on scenarios where an agent selects an action (termed a "super arm") composed of multiple sub-actions (termed "base arms"). This framework is relevant to applications such as robotics, network routing, and recommendation systems. Previous studies in this area have largely assumed stationary reward distributions, meaning the expected reward for pulling an arm does not change over time.

A distinct line of research, known as **rising bandits**, investigates settings where the expected reward of an arm improves each time it is pulled. Examples include learning through practice in robotics or strengthening social influence in recommendation systems. However, these studies have traditionally focused on non-combinatorial settings, where arms are independent and do not share components.

The present work identifies a gap at the intersection of these two areas. It addresses scenarios where a combinatorial action is selected, and pulling constituent base arms leads to an enhancement of future rewards. Crucially, these enhancements can propagate to multiple super arms that share the same base arms, introducing complex dependencies not captured by existing combinatorial bandit models (which lack rising rewards) or rising bandit models (which lack combinatorial structures and shared enhancements). The paper terms this specific challenge "partially shared enhancement," where improving a base arm benefits all super arms that include it, creating coupled reward dynamics. This coupling means that an optimal policy in this combined setting is more intricate than simply repeatedly pulling the best individual arm, a strategy that might be optimal in non-combinatorial rising bandits.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and address the **Combinatorial Rising Bandit (CRB) framework**, which models online learning in scenarios characterized by both combinatorial actions and rising rewards. The motivation for this framework stems from several real-world applications where these two properties are simultaneously present:

1. **Robotics:** In hierarchical robotic planning, complex tasks are decomposed into low-level skills (base arms). These skills improve through repeated execution across various higher-level plans (super arms). The improvement in a low-level skill contributes to the performance of all plans that utilize it.
2. **Social Advertising:** Selecting a set of ads (super arm) for a user, where engagement with individual ad components (base arms) can lead to improved future engagement, and these improvements are shared across different ad combinations.
3. **Network Routing:** Choosing a network path (super arm) composed of individual links (base arms). Repeated use of a link can lead to optimization (e.g., better congestion estimation), enhancing its future performance for all paths that include it.
4. **Recommendation Systems:** Recommending a bundle of items (super arm), where user interaction with individual items (base arms) can refine recommendation models, improving future recommendations that involve those items.

The paper highlights the limitations of existing bandit models in these contexts:
- **Combinatorial bandits without rising rewards:** These models fail to account for the dynamic improvement of base arms, potentially leading to sub-optimal exploitation of early-performing but ultimately inferior options (e.g., sticking with an "early peaker" instead of a "late bloomer" that improves over time).
- **Rising bandits without combinatorial structure:** These models cannot capture the structural dependencies and "partially shared enhancement" that arise when different super arms share base arms. This means they cannot efficiently leverage improvements in shared components across a set of actions.

To address this gap, the research aims to:
- **Formalize the CRB framework:** Define the problem space with clear assumptions regarding reward dynamics (rising nature, concavity) and reward function properties (monotonicity, additive-boundedness).
- **Characterize optimal policies:** Investigate the complexity of finding optimal policies in CRB, particularly highlighting how the "partially shared enhancement" makes constant policies (which are optimal in non-combinatorial rising bandits) generally non-optimal in the combinatorial setting.
- **Develop a provably efficient algorithm:** Design an algorithm that can effectively balance exploration and exploitation in the CRB setting, explicitly accounting for both the combinatorial structure and the rising reward dynamics.
- **Provide theoretical guarantees:** Establish regret bounds for the proposed algorithm and derive corresponding lower bounds for the CRB problem to demonstrate the algorithm's near-optimality.
- **Empirically validate performance:** Demonstrate the algorithm's effectiveness in both synthetic and realistic deep reinforcement learning environments, showcasing its ability to outperform existing baselines that do not fully address the CRB challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the Combinatorial Rising Bandit (CRB) framework and proposes an algorithm called Combinatorial Rising Upper Confidence Bound (CRUCB).

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation (CRB Framework)

- **Base Arms and Super Arms:** The system consists of `K` base arms, and a "super arm" `S_t` is a chosen subset of these base arms (`S_t ⊆ 2^[K]`).
- **Rising Rewards:** Each base arm `i` has an expected outcome `μ_i(n)` that increases with the number of times `n` it has been played. This is formalized by the "rising condition" `γ_i(n) = μ_i(n+1) - μ_i(n) ≥ 0`.
- **Stochastic Outcomes:** When a super arm `S_t` is selected, each base arm `i ∈ S_t` yields an outcome `X_i(t)` drawn from a distribution `D_i(N_i,t-1)`, where `N_i,t-1` is the historical play count of arm `i`. Outcomes are assumed to be `σ^2`-subgaussian.
- **Reward Function:** The reward `R_t` is a function of the chosen super arm `S_t` and its observed outcomes `X_t`. The expected reward `E[R(S,X)]` is assumed to be a monotone function `r(S, μ)` of the mean outcomes `μ = {μ_i : i ∈ S}`.
- **Assumptions:**
 - **Concavity of `μ_i` (Assumption 1):** The rate of improvement decreases over time, i.e., `γ_i(n) ≥ γ_i(n+1)`. This is a common assumption in rising bandit literature.
 - **Monotone Reward (Assumption 2):** The expected reward function `r` is non-decreasing with respect to the mean outcomes of its base arms.
- **Regret Minimization:** The objective is to minimize the cumulative regret, defined as the difference between the total expected reward of an optimal policy and the policy being evaluated over a horizon `T`.

#### 3.2. Characterization of Optimality

The paper first analyzes the optimal policy for CRB. It demonstrates that, unlike in non-combinatorial rising bandit settings where a constant policy (always playing the same arm) can be optimal, a constant policy is generally *not* optimal in CRB (Theorem 1). This is attributed to the "partially shared enhancement" where the rising nature of shared base arms introduces dependencies that favor dynamic selection. However, under an "additive-bounded reward" assumption (Theorem 2), where the reward function is bounded by additive functions, the optimal constant policy can achieve a cumulative reward within a constant factor of the overall optimal policy. For strictly additive rewards, the optimal constant policy is indeed exactly optimal (Corollary 1). This characterization informs the design of CRUCB by highlighting the complexity introduced by combinatorial structures.

#### 3.3. Proposed Algorithm: Combinatorial Rising UCB (CRUCB)

CRUCB operates in two main stages at each round `t`:

1. **Estimation of Future-UCB Index (`´μ_i(t)`):** For each base arm `i`, CRUCB calculates an optimistic estimate of its future expected outcome. This index comprises three components:
 - **(i) Recent Average:** The mean of the most recent `h_i` outcomes of base arm `i`, indicating its current expected performance.
 - **(ii) Predicted Upper Bound of Improvement:** An estimate of the future improvement based on the estimated slope of reward increase (using a finite difference method over the last `h_i` pulls). By the concavity assumption, this provides an optimistic projection.
 - **(iii) Exploration Bonus:** A confidence term `σ(t - N_i,t-1 + h_i - 1) * sqrt(10 log t / h_i^3)` that accounts for uncertainty and promotes exploration of less-pulled arms. This bonus is specifically designed to be larger than standard UCB bonuses to address the greater uncertainty in rising reward settings.
 - **Adaptive Sliding Window (`h_i = εN_i,t-1`):** The window size `h_i` is not fixed but grows proportionally with the number of times `N_i,t-1` a base arm `i` has been pulled. This adaptive design is intended to balance the bias (favoring recent data) and variance (using more data for stability) of the estimates. `ε` is a hyperparameter tuning this trade-off.

2. **Super Arm Selection via Solver:** After computing `´μ_i(t)` for all base arms, CRUCB utilizes a "Solver" to select the super arm `S_t` that maximizes the expected reward given these optimistic estimates: `S_t = arg max_{S ∈ S} r(S, ´μ)`. The Solver is an interchangeable component that depends on the specific combinatorial structure of the problem (e.g., Dijkstra's algorithm for shortest path problems). This modular design allows CRUCB to adapt to various combinatorial optimization tasks.

#### 3.4. Regret Analysis

The paper provides both an upper bound for CRUCB's regret and a lower bound for the CRB problem:

- **Regret Upper Bound (Theorem 3):** CRUCB's regret is bounded by two main terms:
 - Term (i): `O(KT^q * Υ((1-2ε)LT/K, q))`, which quantifies regret due to the inherent difficulty of the rising reward problem, influenced by the cumulative increment `Υ` (overall outcome growth) and `L` (maximum super arm size). `q` is an analytical tool describing reward-growth patterns.
 - Term (ii): `O(KT^(2/3) * (log T)^(1/3))`, which accounts for regret due to stochasticity in observed outcomes.
 - **Corollary 2** further refines this bound based on the explicit form of the slope function `f(n)` (e.g., exponential decay leading to sub-linear regret, power-law decay `(n+1)^(-c)` leading to linear or sub-linear regret depending on `c`).
- **Regret Lower Bound (Theorem 4 & 5):**
 - **General Class (Theorem 4):** Without specific structural assumptions on `γ_i`, the lower bound is `Ω(LT)`, indicating that linear regret is unavoidable in the worst-case general CRB instance.
 - **Fine-grained Class (Theorem 5):** For instances where `γ_i(n) ≤ (n+1)^(-c)` (i.e., bounded reward growth), the lower bound is `Ω(max(L√T, LT^(2-c)))`. This demonstrates that sub-linear regret is achievable for "easier" instances with faster-saturating rewards.

The paper emphasizes that CRUCB's regret upper bound is shown to closely match these regret lower bounds across different problem difficulties (illustrated in Figure 3), indicating its near-optimal efficiency and adaptivity without requiring prior knowledge of instance difficulty parameters like `q` or `c`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical and empirical results that support the effectiveness and efficiency of the CRB framework and the CRUCB algorithm.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

1. **Non-Optimality of Constant Policies in CRB:** The paper theoretically demonstrates that, in general, a constant policy (one that always selects the same super arm) is not optimal for CRB (Theorem 1). This is a significant departure from non-combinatorial rising bandit settings, where constant policies can be optimal. This finding underscores the added complexity introduced by the "partially shared enhancement" in combinatorial settings.
2. **Approximation by Constant Policies under Additive-Bounded Rewards:** Despite the general non-optimality, the paper shows that if the reward function satisfies an "additive-bounded reward" assumption (where rewards are bounded by additive functions), the optimal constant policy can achieve a cumulative reward within a constant factor (`B_U/B_L`) of the overall optimal policy (Theorem 2). For purely additive reward functions, the optimal constant policy is exactly optimal (Corollary 1). This provides insight into problem instances where a simpler strategy might still perform well.
3. **Tight Regret Bounds for CRUCB:**
 - **Upper Bound (Theorem 3):** CRUCB achieves a regret upper bound composed of two terms: one related to the inherent difficulty of the rising reward problem (`O(KT^q Υ)`) and another related to the stochasticity of outcomes (`O(KT^(2/3) (log T)^(1/3))`). The term `Υ` quantifies outcome growth, making the bound adaptive to the instance difficulty.
 - **Difficulty-Adaptive Regret (Corollary 2):** By analyzing specific forms of reward growth (e.g., `γ_i(n) = exp(-n)` or `γ_i(n) = (n+1)^(-c)`), the corollary shows that CRUCB's regret bounds adapt to whether outcomes saturate quickly (sub-linear regret) or slowly (potentially linear regret). For `f(n)=(n+1)^(-c)` with `c > 1`, the regret becomes `O(max(T^(2/3)log^(1/3)KT, T^(1/c)log(LT/K)))`, demonstrating explicit dependence on `c`.
 - **Lower Bounds (Theorem 4 & 5):** A general regret lower bound of `Ω(LT)` is established for any policy, indicating linear regret in the worst case. For a fine-grained class of instances with `γ_i(n) ≤ (n+1)^(-c)`, the lower bound becomes `Ω(max(L√T, LT^(2-c)))`.
 - **Near-Optimality:** The paper demonstrates that CRUCB's regret upper bounds closely match the derived lower bounds, particularly for various reward growth patterns (Figure 3). This indicates that CRUCB is nearly optimal in terms of theoretical efficiency, without requiring prior knowledge of instance-specific parameters like `c` or `q`.

#### 4.2. Empirical Findings

Experiments were conducted across synthetic and deep reinforcement learning environments, comparing CRUCB against five baselines: R-ed-UCB (non-combinatorial rising bandit), SW-UCB (non-stationary non-combinatorial UCB), SW-TS (non-stationary non-combinatorial Thompson Sampling), SW-CUCB (non-stationary combinatorial UCB), and SW-CTS (non-stationary combinatorial Thompson Sampling).

1. **Synthetic Environments (Online Shortest Path, k-MAX, Maximum Weighted Matching, Minimum Spanning Tree):**
 - **Consistent Outperformance:** CRUCB consistently achieved lower cumulative regret across all synthetic tasks (Figures 5, 9, 11, 13) compared to all baselines, in both "easy" and "complex" graph structures.
 - **Limitations of Baselines:**
 - **R-ed-UCB:** Underperformed due to its inability to handle combinatorial structure and the "partially shared enhancement." It often incorrectly interprets cumulative increments as immediate growth, leading to inefficient exploration (Figure 1d).
 - **Non-stationary Combinatorial Algorithms (SW-CUCB, SW-CTS):** These algorithms performed worse than their non-combinatorial counterparts (SW-UCB, SW-TS) in some complex tasks. This was attributed to their tendency to exploit early-peaking paths, limiting exploration of late-blooming base arms and preventing their full reward potential from being realized.
 - **Non-stationary Non-combinatorial Algorithms (SW-UCB, SW-TS):** While sometimes outperforming their combinatorial counterparts in complex graphs due to broader exploration, they still generally performed worse than CRUCB as they did not account for the rising reward nature.
2. **Deep Reinforcement Learning (AntMaze Environments):**
 - **Robustness to Assumption Violations:** Despite the AntMaze environment exhibiting non-concave reward growth initially (before successful task completion, as shown in Figure 7a), CRUCB maintained superior performance over baselines, demonstrating its robustness beyond strict theoretical assumptions.
 - **Efficient Exploration in Complex RL:** In both AntMaze-easy and AntMaze-complex tasks, CRUCB achieved lower cumulative regret (Figure 7b,c).
 - **Visualized Exploration Patterns (Heatmaps - Figure 8, 14, 15, 16):**
 - **SW-CUCB:** Showed inefficient exploration by repeatedly attempting "impossible" paths, indicating a failure to correctly estimate long-term potential.
 - **R-ed-UCB:** Exhibited broad, uniform exploration across many paths (breadth-first-like search) due to its inability to leverage partially shared enhancements. This led to inefficient use of the exploration budget and failure to converge on the optimal path.
 - **CRUCB:** Demonstrated efficient and focused exploration, quickly avoiding impossible paths, exploiting shared improvements, and concentrating on the optimal path. Its exploration patterns closely resembled the oracle optimal policy.

#### 4.3. Sensitivity Analysis to Hyperparameter `ε`

A sensitivity analysis on the `ϵ` hyperparameter (which controls the adaptive window size) for CRUCB in the Path-easy environment revealed that CRUCB's performance was stable across a range of `ϵ` values (0.05 to 0.4), indicating that the algorithm is not overly sensitive to this specific hyperparameter choice in practical settings (Table 3).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces the Combinatorial Rising Bandit (CRB) framework, addressing a previously unmodeled intersection of combinatorial online learning and rising bandit problems. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Bridging a Research Gap:** The CRB framework formally addresses the unique challenges posed by "partially shared enhancement," where improvements in base arms propagate across multiple super arms. This mechanism is common in real-world systems but was not fully captured by prior bandit formulations, which either lacked combinatorial structure or the rising reward dynamic.
2. **Theoretical Advancements:**
 - The rigorous characterization of optimality, demonstrating that constant policies are generally not optimal in CRB, deepens the theoretical understanding of this problem class.
 - The derivation of tight regret upper and lower bounds for CRB, and the demonstration of CRUCB's near-optimality, represent a notable theoretical contribution, particularly in the rising bandit literature. The adaptive nature of the regret bounds to problem difficulty (via parameters like `c`) provides a nuanced understanding of learning complexity.
3. **Practical Algorithm Development:** The proposed CRUCB algorithm offers a provably efficient and empirically effective solution for complex decision-making scenarios. Its Future-UCB index design, incorporating recent averages, predicted improvement, and a robust exploration bonus, along with an adaptive sliding window, enables it to balance exploration and exploitation in evolving combinatorial environments.
4. **Demonstrated Robustness and Efficacy:** Extensive experiments in both synthetic and deep reinforcement learning environments highlight CRUCB's practical utility. Its superior performance over state-of-the-art baselines, even when theoretical assumptions (like concavity) are not strictly met, suggests its robustness and applicability to complex, real-world systems. The visualization of exploration patterns clearly illustrates how CRUCB efficiently navigates the trade-offs, avoiding sub-optimal traps that hinder other methods.
5. **Broad Applicability:** By modeling scenarios where components (base arms) improve over time and are reusable in complex actions (super arms), CRB and CRUCB have potential implications for various domains, including:
 - **Robotics:** For learning and optimizing hierarchical skills.
 - **Network Routing:** For adaptive path selection in dynamic networks.
 - **Recommendation Systems:** For learning user preferences and improving item bundles.
 - **Crowdsourcing and LLM-based Planning:** For optimizing task assignments or iterative reasoning processes where sub-tasks improve with practice.

**Future Directions:** The paper identifies extending CRB to handle dynamic action spaces (e.g., in skill discovery for robotic systems) as a promising avenue for future research. This would further enhance the framework's applicability to even more adaptive and evolving environments.
