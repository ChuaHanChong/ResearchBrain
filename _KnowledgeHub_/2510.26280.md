# Thor: Towards Human-Level Whole-Body Reactions for Intense Contact-Rich Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Thor, a reinforcement learning framework, enables humanoid robots to exhibit human-level whole-body reactions in intense contact-rich environments. The framework uses a decoupled policy and a Force-Adaptive Torso-Tilt (FAT2) reward function, allowing the Unitree G1 robot to pull forces up to 48% of its body weight and successfully execute tasks like opening fire doors and pulling loaded carts.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to maintain stability and generate adaptive forces during intense, contact-rich interactions due to high dimensionality and inherent instability.
- Existing model-based and learning-based control methods are limited by accurate modeling requirements, conservative balance assumptions (CoM within support polygon), or suboptimal performance in rich force interactions.
- Many reinforcement learning approaches for humanoids fail to leverage the waist's potential for efficient force distribution and effective high-intensity force interaction.

## Method

![[2510.26280-method.png]]
*Complete system architecture showing the decoupled policy structure with three Actor-Critic networks, training process with FAT2 rewards, and deployment pipeline from simulation to real-world implementation.*

- Thor introduces a novel decoupled policy architecture that partitions control into lower body, waist, and upper body modules, inspired by human biomechanics and multi-agent RL.
- A Force-Adaptive Torso-Tilt (FAT2) reward function is implemented, encouraging human-like torso tilting beyond the conventional CoM-within-support-polygon constraint to enhance force generation.
- The framework employs two-stage curriculum learning and domain randomization, specifically for force disturbances, to enhance robustness and facilitate sim-to-real transfer on the Unitree G1 robot.

## Results

- The Unitree G1 robot achieved peak pulling forces of 167.7 N (approximately 48% of its body weight) in backward locomotion, demonstrating a 68.9% improvement over state-of-the-art baselines.
- Thor successfully performed complex real-world tasks, including opening a fire door with one hand (requiring ~60 N of force) and pulling a cart loaded with 70 kg.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the FAT2 reward function was the primary contributor to enhanced force-interaction capabilities, while the decoupled architecture significantly mitigated high-dimensionality issues and anomalous waist behaviors under high loads.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling humanoid control into distinct lower body, waist, and upper body modules effectively mitigates high-dimensionality challenges and improves force transmission efficiency.
> - Human-inspired adaptive torso tilting, as encouraged by the FAT2 reward, is critical for enabling humanoids to generate and withstand greater interaction forces by dynamically adjusting their Center of Mass.
> - The robot can implicitly perceive interactive forces via proprioceptive signals, allowing for adaptive posture adjustments during deployment without needing direct force measurements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of navigating and interacting effectively with human-centric environments is a pivotal area of robotics research. Humanoids offer unparalleled advantages in service, industrial, and rescue applications due to their anthropomorphic design, which allows for enhanced maneuverability and accessibility in spaces designed for humans. However, realizing this potential necessitates robots that can maintain stability and generate adaptive forces during intense, contact-rich interactions—a persistent grand challenge in the field.

The broader research landscape for humanoid control is bifurcated between traditional model-based methods and more recent learning-based approaches, particularly Reinforcement Learning (RL). Traditional model-based methods, while providing precise control for predefined tasks in structured environments, are fundamentally limited by their reliance on accurate robot modeling and hard-coded policies. They often struggle with unstructured terrains, dynamic task environments, and require explicit measurement or estimation of external forces, thus hindering real-world deployment.

Reinforcement learning (RL) methods have emerged as a promising alternative, offering robustness in unstructured environments and eliminating the need for complex, explicit modeling. Despite these advantages, RL approaches for humanoids face their own set of challenges, primarily the high-dimensionality of humanoid robots and their inherent instability, often likened to a 3D linear inverted pendulum. This high-dimensionality often leads to suboptimal performance in tasks requiring rich force interactions. Furthermore, many existing RL methods for forceful interaction in legged robots, such as Falcon and Facet, often assume that the robot’s Center of Mass (CoM) projection must remain within the support polygon of its feet. This assumption, while simplifying control, severely constrains the full potential of humanoids to exert large forces by preventing more dynamic, human-like postures.

Regarding policy architectures, the field has explored various strategies: from single monolithic policies to multi-modal approaches, teacher-student frameworks, and explicit decoupling of upper and lower body control. While these advancements have improved locomotion and manipulation, they often either overlook the specific challenges of high-intensity force interaction or fail to fully leverage the coordination potential of the waist as an intermediate control module for effective force distribution.

"Thor" positions itself directly to address these critical gaps. By introducing a novel decoupled policy architecture that includes the *waist* as a distinct control module, it aims to mitigate the high-dimensionality problem more effectively and facilitate superior force transmission. Crucially, Thor's Force-Adaptive Torso-Tilt (FAT2) reward function directly challenges the conventional CoM-within-support-polygon assumption, explicitly encouraging human-like torso tilting. This allows the humanoid’s CoM to move outside the foot support region in specific situations, thereby significantly enhancing its ability to generate and withstand greater interaction forces. In essence, Thor advances the state-of-the-art by combining an anatomically inspired control architecture with a biomechanically motivated reward function to enable more robust, adaptive, and human-level whole-body reactions in highly demanding contact-rich environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the "Thor" research is to enable humanoid robots to exhibit human-level, adaptive whole-body reactions, particularly in intense and contact-rich environments. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific aims:

1. **Develop a Robust Whole-Body Control (WBC) Framework:** The authors aim to create an RL-based WBC framework that can handle the complexities of humanoid kinematics and dynamics under external forces.
2. **Mitigate High-Dimensional Control Challenges:** A key technical objective is to overcome the inherent difficulties arising from the large number of degrees of freedom (DoFs) in humanoids, which complicate control and learning processes.
3. **Ensure Stable Locomotion and Sufficient Force Output:** The framework must guarantee the robot's stability while simultaneously maximizing its capacity to exert or withstand significant forces at its end-effectors (EEs).
4. **Promote Human-like Adaptive Postures:** A core objective is to imbue the robot with the ability to adjust its posture, specifically by tilting its torso, in a manner analogous to how humans react to strong external forces, thereby enhancing its interaction capabilities.
5. **Validate Performance in Real-World Scenarios:** The research seeks to demonstrate the practical effectiveness and generalizability of the proposed framework across various challenging real-world tasks, such as opening heavy doors or pulling loaded objects.

The motivation behind "Thor" stems from the immense, yet largely untapped, potential of humanoids in crucial applications like service industries, complex manufacturing, and post-disaster rescue. These environments invariably demand robots that can perform high-intensity force-interaction tasks while maintaining smooth and stable motion. For instance, opening a fire door with one hand requires the robot to apply substantial force while counteracting significant torsional moments, necessitating sophisticated whole-body coordination.

Current limitations in both traditional and learning-based control methods—such as over-reliance on accurate models, restriction to structured environments, high-dimensionality issues, and overly conservative balance assumptions (e.g., CoM within support polygon)—impede humanoids from fulfilling this potential. The authors are particularly motivated by the observation that humans naturally leverage torso tilting to generate greater forces and maintain balance during strenuous physical interactions, like tug-of-war. By drawing inspiration from human biomechanics and integrating this insight into a reinforcement learning framework, Thor seeks to unlock a new level of performance for humanoids in physically demanding tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Thor proposes a novel Reinforcement Learning (RL) based Whole-Body Control (WBC) framework for humanoids, designed to achieve human-level reactions in contact-rich environments. The core of its methodology revolves around a decoupled policy architecture and a biomechanically inspired reward function.

#### 3.1. Decoupled Policy Architecture

Inspired by multi-agent RL and human biomechanics, particularly the waist's crucial role in force transmission, Thor decomposes the humanoid's WBC strategy into three distinct, yet cooperative, control modules:
- **Lower Body (π_l):** Primarily responsible for robust locomotion and generating ground friction forces.
- **Waist (π_w):** Focuses on tracking waist control commands and efficiently transmitting forces from the lower body to the upper body end-effectors (EEs). This is a unique inclusion, differentiating Thor from common upper/lower body decoupling approaches.
- **Upper Body (π_u):** Tracks upper-body motions, initially trained by sampling from human motion datasets (AMASS).

Each of these three components operates as an independent agent within a shared environment. They each possess their own Actor-Critic network, where the actor network (π_θ_i) takes global observations (robot's proprioception, previous actions, control commands, target joint angles) as input. The critic network (V_φ_i) additionally receives privileged information (e.g., actual linear velocity, orientation, external force magnitudes and directions on EEs) during simulation training, which accelerates convergence without being directly available during real-world deployment.

The outputs of the three actor networks—desired joint positions for the upper body, waist, and lower body—are concatenated to form the complete desired joint positions for the humanoid. These sub-agents are trained collectively using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. Each agent computes its own TD-residual and Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) for parameter updates, with an individual clipped policy objective, value function MSE loss, and entropy regularization. The overall optimization objective is the sum of these individual objectives, plus a torque regularization term to prevent excessive output from specific components and coordinate overall energy consumption.

To further enhance learning and address the sim-to-real gap, Thor employs:
- **Two-stage Curriculum Learning:** Training begins in a low-force disturbance environment to establish stable motion capabilities, then transitions to a high-difficulty stage with extreme force disturbances to improve high-intensity force-interaction performance.
- **Domain Randomization:** Gaussian noise is applied to the direction and magnitude of force disturbances on the robot’s EEs during training, making the learned policies more robust to variations in real-world contact conditions.

#### 3.2. Force-Adaptive Torso-Tilt (FAT2) Reward Function

Inspired by how humans lean into a pull or push to generate more force, Thor introduces the FAT2 reward function. This function encourages the robot to adaptively tilt its torso in response to interactive forces, moving beyond the traditional Zero Moment Point (ZMP) constraint that typically keeps the CoM projection within the foot support polygon.

The theoretical basis for FAT2 is a quasi-static force and torque equilibrium analysis using the ZMP criterion. By modeling the robot as a rigid body, the authors derive an equation for the *expected* torso tilt angle (β) given the interactive force (F_h), gravity (F_g), and the robot’s geometry (CoM position, EE position, contact angles). This allows for situations where the CoM projection lies *outside* the support polygon, mimicking human behavior for enhanced force generation. The reward function is an exponential term that penalizes the squared difference between the robot's current torso tilt and this calculated expected tilt angle.

During deployment, the actual interactive force is implicitly perceived by the robot through proprioceptive signals like torso angular displacement and velocity along the y-axis, allowing it to adapt its posture without direct force measurements. This adaptive tilting strategy is critical for increasing the robot's effective interaction force.

#### 3.3. Deployment

In real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 robot, the trained actor networks run at 50 Hz, producing desired joint angles. These are then fed into a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller, operating at 500 Hz, to generate the final joint torques applied to the motors. Motion commands for the robot can be derived from a remote controller or from Virtual Reality (VR) via inverse kinematics for the upper body.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted extensive quantitative evaluations through both simulation and real-world experiments on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, addressing three key questions: the effect of FAT2, Thor's performance relative to baselines, and the individual contributions of FAT2 and the decoupled policy structure.

#### 4.1. Impact of Force-Adaptive Torso-Tilt (FAT2) (Q1)

- **Simulation Observations:** Sequential plots in the simulation environment clearly demonstrated that Thor's policy, incorporating FAT2, enables the robot to adapt its posture dynamically. Under small pulling forces, the robot remained upright. As the force increased, particularly during forward motion, its body gradually tilted to accommodate stronger interactions. Crucially, in high-force scenarios, the robot's Center of Mass (CoM) projection was observed to be entirely outside the support polygon of its feet, enabling it to exert greater force. As the pulling force decreased, the robot's posture adaptively returned to normal.
- **Real-World Validation:** Real-world experiments on the Unitree G1 robot confirmed this adaptive behavior. Data showed a direct correlation between the pulling force generated by the robot and its torso tilt angle, reinforcing that Thor, like humans, inclines its torso to generate greater pulling force.

#### 4.2. Overall Performance Compared to Baselines (Q2)

Thor consistently and substantially outperformed state-of-the-art baseline methods, including Falcon, Homie, and the Unitree G1’s official default policy, across a wide range of force-interaction tasks.
- **Peak Pulling Forces:**
 - **Backward Locomotion (dual-hand):** Thor achieved a peak pulling force of 167.7 ± 2.4 N, which is approximately 48% of the G1’s body weight. This represents a remarkable 68.9% improvement compared to the best-performing baseline, Falcon (99.3 N).
 - **Forward Locomotion (dual-hand):** Thor reached 145.5 ± 2.0 N, showing a 74.7% improvement over Falcon (83.3 N).
- **Directional and Hand Usage Differences:**
 - Pulling forces generated in the backward direction were generally higher than in the forward direction. This is attributed to Thor's human-inspired coordination strategy, which leverages the robot's body weight more effectively by leaning backward.
 - Single-hand pulling forces were significantly smaller than dual-hand forces due to the robot having to counteract its own torsional moments.
- **Qualitative Real-World Task Success:**
 - Thor successfully **opened a fire door with one hand**, requiring approximately 60 N of steady pulling force, a task where baseline methods failed due to insufficient force and lateral deviation.
 - It successfully **pulled a cart loaded with 70 kg** (requiring 130 N of force) and **pushed a wheelchair carrying 60 kg** while maneuvering, demonstrating robust loco-manipulation capabilities.
 - The robot also performed tasks like **wiping a blackboard**.
 - (Note: Custom shoe covers were used in some experiments to ensure consistent ground friction, particularly in scenarios where natural friction might be insufficient.)

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (Q3)

To ascertain the individual contributions of FAT2 and the decoupled policy architecture, ablation studies were conducted:
- **Thor_1 (FAT2 Only):** This variant, which incorporated only the FAT2 mechanism without the decoupled network structure, achieved approximately 80%–90% of Thor’s overall performance, and in certain tasks, even matched it. This strongly indicates that the **FAT2 reward function is the primary contributor** to the humanoid’s enhanced force-interaction capability.
- **Thor_2 (Decoupled Network Only):** While specific performance percentages were not detailed for this variant, the authors noted that during experiments with Thor_1, high-dimensionality issues led to anomalous waist behaviors (e.g., deviations in roll angle) under large pulling forces. The decoupled network architecture in Thor_2 (and full Thor) effectively **mitigated these high-dimensionality problems**, significantly reducing such unreasonable waist movements. This suggests that while FAT2 drives the force generation, the decoupled architecture provides the necessary stability and coordination to fully exploit FAT2's potential and prevent catastrophic failures under high loads.

In summary, Thor's superior performance is a synergistic result of its human-inspired adaptive posture mechanism (FAT2) and its robust, high-dimensional problem-mitigating, decoupled control architecture.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Thor" framework represents a significant advancement in the field of humanoid robotics, particularly in enabling robots to perform complex, high-intensity physical interactions with their environment. Its contributions have several far-reaching implications:

1. **Breakthrough in Force-Interaction Capabilities:** By achieving pulling forces up to 48% of the robot's body weight and demonstrating substantial improvements (over 68%) compared to state-of-the-art baselines, Thor significantly pushes the boundaries of what humanoid robots can physically accomplish. This level of force interaction is crucial for real-world applications that involve moving heavy objects, opening stiff doors, or stabilizing against strong external disturbances.

2. **Addressing Core Challenges in Humanoid Control:** Thor effectively tackles two long-standing challenges: the **high-dimensionality problem** of humanoids and their **instability in contact-rich environments**. The novel decoupled policy architecture, particularly the inclusion of the waist as a dedicated control module, is key to managing the complexity of humanoid control and facilitating efficient force transmission.

3. **Human-Inspired Adaptation for Enhanced Robustness:** The introduction of the Force-Adaptive Torso-Tilt (FAT2) reward function is a paradigm shift. By allowing the robot's Center of Mass (CoM) to move outside the support polygon, a behavior observed in humans during strenuous tasks, Thor enables a more dynamic and effective response to external forces. This biomimetic approach not only increases the robot's force generation capabilities but also enhances its overall robustness and balance under extreme conditions. This represents a move towards more intelligent, biologically plausible control strategies.

4. **Demonstrated Practicality and Generalizability:** The successful deployment of Thor on the Unitree G1 robot for diverse, challenging real-world tasks—such as single-handed fire door opening, pulling a heavily loaded cart, and pushing a wheelchair—validates its practical utility and generalizability. These demonstrations are critical steps towards bringing humanoids out of controlled lab environments and into real-world service, industrial, and rescue scenarios.

5. **Accelerated Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The systematic use of curriculum learning and domain randomization, specifically applied to force disturbances, contributes to bridging the notorious sim-to-real gap, ensuring that policies learned in simulation translate effectively to physical robots.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Broader Deployment of Humanoids:** Thor's capabilities make humanoids more viable for deployment in a wider range of physically demanding applications, from logistics and manufacturing (e.g., moving heavy equipment, assembling large components) to domestic assistance (e.g., re-arranging furniture) and disaster relief (e.g., clearing debris, manipulating rescue tools).
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The decoupled architecture and the FAT2 mechanism provide a strong foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages further exploration of biomechanically inspired control, more sophisticated multi-agent RL architectures for complex robots, and adaptive impedance control strategies.
- **Inspiration for Human-Robot Collaboration:** A robot capable of human-level reactions and robust force interaction can engage in more natural and effective physical collaboration with humans, leading to new paradigms in assistive and industrial robotics.

The authors acknowledge that achieving optimal performance still requires manual tuning of hyperparameters, a common limitation in RL. Their plan to incorporate learning from human demonstration videos in future work indicates a clear path towards making these complex policies more autonomous and easier to develop, further accelerating the impact of such advanced control frameworks on the future of humanoid robotics.
