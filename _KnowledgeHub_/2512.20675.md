# Revisiting the Learning Objectives of Vision-Language Reward Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This work demonstrates that a simple triplet loss objective, when used for finetuning Vision-Language Models for reward modeling, often surpasses or rivals the performance of more complex state-of-the-art objectives under controlled experimental conditions. It suggests that observed performance gains in previous methods might stem from differences in pre-training data and model architecture rather than objective complexity.

## Problem

- Developing generalizable reward functions for embodied AI agents is challenging, often relying on laborious, domain-specific human supervision.
- Prior research on VLM-based reward models lacked systematic comparison, with methods pre-trained on different datasets and architectures, making it difficult to attribute performance gains to specific learning objectives.
- Existing complex learning objectives for finetuning VLMs on video demonstrations sometimes struggled with domain shifts, action specificity, and complex task dynamics.

## Method

- A unified experimental framework was established, controlling for the VLM backbone (SigLIP2 with ==LoRA==), finetuning data (Meta-World expert demonstrations), and evaluation environments (held-out Meta-World tasks).
- Popular learning objectives (TCN, VIP, R3M, LIV, and their language adaptations) were systematically re-evaluated alongside a newly proposed ==simple triplet loss baseline==.
- Performance was quantified using two metrics: consistency with ground-truth reward (==ranking accuracy==) and alignment with expert progress (==Value-Order Correlation - VOC==).

## Results

- The triplet loss achieved the highest overall accuracy (**68.88%** average) in ranking consistency with ground-truth reward across held-out Meta-World tasks, outperforming all other objectives.
- TCN-based objectives performed similarly to each other, securing the second-best tier (e.g., L_R3M at **66.40%** average accuracy), while VIP-based objectives showed considerably lower average accuracies (e.g., L_LIV at **52.07%**).
- In terms of alignment with expert progress (VOC), the triplet loss demonstrated strong positive correlations and generally outperformed VIP-based methods, but all objectives, including triplet loss, struggled significantly on more complex multi-step tasks like 'door-open' (e.g., L_Triplet multi-view VOC was **6.70%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A simple triplet loss, which aligns language goals with later images and pushes earlier images away, proved surprisingly effective and robust in VLM-based reward modeling.
> - The performance advantages observed in prior, more complex learning objectives might be primarily due to variations in pre-training data, model architectures, and evaluation setups, rather than the inherent sophistication of the objectives themselves.
> - VIP-based objectives showed inconsistent and often poor performance, struggling to capture monotonic progress accurately, highlighting limitations in their design for reward modeling.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself directly within the rapidly expanding and critically important field of **embodied intelligence**. A central challenge in embodied intelligence, particularly for reinforcement learning (RL) agents, is the creation of generalizable reward functions. Traditional methods often rely on hand-crafted human supervision, which is laborious, domain-specific, and lacks scalability. This limitation has spurred significant interest in developing methods for *learning* reward functions autonomously.

Recent advancements have seen a surge in leveraging large **vision-language models (VLMs)**, such as CLIP, as a foundation for constructing dense, domain-agnostic reward signals. The initial promise of these models was their ability to provide "zero-shot" reward signals, where alignment between visual observations and language goals could directly translate into a reward. However, the paper acknowledges that purely zero-shot VLM approaches, as shown by Rocamonde et al. (2024), often struggle with domain shifts, action specificity, and the nuances of environmental dynamics in complex robotic tasks.

To mitigate these shortcomings, the research landscape has shifted towards **finetuning VLMs** on large-scale video demonstration datasets (e.g., Ego4D, EpicKitchens). This finetuning aims to imbue VLMs with a better understanding of task-relevant behaviors and temporal semantics from real-world interactions. Concurrently, researchers have proposed a variety of increasingly complex **learning objectives**—such as those underpinning R3M (Nair et al., 2022), VIP (Ma et al., 2023b), and LIV (Ma et al., 2023a)—designed to extract more accurate and meaningful signals of task progress from these video demonstrations.

However, a critical methodological issue has emerged: the performance improvements reported by these advanced methods are difficult to attribute definitively. As the paper highlights, many of these methods were pre-trained using different datasets, initialized with varying architectures, and evaluated under diverse settings. This heterogeneity creates confounding factors, making it nearly impossible to isolate whether observed gains are truly due to the sophistication of the proposed learning objective or merely artifacts of superior pre-training data, larger model capacity, or tailored evaluation environments.

This paper directly addresses this gap by adopting a **meta-analysis** approach, systematically comparing these learning objectives under a rigorous, unified framework. By controlling for model backbone, finetuning data, and evaluation environments, the authors aim to decouple the impact of the learning objective from other variables. In doing so, this work aims to provide clarity and foundational insights into the true effectiveness of different learning objectives, offering a crucial contribution to the scientific rigor and future direction of VLM-based reward modeling in embodied intelligence. It challenges the implicit assumption that more complex objectives inherently lead to better performance, pushing for a more evidence-based understanding of what truly drives progress in this domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **systematically evaluate and compare the effectiveness of different learning objectives** used in finetuning Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for reward modeling in embodied intelligence. The authors aim to achieve this by isolating the specific impact of the learning objective, thereby removing confounding variables that have plagued previous comparisons.

More specifically, the key objectives include:
1. **Develop a Unified Framework:** Establish a standardized experimental setup where the pre-trained model backbone, finetuning data, and downstream evaluation environments are held constant across all tested learning objectives. This framework is crucial for ensuring a fair and apples-to-apples comparison.
2. **Benchmark Existing Learning Objectives:** Re-evaluate popular and state-of-the-art learning objectives—including adaptations of Time-Contrastive Networks (TCN) for language, VIP (Value-Implicit Pre-training), R3M (A Universal Visual Representation for Robot Manipulation), and LIV (Language-Image Representations and Rewards for Robotic Control)—under the controlled conditions of the unified framework.
3. **Propose a Simple Baseline:** Introduce and assess a simple triplet loss as a minimalist baseline for VLM-based reward modeling, hypothesizing that its simplicity might offer surprising effectiveness.
4. **Quantify Performance with Robust Metrics:** Evaluate the performance of the finetuned reward models using two distinct and complementary benchmarks:
 - **Consistency with Ground-Truth Reward:** To measure the model's robustness and accuracy in ranking the progress of individual timesteps.
 - **Alignment with Expert Progress:** To assess how well the model's predicted rewards correlate with goal-directed behavior along expert trajectories, indicating its potential for guiding agents.

The core motivation for this work stems from the observed difficulty in interpreting the results of prior research in VLM-based reward modeling. The authors explicitly state that "most of these methods were pre-trained using different datasets and architectures, making it difficult to isolate the choice of learning objective from downstream performance." They cite examples like R3M and LIV, highlighting how differences in pre-training, initialization, and encoder choices complicate direct comparisons. This ambiguity hinders the field's ability to identify the most promising avenues for developing generalizable reward functions. By systematically decoupling the learning objective from other influential factors like data and model architecture, the authors aim to provide clear, actionable insights into which types of objectives are truly effective, thereby guiding future research and development more efficiently towards robust and transferable reward models. Their underlying hypothesis is that much of the apparent progress might not be due to objective complexity but rather to external factors, a hypothesis they aim to test and validate.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this paper's methodology lies in its systematic and controlled comparison of different learning objectives for Vision-Language Model (VLM)-based reward functions. The authors achieve this by implementing a **unified experimental framework** designed to isolate the impact of the learning objective from other confounding factors.

**A. Learning Objectives Under Investigation:**
The study focuses on a selection of prevalent and state-of-the-art learning objectives, as well as a novel, simpler baseline:

1. **Time-Contrastive Networks (TCN) and its Language Adaptation (L_TCN_text):** TCN (Sermanet et al., 2018) aims to reduce embedding distances between temporally close images and increase distances for temporally distant images. The authors adapt L_TCN by replacing a temporally distant image with a language annotation for the given sequence, thus aligning visual progress with textual goals.
2. **Value-Implicit Pre-training (VIP) and its Language Adaptation (L_VIP_text):** VIP (Ma et al., 2023b) takes a goal-conditioned approach, separating embeddings of temporally adjacent images while drawing together embeddings of images that are farther apart in time. Similar to TCN, L_VIP is adapted to language by substituting a distant image with the language annotation.
3. **R3M (L_R3M):** This objective is recreated as a combination of both TCN and L_TCN_text, with a specific note that the authors omit the initial embedding prediction MLP used in the original R3M for fairness, to avoid introducing additional parameters not present in other methods.
4. **LIV (L_LIV):** This is composed of both VIP and L_VIP_text, augmented with an InfoNCE objective (Oord et al., 2018), which is also included in their experimental setup.
5. **Triplet Loss (L_Triplet) - Proposed Baseline:** The authors introduce a simple triplet loss (Schroff et al., 2015) as their baseline. In this context, the language goal acts as the anchor, a later image in the sequence serves as the positive example (indicating progress), and an earlier image is the negative example (indicating less progress or regress). The objective minimizes the distance between the language goal and the later image while pushing the earlier image further away, controlled by a margin parameter ($\alpha$).

**B. Unified Framework Components:**

1. **Model Backbone:** To ensure consistent model capacity and pre-training knowledge, all methods utilize the **base version of SigLIP2** (Tschannen et al., 2025) as their backbone. This choice provides a strong, unified foundation for vision-language understanding. To prevent overfitting during finetuning, **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)** (Hu et al., 2021) is employed, a technique that significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters.
2. **Finetuning Data:** All models are finetuned on expert demonstrations collected from the **Meta-World environments** (Yu et al., 2021). To control for consistency, a maximum of 50,000 samples are used, generated evenly across 3 expert trajectories per task with randomized end-effector initializations. Crucially, 3 distinct camera views are recorded for each timestep, enabling multi-view training. These views are randomly reassigned at each iteration, acting as data augmentation to promote view-invariant representations and reduce overfitting.
3. **Evaluation Environments:** To assess the generalizability and out-of-domain performance of the learned reward models, three specific tasks are excluded from the finetuning data and reserved solely for evaluation: **button-press, drawer-open, and door-open**. These tasks vary in complexity, from simple (button-press) to compositional multi-step tasks (drawer-open, door-open).

**C. Evaluation Metrics:**

1. **Consistency with Ground-Truth Reward:** This benchmark measures the robustness of the reward model. Per-task datasets of 10,000 timestep pairs are constructed from random and sub-optimal rollouts. Each pair is labeled according to which timestep has a higher ground-truth reward. The model predicts the higher-reward timestep by comparing the similarity of each image in the pair to the language goal. Accuracy is defined as the percentage of pairs where the model's prediction matches the ground-truth ordering.
2. **Alignment with Expert Progress (Value-Order Correlation - VOC):** To evaluate a model's capacity to guide an agent towards a goal, 50 expert trajectories are collected per task. Rewards are predicted along these trajectories. Assuming expert timesteps inherently correlate with increasing ground-truth reward, the **Value-Order Correlation (VOC)** (Ma et al., 2024) is computed. VOC measures how well the model's predicted reward ordering aligns with this goal-directed behavior. A model should ideally perform well on both metrics to be considered effective and transferable.

The approach is meticulously designed to create a level playing field, ensuring that any observed differences in performance can be confidently attributed to the distinct learning objectives rather than variations in data, architecture, or evaluation conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results present a compelling and somewhat counter-intuitive finding that challenges current assumptions in VLM-based reward modeling. The core revelation is the remarkable effectiveness of the simple triplet loss compared to more complex, state-of-the-art objectives when evaluated under a unified framework.

**A. Consistency with Ground-Truth Reward (Table 1):**
- **Triplet Loss (L_Triplet) Dominance:** The simple triplet loss consistently achieved the highest overall prediction accuracy across all held-out Meta-World tasks (button-press, open-drawer, open-door), with an average accuracy of 68.88%. This significantly surpassed all other evaluated objectives. For example, on the 'button-press' task, L_Triplet achieved 76.44% accuracy with multi-view, compared to 73.76% for L_R3M and 55.51% for L_LIV.
- **TCN-based Objectives (L_TCN_text, L_R3M) Performance:** These objectives performed similarly to each other and generally well, securing the second-best performance tier. Their average accuracies (65.93% and 66.40% respectively) were notable, but still trailed L_Triplet.
- **VIP-based Objectives (L_VIP_text, L_VIP_text + L_VIP, L_LIV) Underperformance:** Objectives incorporating VIP components exhibited inconsistent and often poor ranking ability. Their average accuracies (58.12%, 56.10%, 52.07%) were considerably lower than TCN-based methods and L_Triplet, with L_LIV often performing close to random chance (50%).
- **Baseline SigLIP2:** The base SigLIP2 backbone, without finetuning, performed slightly below 50% accuracy on average (46.85%), confirming that finetuning on expert demonstrations is indeed necessary to encode temporal and task-relevant semantics.
- **Multi-view Benefits:** The multi-view aggregation generally improved performance across all methods, suggesting the efficacy of the multi-view augmentation in learning view-invariant representations.

**B. Alignment with Expert Progress (Value-Order Correlation - VOC) (Table 2):**
- **Triplet Loss (L_Triplet) Strong Correlation:** Consistent with the accuracy results, the triplet objective demonstrated strong positive correlations with expert progress. It notably outperformed all VIP-based objectives across all tasks and rivaled TCN-based objectives, especially on 'button-press' (e.g., 95.07% multi-view VOC for L_Triplet vs. 94.53% for L_TCN_text) and 'drawer-open' (e.g., 90.17% for L_Triplet vs. 87.22% for L_R3M).
- **VIP-based Objectives High Variance and Negative Correlations:** VIP-based objectives showed high variance and frequently exhibited negative correlations, particularly on the 'button-press' task (e.g., L_VIP_text + L_VIP had -21.03% VOC on View 1), indicating significant reward inconsistencies along expert trajectories. This suggests these objectives struggle to capture monotonic progress accurately.
- **Challenges with Multi-step Tasks:** A notable finding was that **all methods, including L_Triplet, struggled significantly on the 'door-open' task**, which is a more complex multi-step task requiring navigation and manipulation. This indicates a general limitation of the current approaches in handling highly compositional tasks. The average VOC for L_Triplet on 'door-open' multi-view was only 6.70%, showing a steep drop from simpler tasks.
- **Overall Conclusion:** The findings from both benchmarks strongly support the conclusion that simpler, ranking-based losses, specifically the triplet loss, are capable of producing highly accurate and robust reward models.

In summary, the most critical finding is that the performance advantage often attributed to complex learning objectives in recent literature might largely be a consequence of differences in data, model architectures, and pre-training rather than the intrinsic sophistication of the objective function itself. The simple triplet loss, under controlled conditions, proved to be remarkably effective, suggesting that the field might have been overcomplicating its objective functions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of embodied intelligence and the broader development of VLM-based reward models:

**1. Challenging Conventional Wisdom and Shifting Research Focus:**
The most profound impact of this work is its direct challenge to the prevailing assumption that increasingly complex learning objectives are necessary for improved performance in VLM-based reward modeling. By demonstrating that a simple triplet loss can outperform or rival state-of-the-art methods under controlled conditions, the paper suggests that researchers may have been overcomplicating their approaches. This could lead to a re-evaluation of current research directions, encouraging a focus on the fundamental elements driving performance, such as data quality, diversity, and model architecture, rather than solely on objective function intricacy.

**2. Guiding More Efficient Research and Development:**
If simpler objectives are demonstrably more effective or equally effective, it streamlines the research and development process. Designing, implementing, and optimizing complex loss functions can be time-consuming and prone to instability. Discovering that a basic triplet loss offers superior performance provides a clear and robust baseline, potentially simplifying the development of reward models and allowing researchers to allocate more resources to other critical areas like data collection, multi-modal integration, or hierarchical task decomposition. This could accelerate progress in domains where learning generalizable rewards is a bottleneck.

**3. Emphasis on Standardized Benchmarking:**
The paper explicitly underscores "the need for standardized benchmarks for fair and consistent evaluation." By meticulously designing a unified framework, the authors highlight the critical importance of controlling confounding variables. This work serves as a powerful example and advocate for the adoption of more rigorous, standardized evaluation protocols across the embodied AI community. Such standardization would enable true head-to-head comparisons of methods, foster cumulative scientific progress, and prevent misleading conclusions driven by uncontrolled experimental settings.

**4. Implications for Embodied AI and Robot Learning:**
Robust and generalizable reward functions are fundamental for enabling embodied agents (like robots) to learn complex tasks in diverse, unstructured environments without extensive human intervention. The finding that a simpler objective can yield accurate rewards is highly significant for robotic learning. It could lead to more stable and easier-to-train reward models, facilitating their deployment in real-world applications. While the current results show limitations for highly compositional tasks (e.g., 'door-open'), understanding the strengths of simpler objectives provides a stronger foundation for addressing these more complex challenges.

**5. Re-evaluating the Role of Data and Architectures:**
The authors' conclusion that "much of the apparent progress in recent methods could instead be due to differences in data and model architecture" is a critical insight. This shifts the spotlight towards the immense impact of large-scale, diverse pre-training data and sophisticated foundational models (like SigLIP2) as primary drivers of VLM capabilities. Future work might therefore prioritize innovations in data curation, self-supervised pre-training, and architectural design over solely developing new loss functions for finetuning.

**6. Future Research Directions:**
The paper itself points to several avenues for future work, including "deeper analysis of each objective to understand their failure cases, scaling to larger datasets, more challenging multi-step tasks, and temporal compression methods." The robust baseline provided by the triplet loss can serve as a strong reference point for these explorations, enabling researchers to better identify the specific limitations of existing approaches and focus on targeted improvements.

In conclusion, this research is a significant methodological contribution that provides much-needed clarity in the rapidly evolving landscape of VLM-based reward modeling. By demonstrating the surprising efficacy of a simple triplet loss under controlled conditions, it not only offers a powerful and efficient baseline but also redirects the field's attention towards the fundamental drivers of performance, emphasizing the critical need for standardized evaluation practices and potentially simplifying the design philosophy for future reward learning systems.
