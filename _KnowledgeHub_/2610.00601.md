# When Reasoning Helps Action: Monitoring and Steering Chain-of-Thought in Vision-Language-Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> TRUST is a separate value model, trained offline, that reads a frozen VLA policy's observation and partial chain-of-thought and predicts whether the finished reasoning will be correct. It uses that estimate both to monitor reasoning and to steer it through gated value-augmented sampling. Steering lifts reasoning correctness and cuts driving collisions. In manipulation, though, corrected reasoning leaves task success flat, which exposes the gap between being able to correct reasoning and the policy acting on it.

## Problem

- Reasoning-enabled VLAs expose chain-of-thought traces that look like a runtime safety interface, but no prior method detects and corrects unreliable reasoning while it is being generated in frozen policies.
- Prior faithfulness studies corrupt or audit reasoning offline, without testing whether corrections made at runtime actually change downstream actions in the intended direction.
- Existing embodied runtime monitors watch perception inputs or executed actions, so they catch failures only after reasoning has already committed the policy to a flawed plan.

## Method

- **Two evaluation axes.** ==Correctability== asks whether unreliable reasoning can be detected and improved during generation. ==Actionability== asks whether those corrections change behavior in the intended direction.
- **TRUST** value model: a separate ==LoRA==-tuned multimodal transformer with an ==MLP scalar head==, trained with ==trace-normalized binary cross-entropy== to predict eventual correctness from observation plus reasoning prefix, without needing policy weights or hidden states.
- ==Gated value-augmented sampling==: when predicted correctness drops below a threshold calibrated for balanced accuracy, TRUST re-weights the policy's top-10 next-token candidates by ==value log-odds==. Training data comes from contradiction-mined traces labeled by a VLM judge.

## Results

- On Alpamayo 1.5, TRUST matches the judge's correctness verdict **88.9%** of the time and raises reasoning correctness from **75.9%** to **90.0%**, ahead of compute-matched Best-of-4 at **85.9%**.
- On the hard AlpaSim subset, collision rate drops from **15.33%** to **10.67%** (**30.4%** relative) and maximum trajectory error falls **11.5%**, while braking statements produce three times the speed reduction.
- On DeepThinkVLA, grasp-state correctness rises from **69.3%** to **90.2%**, yet LIBERO-Plus success stays at **78.86%** vs **78.26%**. Gibberish or no-CoT substitution performs as well as ground-truth reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Better reasoning correctness does not automatically produce better embodied behavior. Test whether the action decoder actually responds to the reasoning before treating chain-of-thought as a safety lever.
> - Token-level steering beats Best-of-N under the same compute because it can build correct traces the policy never sampled, whereas reranking only picks among sampled outputs.
> - RL post-training meant to tie reasoning to actions can still leave the stated intent and the gripper motion largely disconnected. Substitution tests with gibberish controls expose this cheaply.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A reasoning-enabled vision-language-action (VLA) policy writes a textual account of what it sees and intends to do, and then it acts. In one of the paper's examples, a vehicle's reasoning treats a green straight-through signal as governing even though a yellow left-turn arrow calls for stopping. That text could offer a runtime safety handle. It can do so only if a system catches a flawed explanation in time to change it, and the policy then acts on the correction.

The authors' approach scores the reasoning as it is being written. When the partial reasoning looks unreliable, it nudges the next words toward a better-supported continuation. In the driving example, replacing the mistaken reasoning with a stop instruction makes the vehicle decelerate toward a stop. In a contrasting manipulation example, correcting a false grasp claim barely changes the action chunk. These are illustrative interventions, not proof that every corrected trace improves behavior.

Top: the unsteered text says to accelerate through the intersection, and the corrected text says to stop for the yellow arrow. The plotted speed falls under correction. Minimum ADE is 8.83 m versus 2.02 m across six samples. Bottom: after a hallucinated grasp trace is corrected, the distance and path curves change little. Both panels show single interventions, not aggregate results.

### 2. Two tests, one monitor

The authors separate two questions. **Correctability** asks whether unreliable reasoning can be detected and improved during generation. **Actionability** asks whether correcting it changes behavior in the intended direction. Better text alone does not establish better behavior.

Their method is TRUST (Token-level Reward for Utility-Steered Chain-of-Thought). A separate value model is trained offline. It reads the observation and the reasoning prefix so far, then estimates whether the completed trace will be correct. A VLM judge defines "correct" against the observation and whether the proposed behavior is appropriate. Traces the judge deems ambiguous are excluded.

At runtime, TRUST flags prefixes whose predicted correctness falls below a validation-set threshold. For a flagged prefix, it scores the policy's top ten next-token candidates and reweights their sampling toward continuations with higher predicted correctness. It reevaluates this gate as generation continues. The resulting reasoning then passes through the frozen VLA's existing reasoning-to-action pathway. The scorer does not need policy weights or hidden states.

### 3. Driving: correction reaches the road

On Alpamayo 1.5, TRUST agrees with the judge's verdict 88.9% of the time. Reasoning correctness is the fraction of traces the judge labels correct. Across 730 held-out traces, it rises from 75.9% unsteered to 90.0% with TRUST.

The stronger test is a compute-matched Best-of-4 baseline. It samples four ordinary traces and acts on the one with the highest TRUST value. Best-of-4 can only choose among completed traces, while TRUST can steer token by token. Best-of-4 reaches 85.9%, and TRUST's advantage over it is reported at p = 0.0073.

For closed-loop driving, 888 clips are simulated in AlpaSim. The highlighted subset contains 300 clips, selected solely because the unsteered policy's maximum trajectory distance exceeded 30 m. On that challenging subset, TRUST lowers collision rate from 15.33% to 10.67%, a statistically significant reduction. It also lowers maximum distance from the ground-truth trajectory from 67.33 m to 59.56 m, an 11.5% improvement. On the same subset, Best-of-4 has the same collision rate as unsteered and higher maximum trajectory error than TRUST.

Some metrics do not improve. Close encounters rise from 5.00% to 6.00% on this subset.

### 4. Manipulation: better text, same success

On DeepThinkVLA, TRUST raises judged grasp-state correctness from 69.3% to 90.2% and action-choice correctness from 68.8% to 85.9%. Both gains are statistically significant. Task success on 1,509 LIBERO-Plus episodes, however, is 78.86% unsteered versus 78.26% steered, with no significant change across the four suites.

The policy still responds to the reasoning. Steering changed the outcome in 145 episodes: 68 failures became successes and 77 successes became failures. Overall, TRUST raises the share of stated grasp/release intents followed by motion in the demanded direction from 9.0% to 14.0%. About 76% of intents have no detectable finger motion under either policy.

Share of stated intents that are followed (blue), contradicted (red), or followed by no movement beyond 2 mm (gray). "Follows" rises from 9.0% to 14.0% for all intents. The no-movement share is about 76% under both policies.

### 5. Correcting isn't acting

Correctability and actionability are distinct. TRUST improves judged reasoning in both tested policies, but only the driving subset shows behavioral gains.

The method also has costs and limits. Steering adds about 81% inference time over unsteered Alpamayo 1.5. Correctness depends on a VLM judge and its rubric. The value model is trained on unsteered rollouts, even though steering changes the reasoning distribution.
