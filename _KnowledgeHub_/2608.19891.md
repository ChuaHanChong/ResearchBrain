# EXIMO: VLM Guided Exploration of VLA Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Eximo finetunes a pretrained vision-language-action manipulation policy on new long-horizon and reasoning tasks without extra teleoperation, in three stages: a vision-language model orchestrates the VLA in closed loop with natural-language subgoals to explore, successful episodes are filtered and distilled back into the VLA by supervised finetuning, then a residual off-policy RL controller optimizes further. It beats the base VLA and its RL-finetuned variant on 22 Aloha simulation tasks in sample-efficiency and final success.

## Problem

- Large-scale behaviour cloning of VLA policies needs hundreds of teleoperation hours per new task and robot, and generalizes poorly outside its collected data distribution.
- Reinforcement learning is sample-inefficient under sparse rewards on long-horizon tasks, and large diffusion-transformer VLAs that emit action chunks are hard to train directly with RL.
- Prior language-model planners spend effort grounding generated plans into robot APIs or fixed skill sets, and keep the planner online at evaluation, incurring its latency.

## Method

- ==Explore==: a Gemini VLM orchestrator conditions on image history plus the goal and emits one closed-loop next subgoal per step to a 3B **Gemini Robotics On-Device** VLA, needing no grounding layer.
- ==Imitate==: episodes passing a ground-truth success detector are filtered, and the VLA is behaviour-cloned on action chunks conditioned on the original task goal, distilling the orchestrator away.
- ==Optimize==: a ==residual policy== conditioned on state, goal and VLA action outputs a correction added to the VLA action, trained by off-policy ==MPO== on the sparse success indicator.

## Results

- Across **22** Aloha tasks evaluated over **1000** episodes each, VLM orchestration raises base-VLA success at comparable time-to-success and shortens episodes, with largest gains on chained and reasoning tasks.
- Distilled supervised finetuning beats both the base VLA and orchestration kept at evaluation time; residual RL on top raises success further on every task, averaged over **five** seeds.
- Base VLA plus residual RL never matches finetuned-plus-RL despite running past **2.5e7** environment steps to compensate for exploration data, and free-form versus pick-and-place instructions perform on par.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Split labour by competence: use foundation-model semantics for task decomposition and the action model for atomic motor skills, then distill so deployment needs no planner.
> - Filtering to successful episodes plus goal-relabelled distillation can exceed the orchestrated system that generated the data, since supervision is high-quality and single-policy.
> - Distilling the orchestrator into the residual controller instead of the base policy backfires: the offline-to-online distribution shift made it learn slower than plain residual RL.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is currently undergoing significant advancements, largely driven by large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models trained via extensive behavioral cloning (BC) on teleoperation datasets. While this approach has enabled robots to perform a repertoire of atomic skills, it faces limitations in generalization to novel, long-horizon, or reasoning-intensive tasks. Such tasks typically require chaining multiple skills or interpreting goals outside the training distribution.

Two primary paradigms exist for robot policy learning:
- **Large Scale Behavioral Cloning (LBC):** This method, while simple and more sample-efficient than reinforcement learning, suffers from generalization challenges. Policies are confined to the data distribution of teleoperation, and collecting data for new tasks is expensive and time-consuming, requiring extensive human labor.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** RL allows agents to self-improve through interaction with the environment. It has shown success in domains with high-fidelity simulators (e.g., games, locomotion). However, RL is known for its high sample complexity, especially for long-horizon tasks, which limits its direct application in real-world robotics. Furthermore, applying RL to large VLA models is challenging due to their size and architectural design (e.g., diffusion models predicting action chunks).

The current research positions itself at the intersection of these two paradigms, aiming to bridge their respective gaps. It leverages advancements in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and builds upon prior work in several areas:
- **Foundation Models as High-level Planners:** Previous research has explored using large language models (LLMs) or VLMs as planners for robotic tasks. These approaches often involve LLMs generating high-level plans that require grounding into executable robot actions. Solutions for grounding include providing robot APIs in prompts, using masked LLMs, or employing multiple LLMs for planning and grounding. This work utilizes VLAs (e.g., GROD) that can directly interpret natural language instructions, thereby mitigating the explicit grounding challenges faced by many LLM-based planners. Some prior work also focuses on jointly training VLM and VLA for decomposition.
- **Foundation Models for Exploration:** Foundation models have been utilized to guide exploration in RL by decomposing long-horizon problems into subtasks, quantifying novelty, measuring interestingness, or proposing goals. Unlike some of these works that train vision policies from scratch via RL and focus on grounding, this study focuses on the sample-efficient finetuning of existing VLA policies directly instructed by a VLM, without requiring additional grounding.
- **RL Finetuning of VLAs:** Finetuning large VLA policies with RL is a known challenge. Existing methods include learning Q-functions to optimize actions or learning a residual policy to correct VLA outputs. The current work adopts a residual policy approach similar to some prior research, but integrates VLM-guided exploration to enhance sample efficiency.

In summary, this work contributes to the landscape by proposing a multi-stage finetuning algorithm that synergistically combines the semantic reasoning capabilities of VLMs with the motor skills of VLAs, aiming to overcome the limitations of conventional BC and RL for efficient adaptation to new, complex robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an efficient algorithm for finetuning pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies to enable them to learn and execute new, complex tasks on the fly. This adaptation is targeted to be sample-efficient and achievable without requiring additional human teleoperation.

The primary motivations for this work stem from critical challenges in contemporary robotic learning:
- **Limitations of Pre-trained VLA Policies:** While state-of-the-art VLA policies, trained through large-scale behavioral cloning (LBC) on extensive teleoperation datasets, reliably perform a range of atomic skills (e.g., pick and place) within their training distribution, their performance degrades significantly on tasks that:
 - Are **new or unseen** during initial training.
 - Are **long-horizon**, requiring a sequence of interdependent actions.
 - Are **compositional**, demanding the chaining of multiple atomic skills.
 - Are **reasoning-heavy**, necessitating semantic understanding or inference about objects and goals that lie outside the exact linguistic or visual patterns seen during training (e.g., "put the fruit the monkey likes to eat in the bowl").
- **Cost and Inefficiency of Data Collection:** The standard method of collecting high-quality data for training robot policies through human teleoperation is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. It demands hundreds of hours of skilled human labor and must often be repeated for each new task or robotic system. This renders the collection of diverse datasets covering all plausible real-world tasks intractable.
- **Sample Inefficiency of Reinforcement Learning (RL):** While RL offers a path for agents to self-improve through interaction, it is notoriously sample-inefficient, especially for complex, long-horizon problems where effective exploration is challenging. Applying RL directly to large VLA models is further complicated by their architectural design and size, making training challenging.

To address these motivations, the authors propose an approach that aims to:
- **Leverage World Knowledge of VLMs:** Utilize the rich world knowledge and semantic understanding embedded in state-of-the-art Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to provide high-level guidance and task decomposition.
- **Automate Data Collection:** Generate high-quality training data for novel tasks through VLM orchestration, thereby bypassing the need for human teleoperators.
- **Distill Knowledge into VLAs:** Transfer the learned capabilities from VLM-guided interactions back into the VLA policy through supervised finetuning, allowing the VLA to perform complex tasks autonomously without constant VLM query at inference time.
- **Enhance with RL:** Further refine the distilled policy using online reinforcement learning, building upon the improved baseline performance achieved through VLM orchestration and supervised finetuning to achieve higher task success and robustness.

The overarching objective is to adapt VLA policies to novel tasks specified solely by a natural-language goal and a success detector, achieving high sample efficiency and final performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed algorithm, named Eximo, operates as a three-stage training pipeline: Explore, Imitate, and Optimize. The methodology is designed to efficiently finetune pre-trained VLA policies for new, complex manipulation tasks without requiring additional human teleoperation.

The foundational components of Eximo include:
- **Initial VLA Policy:** The research uses the 3B variant of Gemini Robotics On-Device (GROD) as the base VLA. GROD is a language-conditioned, goal-conditioned manipulation policy built on the PaliGemma VLM backbone with a diffusion policy head. It is trained on teleoperated data for the ALOHA robot in both real and simulated environments. GROD can perform fundamental skills but struggles with tasks outside its training distribution, particularly those requiring chaining of skills or complex reasoning.
- **Vision-Language Model (VLM):** Gemini (Team et al., 2023) serves as the VLM, providing high-level semantic understanding and planning capabilities.
- **Environment Success Detector:** A ground-truth success detector in the simulation environment is assumed to filter successful episodes.

The three stages of Eximo are as follows:

#### 3.1. Explore: VLM-Guided Data Collection

The first stage focuses on efficiently collecting high-quality data for new, unseen tasks.
- **VLM as an Orchestrator:** The VLM acts as an orchestration policy, serving as a planner. Given the history of states ($s_{\le t}$) and the overall natural language goal ($g$), the VLM generates a sequence of tractable intermediate goals ($g_t$) in natural language for the VLA to execute. This decomposition breaks down challenging, long-horizon problems into shorter, more manageable sub-problems.
- **Closed-Loop Interaction:** The VLM and VLA interact in a closed-loop manner. The VLA executes actions ($a \sim \pi_{VLA}(\cdot|s, g_t)$) based on the VLM's instruction, and the VLM continuously adapts its instructions based on the observed state.
- **Data Collection and Filtering:** The VLM-orchestrated policy is rolled out in the environment to collect trajectories for the new tasks. Only successful episodes, as determined by the environment's ground-truth success detector, are stored in a data buffer. This filtering process ensures that the collected dataset contains only high-quality demonstrations of task completion.
- **Semantic Exploration:** This stage leverages the VLM's world knowledge for semantic exploration, allowing the VLA to discover successful action sequences for tasks it previously struggled with, without direct human intervention. This separation leverages the VLA's precise physical skills and the VLM's semantic understanding.

#### 3.2. Imitate: Supervised Finetuning on VLM Orchestrated Data

In the second stage, the knowledge acquired during VLM-guided exploration is distilled into the VLA policy.
- **Distillation Objective:** The VLA (GROD) is finetuned using supervised learning (behavioral cloning) on the filtered, successful episodes collected in the "Explore" stage. The objective is to train the VLA to predict action chunks ($a_{0:K-1}$) directly conditioned on the original, overall task goal ($g$) and the current state ($s$).
- **Training Data:** The dataset consists of tuples $(s, g, a)$, where $s$ is the state, $g$ is the overall task goal, and $a$ is the action chunk executed by the VLA under VLM guidance during a successful rollout.
- **Benefits of Distillation:**
 - **Reduced Latency:** By distilling VLM's knowledge into the VLA, the finetuned VLA can operate in real-time without requiring online VLM queries, which typically introduce latency.
 - **Minimized VLM Calls:** After finetuning, the VLM is no longer needed during evaluation or subsequent learning phases, reducing computational overhead.
 - **Simplified Downstream RL:** A standalone VLA simplifies the architecture for the subsequent online RL stage.
 - **Performance Improvement:** Distillation allows the VLA to directly learn the multi-step, VLM-guided behaviors, often leading to better performance than continuous online VLM orchestration.

#### 3.3. Optimize: Finetuning via Online RL

The final stage further enhances the performance and robustness of the finetuned VLA using online reinforcement learning.
- **Residual Policy:** Given the challenges of directly applying RL to large VLA models, a residual policy approach is adopted. Instead of directly controlling the robot, a smaller, trainable residual policy ($\pi_{ref}$) learns to output a correctional action ($\Delta a$). The executed action is the sum of the VLA's base action and the residual action: $a = a_{VLA} + \Delta a$.
- **Conditioning:** The residual policy is conditioned on the current state ($s$), the overall goal ($g$), and the action proposed by the finetuned VLA ($a_{VLA}$), i.e., $\Delta a \sim \pi_{ref}(\cdot|s, g, a_{VLA})$.
- **Residual MDP:** The state space for the residual MDP is defined as $x = (s, a_{VLA})$.
- **Sparse Reward:** The residual policy optimizes for the underlying probability of success, where a sparse reward $\mathbb{1}_{s_t \in \text{Success}(g)}$ is provided upon task completion. The preceding SFT phase ensures that the VLA already achieves non-trivial success rates, making the sparse reward problem more tractable for RL exploration.
- **RL Algorithm:** The MPO (Maximum a Posteriori Policy Optimization) algorithm is employed for training the residual controller.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Robotic Platform and Environment:** The method is empirically validated on the ALOHA robotic platform within a simulation environment.
- **Task Suite:** Evaluation is conducted on 22 diverse manipulation tasks (Table 1), including dish-placement, reasoning-intensive, spatial-understanding, and chained tasks. These tasks specifically target scenarios where the base VLA struggles due to compositional or out-of-distribution elements.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Performance is assessed using success rate, time-to-success, and episode length.
- **Ablation Studies:** The study performs ablation studies to investigate the individual contributions of each Eximo stage. Baselines include the base GROD, GROD + VLM-Orchestration, GROD + SFT, and GROD + RL, with Eximo representing GROD + SFT + RL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Eximo, conducted across 22 manipulation tasks in simulation, yielded several findings regarding the efficacy of its multi-stage approach.

#### 4.1. VLM Orchestration for Enhanced Exploration

The study compared the performance of the base VLA (GROD) with the VLA operating under VLM orchestration (GROD + VLM-Orchestration).
- **Increased Success Rate:** VLM orchestration significantly increased the success rate of GROD across the task suite, as shown in Figure 3 (top). This improvement was particularly notable for long-horizon tasks requiring chaining of skills (e.g., "PlateBowlOnRack") and reasoning tasks (e.g., "BananaInBowl-Reasoning0").
- **Similar Time-to-Success:** The time-to-success remained comparable or slightly improved with VLM orchestration (Figure 3, middle).
- **Improved Sample-Efficiency:** The VLM-orchestrated policy demonstrated shorter episode lengths (Figure 3, bottom), indicating that VLM guidance leads to more efficient and successful data collection.
These results suggest that VLMs can effectively guide semantic exploration, enabling the collection of higher-quality and more efficient training data for novel tasks.

#### 4.2. Benefits of Supervised Finetuning (SFT) on Orchestrated Data

The performance of the finetuned GROD agent (GROD + SFT) was compared against the VLM-orchestrated and non-orchestrated agents.
- **Substantial Performance Gains:** Finetuning GROD on the filtered, successful VLM-orchestrated data ("GROD + SFT") resulted in a substantial improvement in success rate compared to both the base GROD and the GROD + VLM-Orchestration configurations (Figure 3, top).
- **Superior to Online Orchestration:** The finetuned VLA outperformed the online orchestrated agent, indicating that distilling VLM-guided behaviors into a standalone VLA policy is more effective than relying on continuous online VLM queries during evaluation.
- **Attributed Factors:** These performance gains are attributed to two main factors: (i) the SFT dataset contained only successful, high-quality episodes, providing effective supervision; and (ii) distillation compiled the multi-step, VLM-guided logic into a single policy, eliminating VLM latency and simplifying direct control conditioned on the overall task goal.

#### 4.3. Sample-Efficient Online Reinforcement Learning (RL) Finetuning

The study evaluated the impact of online RL on both the base GROD model and the GROD + SFT model.
- **Higher Starting Performance:** GROD + SFT + RL (Eximo) commenced with a notably higher success rate compared to GROD + RL, due to the preceding finetuning on VLM-orchestrated data (Figure 4).
- **Superior Convergence:** Eximo converged to a higher final success rate and maintained better time-to-success performance than GROD + RL, even when the latter was allowed significantly more environment steps. This indicates that the improved data quality from VLM orchestration and subsequent SFT provides a stronger foundation for RL, making the learning process more effective and sample-efficient.
- **Consistent Across Tasks:** Online RL consistently boosted the success rate of the agent across nearly all tasks post-SFT (Figure 5), demonstrating the benefits of the "Optimize" phase of Eximo. Furthermore, on several tasks, GROD + SFT alone surpassed the performance of the base GROD model even after it underwent RL finetuning with considerably more data.

#### 4.4. Additional Experiments

- **VLM Instruction Robustness:** An investigation into the impact of VLM instruction format (free-form natural language vs. restricted "pick and place" commands) found that GROD performed comparably with both, demonstrating its robustness to diverse natural language commands (Figure 6).
- **Challenges in Residual Policy Distillation:** Attempts to distill VLM orchestration directly into a residual policy using offline RL (AWBC) showed that while the residual policy improved offline, it learned significantly slower during online RL compared to a pure online RL baseline (Figure 7). This was attributed to a distribution shift between the offline VLM-orchestrated data and online rollouts.
- **Partial Online VLM Orchestration:** Introducing VLM orchestration for a fraction of episodes ($p$) during online residual policy training did not improve evaluation performance (without VLM) compared to not using the VLM at all (Figure 8, top). This finding suggests that training the residual policy to correct VLM-orchestrated actions for a portion of the data may hinder its ability to correct the base VLA actions in an evaluation setting where the VLM is absent, despite the VLM enhancing data collection success (Figure 8, bottom).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Eximo framework presents a methodological contribution to the field of robotic learning, addressing key limitations of existing approaches and indicating potential for more efficient and autonomous robot skill acquisition.

#### 5.1. Paradigm Shift in VLA Training

The work proposes a novel three-stage post-training procedure for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This framework suggests that VLAs, initially trained on fundamental robotic skills via behavioral cloning, can be efficiently adapted to learn complex, long-horizon, and reasoning-intensive tasks by leveraging the semantic capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). This approach offers a departure from traditional teleoperation-heavy data collection, potentially reducing the cost and time required to deploy robots to new domains.

#### 5.2. Addressing Sample Efficiency and Generalization

Eximo directly confronts two persistent challenges in robot learning: the high sample complexity of reinforcement learning (RL) and the generalization limitations of large-scale behavioral cloning (LBC). By using VLMs to guide efficient data collection (Explore phase) and then distilling this knowledge into the VLA (Imitate phase), the framework generates a strong initial policy that makes subsequent RL finetuning more sample-efficient and tractable. This integration facilitates robust learning for tasks that VLAs alone would struggle with due to being out-of-distribution.

#### 5.3. Synergistic Combination of Foundation Models

The research demonstrates an effective strategy for combining the strengths of different foundation models. It utilizes VLMs for high-level planning, semantic understanding, and task decomposition, while relying on VLAs for precise, low-level motor control and action generation. This separation allows each model to operate within its area of expertise, leading to improved overall performance. The distillation process further optimizes this synergy by integrating the VLM's planning capabilities into the VLA for efficient real-time control.

#### 5.4. Enhanced Performance and Autonomy

The experimental results indicate consistent performance improvements across a diverse suite of manipulation tasks. The ability to achieve higher success rates and greater sample efficiency without additional human teleoperation hours suggests a pathway toward more autonomous robot learning systems. The authors' discussion of future work, including VLM-based success detectors and environment resets, hints at the potential for a fully self-supervised learning loop for robots.

#### 5.5. Broader Applicability

Given that the core components are large, pre-trained foundation models (Gemini VLM and GROD VLA), the principles demonstrated by Eximo may be transferable to other robotic platforms and task settings, provided that suitable base VLAs and VLMs are available. This could accelerate the development and deployment of generalist robots capable of adapting to a wide range of human-specified tasks.

In summary, Eximo contributes a structured and effective methodology for expanding the capabilities of pre-trained robotic policies. By integrating VLM-guided exploration and knowledge distillation with residual reinforcement learning, the work provides a framework that can significantly improve the efficiency and adaptability of robotic systems for complex, real-world tasks.
