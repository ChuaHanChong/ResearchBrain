# CEER: Compliant End-Effector and Root Control as a Unified Interface for Hierarchical Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> CEER introduces a compliant end-effector and root (EE-root) control abstraction for humanoid robots, providing a unified task-space interface for hierarchical loco-manipulation. The framework, built on a learned policy using a teacher-student setup, enables smoother end-effector motions and facilitates diverse contact-rich tasks in both simulation and hardware experiments.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid control methods often require high-dimensional, embodiment-specific joint trajectories from planners, which is computationally demanding and limits transferability.
- Traditional joint-tracking approaches lack explicit compliance modulation, hindering safe and robust interaction with objects and the environment during contact-rich tasks.
- A mismatch between sparse, task-level goals from high-level planners and dense, full-body references required by low-level execution complicates modular system integration.

## Method

- Developed a compliant End-Effector–Root (EE-root) control abstraction, defining a unified, interpretable task-space interface for humanoid loco-manipulation.
- Trained a low-level whole-body policy using a teacher-student framework to translate high-level EE-root commands into dynamically feasible and compliant joint actions, incorporating estimators for privileged states and target joints.
- Constructed a three-layer hierarchical architecture that integrates the low-level **CEER** policy with modular mid-level skills (e.g., teleoperation, path planning, VLM-based) and a high-level LLM-based task manager.

## Results

- Achieved an end-effector position RMSE of **3.3** cm and an RMS jerk of **4.5** × 10^3 m/s^3 in simulation, demonstrating smoother and more stable motions compared to baseline methods.
- Successfully executed complex contact-rich teleoperation tasks on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, including forceful manipulation (e.g., whiteboard writing, box rotation) and delicate object handling like opening a paper bag.
- The hierarchical system, managed by an LLM, showed comparable or better performance than human teleoperation in single-object loco-manipulation tasks in simulation, with faster average completion times.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A compact, compliant EE-root abstraction effectively bridges high-level task planning and low-level whole-body execution, promoting modularity and transferability across different robot platforms.
> - Integrating impedance control into a learned policy enables compliant interaction, resulting in significantly smoother end-effector motions (reduced jerk) and robust execution of contact-rich tasks.
> - The hierarchical system, utilizing the EE-root interface as a universal communication protocol, allows for seamless integration of heterogeneous planners and mid-level skills without requiring retraining of the foundational low-level controller.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of operating effectively in human environments necessitates the ability to perform complex loco-manipulation tasks, particularly those involving contact and long operational horizons. Current advancements in humanoid robotics, particularly those leveraging motion-capture data and imitation learning, have demonstrated significant progress in dynamic locomotion, enabling behaviors such as cartwheels and backflips. These approaches typically involve policies that track high-dimensional whole-body joint angle targets, outputting precise and robust joint commands.

However, existing motion imitation policies are often primarily optimized for locomotion and ground interaction. When extended to object manipulation, they frequently require task-specific retraining or retargeting to accommodate contact dynamics. More fundamentally, joint-tracking methods present several challenges for fully automating the perception-planning-execution pipeline:
- **High-dimensional planner output:** Planners must generate full-body joint trajectories, which is computationally demanding and data-intensive.
- **Embodiment-specificity:** Joint-level commands are specific to a robot's kinematic structure, hindering transferability across different humanoid platforms.
- **Lack of explicit compliance:** Pure motion tracking often lacks explicit modulation of compliance, which is crucial for safe and robust interaction with objects and humans during contact-rich tasks.

To address these limitations, researchers have explored incorporating compliance and force adaptation into whole-body control. This includes injecting impedance-like behavior into motion tracking, augmenting training data with compliant variants, or learning stiffness modulation for specific contact tasks. While these methods improve contact robustness, they often rely on tracking-centric interfaces such as joint trajectories, dense full-body references, or task-specific force/impedance setpoints. This creates a mismatch with modular planners and mid-level skills, which typically generate sparse, task-level goals like root navigation or end-effector poses, thereby requiring additional retargeting or retraining for system integration.

Humanoid teleoperation and control interfaces also reflect this spectrum of abstraction levels, from full-body motion states to task-space targets. While motion-tracking pipelines enable high-fidelity imitation, a complementary trend involves exposing more interpretable task-level targets, such as end-effector (EE) poses or sparse keypoints, for execution by low-level whole-body trackers. Such interfaces naturally support hierarchical system designs where high-level planners or mid-level policies generate trajectories or waypoint sequences.

This work, CEER, positions itself within this landscape by proposing a unified compliant end-effector–root (EE-root) control abstraction. It aims to bridge the gap between high-level task planning and low-level whole-body execution by providing a compact, interpretable task-space interface that inherently supports compliant interaction, thereby enabling modular and scalable integration of diverse planning and skill modules for contact-rich, long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while humanoid robots have achieved significant progress in locomotion, performing contact-rich and long-horizon manipulation in diverse human environments remains a notable challenge. The limitations of current approaches can be summarized as follows:

1. **Challenges with Joint-Space Tracking:** Existing methods, often based on motion imitation, excel at producing dynamic locomotion but typically require full-body joint targets as input. This necessitates high-dimensional joint trajectory generation by planners, which is difficult given limited data. Furthermore, joint-level commands are robot-specific, limiting transferability, and lack explicit compliance modulation, which is critical for safe interaction.
2. **Need for Task-Level Abstraction:** To overcome the dimensionality and embodiment-specific limitations of joint-space control, an effective task-level representation is required. In manipulation, end-effector (EE) targets are a natural choice. For loco-manipulation, incorporating root motion provides a comprehensive representation that significantly reduces the planner's output dimensionality and unifies task-level commands.
3. **Requirement for Compliance:** Contact-rich manipulation inherently demands compliant whole-body control to ensure stable and safe interaction with objects and the environment. Traditional motion tracking often prioritizes tracking fidelity over interaction behaviors, making it less suitable for tasks involving sustained physical contact.
4. **Enabling Modular and Hierarchical Control:** The diversity and long-horizon nature of manipulation tasks favor modular, planner-compatible interfaces over monolithic, joint-space tracking systems. Decoupling high-level planning from low-level execution through a unified interface facilitates the plug-and-play integration of heterogeneous high-level planners and mid-level skills.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the CEER research are:

1. **Develop a Compliant EE-Root Control Abstraction:** To define a unified execution interface for humanoid loco-manipulation that specifies root motion and end-effector pose targets. This abstraction should enable compliance-aware whole-body control in an interpretable task space, independent of morphology-specific joint targets.
2. **Bridge EE-Root Commands with Whole-Body Execution:** To create a low-level policy that can translate high-level EE-root commands into dynamically feasible and compliant whole-body joint actions, thereby enabling stable interaction and accurate tracking.
3. **Construct a Hierarchical Planning Framework:** To integrate heterogeneous high-level planners and modular mid-level skills through the unified EE-root interface. This framework aims to enable diverse manipulation tasks without requiring retraining of the underlying whole-body policy, promoting modularity and scalability.
4. **Demonstrate Practical Efficacy:** To validate the proposed CEER policy and hierarchical system through extensive experiments in both simulation and on hardware, quantifying end-effector tracking accuracy and smoothness, assessing stable contact-rich manipulation under teleoperation, and evaluating the system's ability to execute long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks.

Ultimately, the goal is to provide a practical and scalable control abstraction that facilitates the development of general-purpose humanoid robots capable of complex interactions in human-centric environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CEER framework employs a multi-faceted approach encompassing impedance control, a teacher-student policy learning paradigm, a specialized reward function, and a three-layer hierarchical system for integration.

#### 3.1. A. Impedance Control Formulation

The interaction between the robot and its environment is modeled using a Cartesian impedance formulation. This formulation defines the relationship between applied force and the robot's deviation from a desired reference trajectory in task space. The model is given by $F = K_m (\ddot{x} - \ddot{x}_d) + K_d (\dot{x} - \dot{x}_d) + K_p (x - x_d)$, where $x$ is the task-space state, $x_d$ is the desired reference, and $K_m, K_d, K_p$ are inertia, damping, and stiffness gain matrices. For practical application, desired acceleration is assumed to be zero, and gains are constant. External interaction is modeled as a spring-like disturbance ($f_{ext}$). Under quasi-dynamic assumptions (negligible acceleration), this formulation leads to an impedance-consistent target $x_{imp,t} = x_{ref,t} + K_p^{-1} f_{ext,t}$, which is utilized as a privileged observation and for reward construction during policy training.

#### 3.2. B. Policy Learning

The core of CEER's low-level control is a learned policy developed through a teacher-student framework, built upon motion imitation.

1. **Teacher Policy (π_T):** A general motion-tracking teacher policy is initially trained using human motion datasets, specifically AMASS. This policy receives a comprehensive observation $o_T_t = [c_T_t, o_t, o_p_t]$. The command $c_T_t \in R^{29}$ is a high-dimensional joint-space reference configuration. The shared policy observation $o_t$ includes proprioceptive signals (projected gravity, root angular velocity, past joint positions, and previous actions). Crucially, the teacher also receives privileged information $o_p_t$, which includes external forces ($f_{ext,t}$), an impedance-consistent tracking state ($s_{ref,t}$), simulator ground-truth states ($s_{sim,t}$), and actuator states ($s_{act,t}$). The joint-space reference command $c_T_t$ is directly fed into the teacher without latent compression to ensure its primary influence on motion tracking.

2. **Student Policy (π_S):** The teacher policy's knowledge is then distilled into a student policy, which is conditioned solely on EE-root commands. The student policy observes $o_S_t = [c_S_t, o_t]$, where $c_S_t \in R^{16}$ represents the task-space reference (root pose including x, y, z, yaw, and end-effector position and orientation). To enable weight inheritance from the teacher, two additional estimators are introduced for the student:
 - **Privileged State Estimator ($f_{\theta_p}$):** Predicts the privileged information ($o_p_t$) based solely on the shared policy observation ($o_t$).
 - **Target Joint Estimator ($f_{\theta_q}$):** Predicts the 29-dimensional reference joint configuration ($c_T_t$) from the student observation ($o_S_t$).
 This design ensures that the student's input structure aligns with the teacher's, allowing for direct weight initialization and stable fine-tuning. Both teacher and student policies output joint commands $a_t \in R^{29}$, which are PD targets. A two-stage PPO-based training procedure is adopted for both policies.

#### 3.3. C. Reward Function

The reward function is designed to encourage accurate tracking, compliant behavior, and stable locomotion. It comprises four weighted components:
- **End-Effector Tracking ($r_{ee}$):** Penalizes deviations between the current end-effector pose ($x_{ee,t}$) and the reference target ($x_{ref,t}$), using an exponential decay function.
- **Compliance ($r_{compliance}$):** Promotes compliant interaction by leveraging the impedance-consistent target.
- **Motion ($r_{motion}$):** Encourages faithful tracking of motion, typically referring to the full-body motion desired by the teacher.
- **Locomotion ($r_{locomotion}$):** Stabilizes lower-body movement and maintains balance.
The total reward is a weighted sum of these terms: $r_{total} = w_{ee} r_{ee} + w_c r_{compliance} + w_m r_{motion} + w_l r_{locomotion}$.

#### 3.4. D. System Integration

CEER is integrated into a three-layer hierarchical architecture to demonstrate its role as an interpretable and planner-compatible interface.

1. **Three-Layer Hierarchical System:**
 - **Low-Level Control:** The trained CEER policy operates at 50 Hz, consuming standardized EE-root commands and mapping them to dynamically feasible joint-level PD targets. This layer decouples task specification from morphology-specific joint targets.
 - **Mid-Level Skill Layer:** This layer consists of modular, plug-and-play skills operating at 10–20 Hz. Each skill generates commands in the unified EE-root space, making them directly compatible with the low-level controller. Examples include teleoperation interfaces, programmed locomotion and manipulation primitives, and learning-based skills (e.g., diffusion policies). This layer facilitates flexible integration without low-level controller modification.
 - **High-Level Task Manager:** An LLM-based task manager forms the top layer, interpreting natural language instructions and decomposing them into sequences of mid-level skill invocations. It operates on a slower timescale, ensuring decoupling from low-level dynamics.

2. **Middle-Level Skills:**
 - **Teleoperation Skills:** Keyboard and VR interfaces generate direct EE-root commands. VR control maps 6-DoF hand poses from controllers to end-effector targets. Keyboard control provides rapid testing and symmetric arm motion for grasping.
 - **Locomotion Skill:** A path planning method (hybrid A* on a grid map) generates waypoints from high-level navigation goals (x, y, θ). A waypoint tracker converts these into root velocity commands, while end-effector targets remain static.
 - **Manipulation Skills:** Various manipulation skills are supported: planned (parameterized waypoints, rule-based trajectories), vision-language model (VLM)-based (predict EE trajectories from visual observations and language), and learned (diffusion-based imitation learning). All outputs are aligned to the EE-root command format.

3. **High-Level Task Manager:**
 An LLM serves as the task manager, using a function-calling interface to invoke mid-level skills. It operates in a closed-loop "reason-act-observe" cycle, allowing for verification of action outcomes and adaptation. Manipulation expertise, including workspace coordinates, canonical hand states (REST, HOLD, READY, GRASP), and recommended action sequences, is encoded in a system prompt rather than fine-tuning the LLM. The interpretability of the EE-root interface allows the LLM to reason about spatial goals and safety constraints purely in terms of end-effector positions and root poses, enabling a general-purpose language model to interface with the humanoid system.

The overall methodology aims to create a robust, modular, and interpretable system for humanoid loco-manipulation by leveraging compliant task-space control and a hierarchical planning framework.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates CEER from both control and system integration perspectives across various experiments in simulation and on a physical humanoid robot.

#### 4.1. A. Tracking Performance of Low-Level Policy (Simulation)

The end-effector tracking performance of the CEER policy was quantitatively assessed in simulation and compared against three baselines:
- **Ours w/o $f_{\theta_q}$**: A conventional teacher-student framework without the proposed joint target estimator.
- **No-force RL**: Trained without external force disturbances, representing a non-compliant motion-tracking approach.
- **End-to-end RL**: Modified from SkillBlender, learning the EE-root controller directly from task rewards without motion imitation.

**Key Findings:**
- CEER achieved an end-effector position RMSE of 3.3 cm and an orientation error of 0.32 rad.
- Significantly, CEER demonstrated substantially reduced RMS jerk (4.5 × 10$^3$ m/s$^3$) compared to baselines, indicating smoother and more stable end-effector motions. The compliant design is inferred to dampen high-frequency error corrections and mitigate abrupt force responses.
- In terms of accuracy, CEER outperformed "End-to-end RL" and "Ours w/o $f_{\theta_q}$", and achieved comparable accuracy to "No-force RL." The performance gap between CEER and "Ours w/o $f_{\theta_q}$" suggests the critical role of the joint target estimator for learning a high-quality tracking policy within the teacher-student framework.

#### 4.2. B. Teleoperation for Real-world Tasks (Hardware)

Real-world experiments on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot using a Pico 4 VR headset demonstrated CEER's capabilities in contact-rich manipulation tasks across three categories:

1. **Simple Manipulation:** Tasks included grasping boxes of varying sizes and object handover (e.g., single-shoulder bag, paper bag). The robot successfully performed dual-arm coordination, object transfers, and delicate actions like opening a paper bag without damage.
2. **Forceful Manipulation:** These tasks required precise contact regulation and force modulation.
 - **Whiteboard writing and wiping:** The robot wrote human-recognizable letters ("IROS") in two out of three attempts and consistently performed wiping, using a pen holder and eraser holder.
 - **Box-on-wall rotation:** The robot pressed a box against a wall for friction and applied torque with a hook, completing six consecutive rotations without dropping the object, demonstrating stable multi-contact manipulation.
 - **Pen-cap insertion:** The robot achieved consistent insertion of a pen tip into a stiff pen cap, requiring precise alignment and force application.
3. **Loco-Manipulation:** Using keyboard input, the robot performed cart-pulling with a hook attachment and box transportation (grasping, moving, restoring).
**Overall, the experiments indicated that CEER's compliant nature enabled safe and stable contact during teleoperation, maintaining sufficient tracking accuracy for complex manipulation. Locomotion behaviors were functional but observed to be less natural compared to manipulation, suggesting areas for further improvement.**

#### 4.3. C. Planner Compatibility Study

The planner-agnostic property of the EE-root abstraction was qualitatively verified by integrating three heterogeneous mid-level planners with the CEER low-level controller:
- A rule-based programmed planner.
- A diffusion-based planner.
- A Vision-Language Model (VLM) planner.
All these planners produced task-level trajectories in their native representations, which were successfully aligned to the unified EE-root command space and executed by the same low-level CEER policy without requiring retraining or architectural modifications. This confirmed the flexibility and modularity enabled by the EE-root interface.

#### 4.4. D. System Evaluation (Simulation)

A comprehensive system-level evaluation was conducted in a room-scale simulated environment, featuring a bed, sofa, table, and randomized box positions. The system, leveraging an LLM-based task manager, was tested on tasks of increasing complexity and compared to human teleoperation.

**Task Categories:**
1. **Simple Tasks:** Arm motion and navigation without object interaction (explicit commands and paraphrased natural language).
2. **Single-Object Loco-Manipulation:** Explicit placement, linguistic variation, and spatial-relation tasks.
3. **Long-Horizon Tasks:** Multi-object transport requiring sequential skill invocation.

**Key Results:**
- **Simple Tasks:** The system achieved near-perfect performance, with failures attributed to incorrect LLM skill invocation.
- **Single-Object Loco-Manipulation:** The autonomous system showed comparable success rates to human teleoperation while completing tasks in shorter average times. This indicated reliable coordination between navigation and manipulation skills.
- **Long-Horizon Tasks:** Human operators achieved a higher success rate (4 out of 10) compared to the autonomous system (2 out of 10).
- **Failure Analysis:** Approximately 52.6% of autonomous failures stemmed from LLM-level planning inaccuracies (e.g., misestimating orientation angles and distances leading to unstable grasps). Human failures were more frequently linked to manipulation errors, possibly due to limited force feedback, while the robot's grasping primitive provided higher consistency. Humans, however, demonstrated stronger recovery ability in long-horizon tasks, adapting more reactively.
**In summary, CEER facilitated reliable execution for tasks of moderate complexity. Performance on long-horizon tasks was identified as bounded by the accuracy of LLM-based planning and the current recovery mechanisms.**

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CEER framework contributes to the field of humanoid robotics by addressing a key challenge: enabling compliant, contact-rich, and long-horizon loco-manipulation through a unified, modular control interface.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Unified Control Abstraction:** By proposing the compliant End-Effector–Root (EE-root) control abstraction, CEER offers a novel and interpretable task-space interface that effectively decouples high-level task specification from low-level joint control. This addresses the limitations of traditional joint-space tracking methods, which are high-dimensional, embodiment-specific, and lack explicit compliance.
2. **Enhanced Compliance for Interaction:** The integration of impedance control into the learning framework allows the humanoid to exhibit compliant behavior crucial for stable and safe interaction with objects and the environment. This is demonstrated by the reduced jerk in end-effector motion and successful execution of forceful manipulation tasks on hardware.
3. **Modular and Scalable System Integration:** The hierarchical three-layer architecture, with the EE-root interface serving as a universal communication protocol, enables the plug-and-play integration of heterogeneous planners and mid-level skills. This design promotes modularity, allowing new capabilities (teleoperation, programmed, VLM-based, or learned skills) to be added without retraining the foundational low-level controller, thereby reducing development complexity and increasing system scalability.
4. **Enabling High-Level Reasoning:** The interpretability of EE-root commands facilitates the integration of advanced high-level reasoning systems, such as Large Language Models (LLMs), as task managers. This allows for natural language instruction following and task decomposition, bringing humanoids closer to operating autonomously in complex, human-centric environments.
5. **Practical Demonstrations:** The extensive validation in both simulation and on real hardware, covering diverse tasks from precise forceful manipulation to long-horizon loco-manipulation, indicates the practical applicability and robustness of the CEER approach. The comparison with human teleoperation provides a realistic benchmark for current capabilities and highlights areas for future improvement.
6. **Future Research Directions:** The identified limitations, such as fixed impedance parameters and the need for explicit end-effector targets during locomotion, point towards significant avenues for future research, including adaptive impedance control, flexible contact specifications, and more autonomous locomotion behaviors.

Overall, CEER represents a step towards creating more versatile and autonomous humanoid robots by providing a robust and flexible control interface that bridges the gap between high-level task planning and compliant whole-body execution, fostering modularity and scalability in complex loco-manipulation scenarios.
