# RIDER: 3D RNA Inverse Design with Reinforcement Learning-Guided Diffusion

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, RIDER, performs 3D RNA inverse design by integrating a conditional diffusion model with reinforcement learning to directly optimize for structural similarity rather than native sequence recovery. The approach achieves over 100% improvement in 3D structural self-consistency metrics compared to existing methods, enabling the discovery of high-fidelity RNA designs distinct from known native sequences.

## Problem

- Computational RNA inverse design primarily focused on 2D secondary structures, but controlling the 3D tertiary structure is essential for precise biological function.
- Existing deep learning methods rely on Native Sequence Recovery (NSR), which is an indirect and insufficient proxy for 3D structural fidelity due to the degenerate RNA sequence-structure relationship.
- Optimizing solely for NSR restricts the discovery of novel, non-native sequences that could still fold into the desired structure, limiting exploration of the vast sequence space.

## Method

- A two-stage **RIDER** framework combines a supervised pre-training stage (RIDE) with a reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning stage.
- RIDE is a conditional variance-preserving diffusion model that learns sequence-structure relationships from 3D RNA tertiary structures, represented as geometric graphs and encoded by GVP-GNN.
- The pre-trained RIDE model is fine-tuned using an improved policy gradient algorithm, guided by reward functions based on 3D self-consistency metrics (GDT TS, RMSD, TM-score) and an advantage estimation strategy with a moving average baseline.

## Results

- The pre-trained RIDE model achieved an average native sequence recovery of **61%** on the test set, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines such as RiboDiffusion (**56%**) and gRNAde (**55%**).
- RIDER demonstrated over **100%** improvement on average across GDT TS, RMSD, and TM-score structural self-consistency metrics compared to the best-performing baseline, with **72%** of RIDER designs exceeding a GDT TS of **0.5**.
- RIDER discovered high-quality designs that were distinct from native sequences (lower NSR but higher structural similarity post-RL fine-tuning), and maintained performance when evaluated with an independent structure prediction oracle (AlphaFold3), showing a **119%** GDT TS improvement over baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Native Sequence Recovery (NSR) showed no clear correlation with 3D structural similarity, highlighting its inadequacy as a direct optimization target for 3D RNA inverse design.
> - Reinforcement learning can effectively guide generative diffusion models to directly optimize for complex 3D structural fidelity, moving beyond the limitations of supervised learning based on sequence similarity.
> - A composite reward function, combining GDT TS and RMSD, provided the most balanced and effective signal for optimizing both global fold similarity and local atomic accuracy in RNA inverse design.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) molecules are fundamental to biological processes, with their diverse functions closely linked to their complex three-dimensional (3D) structures. The RNA inverse design problem, which involves identifying nucleotide sequences that fold into a desired target structure, is a critical area for advancements in synthetic biology and therapeutic applications.

Historically, computational RNA inverse design has predominantly focused on predicting and designing RNA secondary structures, which represent local base pairing interactions. However, controlling the tertiary (3D) structure is often essential for achieving precise biological function, as secondary structure only partially dictates the final 3D fold. Consequently, recent research has shifted towards 3D structure-based inverse design, leveraging deep learning methodologies, including graph neural networks (GNNs) and generative diffusion models. These deep learning approaches, inspired by successes in protein design, have demonstrated improvements in performance and speed compared to traditional physics-based methods like Rosetta.

A common characteristic among many state-of-the-art deep learning methods in this domain is their reliance on native sequence recovery (NSR) as the primary optimization and evaluation metric. NSR measures the ability of a model to recover the original sequence for a given native structure from the training data. The authors of this paper argue that optimizing solely for NSR presents several limitations. The relationship between RNA sequence and structure is degenerate; multiple distinct sequences can fold into similar structures, and conversely, similar sequences do not always lead to similar structures. Therefore, high sequence similarity to a native sequence does not guarantee correct 3D folding, and optimizing for NSR does not directly optimize for structural fidelity. Additionally, over-optimizing for NSR may restrict the discovery of novel, non-native sequences that could still fold into the desired structure, thereby limiting the exploration of the vast sequence space for potential functional RNAs.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing a framework that moves beyond NSR and directly optimizes for 3D structural similarity. It integrates generative models with reinforcement learning, aiming to produce sequences that fold into target structures with high fidelity, irrespective of their similarity to known native sequences.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust computational framework for 3D RNA inverse design that directly optimizes for structural fidelity, rather than relying on native sequence recovery (NSR) as a proxy. The motivation stems from the perceived inadequacies of NSR as a sole metric for evaluating the success of RNA inverse design, based on two main points:

1. **Limitations of Native Sequence Recovery (NSR) as a surrogate objective:**
 - **Indirect optimization:** NSR does not directly measure the structural similarity between the folded structure of a designed sequence and the target structure. An improvement in sequence similarity to a native sequence does not automatically translate into a correct 3D fold. The degeneracy of the RNA sequence-structure relationship means that many different sequences can adopt similar structures, and conversely, small sequence changes can lead to large structural differences. Therefore, designing a sequence that is merely similar to a known native sequence for a target structure does not guarantee that the designed sequence will fold into the intended 3D conformation.
 - **Restricted exploration:** Focusing on NSR can restrict the model's exploration to sequences that are close to the native sequences found in the training dataset. The ultimate goal of RNA inverse design is to discover *any* sequence that folds correctly into a desired structure, not necessarily to recover the exact native sequence. Over-optimization for NSR may prevent the discovery of novel, non-native RNA sequences that could possess desirable structural and functional properties, thereby hindering innovation in synthetic biology and therapeutics.

2. **Addressing the need for direct structural similarity optimization:**
 The authors propose that a more effective approach is to directly optimize the structural similarity between the predicted structure of a designed sequence and the target structure. This approach aligns with the true objective of RNA inverse design, which is to achieve a specific 3D fold. By integrating reinforcement learning (RL), the framework is designed to overcome the limitations of supervised learning methods that rely solely on NSR. The RL component allows for an objective function based on structural metrics, enabling the model to explore a broader sequence space and potentially discover novel, high-fidelity designs.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Propose the first reinforcement learning framework for RNA 3D inverse design.
- Develop a generative diffusion model that learns sequence-structure relationships, outperforming existing state-of-the-art methods in initial sequence recovery.
- Fine-tune this model using an improved policy gradient algorithm guided by task-specific reward functions based on 3D self-consistency metrics.
- Demonstrate that this RL-guided approach significantly improves structural similarity and can discover designs distinct from native sequences.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RIDER framework for 3D RNA inverse design operates in two main stages: a supervised pre-training stage (RIDE) and a reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning stage (RIDER).

#### 3.1. Tertiary Structure Representation

RNA tertiary structures are represented as geometric graphs where nodes correspond to nucleotides and edges encode spatial proximity.
- **Featurization:** Each nucleotide node is defined by the centroid of its P, C4', and N1/N9 backbone atoms. Edges connect each node to its 32 nearest spatial neighbors. Node features include scalar components (e.g., intra-nucleotide distances, angles) and vector components (e.g., backbone orientation vectors). Edge features include scalar components (e.g., 3D distance encoded via radial basis functions, sequence separation via sinusoidal positional encodings) and vector components (e.g., unit vector between nodes).
- **Structure Encoder:** A Geometric Vector Perceptron Graph Neural Network (GVP-GNN) encoder, consisting of 5 GVP-GNN layers, processes the featurized graph. This encoder extracts E(3)-equivariant node-level conditional embeddings, `h_c`, which encapsulate essential 3D structural information.

#### 3.2. Conditional Diffusion Model for RNA Sequence Generation (RIDE)

The first stage involves developing and pre-training RIDE, a conditional generative diffusion model.
- **Formulation:** RNA sequence design is formulated as learning a conditional distribution `p(x_0 | h_c)`, where `x_0` is the one-hot encoded RNA sequence and `h_c` is the structural embedding from the encoder.
- **Diffusion Process:** A variance-preserving diffusion probabilistic model is employed. The forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to a clean sequence `x_0` over a continuous time variable `t`, yielding a noisy sample `x_t`.
- **Noise Prediction Network:** The core of the generative model is a noise prediction network, `ϵ_θ(x_t, t, h_c)`, parameterized by `θ`, composed of 5 GVP-GNN layers. This network is trained to predict the noise `ε` from `x_t`, conditioned on time `t` and the structural context `h_c`.
- **Pre-training Objective:** The model is trained by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between the true noise `ε` and the predicted noise `ˆε_θ`.
- **Inference:** During inference, sequences are generated by reversing the diffusion process using the Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) sampler over 50 denoising steps, starting from random Gaussian noise. The final discrete sequence is obtained by an argmax operation.
- **Dataset and Hyperparameters:** The model is trained on a dataset derived from the RNASolo repository, containing 4,223 unique RNA sequences and 12,011 structures, partitioned into training, validation, and test sets. It utilizes an Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 3e-4, decayed by a factor of 0.9 if validation performance does not improve for 5 epochs, trained for 150 epochs.

#### 3.3. Reinforcement Learning for Structural Similarity Optimization (RIDER)

The second stage fine-tunes the pre-trained RIDE model using reinforcement learning (RL) to directly optimize for structural similarity.
- **Markov Decision Process (MDP) Formulation:**
 - **State (`s_t`):** The current noisy sequence, time step, and structural condition (`(x_t, t, h_c)`).
 - **Action (`a_t`):** The denoising step from `x_t` to `x_t-∆t`, determined by the noise predicted by the model `ϵ_θ`.
 - **Policy (`π_θ(a_t|s_t)`):** Parameterized by the diffusion model `ϵ_θ`.
 - **Reward (`R(ˆx_0, h_target_c)`):** Obtained only at the end of a complete sequence generation trajectory.
- **Advantage Estimation:**
 - To stabilize training and reduce variance, the policy gradient method uses an advantage function `A(ˆx_0, h_target_c)` instead of raw rewards.
 - The advantage is calculated as `R_traj - b`, where `b` is a baseline.
 - A moving average strategy is adopted for baseline estimation: `b^(i) = β_baseline · b^(i-1) + (1 - β_baseline) · ¯R^(i)_batch`, where `¯R^(i)_batch` is the average reward of the current batch. This stabilizes the baseline against stochasticity.
 - The policy gradient objective is modified using this advantage, with importance sampling ratios (`r_k(θ)`) and a clipping technique (similar to PPO) to constrain updates.
- **Reward Functions:** The reward function quantifies the structural similarity between the predicted 3D structure (folded from the generated sequence using RhoFold) and the target 3D structure. Three widely used self-consistency metrics are employed:
 - **GDT TS (Global Distance Test Total Score):** Measures the percentage of backbone atoms within predefined distance thresholds (1Å, 2Å, 4Å, 8Å) after optimal superposition. Higher scores indicate better alignment.
 - **RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation):** Computes the average displacement between corresponding backbone atoms after superposition. Lower values indicate higher similarity.
 - **TM-score (Template Modeling Score):** Evaluates global fold similarity using a length-normalized function, tolerant to local deviations. Values range from 0 to 1, with higher scores reflecting better agreement.
 - **Base Reward Functions:** `R_gdt`, `R_tm`, `R_rmsd` (negative for RMSD) are defined using individual metrics, scaled and squared. A combined reward `R_gdt_rmsd` is also defined.
 - **Bonus Reward:** `R_bonus` is added for sequences meeting acceptable design standards (GDT TS > 0.5 or RMSD < 2.0Å), providing additional incentive for high-fidelity designs.
 - **Final Reward (`R_final`):** `R_base + R_bonus`.
- **RL Hyperparameters:** Fine-tuning is conducted for 80 RL epochs with 2 policy updates per epoch, using a learning rate of 5e-5 and a batch size of 60 trajectories. DDIM denoising steps are set to 30 during RL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings demonstrating the capabilities of the RIDER framework.

#### 4.1. Supervised Learning Pre-training (RIDE)

- **Superior Sequence Recovery:** The pre-trained RIDE model achieved an average native sequence recovery (NSR) of 61% on the test set, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines: RiboDiffusion (56%) and gRNAde (55%). This indicates that the initial diffusion model effectively learns sequence-structure relationships, even before RL fine-tuning.
- **NSR vs. Structural Similarity:** Analysis revealed no clear correlation between NSR and 3D structural similarity. For instance, sequences with approximately 50% NSR exhibited GDT TS values ranging from 0 to 0.9. Even at high NSR levels, the resulting structures could show low similarity to the target. This finding supports the motivation that NSR is an insufficient proxy for structural fidelity in 3D RNA inverse design.
- **Correlation of Structural Metrics:** GDT TS and TM-score showed a strong Pearson correlation of 0.885. While generally correlated, some discrepancies were observed, such as low RMSD and high GDT TS paired with unexpectedly low TM-scores, particularly in shorter structures where TM-score is more sensitive to deviations. This diversity in metric behavior influenced the design of reward functions.

#### 4.2. Reinforcement Learning Fine-tuning (RIDER)

- **Substantial Improvement in Structural Similarity:** RIDER achieved significant improvements in 3D structural similarity metrics compared to baselines. Across the test set, RIDER demonstrated over 100% improvement on average for all three self-consistency metrics (GDT TS, RMSD, TM-score) relative to the best-performing baseline.
 - For GDT TS, 72% of RIDER designs exceeded the 0.5 threshold, compared to 27% for gRNAde.
 - For RMSD, 33% of RIDER designs achieved ≤ 2Å, in contrast to 3% for gRNAde.
- **Discovery of Novel Designs:** RIDER demonstrated the ability to discover high-quality designs that are distinct from native sequences. In many cases, RIDER designs exhibited lower NSR compared to the pre-trained RIDE model, yet achieved higher GDT TS values. This suggests that the RL fine-tuning successfully guided the model to explore novel sequence space for optimal structural fidelity rather than merely mimicking native sequences.
- **Generalization Across Oracles:** To evaluate generalization, RIDER was tested using AlphaFold3 as an independent structure prediction oracle instead of RhoFold. The RIDER model maintained its performance, with the `R_gdt_rmsd` reward function yielding a GDT TS of 0.57, more than doubling (+119%) the baseline score of 0.26 from gRNAde when also evaluated with AlphaFold3. This indicates that RIDER captures generalizable principles of RNA design and does not overfit to a specific predictor.
- **Impact of Reward Functions:** The study evaluated four reward functions: `R_tm`, `R_gdt`, `R_rmsd`, and `R_gdt_rmsd`.
 - `R_tm` performed well on TM-score and GDT TS but less effectively on RMSD.
 - `R_gdt` and `R_rmsd` individually showed weaker overall performance, potentially due to GDT TS ignoring errors in poorly modeled regions and RMSD's sensitivity to large deviations without length normalization.
 - The composite `R_gdt_rmsd` reward, combining GDT TS and RMSD, achieved the most balanced performance across all metrics. This is attributed to the complementary nature of these two metrics, which collectively capture both local atomic accuracy and global fold similarity.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Advantage Estimation Strategy:** Comparing RIDER (with moving average baseline for advantage), RIDER-ADV (batch-based advantage without moving average), and RIDER-RWD (raw rewards), RIDER consistently achieved superior performance across all structural metrics. RIDER-RWD showed instability and lower performance, while the moving average baseline in RIDER provided a ~15% improvement in GDT TS over RIDER-ADV, highlighting its role in stabilizing learning.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** An analysis of RIDE's hyperparameters (optimizer, activation, learning rate, GVP-GNN layers) confirmed that the chosen configuration (Adam, SiLU, 3e-4 LR, 5 GVP-GNN layers) yielded the best NSR (0.61 ± 0.02). Deeper networks (8 layers) and alternative optimizers/activations generally resulted in lower performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RIDER framework represents an advancement in the field of 3D RNA inverse design by directly optimizing for structural fidelity rather than relying on native sequence recovery (NSR). This approach addresses a fundamental limitation of previous deep learning methods, which were constrained by the degeneracy of the RNA sequence-structure relationship.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:

1. **Enhanced Design Accuracy:** By achieving over 100% improvement in structural similarity metrics (GDT TS, RMSD, TM-score) compared to state-of-the-art baselines, RIDER provides a more reliable method for designing RNA sequences that fold into desired 3D structures. This directly contributes to the goal of creating functional RNAs with predictable forms.
2. **Discovery of Novel Sequences:** RIDER's ability to generate high-quality designs that differ significantly from native sequences (demonstrated by lower NSR but higher structural similarity post-RL fine-tuning) is crucial. This capability allows for the exploration of a broader sequence space, which may lead to the discovery of novel RNA molecules with desired functions that are not present in existing biological datasets.
3. **Robustness and Generalization:** The cross-oracle validation using AlphaFold3 demonstrates the generalizability of the RIDER framework, suggesting that it learns fundamental principles of RNA design rather than overfitting to a specific structure prediction model. This enhances confidence in its applicability across different computational pipelines.
4. **Framework for Direct Optimization:** The integration of reinforcement learning with diffusion models provides a flexible framework that can be adapted for various design objectives. The use of an advantage function with a moving average baseline contributes to stable and effective training, which can inform future developments in generative model fine-tuning for specific tasks.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Synthetic Biology:** RIDER could accelerate the engineering of synthetic RNA devices, such as riboswitches, RNA sensors, and RNA-based gene regulators, by enabling the precise design of their 3D conformations. This can lead to the creation of novel biological circuits and cellular control mechanisms.
- **RNA-based Therapeutics:** The ability to design RNAs with specific 3D structures is critical for developing new therapeutic modalities, including RNA aptamers for targeted drug delivery, small activating RNAs (saRNAs), and ribozymes for gene editing. RIDER could facilitate the design of more effective and stable RNA therapeutics.
- **Fundamental RNA Biology:** The framework can aid in understanding the complex sequence-structure-function relationships in RNA by allowing researchers to systematically test hypotheses about how sequence variations impact 3D folds.

**Future Work and Limitations:**

- **Multi-objective Rewards:** Future research may explore multi-objective reward functions that incorporate additional factors such as sequence diversity, biophysical metrics (e.g., thermodynamic stability), or specific functional properties, to generate more practical and robust designs.
- **Experimental Validation:** The current work focuses on in silico design and evaluation. Experimental validation of the generated sequences will be a critical next step to confirm their folded structures and functional efficacy in real-world settings.
- **Dynamic RNA Structures:** RNA molecules are dynamic and can adopt multiple conformations. Extending the framework to design sequences that exhibit specific dynamic behaviors or transition between desired states poses an additional challenge.

In conclusion, RIDER offers a significant advancement in 3D RNA inverse design by shifting the focus to direct structural optimization. Its capabilities for generating high-fidelity, novel RNA sequences hold promise for accelerating research in synthetic biology and RNA-based drug discovery.
