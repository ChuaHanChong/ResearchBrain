# NavOL: Navigation Policy with Online Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> NavOL introduces an online imitation learning framework that leverages GPU-parallel simulation to train robust visual navigation policies for robots. This approach mitigates distribution shift and eliminates the need for manual reward engineering, achieving up to 63% higher success rates on a new benchmark and demonstrating strong sim-to-real transfer on a quadruped robot.

## Problem

- Offline imitation learning policies suffer from distribution shift and compounding errors, leading to unstable navigation in unseen states.
- Reinforcement learning methods for navigation often require extensive, complex reward engineering and exhibit low sample efficiency.
- Scaling DAgger-style online learning algorithms to high-dimensional visual diffusion policies has historically been computationally prohibitive.

## Method

- **NavOL** employs an online imitation learning paradigm with a highly parallelized rollout-update pipeline in a high-fidelity simulator (IsaacLab), continuously collecting expert demonstrations.
- The model uses a multimodal ==diffusion policy== (Trajectory Generator) to predict waypoint sequences, complemented by a Critic network to evaluate trajectory safety.
- Training involves iteratively optimizing the policy with data from recent rollouts, where actions are mixed between the policy's proposal and an optimal expert planner's guidance to stabilize learning.

## Results

- NavOL achieved a mean Success Rate (SR) of **69.0** and Success-weighted Path Length (SPL) of **63.7** on a new, challenging zero-shot benchmark, outperforming NavDP (**42.2** mSR, **37.7** mSPL) by a large margin.
- The policy demonstrated robust sim-to-real transfer and cross-embodiment generalization, successfully navigating a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot in real-world environments with consistently higher success rates than NavDP (e.g., 8/10 vs. 4/10 in an office scene).
- Ablation studies confirmed the importance of expert rollout guidance, pre-training initialization, and domain randomization for NavOL's performance and generalization, with training from scratch still outperforming NavDP but at a higher compute cost.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Online, on-policy data aggregation from interactive rollouts effectively mitigates distribution shift, leading to more stable and robust navigation behaviors compared to purely offline training.
> - Leveraging GPU-parallel simulation allows for scalable online imitation learning, making DAgger-style interactive policy refinement feasible for complex visual navigation tasks.
> - Initializing with a strong pre-trained behavioral prior (from offline IL) and integrating expert guidance during online rollouts are crucial for efficient and stable policy optimization in complex environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied navigation for robots aims to enable autonomous movement in unfamiliar, dynamic, and partially observable environments while observing surroundings and reaching specified targets. Traditional approaches, which often decompose navigation into modular sub-tasks, encounter limitations when confronted with high-dimensional sensory inputs and complex environments. This has led to a shift towards end-to-end learning-based methods that integrate expressive visual representations.

Within learning-based navigation, two prominent paradigms exist: offline imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL). Offline IL, as seen in works like NavDP, involves training policies on large, pre-collected datasets of expert demonstrations. While efficient in data generation compared to real-world collection, offline IL is constrained by the quality and diversity of its fixed datasets. It often suffers from distribution shift, where the policy encounters states during deployment that were not present in the training data, leading to compounding errors and unstable navigation. DAgger-style algorithms aim to address this by iteratively collecting data from the policy's own rollouts, but scaling them to high-dimensional visual diffusion policies has historically been computationally intensive.

Reinforcement learning (RL) allows agents to improve through direct interaction with the environment, enabling continual policy refinement beyond initial demonstrations. However, RL methods typically require extensive reward engineering, often contend with sparse rewards, and exhibit low sample efficiency, making large-scale training in complex environments challenging. Unsupervised methods, such as iPlanner and ViPlanner, rely on hand-designed cost-map supervision and simplified dynamics, which can lead to agents getting stuck in local minima and limit generalization or sim-to-real transfer capabilities.

NavOL is positioned as a framework that bridges the limitations of offline imitation learning and reinforcement learning. It seeks to combine the sample efficiency characteristic of imitation learning with the interactive benefits of online learning. By operating within a simulator and updating its policy using expert demonstrations gathered online, NavOL aims to mitigate distribution shift, improve learning efficiency, and avoid the need for manual reward function design, which are common drawbacks of the aforementioned paradigms. The work leverages high-fidelity simulators and synthetic data generation, building upon efforts to enhance sim-to-real transfer and cross-embodiment policy learning, to address the bottleneck of data scarcity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and generalizable visual navigation policy that can effectively operate in complex, unseen environments. The authors identify several core challenges and limitations in existing approaches that motivate the development of NavOL:

1. **Addressing Distribution Shift and Compounding Errors in Offline Imitation Learning:** Offline imitation learning policies, while benefiting from large datasets, tend to drift out of their training distribution during real-world deployment. This "covariate shift" leads to compounding errors, where small deviations from the expert's behavior accumulate over time, resulting in unstable and often failed navigation. The authors motivate NavOL as a solution that, by continuously interacting with the environment and learning from its own rollouts, can mitigate these issues. The example of NavDP, which exhibits high variance and "shaking behavior" near obstacles due to fixed pre-collected data, highlights this problem.

2. **Overcoming Reward Engineering and Inefficiency in Reinforcement Learning:** Reinforcement learning, despite its ability to refine policies through interaction, is often impractical for complex navigation tasks. Designing effective reward functions for sparse or intricate scenarios is challenging and time-consuming. Additionally, RL methods are generally sample-inefficient, requiring vast amounts of environmental interaction to learn competent policies. NavOL is motivated by the desire to eliminate the need for reward engineering and enhance learning efficiency by adopting an online imitation paradigm.

3. **Scaling DAgger-style Algorithms to High-Dimensional Visual Policies:** While DAgger-style algorithms are theoretically capable of addressing distribution shift, their application to high-dimensional visual diffusion policies has historically been computationally prohibitive. The authors aim to demonstrate how such online fine-tuning can be stabilized and scaled by leveraging massive parallel simulation, thereby enabling interactive learning for complex, generative navigation policies.

4. **Enhancing Robustness and Generalization through Online Interaction:** The authors seek to develop a policy that exhibits strong zero-shot generalization capabilities across diverse, unseen environments and different robot embodiments. They hypothesize that an online learning framework, which continuously collects data from realistic, interactive rollouts and incorporates domain randomization, will yield a more robust policy compared to those trained solely on static datasets.

To achieve these objectives, the paper articulates four specific contributions:
- The presentation of NavOL, an online imitation learning framework for navigation policy learning.
- The implementation of a highly parallelized rollout–update pipeline capable of efficiently collecting and learning from large volumes of data (over 2,000 trajectories per hour in 50 scenes) using modern GPU hardware.
- The introduction of a new visual indoor navigation benchmark for comprehensive and fair evaluation of navigation policies, specifically designed to test zero-shot generalization.
- Extensive experimental validation in both simulation and real-world scenarios, demonstrating consistent performance gains attributable to the online imitation learning approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NavOL employs an online imitation learning paradigm, operating within a rollout–update loop, built upon a pre-trained navigation diffusion policy. The system is designed to interact with a simulator, gather expert demonstrations online, and update its policy iteratively.

**Model Architecture:**
NavOL’s architecture is based on a multimodal diffusion policy, adapted from NavDP, and consists of two main components:
1. **Trajectory Generator (f):** This component takes ego-view RGB-D observations and a target goal as input and predicts a short-horizon sequence of relative waypoints. The RGB and depth streams are encoded using `DepthAnythingV2` ViT backbones, and a transformer decoder compresses these features into 16 fused tokens. Goal instructions are embedded and combined with these visual tokens to form the conditioning context. A Diffusion Transformer (DiT) with a DDPM scheduler then denoises from Gaussian noise to predict a K-step waypoint sequence via cross-attention to the condition embeddings.
2. **Critic (V):** This network evaluates the safety and feasibility of proposed trajectories. It shares visual tokens and the DiT backbone with the generator for consistent spatial feature extraction. A candidate trajectory is encoded into a trajectory token, which, along with the fused RGB-D tokens (excluding goal tokens to maintain goal-agnosticism), is processed by the shared DiT to output a scalar safety score. During deployment, this critic is used to rank trajectories sampled by the diffusion policy, with the highest-scoring plan being executed.

**Online Imitation Learning Pipeline:**
The core of NavOL is its online imitation loop, which alternates between a rollout phase in a simulator and an update phase for policy optimization.
1. **Expert Planner:** During training, NavOL relies on a global planner with privileged access to the environment map, which is not available at inference time. This planner, implemented on the navigation mesh (NavMesh) from Habitat-Sim, computes globally optimal shortest paths between agent poses and goal positions. The raw paths are post-processed through densification, smoothing with a 2D cubic spline, and obstacle clearance maximization via local line searches. To enhance training data quality, start-goal pairs are sampled such that the raw path contains a minimum number of keypoints, favoring more challenging navigation scenarios. The expert planner operates efficiently, adding minimal overhead to training.
2. **Online Data Aggregation (Rollout Phase):** This phase is conducted using IsaacLab, a high-performance GPU-parallel simulator, running 256 parallel environments. At each simulation step, the agent receives RGB-D observations and a goal. The current policy proposes waypoint actions. Concurrently, the expert planner provides optimal waypoints. The executed action is sampled from either the policy's proposal (with probability ρ = 0.8) or the expert's optimal path (with probability 1 − ρ). This mixed policy execution stabilizes training. The waypoints are then converted into velocity commands via Model Predictive Control (MPC) and subsequently into joint controls by a low-level controller. Crucially, this embodied online rollout captures motion-induced visual effects, aiding generalization. A target safety score for the critic is computed based on the distance of waypoints to the nearest obstacles. The collected data, comprising observation-goal-expert trajectory-safety score tuples, is stored.
3. **Policy Optimization (Update Phase):** In this phase, the navigation diffusion policy and critic are optimized using only the data collected from the most recent rollout. This ensures on-policy training and continuous adaptation. The actor (generator) is trained with a standard denoising objective (MSE loss between sampled noise and predicted noise) conditioned on observations, goals, and expert trajectories. Visual tokens computed during rollout are reused to accelerate training. The critic network is trained with an MSE loss to predict the computed target safety values for observed trajectories. The overall training loss combines the actor and critic losses.

**Scene Preparation and Initialization:**
Training environments are constructed from the 3D-Front dataset, which provides large-scale indoor 3D assets.
- **Scene Setup:** Raw meshes are processed using BlenderProc for re-texturing and corrected for navigability. Scenes are prepared for efficient rendering in IsaacLab, including normalization of heights, vertical stacking of rooms, conversion to USD format, and enabling high-fidelity rendering features like global illumination and anti-aliasing.
- **Domain Randomization:** To improve robustness and generalization, various parameters are randomized at the start of each episode, including the agent's initial orientation, height, and radius. The camera pose (height and pitch angle) is also randomized to simulate diverse viewpoints.
- **Evaluation Benchmark:** A new benchmark based on 3D-Front is introduced for zero-shot evaluation. It consists of 8 large, cluttered, out-of-domain scenes, each with 100 fixed start-goal pairs of varying difficulty, ensuring consistent and fair comparisons.

The model is initialized with pre-trained weights from NavDP and predicts a 24-step waypoint sequence. Training is conducted on 50 scenes for 1,000 iterations over 2 days using 8 RTX 4090 GPUs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of NavOL was conducted using two primary metrics: Success Rate (SR), defined as reaching within 1 meter of the goal, and Success-weighted Path Length (SPL), which measures navigation efficiency for successful episodes. Comparisons were made against DD-PPO, iPlanner, ViPlanner, and NavDP, all in a zero-shot configuration without fine-tuning.

**Results on NavDP Benchmark:**
On the existing NavDP benchmark (Cai et al., 2025), NavOL demonstrated improvements across all reported metrics and most scenes compared to the baselines. Notably, NavOL achieved a mean SR of 80.4 and a mean SPL of 76.3, surpassing NavDP's 77.8 mSR and 74.8 mSPL. The paper highlights that NavDP often exhibits high variance in its generated trajectories, leading to "shaking behavior" and failures near obstacles, attributed to the limited coverage of challenging scenarios in its offline training data. In contrast, NavOL produced stable trajectories even in close proximity to obstacles. The performance gains on this benchmark were noted to be somewhat less pronounced due to certain unfavorable initial configurations that immediately led to failures in the test scenarios.

**Results on NavOL Benchmark:**
To provide a more comprehensive and robust evaluation, the authors introduced a new benchmark comprising 8 out-of-domain scenes from the 3D-Front dataset, characterized by rich spatial layouts and photorealistic textures. On this benchmark, NavOL achieved substantial performance gains, with a mean SR of 69.0 and a mean SPL of 63.7. This significantly exceeded NavDP (42.2 mSR, 37.7 mSPL), ViPlanner (33.0 mSR, 31.5 mSPL), iPlanner (18.7 mSR, 18.4 mSPL), and DD-PPO (2.9 mSR, 2.4 mSPL). The observed failures of baselines included DD-PPO's inability to find feasible paths, and iPlanner/ViPlanner frequently getting stuck. NavDP's limited performance was attributed to inconsistent data distribution. These results validate the effectiveness of NavOL's interactive online training paradigm in mitigating distributional shift and enhancing robustness and generalization. Qualitative visualizations of predicted trajectories further illustrated NavOL's ability to generate stable, collision-free paths in zero-shot scenarios.

**Real-world Results:**
NavOL's sim-to-real transfer capabilities and cross-embodiment generalization were evaluated by deploying the trained policy on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot equipped with a RealSense D435i camera. Experiments were conducted in three real-world indoor scenarios: Office, Gym, and Corridor, with 10 trials per method. NavOL consistently outperformed NavDP in success rate across all environments. For example, in the Office scene, NavOL achieved an 8/10 success rate compared to NavDP's 4/10; in the Corridor, NavOL achieved 10/10 successes while NavDP only managed 2/10. The paper highlights NavOL's ability to identify safe trajectories and navigate effectively around obstacles, even in cluttered environments and close quarters, whereas NavDP frequently became stuck. The successful deployment on a different robot platform (Unitree Go2 vs. Dingo in simulation) indicates the policy's learned transferable spatial representations and robust navigation behaviors.

**Ablation Study:**
An ablation study on the NavOL benchmark investigated the contribution of key components:
- **Number of training scenes:** Performance (SR and SPL) increased with the number of training scenes. A model trained on 1 scene (57.8 SR) still outperformed NavDP, suggesting benefits beyond overfitting.
- **Rollout configuration:** Varying rollout length (8 vs. 32 steps per iteration) showed similar performance, likely due to long average episode lengths. However, training without expert rollout (ρ = 1, executing only policy actions) significantly degraded performance (43.9 SR), indicating the importance of expert guidance for stable optimization.
- **Initialization:** Training NavOL from scratch (without NavDP pre-training) resulted in a substantial performance drop (48.3 SR), underscoring the value of a strong initial behavioral prior.
- **Domain randomization:** Removing the expert planner's local line search (w/o path processing) led to the policy frequently getting stuck near obstacles (64.2 SR). Disabling camera randomization (w/o camera rand) also reduced zero-shot generalization (66.0 SR).
- **Critic network:** Removing the critic yielded a modest performance decrease (67.1 SR). The critic provided a safety margin but the policy, through on-policy training, already learned to avoid many unsafe trajectories.

**Compute and Performance Comparison:**
The paper also provided a comparison of compute resources. While NavDP required approximately 1024 GPU-days for its original training, a NavOL variant trained from scratch used only 16 GPU-days and still attained a higher mSR (48.3 vs. 42.2 for NavDP). When initialized with NavDP's weights and trained for an additional 16 GPU-days, NavOL achieved a mSR of 69.0. This comparison suggests that NavOL's on-policy online supervision approach is more data-efficient than the offline rendering of massive trajectory datasets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

NavOL represents a significant contribution to the field of embodied visual navigation by introducing an online imitation learning framework that addresses several limitations of existing paradigms. Its core significance lies in bridging the gap between offline imitation learning and online reinforcement learning, leveraging the strengths of both while mitigating their respective weaknesses.

**Key Contributions and Advantages:**
- **Mitigation of Distribution Shift:** By continuously interacting with the environment and training on its own explored rollouts, NavOL effectively reduces distribution shift and compounding errors, a prevalent issue in offline imitation learning. This leads to more stable and robust navigation behaviors, as evidenced by its performance near obstacles compared to NavDP.
- **Improved Learning Efficiency and Reward-Free Learning:** The framework eliminates the need for manual reward engineering, a common bottleneck in reinforcement learning. By using an online expert planner for supervision, NavOL achieves a more data-efficient learning process, making it more practical for complex, real-world navigation tasks.
- **Scalability via Parallel Simulation:** The implementation on IsaacLab, utilizing GPU-parallel simulation, demonstrates a highly scalable approach to data collection and policy improvement. The ability to generate over 2,000 new trajectories per hour across 50 scenes highlights its capacity for rapid iteration and learning.
- **Robustness and Generalization:** Extensive evaluations on novel simulation benchmarks and real-world scenarios show that NavOL exhibits strong zero-shot generalization to unseen environments and robust cross-embodiment capabilities (e.g., training on Dingo, deployment on Unitree Go2). This suggests the policy learns transferable spatial representations.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The ablation study indicates that NavOL, even when trained from scratch, achieves superior performance to NavDP with substantially less compute, demonstrating the data efficiency of its online supervision mechanism.

**Potential Impact:**
NavOL's approach has the potential to impact the robotics research community by offering a more viable and scalable alternative for training robust embodied agents. By providing a method that is both data-efficient and capable of learning without explicit reward functions, it could lower the barriers to developing advanced navigation policies for diverse robotic platforms. The proposed indoor navigation benchmark further provides a valuable resource for standardized evaluation, fostering consistent progress in the field.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges several practical limitations:
- **Asset Pipeline Overhead:** The scene preparation pipeline (BlenderProc and IsaacSim) involves a one-time setup cost for creating training data. While this does not affect deployment, it represents an initial investment in infrastructure.
- **Training Compute Cost:** Although more efficient than purely offline methods, training NavOL still requires significant hardware resources (e.g., 8 RTX 4090 GPUs for 2 days), which might be prohibitive for some research groups.
- **Real-world Failure Modes:** Observed failures in real-world deployments include "scene-edge exploitation" (robot attempting to navigate along boundaries) and "low-obstacle misjudgment" (robot failing to perceive and avoid short obstacles due to limited camera height). These point to areas for future improvement.

Future work directions include addressing the identified real-world failure modes through research into multi-height obstacle perception and enhancing scene-boundary robustness. These efforts could further improve the reliability and versatility of online imitation learning for autonomous navigation.

In summary, NavOL represents a methodological advancement in learning-based navigation, offering a robust, efficient, and generalizable solution for robot control in complex environments. Its contributions primarily serve to enhance the scientific understanding and practical capabilities within the field of embodied AI and robotics.
