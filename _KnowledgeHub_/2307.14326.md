# Waypoint-Based Imitation Learning for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Automatic Waypoint Extraction (AWE) provides a fully automated method for identifying a minimal set of waypoints from expert demonstrations using only proprioceptive information, by selecting points that allow for linear reconstruction of the original trajectory within a specified error. When integrated as a preprocessing step, AWE consistently improves the success rates of state-of-the-art imitation learning policies, yielding up to 28% higher success on real-world bimanual manipulation tasks and significant gains in data-limited simulated scenarios.

## Problem

- Behavioral cloning (BC) suffers from compounding errors, where small deviations accumulate over long-horizon tasks, leading to policy drift and task failure.
- Existing waypoint-based methods for mitigating these errors either require labor-intensive human labeling or rely on task-specific heuristics that are not generalizable and may extract suboptimal waypoints.
- The absence of a principled, automatic method for extracting a minimal set of relevant waypoints from demonstrations hinders the scalability and robustness of imitation learning.

## Method

- Proposes **Automatic Waypoint Extraction (AWE)**, a preprocessing module that applies a ==dynamic programming algorithm== to extract a minimal set of ==waypoints== from expert trajectories.
- Waypoints are selected such that ==linearly interpolating== between them reconstructs the original trajectory with a maximum deviation below a defined ==error budget==.
- The method uses only ==proprioceptive information== from demonstrations and re-labels all intermediate observations with the next waypoint as the target for ==behavioral cloning==, effectively reducing the learning horizon.

## Results

- AWE combined with ACT achieved up to a **25%** increase in success rates on simulated bimanual tasks and improved real-world success rates by up to **28%** on challenging bimanual manipulation tasks like Coffee Making.
- Integrating AWE with Diffusion Policy consistently improved performance on the RoboMimic suite, demonstrating significant gains (e.g., an **18%** absolute increase on the Square task) particularly in data-limited scenarios.
- The AWE method is computationally efficient, averaging **0.8 seconds** to label one trajectory, and demonstrates superior performance over heuristic-based waypoint extraction techniques.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The dynamic programming approach naturally identifies critical points in a trajectory, selecting fewer waypoints for linear segments and more for complex, non-linear movements, thus adapting waypoint density to trajectory complexity.
> - AWE can introduce multi-modality into the target action distribution, indicating that expressive policy classes (e.g., Diffusion Policy, GMMs) capable of modeling such distributions are essential for realizing its performance benefits.
> - AWE's principled, optimization-based waypoint selection consistently outperforms common heuristic-based methods for waypoint extraction, which often select less meaningful or effective waypoints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation learning (IL), which involves training robots by learning from expert demonstrations, has seen a resurgence of interest and achieved compelling results in various robotic domains, from autonomous driving to complex manipulation tasks. However, a persistent and well-known challenge in IL, particularly for behavioral cloning (BC), is the problem of **compounding errors**. Small deviations from the demonstrated trajectory can accumulate over time, causing the robot's policy to drift into unseen states, leading to task failure, especially in long-horizon tasks. The error in BC has been theoretically shown to grow quadratically with episode length, underscoring the severity of this issue.

The broader research landscape for addressing compounding errors in IL is diverse, with approaches falling into several categories:
- **Novel Policy Architectures:** Developing new neural network designs to improve learning and generalization.
- **Expressive Action Distributions:** Using models that can capture the multi-modal nature of human demonstrations, such as Diffusion Policy.
- **Modified Action Spaces:** Redefining how actions are represented or chunked to simplify the learning problem.
- **Improved Training Objectives:** Designing specialized loss functions or training procedures, exemplified by ACT.
- **Visual Representations & Data Augmentation:** Enhancing perception through learned visual representations or augmenting training data.
- **Online Data Collection:** Actively querying the expert or collecting additional data during training to correct for deviations.

Within this landscape, **waypoint-based methods** represent a distinct strategy to mitigate compounding errors by breaking down long-horizon tasks into shorter, more manageable segments. The core idea is that by learning to reach a sequence of "key" states (waypoints) instead of predicting every single action, the effective horizon of the decision-making problem is significantly reduced. This approach has parallels with hierarchical control and task decomposition.

Prior work on waypoints includes:
- **Hand-defined high-level primitives:** Some methods rely on pre-defined, abstract actions (e.g., "grasp," "move to"). This requires expert domain knowledge and can limit the robot's motion versatility.
- **Heuristic-based waypoint extraction:** Other approaches automatically extract waypoints based on simple rules, such as points of zero velocity or gripper state changes. While successful in certain contexts, these heuristics are often task-specific, may not generalize, and can miss critical intermediate states or identify too many irrelevant ones. The paper specifically cites Shridhar et al. as a common heuristic.
- **Human-labeled waypoints:** More recent methods, like Hydra, introduce innovative hybrid action spaces that incorporate waypoints, but still necessitate human supervision for labeling these waypoints, either during data collection or post-processing. This limits their scalability and ease of use.

This paper's proposed **Automatic Waypoint Extraction (AWE)** method distinguishes itself by addressing the limitations of existing waypoint approaches. It introduces a novel, principled, and *fully automatic* way to extract a *minimal set* of relevant waypoints directly from expert demonstrations. Crucially, AWE relies *only* on the robot's proprioceptive information, without requiring external state estimation, point clouds, additional human labels, or task-specific heuristics. By treating AWE as a pre-processing module, the authors demonstrate that it is **orthogonal and complementary** to many existing advancements in BC, as it can be seamlessly combined with state-of-the-art policy architectures and training objectives (e.g., Diffusion Policy and ACT) to further boost their performance. This position allows AWE to enhance the efficacy of current and future IL methods by providing a more robust and efficient way to structure the learning problem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for **automatically identifying and extracting a minimal set of waypoints from expert demonstrations without any additional human supervision or domain-specific heuristics.** This automatic extraction aims to address the well-known compounding errors problem in imitation learning, particularly behavioral cloning (BC), by effectively reducing the horizon of the learning problem.

The key motivations driving this objective are:

- **Mitigating Compounding Errors in Behavioral Cloning:** Behavioral cloning suffers from the problem of errors compounding over time, where small deviations from the expert trajectory accumulate, leading to task failure, especially in long-horizon tasks. Waypoints offer a solution by breaking down the trajectory into shorter segments, thereby reducing the "effective length" of the decision-making horizon for the learned policy. By learning to reach these fewer, significant waypoints, the policy experiences fewer opportunities for error accumulation.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Manual Waypoint Labeling:** Current approaches to using waypoints often rely on human supervisors to manually label key states or "events" in demonstrations. This process is inherently underspecified, subjective, and highly labor-intensive, making it impractical for scaling to large datasets or diverse tasks. The authors seek to eliminate this reliance on human annotation.
- **Addressing the Generality Issues of Heuristic-Based Waypoint Extraction:** Some methods employ simple heuristics (e.g., zero-velocity points, gripper state changes) to automatically extract waypoints. However, these heuristics are often task-specific and may not capture all critical points, or they might identify irrelevant ones, leading to sub-optimal performance or failure in new scenarios. The motivation is to find a more general and robust criterion for waypoint selection.
- **Leveraging the Effectiveness of Low-Level Controllers:** The core insight is that if a segment of a robot's trajectory can be accurately approximated by simple, linear motion (e.g., straight-line movement in joint or end-effector space), then a robust low-level controller can reliably execute this segment. In such cases, only the endpoints of this linear segment are necessary as "waypoint" targets for the higher-level policy, rather than requiring the policy to learn the precise intermediate states. This offloads complexity from the learned policy to a reliable, pre-existing controller.
- **Enhancing Existing State-of-the-Art BC Methods:** Instead of proposing an entirely new imitation learning algorithm, the authors aim to develop a preprocessing module that can seamlessly integrate with and improve the performance of current state-of-the-art behavioral cloning techniques like Diffusion Policy and ACT. This highlights a motivation for building upon existing successes to achieve even greater capabilities.
- **Minimizing Additional Information Requirements:** A key design principle is to make AWE self-contained, relying only on proprioceptive information (e.g., joint angles, end-effector pose) that is already collected during typical teleoperation demonstrations. This avoids assumptions about external state estimation or requiring new sensors, making the method broadly applicable and easy to deploy.

In essence, the research is motivated by the desire to make imitation learning for robotic manipulation more practical, robust, and scalable by providing an intelligent, automatic way to structure complex, long-horizon tasks through meaningful waypoint extraction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of Automatic Waypoint Extraction (AWE) revolves around decomposing a continuous expert trajectory into a minimal sequence of waypoints, such that linearly interpolating between these waypoints reconstructs the original trajectory within a specified error tolerance. AWE is presented as a preprocessing module that can be "plugged into" any behavioral cloning (BC) pipeline.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup and Definitions

- **Demonstration Data:** An expert dataset `D = {τ0, τ1, ..., τn}` where each trajectory `τi = {(oj, xj)}` consists of paired raw visual observations (`o`) and proprioceptive information (`x`). Proprioceptive information includes end-effector pose or joint angles, and gripper width. In this work, position control is used, meaning `x` also represents the action outputs.
- **Waypoint Definition:** A waypoint `w` is a specific proprioceptive state (`x`) from the expert trajectory. A sequence of waypoints `W = {w0, ..., wL}` represents a simplified version of the original trajectory.
- **Trajectory Reconstruction:** An approximate trajectory `τ̂` is reconstructed by linearly interpolating between the selected waypoints `W`. While linear interpolation is used, the framework could be extended to splines.

#### 3.2. Reconstruction Loss

To quantitatively assess how well a sequence of waypoints approximates the true trajectory, the authors define a **reconstruction loss `L(τ̂, τ)`**. This loss measures the *maximum distance* of any state in the original trajectory from the reconstructed (linearly interpolated) trajectory.
- `L(τ̂, τ) = max_x∈τ_p min_x̂∈τ̂ ℓ(x, x̂)`
 - `τ_p`: The sequence of proprioceptive states from the original expert trajectory `τ`.
 - `x`: A proprioceptive state from `τ_p`.
 - `x̂`: A state on the linearly interpolated trajectory `τ̂`.
 - `ℓ(·,·)`: A distance function. For end-effector positions, L2 Euclidean distance is used. For orientation, axis-angles are converted to quaternions, and spherical linear interpolation (slerp) is used to find projections. Position and orientation distances are summed.
- **Rationale for Maximum Error:** Using the maximum projection error (rather than, e.g., mean error) is crucial because task success often hinges on accurately reaching key states. A low mean error could still hide significant deviations at critical points, leading to failure. Minimizing the maximum error encourages accurate tracking of the entire trajectory, including potentially critical outlier points.

#### 3.3. Waypoint Selection via Dynamic Programming (DP)

The core optimization problem is formulated as: `min_W |W|` subject to `L(f(W), τ) ≤ η`. This means finding the *shortest* sequence of waypoints `W` such that the reconstruction loss (maximum deviation) is below a specified error budget `η`. The waypoints are restricted to be states visited in the expert trajectory `τ`.

This problem is efficiently solved using a **dynamic programming algorithm (Algorithm 1 in Appendix A)**:
- **Base Case:** For any trajectory segment, if linearly interpolating between its start and end points results in a reconstruction loss less than or equal to `η`, then these two endpoints are sufficient waypoints for that segment.
- **Recursive Step:** If the base case condition is not met, the segment is too complex for simple linear interpolation. The algorithm then iterates through every intermediate state within that segment. For each intermediate state `w`, it recursively breaks the trajectory into two sub-segments (before `w` and after `w`). It then finds the shortest waypoint sequences for these two sub-segments. The intermediate state `w` that yields the overall shortest combined waypoint sequence (after merging and deduplicating `w`) is chosen.
- **Memoization:** A dictionary `M` is used to memoize previously computed waypoint sequences for sub-segments, ensuring efficiency.
- **Input:** The DP algorithm takes the expert trajectory `τ`, the error budget `η`, and the memoization dictionary `M`.
- **Output:** The minimal set of waypoints `W` that satisfy the error budget.

#### 3.4. Preprocessing Demonstrations for BC Training

Once waypoints `W = {(w0, t0), ..., (wL, tL)}` are extracted for an expert trajectory `τ`, the original BC training problem (predicting the next proprioceptive state) is transformed into **predicting the next waypoint**.
- To avoid a drastically reduced and sparse training dataset (as `|W| << |τ|`), all observations `(ot, xt)` between two consecutive waypoints `wk` and `wk+1` are re-labeled with `wk+1` as the target waypoint.
- The final augmented dataset `D_τ_waypoint = {(ot, xt, w_next_wp(t))}` is created, where `w_next_wp(t)` is the closest waypoint *after* the current time `t`. This ensures that from any point in the trajectory, the robot is guided towards the *next* critical state.
- This process is repeated for all expert demonstrations, and the augmented datasets are merged to form the final training data for the BC algorithm.

#### 3.5. Integration with State-of-the-Art BC Algorithms

AWE is designed to be a preprocessing module, making it compatible with various BC methods. The authors demonstrate its effectiveness by combining it with two leading algorithms:
- **Diffusion Policy:** Models the conditional action distribution using a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM), which is highly capable of representing multi-modal action distributions.
- **Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT):** Models the policy distribution as a conditional VAE with a transformer architecture, effective for high-frequency, fine-grained manipulation.

#### 3.6. Practical Considerations

- **Multi-Modality:** AWE can introduce multi-modality into the conditional action distribution because different expert demonstrations for similar states might lead to different sequences of waypoints due to the reconstruction error threshold. Therefore, using expressive policy classes (like Diffusion Policy or Gaussian Mixture Models) capable of representing these multi-modal distributions is critical for AWE to yield performance improvements. Less expressive policies may even degrade.
- **Test-Time Execution:** Since waypoints are farther apart than original discrete actions, the low-level controller needs sufficient time to reach predicted waypoints. This might involve adjusting control parameters (e.g., "control multiplier" for Diffusion Policy) or increasing step times for blocking controllers.
- **Meaningful Waypoints:** The DP-based selection naturally identifies "key events" where the robot's motion deviates significantly from linearity (e.g., grasping, inserting), as these points require more waypoints to stay within the error budget. Simpler, linear movements (e.g., free-space reaching) require fewer waypoints.
- **Hyperparameter (`η`) Selection:** The error budget `η` is the main hyperparameter. It's empirically found that there's an optimal range for `η`; too high (too few waypoints) or too low (too many waypoints) can reduce performance. A recommended starting point is to aim for a ratio of 1:8 waypoints to original trajectory length.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors demonstrate the consistent and significant performance improvements achieved by integrating AWE with state-of-the-art behavioral cloning methods across various simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Bimanual Simulation Suite

- **Tasks:** Cube Transfer and Bimanual Insertion, both long-horizon, fine-grained tasks requiring coordination between two robot arms in MuJoCo.
- **Datasets:** Scripted data (50 demonstrations) and human-teleoperated data (50 demonstrations) for each task.
- **Key Finding:** AWE, when combined with ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), significantly outperforms ACT alone and other competitive BC baselines (BC-ConvMLP, BeT, RT-1, VINN).
 - **Success Rate Increase:** AWE + ACT achieved up to a 25% increase in success rate (e.g., on scripted bimanual insertion, Table 1). On average, it increased ACT's success rate by 17%.
 - **Human Data Performance:** Notably, AWE improved performance on human demonstrations, which are inherently more multi-modal. For Bimanual Insertion with human data, ACT improved from 20% to 30% with AWE (a 50% relative increase).
 - **Horizon Reduction:** AWE reduced the effective length of training demonstrations by a factor of 7x to 10x, directly addressing the compounding errors problem.

#### 4.2. RoboMimic Suite

- **Tasks:** Lift, Can, and Square, standard manipulation tasks from the RoboMimic benchmark.
- **Datasets:** Varying numbers of proficient human demonstrations (30, 50, 100, 200).
- **Key Finding:** AWE consistently improved the performance of Diffusion Policy, which is the state-of-the-art method on this benchmark, especially in data-limited scenarios.
 - **Data Efficiency:** AWE's benefits were most pronounced when fewer demonstrations were available. For example, on the challenging Square task with only 30 demonstrations, AWE + Diffusion Policy achieved a 62.3% success rate, an 18% absolute increase over Diffusion Policy alone (44.3%).
 - **Overall Improvement:** Even when Diffusion Policy achieved high success rates (e.g., 90%+ with more data), AWE still offered small but consistent gains, indicating its broad applicability.
 - **Outperformance of Traditional Methods:** Both Diffusion Policy and AWE + Diffusion Policy significantly outperformed older methods like LSTM-GMM and Implicit BC, often with less than half the demonstration data.

#### 4.3. Real-World Bimanual Tasks (ALOHA Hardware)

- **Tasks:** Screwdriver Handover, Wiping the Table, and Coffee Making. These tasks are long-horizon, require precise bimanual coordination, and involve challenging objects (deformable, transparent).
- **Key Finding:** AWE consistently improved the real-world success rates of ACT on all three challenging tasks.
 - **Substantial Gains:** AWE + ACT improved success rates by 8% (Screwdriver Handover), 4% (Wiping the Table), and a substantial 28% (Coffee Making) compared to ACT alone.
 - **Compounding Error Mitigation in Practice:** The authors observed that ACT often failed due to compounding errors leading to inaccurate initial action predictions (e.g., mis-grasping the coffee pod), which cascaded into subsequent failures. AWE, by reducing the decision horizon, allowed for more accurate initial actions and subsequent successful task execution.

#### 4.4. Analysis and Insights

- **Waypoint Selection vs. Error Budget (η):** Visualizations (Figure 4) showed that AWE adapts waypoint density: fewer waypoints are selected for linear trajectory segments, while more are added for complex, non-linear parts of the task. There's an optimal `η` range; setting it too high (too few waypoints) or too low (too many, negating the benefit of horizon reduction) degrades performance (Figure 5).
- **Importance of Expressive Policy Classes:** AWE can increase the multi-modality of the target action distribution. Experiments confirmed that AWE significantly benefits expressive policy classes (e.g., Gaussian Mixture Models or Diffusion Models) which can represent multi-modal distributions. Conversely, it can degrade performance for less expressive, unimodal policy classes (e.g., vanilla BC with MSE loss and a unimodal Gaussian) due to their inability to cover multiple modes effectively (Figure 6).
- **Superiority over Heuristics:** AWE was rigorously compared against common heuristic-based waypoint selection methods (zero-velocity/gripper state changes, fixed-interval sampling). Even in simple replay scenarios, AWE consistently yielded higher success rates, demonstrating that its principled, optimization-based approach selects more meaningful and effective waypoints than naive heuristics (Figure 7).
- **Computational Efficiency:** AWE is computationally inexpensive as a preprocessing step, taking on average only 0.8 seconds to label one trajectory in the Lift task.

In summary, the main findings strongly support AWE as a valuable and robust method for enhancing imitation learning, particularly for complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks, both in simulation and on real robots, by effectively addressing the compounding errors problem through automatic, principled waypoint extraction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research on Automatic Waypoint Extraction (AWE) presents several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of robotic imitation learning:

**Addresses a Core Challenge in Imitation Learning:** AWE directly tackles the well-known and pervasive problem of compounding errors in behavioral cloning. By effectively reducing the policy's decision-making horizon, it makes BC a more reliable and robust approach for long-horizon and complex tasks, which are often the most challenging for learned robotic policies. This is a fundamental improvement that can unlock higher levels of autonomy.

**Enables Fully Automatic Waypoint Extraction:** AWE eliminates the need for labor-intensive, subjective, and unscalable human supervision for waypoint labeling. It also moves beyond brittle, task-specific heuristics, offering a general and principled method that relies solely on the robot's proprioceptive data. This automation is critical for deploying imitation learning systems in diverse, real-world scenarios and for scaling up the amount of training data that can be efficiently processed.

**Enhances State-of-the-Art Behavioral Cloning Methods:** AWE is designed as a plug-and-play preprocessing module. Its demonstrated compatibility and performance gains when combined with leading methods like Diffusion Policy and ACT are highly significant. This means that AWE is not a competing algorithm but a complementary tool that can enhance the capabilities of current and future advancements in policy architectures and training objectives, offering a synergistic approach to robot learning.

**Demonstrates Robustness Across Diverse Tasks and Real-World Scenarios:** The extensive experimental validation, spanning two different simulation suites (RoboMimic and Bimanual Simulation) and three challenging real-world bimanual manipulation tasks, underscores AWE's robustness and generality. The consistent improvements, even on highly dexterous and precise real-world tasks like Coffee Making, highlight its practical utility and readiness for deployment. This bridges the sim-to-real gap by showing benefits in real hardware settings.

**Improves Data Efficiency:** By making the learning problem easier, AWE shows particular promise in data-limited settings, where collecting expert demonstrations is expensive and time-consuming. The marked improvements observed with fewer demonstrations (e.g., on RoboMimic tasks with 30-50 demos) mean that robots can learn complex skills with less human effort, accelerating the development and deployment cycle.

**Provides Insights for Future Policy Design:** The analysis regarding the necessity of expressive policy classes (e.g., multi-modal distributions) when using AWE provides valuable guidance for future research. It emphasizes that while waypoints simplify the temporal aspect of the learning problem, they might introduce new complexities in the action distribution that policies need to effectively model. This insight can drive the development of more appropriate policy architectures.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Wider Adoption of Imitation Learning:** By making IL more robust and easier to apply, AWE could encourage its wider adoption in industrial automation, domestic robotics, and other fields requiring complex manipulation.
- **Faster Robot Skill Acquisition:** Reduced data requirements and improved training stability can lead to quicker development cycles for new robot skills, allowing robots to learn and adapt to changing tasks more rapidly.
- **Foundation for Hierarchical Learning:** AWE provides a practical method for automatically identifying subgoals or key states, which is a fundamental component for effective hierarchical reinforcement learning and task planning in robotics.
- **Safer Robot Operation:** By reducing compounding errors and ensuring adherence to critical trajectory points, AWE could contribute to safer and more predictable robot behavior, especially in environments interacting with humans.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as noted by authors):**
The primary limitation is AWE's reliance on proprioceptive information to define waypoints. This approach may not be suitable for:
- **Torque-controlled robot arms:** Where direct position control is not the primary mode of action.
- **Tasks requiring forceful manipulation:** Where interaction forces are crucial and not solely captured by proprioception.
- **Purely visual navigation or legged locomotion:** Where proprioception alone might be insufficient to define meaningful waypoints for complex tasks.
- **Tasks demanding extreme precision:** A very tight error budget (`η`) might be needed, which would increase the number of waypoints and reduce the benefit of horizon reduction. Future work could involve automatically identifying such precision needs or incorporating minimal human input for critical task segments.
- **Advanced Trajectory Reconstruction:** The current work uses linear interpolation. Future work could explore more sophisticated interpolation methods like splines to potentially achieve better reconstruction with fewer waypoints or a looser error budget.

Despite these limitations, AWE represents a significant step forward in making imitation learning more practical and effective for complex robotic manipulation, laying important groundwork for robust robot skill acquisition.
