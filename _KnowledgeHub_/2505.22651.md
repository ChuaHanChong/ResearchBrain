# Sherlock: Self-Correcting Reasoning in Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The Sherlock framework introduces a self-correction and self-improvement training methodology for Vision-Language Models, enabling them to intrinsically identify and rectify reasoning errors. This approach achieves higher average reasoning accuracy across eight multimodal benchmarks using only 20,000 annotated examples, outperforming baselines that use substantially more data, and consistently improves performance through iterative self-correction.

## Problem

- Current reasoning Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are highly susceptible to error propagation, where an early mistake in a multi-step reasoning process often leads to an incorrect final answer.
- Existing methods for training reasoning VLMs (Supervised Fine-Tuning and Reinforcement Learning) demand vast amounts of high-quality annotated data or accurate external verifiers, incurring high computational and human labeling costs.
- VLMs often exhibit limited generalization, struggling to perform well across diverse domains or problem settings beyond their training distribution, especially where precise supervision is scarce.

## Method

- Sherlock employs a ==multi-stage training framework== that initially cold-starts the VLM with foundational reasoning and a nascent self-correction ability using a joint SFT objective on limited annotated data.
- It refines self-correction through an ==offline preference training stage==, introducing a novel ==trajectory-level objective== that guides the model to revise only erroneous suffixes of reasoning paths, using visual perturbations to construct preference data and a dynamic Beta to stabilize training.
- The framework enables continuous self-improvement via ==iterative online preference training==, where the model generates its own preference data by performing sequential self-correction and applies a self-consistency rule to filter high-quality examples.

## Results

- Sherlock Iter2 achieved an average accuracy of **64.1%** for direct generation and **65.4%** after self-correction across eight challenging multimodal benchmarks, outperforming baselines that utilized **5-13** times more annotated data.
- The framework consistently yielded performance gains from self-correction (up to **1.3%** increase for Sherlock Iter2), unlike existing VLMs which frequently showed accuracy degradation post-correction.
- Sherlock's integrated self-correction objective achieved **0.8%** higher average direct reasoning accuracy than a standard SFT model trained on the same limited **20,000** examples, demonstrating a "free lunch" for performance improvement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Existing SFT and RL-based reasoning VLMs fundamentally struggle with both step-wise and response-wise self-correction, often failing to recover from errors or even degrading performance when prompted to correct.
> - Learning self-correction and direct reasoning are mutually reinforcing capabilities; integrating a self-correction objective into SFT, even with minimal data, can improve direct reasoning performance without additional supervision.
> - Trajectory-level self-correction, which focuses on revising only the erroneous suffix of a reasoning path, is significantly more effective than attempting to rewrite an entire response, leading to more fine-grained and robust error rectification.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has been a rapidly evolving field, demonstrating remarkable progress in understanding and generating human-like text and interpreting visual information. A significant breakthrough in LLMs has been the incorporation of reasoning capabilities, often achieved through "Chain-of-Thought" (CoT) prompting or Reinforcement Learning (RL), enabling models to break down complex problems into step-by-step thinking processes. This has led to substantial performance gains in challenging tasks such as mathematics and code generation.

Inspired by this success, researchers have extended reasoning capabilities to VLMs, aiming to enhance their performance on complex multimodal tasks that require both visual understanding and logical inference. Current approaches for equipping VLMs with reasoning primarily involve Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on large, meticulously annotated CoT datasets or Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques that reward desired reasoning behaviors. While these methods have shown promising results, particularly in domains like multimodal mathematical reasoning, they face critical limitations:

1. **Sensitivity to Reasoning Errors:** In multi-step reasoning, an error in an early step often propagates through the entire trajectory, leading to an incorrect final answer. Current models struggle to recover from such mistakes.
2. **High Data Demands:** Both SFT and RL approaches typically require vast amounts of high-quality annotated data or accurate external verifiers, making data acquisition and curation a bottleneck due to high computational and human labeling costs.
3. **Limited Generalization:** These models often struggle to generalize beyond the specific domains or problem settings for which they were trained, especially when precise supervision or readily verifiable answers are scarce.

Against this backdrop, "Sherlock" positions itself by exploring **self-correction** as a fundamental strategy to address these limitations. While self-correction has been investigated in LLMs, its application to *reasoning VLMs* has been largely underutilized. Existing LLM self-correction methods often involve prompting the model to revise its output, which can sometimes degrade performance, or rely on training additional critic models or on domains with rule-verifiable answers. Sherlock uniquely tackles these challenges by introducing an *intrinsic* and *data-efficient* self-correction mechanism specifically designed for the multimodal reasoning context. It moves beyond superficial prompt-based corrections or reliance on external verifiers, instead integrating self-correction deeply into the VLM's learning process. This work represents a significant step towards developing more robust, autonomous, and data-efficient reasoning VLMs that can identify and rectify their own errors, thereby pushing the boundaries of multimodal AI capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of the "Sherlock" research are to:

1. **Enable Intrinsic Self-Correction in Reasoning VLMs:** Move beyond the current limitations where reasoning VLMs are highly susceptible to error propagation and lack effective mechanisms to identify and rectify their own mistakes within a multi-step reasoning process.
2. **Reduce Dependency on Extensive Annotated Data and External Verifiers:** Overcome the substantial data demands and computational costs associated with training high-performing reasoning VLMs, making the development process more efficient and scalable.
3. **Improve Generalization and Robustness:** Enhance the models' ability to perform well across diverse domains, especially those where precise supervision or easily verifiable answers are scarce, thereby broadening their applicability.

The motivation for these objectives stems directly from the identified gaps in existing reasoning VLM capabilities. The authors observe that current models, whether SFT- or RL-based, struggle with both step-wise and response-wise self-correction. Their initial analysis demonstrates that even when "aha moments" (signs of self-reflection) occur, they rarely lead to correct answers, and external critiques or correction prompts often fail to improve performance, sometimes even causing degradation. This highlights a critical need for VLMs to acquire genuine self-correction capabilities as an inherent part of their reasoning process. By enabling self-correction, the model can inherently improve its outputs, reducing the need for costly external supervision and fostering better generalization. The authors propose that the natural formation of preference pairs (original vs. corrected response) through self-correction can serve as implicit supervision, offering a path towards self-improvement with minimal external data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Sherlock is a novel self-correction and self-improvement training framework designed to enhance reasoning in Vision-Language Models. It operates through three distinct stages:

#### 3.1. Stage I: SFT Cold-start for Reasoning and Self-correction

This initial stage aims to equip the base VLM with foundational reasoning and a nascent ability for self-correction using a limited dataset.
- **Base Model Initialization:** The framework starts with a Llama3.2-Vision-11B-Instruct model.
- **Vanilla SFT:** 10,000 randomly sampled examples from the LLaVA-CoT dataset (denoted as D_A) are used to train a base VLM using standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) loss (Eq. 24). This results in the R0 VLM, capable of generating CoT-templated responses.
- **Sherlock-SFT Dataset Construction:** Another 10,000 randomly sampled examples (D_B) are used. For each example in D_B, the R0 VLM generates a "low-quality" response ($Y_l$), which is then paired with the high-quality ground-truth annotation ($Y_w$) from D_B. These form the Sherlock-SFT dataset.
- **Joint Objective:** The model is then trained on this dataset using a unique pairwise training objective (Eq. 3), $L_{Sherlock-SFT}$. This objective simultaneously encourages direct generation of high-quality responses ($Y_w$) and the ability to self-correct a low-quality response ($Y_l$) into a high-quality one ($Y_w$) given a correction instruction ($t$). This cold-starts the VLM with both reasoning and self-correction capabilities.

#### 3.2. Stage II: Offline Preference Training with Trajectory-level Self-correction

This stage refines the self-correction ability, moving from full-response correction to a more fine-grained, trajectory-level approach.
- **Trajectory-Level Objective:** Unlike previous methods that might rewrite an entire response, Sherlock's objective (Eq. 4, leading to $L_{SC}$ in Eq. 6) focuses on revising only the *erroneous suffix* of a reasoning trajectory. If a mistake occurs at step *i*, the model is guided to correct steps $Y_{\ge i}$ while preserving correct prefixes $Y_{<i}$. This provides a clearer, more targeted learning signal, aligning with the observation that early mistakes can derail the entire reasoning process.
- **Preference Data Construction:** The 10,000 annotated examples from D_A are reused. To create controlled low-quality responses ($Y_l$), a random truncation point *i* is sampled (from summary, caption, reasoning stages). A low-quality suffix $Y_{l \ge i}$ is then generated by applying a random *visual perturbation* ($\epsilon$) to the input image and decoding from the original prefix $Y_{l < i}$. This generates preference pairs $(Y_w, Y_l)$ where $Y_w$ is the original high-quality response and $Y_l$ is its perturbed, low-quality counterpart.
- **Joint Optimization:** The trajectory-level self-correction loss ($L_{SC}$) is jointly optimized with the standard Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) loss ($L_{DPO}$) (Eq. 7), $L_{Sherlock} = L_{SC} + \alpha L_{DPO}$. DPO further enhances the model's direct reasoning ability using the constructed preference data.
- **Dynamic $\beta$ for Stable Training:** To address the sensitivity of preference optimization to the hyperparameter $\beta$, Sherlock introduces a dynamic $\beta$ (Eq. 8). This $\beta$ adaptively scales the learning signal based on the truncation step *i* and visual perturbation level $\epsilon$. Larger $\beta$ values are assigned to samples with wider quality gaps (earlier truncation or stronger perturbation), leading to more conservative updates, while smaller gaps promote stronger learning.

#### 3.3. Stage III: Iterative Online Preference Training with Self-generated Data

This stage enables the model to continuously self-improve without external human supervision, leveraging its acquired self-correction capabilities.
- **Self-Generated Preference Data:** For each unlabeled input question, the current Sherlock model generates an initial response ($Y_1$). This response then undergoes three rounds of self-correction, producing $Y_2, Y_3,$ and $Y_4$.
- **Self-Consistency Filtering:** To ensure high-quality self-generated preference pairs, a self-consistency rule is applied: if the final answers from $Y_2, Y_3,$ and $Y_4$ are semantically identical, then $Y_4$ is chosen as the preferred response ($Y_w$).
- **Rejected Response Generation:** The rejected response ($Y_l$) is constructed by taking the prefix from $Y_1$ ($Y_{1<i}$) and then generating a new suffix ($Y_{l \ge i}$) by injecting visual noise, similar to Stage II. This ensures that $Y_l$ represents a lower-quality version for the preference learning.
- **Iterative Training:** The model is iteratively fine-tuned using the $L_{Sherlock}$ objective (Eq. 7) on these self-constructed preference datasets. The paper reports two iterations, leading to Sherlock Iter1 and Sherlock Iter2 models.

This multi-stage approach, particularly the novel trajectory-level objective, visual perturbation for data construction, and dynamic $\beta$, allows Sherlock to efficiently learn robust self-correction abilities and then leverage them for continuous self-improvement with minimal initial annotated data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The Sherlock framework demonstrates significant advancements in VLM reasoning and self-correction, as supported by comprehensive experimental evaluations.

#### 4.1. Existing Reasoning VLMs Fail to Self-Correct

- **Takeaway 1 (Step-wise Self-correction):** Analysis of SFT-based LLaVA-CoT and RL-based VL-Rethinker on MMStar and MathVista revealed that reasoning VLMs struggle with step-wise self-correction. Even when models showed "aha moments" (self-reflection signals) in less than 10% of cases, these reflections rarely led to a correct final answer.
- **Takeaway 2 (Response-wise Self-correction):** Both self-correction prompts and external critiques (from models like Critic-V or Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct) largely failed to improve the reasoning trajectory in existing VLMs. In some instances, accuracy even declined after correction, highlighting a fundamental inability to effectively revise responses without explicit training for this capability. LlamaV-o1, in particular, showed a sharp degradation after correction prompts, suggesting that its multi-turn reasoning format was disrupted.

#### 4.2. Advancing Reasoning Ability with Minimal Annotation

- **Superior Performance with Less Data:** Sherlock Iter2, trained on only 20,000 randomly sampled annotated examples from LLaVA-CoT, achieved the best overall reasoning performance across eight challenging multimodal benchmarks (MMBench-V1.1, MMVet, HallusionBench, MMMU, MMStar, AI2D, MathVista, MME). It reached an average accuracy of 64.1 with direct generation and 65.4 after self-correction.
- **Outperforming Data-Heavy Baselines:** This significantly outperforms prominent baselines that utilize substantially more annotated data: LLaVA-CoT (100k annotations, 63.2% avg.), Mulberry (260k, 63.9% avg.), and LlamaV-o1 (175k, 63.4% avg.).
- **Self-Improvement Effectiveness:** The online iterative training successfully improved both direct reasoning accuracy (from 63.2% for Sherlock Offline to 64.1% for Sherlock Iter2) and self-correction gains (from 1.2% to 1.3% increase), validating the efficacy of self-generated preference data and iterative self-improvement.

#### 4.3. Sherlock Further Enhances Performance via Self-Correction

- **Consistent Self-Correction Gains:** Unlike existing models, Sherlock consistently showed performance gains after multiple rounds of self-correction. Sherlock SFT improved accuracy by 0.8%, Sherlock Offline by 1.2%, Sherlock Iter1 by 1.2%, and Sherlock Iter2 by 1.3%. This confirms that the trajectory-level objective is effective in teaching fine-grained, intrinsic self-correction.
- **Superior to Other Inference-Time Scaling:** Sherlock's self-correction capability (65.4% accuracy) outperformed other inference-time scaling strategies (Table 3): Majority Vote @8 (65.1%) and methods relying on external critics like Qwen2.5-VL-7B (64.9%). This highlights the robustness and efficiency of Sherlock's internal self-correction mechanism. Critically, the quality of external critics heavily influenced their effectiveness, whereas Sherlock's internal mechanism proved more reliable.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies on Training Objective

- **Self-correction and Reasoning are Mutually Reinforcing (Finding 1):** Ablation studies showed that models trained only with the self-correction objective improved reasoning accuracy, even without explicit direct generation supervision. Conversely, DPO-only models showed some self-correction gains despite limited signals. This suggests that learning to critique and correct inherently improves the underlying reasoning capabilities.
- **Trajectory-level Objective is Superior (Finding 2):** The trajectory-level self-correction (revising only the erroneous suffix) significantly outperformed full-response correction (setting i=1, rewriting the entire response), especially in online iterations. This validated that focused corrections on relevant parts of the reasoning chain are more effective.
- **Dynamic $\beta$ Stabilizes Training (Finding 3):** Incorporating the dynamic $\beta$ consistently improved both direct generation and self-correction performance across iterations, demonstrating its contribution to stable and robust preference learning.

#### 4.5. Data Efficiency Justification

- **Integrated Self-correction as "Free Lunch":** Comparing Sherlock SFT (20k data) with a standard SFT model (LLaVA-CoT with 20k data), Sherlock SFT achieved 0.8% higher average accuracy in direct reasoning. This demonstrates that integrating the self-correction objective into SFT, even with limited data, significantly enhances direct reasoning, effectively acting as a "free lunch" for performance improvement.
- **Maximizing Preference Data Use:** Sherlock-Offline further improves over Sherlock-SFT. Its comprehensive loss function (Eq. 6) not only guides positive self-correction but also discourages negative self-correction and encourages reproducing correct reasoning. These complementary signals fully leverage the preference data for fine-grained and robust self-correction.

#### 4.6. Efficiently Scaling Up Sequential Self-Correction with Verifiers

- When combined with a verifier (MM-Verify), Sherlock’s sequential self-correction achieved higher accuracy (55.9% on MathVista) while drastically reducing GPU usage (8.7 hours) compared to self-correction without a verifier (13.2 hours) and parallel voting methods (40.2 hours). This highlights its potential for efficient inference-time scaling for complex reasoning tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Sherlock framework marks a significant milestone in the development of Vision-Language Models by introducing the first effective approach for **intrinsic self-correction in reasoning VLMs**. Its contributions extend beyond mere performance boosts, fundamentally altering how VLMs can be trained and deployed for complex tasks.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Enabling Robust Reasoning:** By teaching VLMs to identify and correct their own errors at a fine-grained, trajectory level, Sherlock addresses a critical vulnerability of current reasoning models—error propagation. This makes VLMs more reliable and robust for multi-step reasoning tasks, especially where intermediate steps are crucial.
2. **Unlocking Data Efficiency:** Sherlock's ability to achieve state-of-the-art performance with a minimal amount of annotated data (20k examples, less than 20% of baselines) is a game-changer. This drastically reduces the computational and human labeling costs associated with training powerful VLMs, democratizing access to high-performance models and accelerating research in data-scarce domains.
3. **Facilitating Self-Improvement:** The iterative online preference training mechanism allows Sherlock to continuously enhance its reasoning and self-correction abilities without any further external human supervision. This self-improvement loop is crucial for developing truly autonomous and adaptive AI systems that can learn and refine their skills over time.
4. **Novel Training Paradigms:** The introduction of a trajectory-level self-correction objective, visual perturbation for preference data construction, and dynamic $\beta$ offers novel methodological insights for preference learning in multimodal contexts. These techniques can inspire future research in fine-grained feedback and adaptive training for complex sequence generation.
5. **Efficient Inference-Time Scaling:** The integration with verifiers demonstrates that Sherlock's self-correction mechanism can be efficiently scaled during inference, providing higher accuracy at a lower computational cost compared to traditional parallel sampling or critic-based approaches.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Broader VLM Applications:** The improved robustness and data efficiency of Sherlock can make VLMs more practical for a wider range of real-world applications, including complex scientific problem-solving, detailed image analysis in medicine or engineering, and advanced human-AI interaction scenarios where precise and corrigible reasoning is essential.
- **Foundation for Future Reasoning Models:** Sherlock provides a strong foundation for future research into more sophisticated reasoning mechanisms in VLMs. The concept of intrinsic self-correction can be extended to other modalities or integrated with different reasoning paradigms (e.g., symbolic reasoning).
- **Reduced Resource Constraints:** By significantly lowering the data requirements, Sherlock lowers the barrier to entry for developing powerful reasoning VLMs, enabling smaller research groups or institutions with limited resources to contribute to this rapidly advancing field.
- **Inspiration for Other AI Domains:** The principles of trajectory-level self-correction and self-improvement through preference learning, especially with self-generated data, could potentially be adapted to other complex AI tasks beyond VLMs, such as robotics, decision-making, and long-form content generation.

In conclusion, Sherlock represents a pivotal advancement in VLM research, demonstrating that self-correction is not only feasible but also a profoundly powerful strategy for creating more intelligent, robust, and resource-efficient multimodal reasoning systems. Its approach offers a promising direction for developing AI that can truly learn from its mistakes and continuously refine its understanding of the world.
