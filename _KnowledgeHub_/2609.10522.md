# Show-Harness: Just a VLM Agent Can Play Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Show-Harness is a model-agnostic embodied harness that lets foundation VLMs control robots directly through a compact set of discrete semantic action units (view-relative end-effector steps, rotations, grasp/release), grounded by embodiment-specific deterministic interpreters. Perception, reasoning and action plugins close the loop. Zero-shot Gemini-3.1 Pro reaches 89% cross-task success and a LoRA-tuned 2B model 86%, beating VLA and agentic baselines.

## Problem

- VLA models fine-tune VLM backbones to regress embodiment-specific continuous actions, collapsing broad pretrained semantic knowledge into an opaque sensorimotor mapping that needs fresh trajectories per embodiment.
- Hierarchical and programmatic systems preserve semantics but surrender physics: downstream controllers realize intent, so the model never makes fine-grained physical decisions, and each grounding pipeline is system-specific.
- Agentic harnesses expose whole skills, controllers or VLAs as opaque primitives, so the agent decides what to do while an executor decides how, weakening intent-to-execution coupling.

## Method

- ==Semantic action space==: discrete view-relative MV_*, ROTATE_CW/CCW, GRASP, RELEASE and DONE units, each an incremental bounded end-effector step, predicted in the VLM's native vocabulary without action heads.
- ==Embodiment-specific interpreters== deterministically map each unit to a 6-DoF Cartesian setpoint update with calibrated increments, rotation frames, workspace projection and safety limits; a new robot needs only a new interpreter.
- ==Perceive-reason-act plugins== (Multi-View Guidance, Proprioception, Subtask and Situated Planning, Action Chunking, Adaptive Step, Visual Prompt, Action History, Failure Recovery) plus **GUMI**, a GUI collecting demonstrations in the same space.

## Results

- Cross-task success **89%** zero-shot and **86%** fine-tuned, versus **57%** for the code-as-policy agent, **50%** VLA-centric agent and **39%** for pi-0.5; cross-embodiment **93%**/**87%**.
- Sim-to-real: the fine-tuned 2B model scores **13/20** from simulated demonstrations alone while both VLA baselines score **0/20**; unseen **90-degree** rotation extrapolation reaches **70%** versus **20%**.
- Plugin ablations: removing subtask planning drops success **96%** to **60%**, failure recovery to **72%**, proprioception to **68%**; arbitrary symbols without written conventions collapse to **5%**, **23.3%** inferred mappings correct.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating semantic decision from metric execution lets precision change by editing interpreter step size (2cm to 1cm) with no retraining, where regression policies need new fine-grained data.
> - Written conventions describing an action's physical effect, not evocative names, carry most of the grounding; models cannot reliably infer action semantics from before-and-after observation changes.
> - Incremental discrete units compose to extrapolate beyond demonstrated ranges; backbone scale plateaus early, with 2B balancing precision against responsiveness better than 9B.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of advanced vision-language models (VLMs) into robotic control systems is a significant challenge in embodied AI. Foundation VLMs possess extensive world knowledge, including object recognition, spatial relationships, and long-horizon goal decomposition. However, translating this high-level semantic intelligence into precise, low-level physical robot actions remains problematic.

Current approaches to VLM-based robot manipulation generally fall into two categories:
1. **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models**: These methods typically fine-tune VLMs to output embodiment-specific continuous actions. This process often converts broad semantic knowledge into an opaque sensorimotor mapping, which can necessitate repeated adaptation for new tasks, environments, or robot platforms. This approach prioritizes direct control but can lose the semantic richness of the original VLM.
2. **Hierarchical and Programmatic Systems**: In contrast, these systems use VLMs to generate higher-level abstractions, such as subtasks or programs over predefined control APIs. While this preserves the semantic reasoning capabilities of VLMs, the actual physical execution is mediated by downstream controllers. This can weaken the direct link between the VLM's semantic intent and its physical realization, potentially leading to a disconnect between planning and execution.

Agentic robot systems, which keep the VLM within a closed perception-decision-execution loop, represent a further evolution, differing primarily in how decisions are translated into actions (e.g., composing API calls, selecting skills, or steering learned policies). The design of the interface between the VLM and the physical system is a central question in these paradigms, with previous work noting varying performance based on abstraction levels.

Show-Harness positions itself by proposing an interface that bridges these two approaches. It aims to provide an action space that is both semantically interpretable by VLMs and sufficiently fine-grained for direct physical control. This allows the VLM to maintain direct responsibility for fine-grained physical decisions while leveraging its semantic understanding, without requiring embodiment-specific pretraining or excessively abstract programmatic control. The work also connects to developments in digital and simulated agents that use compact, interpretable actions, extending this principle to real-world manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research is the observation that while foundation VLMs exhibit broad intelligence, this intelligence does not readily translate into effective robot control. The paper identifies a gap: existing VLA models often sacrifice VLM semantic knowledge for low-level control, while hierarchical systems delegate physical execution, diminishing the VLM's direct involvement in fine-grained physical decisions.

Against this background, the key objectives of the Show-Harness work are:

- **Develop a suitable interface for VLM-robot interaction**: The primary objective is to design an action space that is simultaneously semantically meaningful for VLMs to reason over and sufficiently fine-grained for direct physical control by robots. This interface is intended to be generalizable across different robot embodiments.
- **Enable direct utilization of frontier VLMs**: To demonstrate the capability of leveraging existing, potentially closed-source, powerful VLMs for robot control in a zero-shot manner, without requiring extensive fine-tuning. This aims to provide a scalable path that benefits from continuous advancements in foundation models.
- **Facilitate lightweight adaptation of smaller VLMs**: To show that even compact, open-source VLMs (e.g., 2B-scale models) can be effectively adapted for robot control with minimal computational resources (e.g., a few GPU-hours of fine-tuning), offering a cost-effective deployment strategy.
- **Unify human and agent control for data collection**: To create a graphical user interface (GUMI) that allows both humans and VLM agents to operate robots and collect demonstrations using the same semantic action space. This aims to simplify demonstration collection, enable cross-embodiment data reuse, and support human-agent collaboration without specialized teleoperation hardware.
- **Demonstrate robust generalization**: To evaluate and confirm that Show-Harness-equipped VLM agents exhibit strong generalization capabilities across diverse tasks, environments, and different robot embodiments.
- **Analyze interface properties**: To conduct studies revealing the key properties and advantages of the proposed VLM-robot interface, particularly in terms of physical and semantic adaptability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Show-Harness operates as a model-agnostic Embodied Harness, integrating foundation VLMs into an iterative perception–reasoning–action loop for robot control.

#### 3.1. Show-Harness Overview

At each step, the system receives multi-view visual observations and robot proprioceptive states (I𝑡, 𝑝𝑡). These inputs, along with interaction history, are processed by configurable reasoning plugins to create a refined context (𝑐𝑡). The VLM then selects a semantic action (𝑎𝑡) from a predefined action space. This semantic action is deterministically grounded into executable robot control (𝑢𝑡) by an embodiment-specific interpreter (𝑔𝐸), which updates the robot and environment.

#### 3.2. Physically Grounded Semantic Action Interface

The core of Show-Harness is a compact set of discrete semantic action units (A) designed to be both interpretable by VLMs and directly executable by robots.
- **Semantic Action Space**: This includes actions for end-effector translation (MV_FWD/BACK, MV_LEFT/RIGHT, MV_UP/DOWN), rotation (ROTATE_CW/CCW about X, Y, or Z axes), and gripper control (GRASP, RELEASE). A DONE action indicates task completion. Key properties of this vocabulary are:
 - **Incremental**: Each unit induces a small, localized physical change, enabling precise control through successive corrections.
 - **Interpretable and Embodiment-agnostic**: Actions are symbolic, abstracting away embodiment-specific low-level control details, making them reusable across different robots.
 - **Visually Grounded**: Movement directions are defined relative to observable camera views, facilitating VLM spatial reasoning.
- **Embodiment Grounding**: An embodiment-specific interpreter (𝑔𝐸) translates each semantic action 𝑎𝑡 into robot-specific control 𝑢𝑡. For movement actions, this involves updating a 6-DoF Cartesian pose setpoint (𝑥𝑡, 𝑄𝑡) based on calibrated translation (𝜎𝑡) and rotation (𝜃𝑡) increments, mapped into the robot's motion frame (𝑅𝐸). A projection (Π𝐸) enforces workspace and step limits. Gripper actions directly map to open/close commands. This modular design means only the interpreter needs adaptation for new robot embodiments, keeping the VLM interface consistent. Safety bounds can be configured within the interpreter to prevent unsafe actions.

#### 3.3. Embodied Harness Architecture (Plugins)

The harness employs modular plugins across three stages:
- **Perception**:
 - **Multi-View Guidance**: Informs the VLM on the use and prioritization of different camera views (e.g., global for scene context, wrist for fine-grained alignment).
 - **Proprioception**: Converts robot state (e.g., gripper height, contact status, step displacement) into textual feedback for the VLM.
- **Reasoning**:
 - **Subtask Planning**: Uses the VLM to decompose tasks into ordered subtasks with visual completion criteria. The VLM advances the plan during execution.
 - **Situated Planning**: Defers uncertain decisions until relevant visual information becomes available, enabling adaptive replanning for conditional tasks.
 - **Action Chunking**: Reduces model query frequency by allowing the VLM to emit short sequences of actions to be executed open-loop when fine-grained feedback is not critical.
 - **Adaptive Step**: Dynamically adjusts translation step size (e.g., larger steps for distant targets, smaller for close-range alignment).
 - **Visual Prompt**: Uses a dedicated VLM call to convert ambiguous verbal targets into visual references, grounding subsequent reasoning.
- **Action**:
 - **Action History**: Provides recent actions as lightweight context to prevent oscillatory behavior.
 - **Failure Recovery**: Detects grasp failures and triggers corrective actions, such as resetting the gripper and reattempting the grasp.

#### 3.4. Two Modes on One Interface

The semantic interface supports two operational modes:
- **Frontier VLM as Zero-Shot Agents (ZS mode)**: Closed-source frontier VLMs (e.g., Gemini-3.1 Pro) can directly control robots without any fine-tuning.
- **Fine-tuning Small VLMs (FT mode)**: Smaller, open-source VLMs (e.g., Qwen3.5-2B) are fine-tuned using low-rank adaptation (LoRA) on demonstrations collected in the shared semantic action space. The policy minimizes token-level cross-entropy for semantic action prediction.

#### 3.5. GUMI: A GUI-based Manipulation Interface

GUMI (GUI Manipulation Interface) provides a unified platform for human and agent interaction. It exposes the same discrete semantic action units through a graphical interface, allowing human teleoperation via keyboard or agents to directly predict and execute these actions. GUMI records pre-execution observations and selected semantic actions, creating policy-ready demonstration data. It supports step-wise control, action chunking, single/dual-arm operation, and mixed human-agent data collection, enabling remote data collection and cross-embodiment data reuse.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted demonstrate the efficacy of Show-Harness across various dimensions, comparing its Zero-Shot (ZS) and Fine-Tuned (FT) modes against representative VLA, VLA-centric agent, and Code-as-policy agent baselines.

#### 4.1. Generalization across Task, Environment, and Embodiments

As detailed in Table 2, both Show-Harness ZS and FT modes consistently achieved higher success rates than all baselines across three generalization categories:
- **Cross-Task**: On 10 diverse object-receptacle tasks (including held-out objects like teddy bear and chess piece), ZS achieved an average success rate of 89.0%, and FT achieved 86.0%, significantly higher than baselines (e.g., 𝜋0.5 at 39.0%, GR00T at 35.0%, CaP-X at 44.0%, RATS at 57.0%).
- **Cross-Environment**: Under variations in background, lighting, viewpoint, and distractors, ZS maintained 100.0% average success, and FT 88.0%. For sim-to-real transfer, trainable baselines (𝜋0.5, GR00T) failed (0/20), while FT, trained solely on simulated demonstrations, achieved 13/20 success.
- **Cross-Embodiment**: The system demonstrated effective transfer between a 7-DoF Franka arm and a 6-DoF AgileX arm. ZS achieved 93.0% average success, and FT 87.0%, compared to baseline averages ranging from 11.0% to 52.0%. This highlights the effectiveness of the embodiment-agnostic semantic interface.

#### 4.2. Capability Analysis: Physical and Semantic Adaptability

The research further probed the system's ability to adapt to new demands without policy retraining or with minimal adjustments (Figure 6).

- **Physical Adaptability**:
 - **Fine-grained control**: By simply reducing the interpreter step size from 2 cm to 1 cm, ZS success improved from 60% to 82% on stacking and peg insertion tasks, and FT from 40% to 65%. Baselines required re-training to achieve comparable performance.
 - **Action composition**: Composing two orthogonal translation units into a diagonal displacement reduced execution steps on Plate tasks without sacrificing success.
 - **Rotation extrapolation**: ZS remained robust on a carrot-grasping task across 0°, 45°, and an unseen 90° orientation. FT achieved 70% success at 90° despite training only on 0° and 45°, outperforming continuous action regression baselines.
 - **Workspace shift**: Show-Harness-equipped agents degraded only mildly when the workspace expanded from 25% to 90% of its range, indicating less sensitivity to distribution shifts.
 - **Multi-arm coordination**: A joint policy for two AgileX arms on "tidy the table" and "pass the banana" tasks substantially improved success and eliminated collisions compared to independent single-arm agents.

- **Semantic Adaptability**:
 - **Reasoning-intensive tasks**: For tasks like finding a hidden block or spelling "SHOW," ZS with Situated Planning achieved 85% success, significantly outperforming FT (10%) and 𝜋0.5 (0%) without this plugin. With Gemini-generated subtask instructions, FT's success rose to 70%, while 𝜋0.5 remained at 5%.
 - **In-context learning from video**: When given a video demonstration of object ordering, ZS achieved 20/20 success on a "tidy up" task, following the demonstrated order, compared to 20% without the demonstration. FT also reached 95% with a task outline extracted by the planner.

**5.3. Ablation Studies** (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10)
- **Frontier VLM Choice and Thinking Effort**: Stronger frontier VLMs generally improved zero-shot performance, tracking their inherent capabilities. Increasing "thinking effort" primarily reduced redundant interaction steps rather than substantially boosting success, and could incur higher costs. Errors were primarily in fine-grained grasping and placement, not planning.
- **Fine-Tuned Backbone Scaling**: Performance was strong with 2B-parameter models (Qwen3.5-2B), offering a balance between precision and responsiveness. Larger models mainly benefited fine-grained tasks like stacking and peg insertion, while smaller models could be more responsive for tasks with moving objects.
- **Harness Plugins**: Each plugin contributed to overall performance. Multi-View Guidance and Proprioception were critical for spatial perception and disambiguating visual cues. Subtask Planning was essential for multi-stage tasks. Action Chunking and Adaptive Step improved efficiency. Visual Prompt and Situated Planning significantly boosted performance in specific scenarios requiring handle-aware grasping or targeted exploration, respectively. Action History prevented oscillatory behavior, and Failure Recovery improved robustness by detecting and correcting empty grasps.
- **Action-space Representation**: Explicit conventions defining action effects were more crucial than semantic names for grounding. Arbitrary symbols with conventions performed almost as well as semantic names with conventions, while arbitrary symbols without conventions severely impaired performance, indicating that inferring action effects from visual changes alone is challenging for VLMs.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative examples (Figure 11) demonstrated Show-Harness's ability to handle diverse real-world manipulation scenarios, including novel objects, background and lighting variations, cluttered scenes, spatial reasoning tasks (e.g., spelling "SHOW"), and bimanual control (e.g., opening a drawer and placing an object). This illustrates the reusability of the semantic action interface across varied task structures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Show-Harness represents a notable advancement in the field of embodied AI, particularly in bridging the gap between the high-level intelligence of foundation vision-language models and the demands of low-level robot control.

**Key Contributions and Implications:**

- **Unlocking VLM Capabilities for Robotics**: Show-Harness offers a novel semantic action interface that enables VLMs to directly participate in fine-grained physical decisions. This approach allows existing foundation models to exert control over robots, mitigating the need for extensive embodiment-specific pretraining typically associated with VLA models. It suggests that a significant portion of the embodied capability required for robot manipulation is already present within foundation VLMs, awaiting an appropriate interaction interface.
- **Scalable and Flexible Deployment**: The framework supports two critical deployment paradigms. Firstly, it allows for zero-shot robot control using powerful, often closed-source, frontier VLMs without any fine-tuning. This provides a direct pathway to leverage the rapid advancements in large-scale foundation models. Secondly, it enables efficient adaptation of smaller, open-source VLMs with minimal computational cost (a few GPU-hours), making VLM-driven robot control accessible for lower-cost and on-device deployments.
- **Unified Data Collection Paradigm (GUMI)**: The introduction of GUMI provides a standardized, accessible, and flexible method for collecting robot demonstrations. By using the same semantic action space for both human teleoperation and agent rollouts, GUMI facilitates cross-embodiment data reuse and promotes human-agent collaborative data collection. This could significantly reduce the costs and technical barriers associated with acquiring diverse and high-quality robotic datasets.
- **Robust Generalization and Adaptability**: The demonstrated generalization across tasks, environments (including sim-to-real transfer), and different robot embodiments, alongside its physical and semantic adaptability, suggests that Show-Harness-equipped agents are more robust to real-world variations compared to previous paradigms. This robustness is attributed to the semantic nature of the VLM-robot interface and the architectural plugins.

The work's findings indicate that strategic interface design is a critical factor in translating foundation model intelligence into practical robot behaviors. By focusing on a compact, interpretable, yet physically grounded action space, Show-Harness offers a promising direction for developing more capable, adaptable, and deployable robotic systems in the future.

**Limitations and Future Directions**:

The paper acknowledges that the current evaluation is primarily focused on single and dual-arm manipulation with parallel-jaw grippers. Future work could explore extending this framework to more complex robot embodiments, such as humanoids or dexterous hands. Additionally, integrating richer embodied modalities like tactile and force feedback could enhance the system's capabilities for contact-rich and even finer-grained physical interactions.
